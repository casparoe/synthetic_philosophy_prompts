I teach a seventy-five-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics, with fourteen students, and I want the whole session to be a game rather than a lecture. Design it for me.

The phenomenon I want them to feel is specific: that a mathematical existence claim can be proved while no one in the room can produce the thing claimed to exist, and that some inference steps we treat as automatic silently depend on being able to decide a case. They should hit that wall themselves, and they should not hear the name of the tradition until the debrief.

Here is the spine I have in mind. Develop it, tighten it, and repair whatever is broken in it. The session is a proof market. Students work in pairs as provers, and I act as the Verifier. There is a public Ledger on the board. Three rules govern it. First, a claim of the form "there exists an x such that F(x)" is entered only with a specific object written next to it as a witness; without a witness it is not accepted. Second, a claim of the form "P or Q" is accepted only if the pair commits to one disjunct and can defend that one. Third, a refutation of a universal claim, reached by assuming it and deriving a contradiction, is accepted on the strength of the reasoning alone, with no witness required. Pairs score points for accepted claims, and there is a large bonus for the target theorem, which is this: there exist irrational numbers a and b such that a raised to the b is rational.

Most pairs will find the familiar two-case argument, and the interesting moment is the one where the rules stop them: they can get everything except the final move, because the case split turns on a number whose rationality nobody in the room can settle, and because naming a witness would require settling it. Let that moment land before I say anything.

Then design a second, shorter round around the gap between the two negative claims. I want a round in which the group can establish that not every member of some collection has a property, by reductio, while being unable to establish that some member lacks it. You choose and specify the collection, but it must be genuinely inaccessible to enumeration or querying, or the round collapses into ordinary search. Give me the setup, the exact claim forms the Ledger will and will not accept, and why the second claim stays out of reach.

Deliver the following: the full rules, the materials, the scoring scheme with no real money, the roles, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes. Give me a short script for the Verifier's refusals, so that my rejections read as principled applications of the Ledger rather than as pedantry. Then the debrief, which is where the naming happens: map each Ledger rule onto the piece of the idea it encodes, say what the failed proof in round one was actually leaning on, and explain what the two rounds together show about a reading of the connectives in terms of what counts as having established them rather than in terms of truth and falsity.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle each failure. Include at least the following: pairs trying to register a witness they cannot identify, students treating the undecided property as decidable, accusations that the Verifier is being unfair, and the risk that round two becomes a search problem. Finish with an honest note on which feature of the idea this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

Write the response in American English.
