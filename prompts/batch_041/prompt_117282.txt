I write about science and ideas for a small magazine, and next month I'm doing a long sit-down Q&A with a theorist who has spent years defending a strong version of the claim that self-replication and Gödel-style self-reference aren't just two things that happen to rhyme — they're one phenomenon seen twice. Her favorite exhibit is typogenetics, the toy system where you've got strings built from the four DNA letters and a string does double duty as instructions: you read off pairs of letters to get enzymes, each enzyme does one little typographical move (cut the strand here, delete this chunk, copy that one, swing the two halves around, flip a strand into its complement, stop), and the puzzle is to find a strand whose own enzymes, turned loose on that very strand, hand you back a copy of it. That, she says, is the DNA case and the Gödel case in one breath, since Gödel's trick also takes a string and threads it through its own guts until it spits out a sentence that talks about itself. I think she's overreading the analogy, and I dont want the Q&A to become her TED talk.

So: be my question-writer. You're a philosopher who knows both proof theory and molecular biology — I don't want to ask anything a logician or a biochemist would wince at. Brainstorm privately first about where this analogy is actually load-bearing and where it's just a nice picture, then give me eight to ten questions, grouped into runs of two or three, where each question comes with the follow-up I'll need for the answer she's most likely to give.

I want at least these three threads. First, one that makes her say whether the isomorphism is supposed to live in the syntax or in the meaning: a self-replicating strand never has to refer to itself, it just gets copied, whereas Gödel's sentence has a truth value and says of itself that it can't be proved — so is she trading on a pun? Second, one on the fact that a human designed typogenetics by hand to replicate, and what a rigged toy can really tell us about a chemistry nobody designed (and I'd like the follow-up in case she says the point was never about DNA but about any system rich enough to carry a description of itself). Third, one on what would break the claim: a self-replicating system with no self-description anywhere in it, or the suspicion that the thesis is unfalsifiable as stated. Then whatever else you think is sharpest — I'd rather have nine good ones than twelve padded ones.

At the end, tell me which of these questions you'd bet against, where the analogy might survive everything I throw at her, and what she could say that would actually change your mind, and mine, about whether it's deep.
