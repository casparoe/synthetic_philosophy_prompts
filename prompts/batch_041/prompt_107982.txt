Hey — I'm a 4th-year PhD student in political theory, and I've got a teaching problem I can't crack on my own. I TA a 90-minute undergrad section (about 16 students) for a course on freedom and its critics. They've read Berlin on negative vs positive liberty and some Pettit/Skinner on freedom as non-domination, and it is all just sliding off them. They can recite "non-interference vs non-domination" back to me word for word, but they don't feel why anyone would say a person who is never actually interfered with could still be unfree, or why the positive ideal of being your own master is supposed to be something to be scared of. I want to stop telling them and make them walk into it.

The thing I want them to feel, first, is the republican claim: that you can be unfree while nobody is getting in your way, because someone has the standing to get in your way if the mood took them. The old seed of this is the thought that a well-treated slave, or the subject of a kindly absolute monarch who happens not to bother him, is still not free. Second beat: the positive-liberty claim that "being your own master" can curdle into being ruled by a collective self you don't identify with, and that a majority telling you it's doing this for your own freedom doesn't make it feel like freedom.

Can you design me a runnable in-class game that does this? Rules, roles, materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and stakes that don't need real money (chocolate, points, bragging rights, whatever). Here's what I actually want out of it:

I want the republican point to land as behavior, not as a definition. So set it up so that some participants are under someone's discretionary power — the power-holder could override them at any time but has been told (privately, by me) not to. Then I want to watch whether the people under that power start deferring, hedging, softening their asks, checking the power-holder's face before they speak — even though the power-holder never once uses the power. Build in a way to actually test whether that happens rather than assuming it, because if they *don't* defer, that's just as good a discussion (maybe the negative-libertarian is right that with no interference there's no unfreedom to speak of). I'd rather the activity be able to come out either way and have the debrief handle both.

Then a second act for the positive-liberty side: some round where the group has to reach a collective decision that binds everyone, and one or two participants end up outvoted on something that matters to them. I want them to feel the pull in both directions at once — this is us ruling ourselves, vs this is a "self" I don't recognize bossing me around.

A couple of specific things I'd love baked into the mechanics if you can:

- Some way to stage the eyeball test at the end — basically, ask people whether they could look the power-holder in the eye without any reason for fear or deference, and treat that as a concrete measure of something. I think that'll land harder than any definition.
- A moment where the power is "friendly" but there's no way to contest it or push back on it, vs a version where you can. I have a hunch the contestable-but-still-powerful version feels different, and I want to see if the students feel it too.

Don't let the words "positive," "negative," "republican," "domination" or any of the names appear until the debrief. I want them to describe what happened in their own words first, and then I map their descriptions onto the vocabulary.

In the debrief, walk me through which moment in the game corresponds to which bit of the idea, and be honest with me about (a) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and (b) which feature of the republican (or positive-liberty) idea the game simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself by talking. I suspect the answer to (b) has something to do with the fact that my students can always leave the room, and that a game is not a life — but tell me what you think the real limitation is.
