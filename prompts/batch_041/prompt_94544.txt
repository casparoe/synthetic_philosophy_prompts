Consider the following thought experiment, which I'll ask you to reason about from scratch rather than recall.

Imagine a book that is a complete description of a human being. It is not a biography; it is a device. For every possible stream of sensory inputs a human body could receive, the book specifies the motor outputs that body would produce. A reader — a mechanism, or a person following instructions — supplies the current input, the book's entry for that input tells the reader what to do, and the body acts accordingly. Call the book, together with its reader and its body, the Registry. By construction, the Registry is behaviorally indistinguishable from an ordinary person across any finite span of time: ask it anything, watch it do anything, and it responds exactly as that person would.

Now vary two things about how the Registry is built, holding its behavior fixed.

First, the grain of its storage. In the flat version, the book is a single lookup table: one entry per possible input history, with no entry sharing any structure with any other. In the compact version, the same input-output map is stored as a program — loops, subroutines, and reused intermediate results — so that a small number of pages is consulted over and over, and the same page plays a role in generating many different outputs.

Second, the rate at which it runs. In the ordinary version, the book is read at the pace of ordinary thought. In the glacial version, a single moment of the Registry's life takes a century of external time to execute, so that the entire life is spread across geological time; nothing internal is faster or slower relative to anything else, and the input-output map is unchanged.

Write an essay on whether any of these versions is a mind — whether there is something it is like to be the Registry, or to be the book itself. Weigh the case for the view that behavioral and functional equivalence settles the matter, so that all four versions stand or fall together, against the case for the view that consciousness depends on features the book lacks no matter how it is organized — the right kind of causal grain, the right kind of physical realization, or the right relation between the parts and the whole. Give particular attention to whether the flat-versus-compact distinction and the ordinary-versus-glacial distinction are relevant to mentality, or whether each is a merely implementational detail that a correct theory should ignore. Reach a verdict: say which versions, if any, you take to be minds, and defend the principle that yields that verdict against the strongest objection you can construct. Do not simply survey the positions; commit to one.
