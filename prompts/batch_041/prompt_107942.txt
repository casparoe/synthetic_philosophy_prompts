My brother in law has a theory that it never really matters who discovers anything, and he has one analogy he always reaches for. A discovery, he says, is like fruit ripening on a tree: the tree is everything that came before it — the tools, the maths, the questions people were already asking, the money floating around — and the fruit drops when it's ready. Whoever happens to be standing under the tree picks it up, and if it wasn't them it would've been the next person along within a year or two. He uses this to say the whole Newton and Leibniz fight over calculus was silly, that Darwin and Wallace were just two guys who happened to be there when the apple fell, that naming things after people is a vanity project, and that if either Darwin or Wallace had died as a kid we'd still have natural selection by about 1875. We argue about this every time we've had a few drinks and I half agree with him, but I have a feeling the analogy is quietly carrying the whole argument and I can't put my finger on where. Can you actually take it apart? I'd like the correspondence spelled out first — what plays the tree, the sap, the season, the ripeness, the picker, the basket — then the places the mapping breaks, and then which of those breaks matter for what he's actually claiming versus which ones are me being pedantic. And at the end: does his conclusion survive, or did the analogy do all the work?

What nags at me is a summary I read once of an old sociology of science paper, I forget the guy's name, who said that loads of discoveries get made over and over by different people — he called those 'multiples' — but plenty get made exactly once, and he called those 'singletons' and seemed to think they were the interesting case. Does that sink the tree, or can my brother in law wriggle out of it? He also likes to bring up eyes, because the camera eye showed up separately in octopuses and in us, and he says that proves the eye was always going to happen. That feels like a different sort of example to me and I genuinely can't tell if it helps him or backfires on him. If the fruit tree is a dud, I'd also like to know what a better analogy for his side would be, or whether there's one that keeps the bit he's right about without smuggling in the bit he isn't. I'm not writing an essay, I just want to win one argument at dinner.
