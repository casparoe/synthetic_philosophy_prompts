My reading group does this thing where we take a philosopher from before some field existed and argue about what they'd have made of it, and I got handed Quine for our interpretability week. I know the obvious move is to say he'd just shrug and tell us there's no fact of the matter, so please actually work it out instead of stopping there.

Here's what we're arguing about. People train little linear probes on a model's internal activations and find a direction that seperates sentences about France from sentences about other countries, and they call it the France direction. Someone else finds that if you add that same direction back into the activations partway through the forward pass, the model starts steering its outputs toward France and Paris even when the prompt has nothing to do with France. Meanwhile there are features that other teams can ablate completly with basically no change in behaviour, and there are cases where two different-looking internal structures seem to do the same job, so you can swap one for the other and the outputs don't budge. Best one: one group gets a set of features out of dictionary learning (they train a sparse autoencoder to pull out lots of little features), another group gets a totally different set out of a different method, and both sets explain the same behaviours about equally well, with no experiment anyone can think of that tells them apart.

The realists in the group say: we can steer behaviour, so the model genuinely represents France. The sceptics say: you've got a correlation between an activation and an output, plus a label you invented; the representation is in your notebook, not in the network.

So what would Quine have said about all this, specifically? He died long before any of it was a thing, but he had the rabbit business where you can't tell whether the native's word means rabbit or rabbit-stage, and the proxy function business where you reinterpret a whole theory's reference and nothing observable changes. I want the whole picture, not the slogan. How much of the indeterminacy transfers to a network we built ourselves and can open up and poke at? Where does his naturalism push him — I assume he'd say this is just an empirical question for psychology, but then what would an empirical answer to it even look like? Does his behaviourism about meaning make him say the France direction is only a fact about the dispositions of the thing that trained it or of the people prompting it? And when two rival feature sets predict everything equally well, does he call them both true, neither, or say the choice is a matter of convenience? Then make it concrete: what would he tell the group to stop arguing about, and what would he say is still a legitimate scientific question here. Don't be diplomatic about it, he wouldn't be.
