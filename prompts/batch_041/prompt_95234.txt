I'm writing a short piece for a reading group that's half Simone Weil people and half democratic theorists, and the hook is a city that doesn't exist. Imagine a mid-sized city that has abolished political parties outright: no slates, no whips, no manifestos, no permanent organisation, nothing you could join. What it has instead is two layers. First, a standing citizens' panel of a few hundred people picked by lot, serving staggered one-year terms, which sets the agenda and drafts the proposals that go to a vote. Second, a phone app through which any resident can hand their vote, issue by issue, to any other resident, revocably and at any time -- you can take it back the next morning, split it across five people, and nobody ever holds your vote as of right. The detail I keep coming back to is that the app also shows, live, how many delegations each proposal has attracted and which arguments the heaviest delegators are backing, so the shape of the room is always visible before you decide. My Weil friends say this is the nearest thing anyone has built to what she wanted; my democratic theory friends say it's a horror show she'd have recognised instantly. Weil died in 1943, so she never saw anything like it, and that's precisely why I want the question taken seriously rather than waved away as anachronistic.

What I want is your best reconstruction of what her view would actually have been, and I want it specific rather than a vibe. Would she have welcomed the abolition of parties here, or would she have said that abolishing the party only clears the ground and leaves untouched the real problem -- which, on my reading of her, is the way a collective can heat a person's thinking until they stop thinking at all, a party being essentially a device for manufacturing collective passion, so that a party is a function rather than a form and a fluid delegation network might just be a party in liquid form?
