I need help ending a stupid arguement that's been going on in my friend group for about a year, and I think the only way out is to make the two people involved write something together.

The setup: Ana is convinced Sweden is the more liberal country. Bjørn is equally convinced it's Norway. Neither of them is a crank — they've both lived in both places, they both read too much, and they've now had this fight maybe forty times. It always goes the same way. She'll say something about the state staying out of peoples private lives, he'll come back with something about alcohol rules, or about what you're allowed to say in public, or about schools or taxes or the EU, and then they're each answering a question the other one didn't ask, and someone changes the subject. I'm the one who ends up refereeing and I'm sick of it. I don't have a dog in this fight and I don't trust my own impressions either.

I want you to act as the neutral chair of an adversarial collaboration between the two of them — a comparative political sociologist who has worked on both countries and is used to getting people who despise each other's conclusions to co-author a document. Think carefully before you write anything, and don't rush to a verdict; the verdict is the last thing, not the first.

What I want, roughly in this order:

1. First, brainstorm on the page the different things "liberal" could mean in this dispute, and say which of those senses actually distinguishes Sweden from Norway and which ones don't. I've been inventing my own labels and I have no idea if they're any good: permission-liberalism (what the law forbids you to do), option-liberalism (what you can actually do, given money, housing, licences, state monopolies, planning rules), deviance-tolerance (how much social punishment you eat for living unlike your neighbours), and exit-liberalism (how cheap it is to walk out of a job, a marriage, a church, a town). Use them if they help, scrap them if they don't, and tell me whether Ana and Bjørn's fight is partly about different senses of one word rather than about the countries. If part of it is verbal, say exactly which part.

2. Then the joint statement — the document they could both put their names to. Start with a numbered agreed-claims section: claims about the two countries, and about what "liberal" means for this exercise, that both of them would sign without crossing their fingers. Be careful here: an agreed claim that's true by definition, or so vague nobody could object, is worthless. I want the claims that would make a partisan of the losing side a little uncomfortable but that they can't deny.

3. A disagreements table, one row per real disagreement, with a column giving each side's position in wording that side would accept as its own (not a strawman, not "my opponent absurdly thinks..."), and a column for what each side thinks is at stake in it.

4. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, give a concrete test, case, or comparison that BOTH of them agree in advance would bear on it, and record each side's prediction before anyone goes and looks. For instance I'd like them to commit in advance on a set of specific vignettes — someone who wants to open a bar on a residential street, a family that wants to homeschool, a person who wants to smoke, someone who wants to publicly insult a religion, someone who wants to work 80 hours a week, someone who wants to live off-grid — with each camp saying which country is more permissive in each case and by how much. But don't just take my list; invent the tests you think are the right ones, and be explicit about what result would embarrass each side. Where the honest answer is "the two countries are close enough that there's no fact of the matter," say so.

5. One thing I keep coming back to, and I want it treated as a real crux rather than a throwaway: both countries are small, rich, high-trust, and until recently quite homogenous by world standards, and I suspect that in a place like that, norms get enforced socially rather than legally — so a country can look extremely permissive on paper and still be quite conformist in practice, a polite kind of pressure that never shows up in a law library. The reverse might also be true: a country with stricter laws might be full of people quietly ignoring them. Say how that hypothesis could be tested, and what each side would predict about it.

Keep it concrete and grounded in things you actually know about the two countries, but skip the reading lists and citations — I'm not going to look anything up, I want the reasoning. Where you're unsure of a fact, flag it and say how much the argument leans on it. And don't let them off the hook: if the collaboration ends without a verdict, that's fine, but then give me a clear statement of what further facts would settle it and who's more likely right on the evidence we have.

Then, after everything else, one last line in exactly this format:

Verdict: <Sweden|Norway|unresolved> — <the sense of "liberal" the verdict is about, in a few words>
