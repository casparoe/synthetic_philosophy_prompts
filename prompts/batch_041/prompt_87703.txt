I'm a philosophy grad student and I've spent the last month defending a view I've been calling threshold negative utilitarianism, and a friend of mine who does decision theory just handed me an argument that it either collapses into the flat "any suffering, however tiny, trumps any pleasure" version or is committed to something incoherent, and I can't tell if he's right. Background on my view, so you can see what's at stake: I think pleasure and pain are commensurable in ordinary cases -- a mildly annoying commute can be worth it for a good sandwich at the end, a mild headache is worth a nice afternoon -- so small amounts of suffering can be compensated by small amounts of pleasure and vice versa. But I also think there's a threshold of suffering above which nothing compensates: once you're at, say, prolonged torture, no quantity of good afternoons, no matter how many people or how many hours, makes it worth it. Below the threshold, hedonic trade-offs work normally; at or above it, suffering lexically dominates. I'd really like to keep both halves of this. Here's his argument, laid out:

(1) Let T be the threshold: suffering at or above T cannot be outweighed by any amount of pleasure, while suffering below T can be outweighed by some finite amount of pleasure. (2) Consider suffering at level T − ε, a hair below the threshold. By (1), there's some finite amount of pleasure that compensates for it. (3) Consider suffering at level T itself. By (1), no finite -- or even infinite -- amount of pleasure compensates for it. (4) The experiential difference between T − ε and T is arbitrarily small; they differ only in the tiniest increment of intensity. (5) So my view says that a difference of a hair in the intensity of suffering flips the compensation required from "some finite amount" to "no amount whatsoever." (6) But nothing in the nature of the experiences themselves distinguishes T − ε from T; whatever reason I give for putting the threshold at T (it's where suffering becomes unbearable, dehumanizing, whatever) applies just as well to T − ε. (7) If that reason applies to T − ε, it applies to T − 2ε, and so on all the way down. (8) So by my own lights every level of suffering is a threshold, and nothing compensates anything. (9) Hence threshold negative utilitarianism collapses into flat negative utilitarianism.

He's convinced (6)–(7) is where the real work is, and that if I resist it I owe him an account of why the threshold sits exactly where it does and why the tiny increment across it does all that work -- he says the same reasoning would dissolve any threshold anywhere, like the line between hot and cold, so either I accept there are no thresholds or I find what's special about mine. I don't know if he's right and I'm too deep in it to see straight. Where does this go wrong, if it does? Name the step you'd reject, say why it looked fine, and price the alternatives -- I'd like to keep both halves of my view, so tell me what it costs to keep the threshold, and seperately what it costs to keep ordinary commensurability below it, if I can't have both.
