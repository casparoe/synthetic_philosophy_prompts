I keep running into a move that I dont have a word for, and everyone I ask says it's just the old thing about not tearing down a fence when you don't know why it's there, which I don't think is quite it. Two flavours. One: a council wants to scrap a weird old bylaw, and the reply is that nobody remembers why it's on the books, which is exactly why it has to stay -- so the older and more opaque the rule gets, the stronger the case for keeping it, even when everyone privately agrees it does nothing good anymore. Two: the same people, when someone proposes a new rule, say we can't possibly foresee what it would break, so we shouldn't try. Either way 'we can't know' lands on the side of whatever already exists, and the burden falls on whoever wants to change things, in a form they could never discharge, because the evidence they'd need is by definition gone. Is there a name for that specific move -- treating the fact that a rationale can't be recovered as evidence that the rationale was good -- and how does it differ from ordinary caution about change, from just arguing from ignorance, or from the precautionary principle? I'm not after a label. I want to know where the line is between the bad version and the respectable one, because sometimes the fence really is load-bearing and nobody knows why.
