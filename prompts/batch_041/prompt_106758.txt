A chapter in a popular history of economic thought summarizes the socialist calculation debate roughly as follows. In the 1920s, Ludwig von Mises argued that a socialist economy is impossible because central planners cannot possibly gather and process the enormous quantity of information required to allocate resources rationally. Without market prices, he claimed, planners would be reduced to calculation in kind — literally weighing tons of steel against bushels of wheat — which is conceptually incoherent. Friedrich Hayek later added that even if the data could be collected, it would be too dispersed and too rapidly changing for any central authority to use. The debate was then apparently settled in socialism's favor by Oskar Lange, who showed that a Central Planning Board could set prices by trial and error, raising them when shortages appeared and lowering them when surpluses appeared, while managers followed two simple rules: minimize average cost and produce up to the point where marginal cost equals price. On this account, Lange's scheme would reproduce the efficient allocation of a competitive market. Hayek, the chapter says, eventually conceded that Lange's model was theoretically sound and retreated to the weaker claim that socialism would fail for political and incentive reasons rather than for any deep problem with economic calculation. The chapter concludes that the calculation debate was a draw on pure theory, and that the historical failures of socialist economies are best explained by authoritarian politics rather than by any inherent defect in planned allocation.
