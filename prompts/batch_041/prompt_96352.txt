I chair the hiring committee in a philosophy department of about thirty, and I have stopped trusting how we reach our recommendation: five to seven members, four or five candidates a year, one vote that shapes the department for a decade, and a process in which we read and interview sequentially, then deliberate in order of seniority. I teach social epistemology, so I know the Condorcet result and the independence assumption it rests on, and I know the cascade literature well enough to worry: under a flat prior with conditionally independent signals of accuracy p greater than one half, the second agreeing report is already sufficient to make it rational for everyone after to ignore their own evidence — at which point the later agreement is evidence of nothing at all, and can be produced just as reliably by reputational caution as by genuine conviction. Cascades of that kind are also fragile in the way that matters to me: one small public remark, from the chair especially, can reverse the whole thing. And I know that groups reliably pool the information everyone already shares while neglecting what only one member knows.

What I want is a procedure, not counsel: something my committee can actually run. In what order do we elicit and record judgments before anyone speaks? What may the first round contain? How do we treat members who have read the candidate's work against those who have only met them for an hour? What counts as a reason to reopen a settled judgment, and what do we do when the discussion converges too fast for the convergence to be credible? It has to be usable by colleagues who have never heard of a cascade, run in the two hours we actually have, and end in a recommendation I have to sign.

Then run it on three cases: one where the first two readers are enthusiastic and everyone afterwards agrees; one where a single member holds genuinely private information the rest lack; and one where the procedure returns an answer I will not want to hear. Mark the steps that are judgment dressed as procedure, and tell me what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether anything in it can address the possibility that our signals were never independent to begin with.
