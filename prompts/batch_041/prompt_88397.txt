I chair the memorial review commission in a mid-sized American city. Two or three times a year we are petitioned about a statue, a school, or a street named for a historical figure, and we must decide whether it stays as it is, stays with added interpretation, is moved, or comes down. We are volunteers — a retired teacher, a librarian, a shop owner, a pastor, and an adjunct history instructor — with no professional training in ethics or historiography. Our ordinance requires a written finding in each case, applied consistently across cases, and the city council can overrule us, so whatever we write has to survive a public hearing and fit on two pages.

What I need from you is not a verdict on any figure but a procedure we can actually run: roughly an hour of meeting time plus a week of research by one member.

- The questions to ask, in the order we should ask them.
- For each question, what a sufficient answer looks like, what kind of evidence would supply it, and when we should treat the evidence as too thin to lean on.
- The points where the procedure stops and returns a no — a figure we should not honor no matter what else is true of him — and the points where it stops and hands the matter to the council because what remains is a disagreement about values rather than a question of fact.
- How to handle the two arguments we hear in every case: that he was a man of his time, and that he also did things we admire.
- How to handle our archive problem. In some cases we can show that the figure's own contemporaries were already making the objection; in others we find no trace of anyone objecting. We don't know how much weight that difference deserves, or how much of it is an artifact of whose letters got kept.
- What to do with the fact that some of our cases concern men honored for the very thing that is now objected to, while others concern men honored for something else entirely, whose offense we discovered by accident.
- Where a bright-line rule is defensible and where it is only a way of avoiding thought. One member wants a rule so we never have this argument again; another says every case is different.

Then run the procedure on Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus, showing the answer at each step and where the procedure runs out. At least one of the three should come out against my own inclination, which is to keep things up and add context; I want to see that the procedure is not merely reproducing my taste.

Finally: tell me which of your steps are value judgments wearing the costume of findings of fact, tell me which cases the procedure cannot settle at all, and tell me the likeliest way it will be misused by a commission that has already decided what it wants.

Write in American English, and keep every term in the procedure to something we could defend out loud in a public hearing without a philosophy degree.
