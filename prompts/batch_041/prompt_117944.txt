I sit on the disability advisory panel that our legislature has convened to comment on a draft assisted-dying bill. The panel includes people with progressive neuromuscular conditions, several lifelong wheelchair users, two palliative care physicians, and me — a philosopher recruited to keep the discussion honest. One member argues that the bill is too narrow, since it would extend assistance only to the terminally ill and exclude people who are not medically dying but say their lives are finished; others regard precisely that extension as what they most fear.

The phrase that does the most work in our meetings, and does it in opposite directions, is "Nothing about us without us." One faction uses it to argue that we should oppose the bill outright, since a statute permitting physicians to end the lives of disabled people is a decision about us taken over our heads. Another faction uses the same phrase to argue that we should support a narrower bill, since a law confining assistance to the terminally ill is itself a decision about us — a judgment, made without us, about which of our lives are worth living. A third member adds a further turn: any of us who asks for assistance speaks in a voice shaped by a society that has always devalued us, so the request cannot be taken at face value. Each faction accuses the others of abusing the slogan, and I have been asked to draft the panel's minority report. Before I can write a sentence of it, I need to know what the slogan can be claiming.

Set out the distinct theses those words could be expressing, ordered from the nearly vacuous to the genuinely radical, and for each say what would have to be true for it to hold and whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim that is simply false. I am chiefly interested in the different work that "about" and "us" can do: whether the slogan makes an epistemic claim (that no one outside a group can know what is good for its members), a procedural claim (that no decision affecting a group is legitimate without that group's participation), a representational claim (that only members may speak for the group), or a claim about authority over one's own life — and, if it is several of these at once, where they come apart. For each reading, say which faction needs it, whether it actually yields their conclusion, and whether it proves too much: which readings would also condemn decisions nobody disputes, such as a guardian's consent for a patient who will never wake, or a clinician's decision about a patient who refuses further treatment. Where a reading is defensible but weaker than any faction wants, say exactly what it does and does not license. Lay this out as a numbered menu with a verdict on each rather than a recommendation; I do not need references.
