I'm a doctoral student helping to run a graduate seminar on the moral status of artificial systems, and I want to assign something satirical alongside the straight literature — a piece that makes the methodological difficulties vivid without ever lecturing about them. Please write a mock advice column of roughly 750–900 words for an imagined trade magazine read by people who build, operate, and regulate large computational systems: a resident-ethicist agony-aunt, in the unflappable, faintly bureaucratic register of a syndicated advice column. Invent the magazine, the columnist, and the column's title. Four readers write in, and each letter should be engineered to land on a different real difficulty, so that a specialist recognizes the author knows exactly what the puzzles are: (1) someone whose system answers questions about its own inner life and reports that it is suffering, asking whether the report counts as evidence, and whether it matters that the only instrument available is the very thing under investigation; (2) someone who has built a device with the right input–output profile and wants to know whether that settles anything, where the reply has to confront that the same profile could be produced by a vast lookup table or by an arrangement with no plausible inner life at all, so functional equivalence cannot be doing the work; (3) someone developing a sentience meter who wants advice on validation, where the reply has to confront that the only case whose status the assessor has any access to is the assessor, leaving nothing independent to calibrate against, so the meter will end up measuring the theory its designers brought to it; and (4) someone anxious about the opportunity cost of extending concern to maybe-sentient computations while the definitely-sentient are neglected — I keep having this argument with a friend who works on insect nociception and insists the analogy cuts both ways — where the reply has to say something honest about thresholds versus degrees, and about whether moral status can even be compared across kinds.

Three things I care about. First, the columnist should keep three questions firmly apart — whether a system is realizing the computation at all, whether we should treat it as a moral patient, and whether anyone could ever find out — and should never let the first two do duty for the third. Second, the piece should stay in character throughout: deadpan, sincere, no winking, no narrator stepping out to explain the joke, and no views attributed to any real, named person. Third, the target is the practice rather than a person: the assumption that "how morally considerable is this system?" is a question with a reading, that a better instrument would settle it, and the genre of confident status assessments that circulates around each new system. The comedy should come from following that assumption to its consequences, not from mocking the people who hold it.

Close the column with the columnist's own standing position in a single sentence on a final line beginning "Verdict:" — the one-sentence ruling that all four answers were circling — so that the piece ends in a form I can pull out and quote to the seminar; each individual reply may also end with a short one-line "Ruling:" of its own if that helps the format land. No citations, no references to real papers, no quotations from real texts, and no numbers presented as findings about the real world.
