I've been arguing with a friend about the St. Petersburg game for about three months now and we havent moved a millimetre. You know the setup: flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if the first head is on flip n you win 2^n. Expected value is infinite, everyone knows that, and everyone also knows nobody will pay more than a few dollars to play it. Fine, that part isn't in dispute.

His position is that the only fix that actually works is to require utility to be bounded. His argument: the log-utility move you learn first isn't a solution, it's a coincidence. Log is unbounded; it just happens to tame this particular payout schedule. Rig the payout so it grows double-exponentially instead of exponentially and you break any unbounded utility function, log included. So the game is a proof that unbounded utility is incoherent rather than a puzzle about money. He also likes a variant where the payoffs alternate sign, so the series can be rearranged to sum to any number you want, and he says that shows the trouble was never really about infinity.

My position is that this is overkill. The standard game can't be offered by anyone -- nobody has an infinite bankroll -- so "infinite expected value" is a fact about a game that doesn't exist, and demanding that a decision theory be immune to every conceivable fast-growing payout schedule is demanding that it handle things that can't happen. I also think the real mistake is upstream: assuming expected value gets to decide a one-shot gamble where you're most likely walking away with two dollars. Log utility fixes that, and it's a fix, not luck.

There's a third friend in the group who thinks the whole paradox is just a fact about how people are wired and has nothing to do with rational choice, and honestly I can't tell whether she's answering the same question we are.

Every argument is on the table and nobody budges. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A verbal one about what "rational" is even supposed to mean? Three people answering three different questions under one name -- what's wrong with the decision theory, what's wrong with the game, why do people refuse? Or is a hidden formal question doing the work underneath? My guess, and it's only a guess, is that it comes down to what a decision theory is for: a description of an ideal agent's preferences, which has to be total over every conceivable gamble, versus a guide for a finite agent with finite money, which only has to handle gambles someone could actually offer. If that's the diagnosis then no theorem is going to settle it, and I'd rather you say that plainly than pretend there's a resolution waiting.

Diagnose it, don't adjudicate it. Be concrete about which parts of this are actually settled by a proof -- I think at least one of them is -- and which parts aren't formal at all. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a result, a change of question. Or argue that there isn't any and we should all go do something else. And skip the history of who said what, I dont need the reading list.
