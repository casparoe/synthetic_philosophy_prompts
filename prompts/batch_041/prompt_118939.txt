I'm a doctoral student in political economy, and I'm writing a chapter on the normative interpretation of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. I'd like an essay that takes and defends a position on a question I keep circling: which of the two things the theorem is usually said to undermine is actually undermined, the possibility of non-dictatorial collective choice, or the demand for strategy-proofness itself? I know the theorem and I know that it entails Arrow's, so please don't spend the essay restating the result, walking through a proof, or surveying the literature. What I want is an argument with a verdict, and one that engages the three things that make me suspect my own instinct, which is that strategy-proofness is the thing to give up.

The first is domain restriction. Once the admissible preferences are restricted, strategy-proofness stops forcing dictatorship: on a single-peaked domain, the rule that picks the median of the reported peaks is strategy-proof, anonymous, and not dictatorial, and on domains where preferences are separable across independent issues, the strategy-proof rules turn out to be, essentially, the ones that settle each issue separately, by a fixed family of winning coalitions for that issue. If the impossibility is driven by the richness of the preference domain rather than by collective choice as such, then it is a theorem about modeling assumptions that we chose.

The second is randomization. If lotteries are allowed, the rules that are anonymous, strategy-proof, and ex post efficient are exactly the random dictatorships: pick a voter uniformly at random and implement her most preferred alternative. I want a verdict on whether that is an escape from the theorem or a restatement of it in probabilistic dress, given that every realized outcome is still somebody's top choice and the dictator is chosen by a die rather than by an electorate.

The third is scale. The gain from a misreport can only materialize if that report is pivotal, so if the chance of being pivotal falls as the electorate grows, the gain from manipulation falls with it, which would make approximate rather than exact strategy-proofness the right requirement. Press on whether an incentive that is vanishingly small for each voter is small in the aggregate, and on whether approximate strategy-proofness is doing real normative work or merely moving the impossibility one step out.

Then take a side on the question I opened with. Say which assumption you think is doing the impossible work, what has to be true of an actual political institution for your verdict to be the right one, and what you would have to concede if it turns out not to be. A clean argument with visible costs is worth more to me than a balanced survey that lands on it depends.

Write it as continuous prose. No headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italics for emphasis, no tables. Paragraphs only, the way you would write a long letter.
