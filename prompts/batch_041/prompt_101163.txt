PhD student in criminology here, though my work is basically legal phil with a stats habit. I've been deep in the weeds on how capital sentencing actually works in the US and there's a cluster of features I can't get to line up philosophically. Would love your take, no cites needed.

Here's the practice as I understand it. After a conviction, the same jury has to find at least one statutory aggravating factor before death is even on the table, that's the narrowing bit, death is supposedly reserved for the worst offenders. Then the defense can put on basically anything as mitigation, and the jury has to weigh it and make an individualized moral call about this particular defendant, the law insists the sentence reflect who this person is and what his life has been, not just the bare act. Some jurisdictions also let the jury treat lingering residual doubt about whether he actually did it as a reason to spare him, even though the guilt phase is over and they already found him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. And then on appeal a lot of states have proportionality review, where a court has to ask whether this death sentence is out of line with what other juries got in similar cases.

What I want: what philosophical commitments does this whole package presuppose? My instinct is the individualization part screams retributivism, desert attaches to this person's whole life, not to an act in isolation, but then proportionality review seems to say desert is comparative, that what this guy deserves depends on what a jury two counties over did with a vaguely similar case, which is nuts on a clean retributive view. And residual doubt seems to presuppose something weird, that the moral warrant for killing someone can be undercut by an epistemic doubt that never met the threshold for acquittal, which smells like a view about how certainty has to scale with the severity of the act (the old Blackstone ratio thing, better to let ten guilty go than one innocent suffer, applied with extra force when the penalty is irreversible?). And narrowing-to-the-worst presupposes we can rank people on some scale of depravity, which I'm not sure is a coherent thing.

So reconstruct the implicit theory for me. Which features strictly presuppose a particular view of desert or justification, and which are just pragmatic hedges that several theories could license? Does the package actually commit to retributivism, or is it a mash of retributive and consequentialist (deterrence, error-cost) elements that don't belong together? Is there any single consistent theory that would license all four features at once, or is the practice philosophically underdetermined such that rival theories can each claim it? And the counterfactual: if we swapped in a clean retributive theory, or a clean deterrence theory, or a pure rule-of-law/consistency theory, which of these four features would have to go and which would survive?

Also tell me if I'm wrong that the individualization vs proportionality thing is a genuine tension, or if there's a standard move that dissolves it. I want the presuppositions laid bare and a verdict on whether the thing hangs together.
