I am a software engineer with no formal training in economics or philosophy, but over the past year I have become involved with a local group campaigning for ranked-choice voting. Next week I am scheduled to give a thirty-minute talk to our city’s election reform coalition, and I would like your help preparing for the question period. My thesis is that the ranked-choice voting system we are advocating is not strategy-proof, and that the campaign’s central promise—that voters can safely rank candidates in their true order of preference—is misleading. I plan to invoke the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem to show that any ranked voting rule with three or more candidates that is not dictatorial can be manipulated by some voter, and I will argue that our local proposal is no exception. I also want to propose an alternative: a two-stage “deliberative revision” system in which voters submit an initial ranking, see the aggregate first-round results, and then have one opportunity to revise their ranking before the final tally. My claim is that this extra round reduces strategic voting because it lets voters coordinate on a consensus without having to guess what others will do.

The audience will be about thirty people, and I need to be ready for the objections they will actually raise. Most will be campaign volunteers who care deeply about ranked-choice voting but have no training in social choice theory; they will hear my talk as an attack on a cause they have invested in. Two local mathematicians will be there, and they know the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem well; they will likely find my “deliberative revision” proposal ad hoc and will press me on whether it really escapes the theorem or merely relocates the manipulation. A retired political science professor who has published on electoral systems will also attend; she is a strong advocate of ranked-choice voting and will object that my theoretical point is irrelevant to practical politics. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I am most likely to face, ordered by how much damage they would do to my position, each phrased in the vocabulary of the audience member who would raise it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
