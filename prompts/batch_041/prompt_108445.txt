My friend Clara has been proselytising for Tolstoy's aesthetics ever since she read What Is Art?, and she now issues verdicts on everything we watch or listen to together. Her working statement of the theory runs roughly like this: art is not the production of beautiful objects but the transmission of feeling, in which an artist who has once felt something calls that feeling up in himself and fixes it in external signs so that others are infected by it; how much feeling gets across depends on the novelty or individuality of the feeling, on the clarity with which it is conveyed, and above all on the artist's sincerity, which is what separates real art from the counterfeit; and the counterfeit is recognisable by its reliance on borrowing from other works, on imitation and the accumulation of realistic detail, on striking effects, and on the mere interest of the story it tells. She adds that this first account of art is deliberately amoral — a cruel feeling sincerely conveyed is still art — while the question of good art is a separate one, answered by content: a work is good insofar as the feeling it transmits draws people together in love and brotherhood rather than dividing them. She also takes it as damning that the celebrated art of the educated classes is unintelligible to ordinary people, who on her view ought to be able to be infected by anything worth calling art.

Her record so far. She calls a phone recording of an old woman singing a lament for her drowned village great art — sincere, plainly and clearly felt, and nobody needs training to be moved by it. She calls a much-praised, exquisitely acted and scored film about a family disintegrating bad art, because it works through borrowed cinematic devices and our curiosity about the story, and because the feelings it deals in are the fashionable feelings of its class rather than the filmmaker's own. She calls an intricate choral piece that moves her deeply, but that her grandmother could make nothing of, bad art, on the ground that art which cannot reach ordinary people has failed at the one thing art does. And she calls a crude, cheaply made ballad that a whole pub sings along to real art — it transmits a feeling, and does so without pretence — though she stops short of calling it good art, and when I pressed her on why, she changed the subject.

Now the new case, which she has not heard. A singer I follow has released a plain, unadorned song, written and performed by her in one sitting, which she says she means every word of; it has spread everywhere and reliably reduces audiences to tears. The tune quotes a phrase from a well-known hymn, and the feeling it carries is a longing for revenge on the group that killed the singer's brother. Predict what my friend will say, and identify which element of her stated theory is actually doing the work in her earlier verdicts. Then set out the rival rules that fit those verdicts just as well — including any under which her practice quietly parts company with the theory she recites — say what each predicts about this song, give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and name the further case, something we could put to her next, that would tell the rival rules apart. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say exactly where it does and what she would be committing herself to either way. Keep the whole thing under 700 words; I want the argument, not a survey of the literature.
