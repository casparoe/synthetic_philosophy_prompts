Hey — I run a small fiction night for a philosphy-of-mind reading group and I need one more prompt for the handout. I want a short story, 1500-2500 words, that's genuinely about higher-order theories of consciousness — the family of views on which a state counts as conscious not in virtue of what it's like in itself but because you're representing it to yourself in the right way, either with a higher-order thought or with something more like an inner sense that scans your own states. Everyone in the group knows the standard objections, so I dont want a story that just stages them like a diagram with legs. I want it to live in the weird part.

Please stick close to this setup: a near-future clinic fits people with a meta-prosthetic, a small implant that manufactures higher-order representations of the wearer's own mental states; it was made for people whose inner life had gone flat. Now three threads. Nadia: after the implant she has crisp higher-order awareness of pains, cravings and moods, except the first-order states aren't there — the implant is producing the higher-order representation anyway. She's certain she's in agony and every first-order measure says she's fine. Sam: the first-order state is plainly there, say a real injury signal, but his implant is off, so nothing represents it to him; he's flat, reports nothing, while his body does everything a body in pain does. And the calibration tech, who has started running an implant on a rock and getting readouts, and who keeps saying that's obviously nonsense while being unable to say why without giving up something the theory needs.

The story should make the reader feel the question rather than hear it asked: if being conscious of a state just is having the right higher-order representation of it, which of these two is the one who's conscious — the woman aware of a state she doesn't have, or the man with a state he isn't aware of? Whichever way you push it, someone owes an account of why the other case isn't consciousness. And the rock: if it can run there too, what's actually doing the work? It's also fine if the higher-order representation itself turns out to be unconscious in the story, since some versions of the view allow that and others don't — that disagreement is fair game for the characters to have.

No philosopher name-drops, no citations, no lecture scene where someone explains the theory to a newcomer. Let the plot and the arguments between the characters carry it, and let the ending stay unresolved in a way that's earned rather than a shrug. And please, no twist where it turns out the whole thing was a simulation.
