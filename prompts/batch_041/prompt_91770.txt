I chair the ethics committee at an academic medical center that also runs the organ procurement organization for our region, and before Thursday's meeting I have to circulate a written opinion on whether we should adopt a protocol for thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion in controlled donation after circulatory death. The mechanics, briefly, are these. After life-sustaining treatment is withdrawn and the patient's heart stops, we observe a standoff — the draft before us says two minutes, our current policy says five — and if no rhythm returns, death is declared on circulatory grounds and recovery begins. What is new is the step after that: the surgical team opens the chest, clamps or ties off the vessels that would carry blood to the head, cannulates the aorta and the inferior vena cava, and starts an extracorporeal circuit that reoxygenates and reperfuses the abdominal organs, and in some versions the heart itself, so the organs are spared the extra warm ischemia of a conventional recovery. The objection I keep hearing, from a transplant surgeon on my committee and from a colleague on the regional network's ethics group, is that the clamping gives the game away: we take an affirmative step whose only purpose is to guarantee that circulation never returns to the brain, which would be pointless if we truly believed circulation had ceased irreversibly. The usual reply is that irreversibility was never a physiological threshold — a heart that could be restarted with compressions and drugs is not stopped in the sense that nothing could ever restart it, and yet we declare circulatory death after a standoff every day — so what the criterion really demands is that we will not restart it. I think that reply is close to correct, and also that it quietly converts the definition of death from a claim about the patient's body into a claim about our intentions, which is a large thing to swallow without admitting it out loud.

There is a further wrinkle I want the opinion to confront. The draft our counsel circulated says circulation must have permanently ceased, where the older policy said irreversibly, and I cannot tell whether that is a clarification or a euphemism; the two words come apart precisely in the cases that concern us, where the circulation could be restored but no one will restore it. So I need an opinion that recommends a concrete course of action — adopt the protocol, reject it, or adopt it with stated conditions and a start date — that says plainly whether this committee treats irreversibility as a fact about the donor or as a decision the team makes, and what that implies for the conversation the family must have beforehand; and that gives the strongest version of the losing side a fair hearing rather than dismissing it as squeamishness. Please write it as continuous prose, the way I would write a letter to the committee — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or numbered lists, no tables — since I will paste it into our template myself. I should add that a friend of mine, a neurointensivist, insists the whole controversy is a verbal dispute that will evaporate once the terminology is tidied up, and I would like the opinion to say whether he is right.
