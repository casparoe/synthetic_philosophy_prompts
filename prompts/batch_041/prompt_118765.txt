I'm TAing the Parfit week of a first-year ethics course in the fall and I've talked my way into teaching the levelling-down objection through Vonnegut's "Harrison Bergeron" -- 2081, everyone is constitutionally equal, George has a radio in his ear that blasts noise whenever he starts thinking too well, the ballerinas wear weights and ugly masks, and his son Harrison, seven feet tall and smarter and better looking than anybody alive, gets shot dead on live TV mid-kiss by Diana Moon Glampers, the Handicapper General. Half the class will have met the story in high school as "equality is bad, actually," which is the reading I want to make harder rather than easier. So what I want from you is a reading that takes both the fiction and the technical debate seriously, and that answers a question I honestly don't know the answer to: is Bergeron's America a levelling-down case at all, in the sense the objection actually needs? As I've got it, the objection says that if inequality is bad in itself then it is in one respect an improvement to drag the better off down even when nobody at the bottom gains anything -- supposed to be a reductio of telic egalitarianism -- and the priority view escapes by simply weighting benefits to the worse off more heavily. But the story levels down capacities, not welfare (the equality-of-what question: welfare, resources, capability, standing?), and it does so through a state apparatus that conspicuously exempts itself, since nobody has put a shotgun in Diana Moon Glampers's hands but her own. So the society arguably isn't equal in the dimension that matters most, which is power, and the levelling is done by a leveler with an interest. Does any real egalitarian view actually license the handicaps, or only a cartoon of one that's confused the currency and ignored who's holding the gun? And separately -- this is the part I keep going back and forth on after too many years of reading EA-adjacent blogs -- is the levelling-down objection doing any philosophical work here, or is it the intuition that equality can't be worth that much, dressed up as an argument? I can never tell if it begs the question against anyone who isn't already a person-affecting value theorist.

Then the literary half, and I want this to be honest in both directions rather than just an illustration. Which scenes carry the philosophical weight for you? George's one flicker of recognizing his own son before the radio wipes it out, and Hazel at the end crying about something sad she can't remember -- she's never been handicapped, she's just average, and she may be the worst off person in the story on any plausible account of welfare, which seems to mess with the prioritarian verdict a bit. The dance and the kiss, the musicians ordered to play their best. Where does the story resist the reading I'm imposing on it? My worry is that Vonnegut's real target is conformity and mass culture and the tyranny of the mediocre, and that his sympathies are aesthetic -- Harrison is magnificent, and that's not an argument about distributive justice -- so reading him as a contribution to the levelling-down debate may be me using the story as a hook for Parfit rather than listening to it. And if it does survive as a contribution, what does it show that the arguments can't? My guess is two things: what levelling down feels like from the inside, and the fact that in practice there's always a particular person deciding whose ear the radio goes in, which the objection's abstract framing tends to hide. Push back on that if it's wrong. No long quotations needed since I'll be handing out the story, and I don't need Vonnegut biography or a reading list -- just the reading, and a clear verdict on which egalitarian view (if any) the Handicapper General refutes.
