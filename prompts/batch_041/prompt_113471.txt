For a graduate seminar on propositions I want the specification before we read any particular account: what must a theory of the unity of the proposition actually deliver? Frame it around what propositional content requires rather than around Bradley exegesis, though Bradley's regress is one requirement among several rather than the whole story. Give me the requirements list, each with the case behind it: that unity not come for free, since otherwise any arbitrary collection of items would count as unified and the problem would only be relocated; that a false proposition, such as that Caesar died in his bed, be exactly as unified as a true one; that the same items compose different propositions in different arrangements and none at all in others (Socrates loves Alcibiades; Alcibiades loves Socrates); that unity not be an act of a mind, since two thinkers grasp one content and some contents go unthought; that the account say what makes a unified thing represent rather than merely hang together, as a heap does; and that it survive one proposition's occurring inside another, as in ascriptions of belief. Include the case I never see handled: a proposition with a single constituent — that Socrates exists, or the impersonal that it is raining — where there is nothing for a unifying relation to relate. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are partisan, name any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied, say which requirement rules out the most otherwise attractive accounts, and score one candidate I have been toying with: the proposition that aRb is the ordered triple <a, R, b>, with unity nothing over and above the ordering. Close by saying how confident you are in the list as a whole and which single requirement you are least sure belongs on it.
