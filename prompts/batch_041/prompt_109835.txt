I'm a philosophy grad student working on a paper about rationality under risk, and I keep circling back to the Allais case. My line — and I'd like you to take it as my actual position rather than just surveying the debate — is that the independence axiom is a genuine requirement of practical rationality, so anyone whose preferences over the Allais prospects go the way most people's do is making a mistake, not expressing a different-but-equally-respectable conception of rationality. I'm not naive about the usual replies: I know the sure-thing framing, I know the difference between the common-consequence and the common-ratio versions, and I know that dropping independence is what buys you regret theory and all the rest of it. What I want from you is the other side of it: what would count against me? Not the observation that people in fact violate the axiom — I mean the arguments and results that should genuinely move someone who already knows that. For example: does the prudential case for the axiom (dynamic consistency, no exploitable inconsistency, the money pump) actually deliver independence on its own, or only independence together with a consequentialism about prospects that you could drop instead? Is the right diagnosis really that the choosers are irrational, or that they're failing some other assumption — the reduction of compound lotteries, or the idea that a prospect's value supervenes entirely on the distribution over final outcomes it induces — in which case I'm attacking the wrong target and my view survives only by mislabelling the error? And what do I say about the reflective, articulate Allais chooser who has heard every argument and still says she prefers the sure thing because the assurance of it is itself something she values, and she'll trade a little expected value for it? Is there anything in my position that shows she's mistaken rather than merely different? The thing I'd personally find most persuasive against me would be a case where an Allais-type preference survives every reframing, including the two-stage version and the version where the prize itself is a ticket in a further lottery, and the chooser can give a reason for it that isn't just a restatement of the preference.

Once you've laid that out, sort it by how decisive it actually is — fatal, seriously awkward, merely embarrassing, or not really a defeater at all — and tell me which of them a committed defender of the axiom would concede as counting against the position, rather than dismissing as confusion or changing the subject. I'm especially keen on the last bit: if every apparent counterexample gets diagnosed as a failure of rationality by definition, has my claim turned into a stipulation dressed up as a finding about what rationality requires, and if so is that a problem for a claim of this kind or just the price of the concept? I've been argueing about this with a friend for weeks and we're going in circles, so I'd rather have the honest map of where my view is exposed than another round of the standard moves.
