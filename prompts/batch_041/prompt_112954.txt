I run a small forecasting outfit — five of us, we sell probability estimates on things like whether a regulator will clear a product, whether a named supplier misses a delivery window, whether a bill gets out of committee. A year ago we started posting our numbers on an internal market so we could trade against each other and find out who's actually good; the stakes are small (a few hundred bucks a quarter split between us) but real, and settlement is mechanical, so a posted price has to be honest in both directions — you can't post 0.7 and then refuse to take the other side of it. That's been great for accountability and terrible for my inbox, because we keep turning up sets of numbers that can just be picked off. Last month someone had 0.75 on 'the FDA accepts the filing' and 0.4 on 'the FDA rejects the filing.' Someone else had 0.2 on 'the supplier misses the window' and 0.35 on 'the supplier misses the window because of the port strike.' Nobody was being dumb, they just thought about each question in isolation and never put the numbers side by side.

What I want is a screening procedure one of my analysts — smart, no probability theory past 'the numbers should add up to one' — can run in fifteen minutes on any batch of numbers before I sign off on posting them. I know the underlying idea is the Dutch book thing: if your prices let someone buy and sell against you in a way that locks in a profit whatever happens, your numbers are incoherent, and that's a property of the numbers themselves, not a forecast about the world. I don't need that explained to me, I need it turned into a checklist: what to elicit first and in what form, the specific inconsistencies to test for and in what order, which ones just flag a number for me versus which end the process outright, what the actual bets are that you'd place against someone who fails each test, and where the checklist runs out and hands me a judgment call. The hard cases are the ones I care about: a failed test tells you at least one number is wrong but not which one; someone who gives a range instead of a point and thinks that's an escape hatch; someone who says the numbers over a set of possibilities don't have to sum to one because they're leaving room for 'unknown'; the difference between a sure loss and a merely bad bet; and the conditional-now-versus-update-later version, which I'm told is a different animal and depends on what exactly gets learned.

Then run it on three batches and show me the bets for each: (1) the complement case above, (2) the conjunction case above, and (3) a set of our own — last quarter we published numbers on three related questions and I want to know whether ours are the exploitable ones, not some client's or analyst's. I especially want the third worked through, because I suspect the answer is going to tell me to change a number I've been defending for a year, and I want the procedure to say that plainly rather than softening it into a suggestion. Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do. I'd rather have a checklist with an explicit stop-here-this-is-mine-to-decide than one that pretends to settle everything.
