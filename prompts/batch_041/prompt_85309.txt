I've been arguing with a colleague — a statistician who now works on credit and hiring models, whereas I come at this from the philosophy side — for the better part of a year about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, and we have finally agreed to do the thing properly: write a joint statement we can both sign, rather than trading blog posts at each other. I'm the one who thinks the results describe a genuine wall. Take the standard setup — a score calibrated within each group, so that a given score means the same thing in both, combined with unequal base rates — and you cannot also give the two groups equal false-positive and equal false-negative rates, except in the degenerate cases usually carved out (perfect prediction, or base rates that coincide). My reading is that this makes fairness a matter of choosing which errors to redistribute and onto whom, that the choice is irreducibly normative, and that anyone selling a purely technical fix is smuggling a value judgment in under the mathematics. His reading is that the wall belongs to the frame rather than to the world: fix the score as the only channel of information, fix the decision as a threshold on that score, and fix the task as sorting a population rather than changing it, and of course you get a trade-off theorem; write the decision problem down properly — costs and benefits, the option to gather more information, the option to alter the outcome instead of merely predicting it — and the impossibility either dissolves or turns out to be a claim about base rates in formal dress. I have been reading these blogs long enough to be suspicious of any disagreement that survives a year, since that usually means one of us is using a word two ways.

What I want from you is the document we should have produced months ago. Start with a numbered list of claims we can both sign: what is actually true about the mathematics, what the results do and do not establish, what each of us ought to concede about the other's framing, and what we both accept about the normative status of whatever choice remains. Then a disagreements table, with each surviving disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept, and stated in its strongest form rather than a caricature. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us — and for each crux the case, argument, or test we would both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why, and keep the rest of the document clear of it.

Two features of our actual setting should shape the document, so that it isn't a summary of a debate we both already know by heart. First, the label is endogenous: what we predict is partly caused by what the system decided last year — default, rearrest, performance after a screen — so the base-rate gap we treat as an input may be an output of the system itself; I want both sides to say whether the impossibility result survives when the ground truth is a function of the model's own history, and to predict whether the gap narrows, widens, or merely relocates. Second, I have proposed a criterion I have not seen stated cleanly anywhere: judge the rule from behind a veil of ignorance, where the person does not know their group and does not know their score, and require that it maximize expected utility from that position; my colleague says that is the ex ante versus ex post equality argument in a costume, and I say it is the only vantage point from which the trade-off can be adjudicated without begging the question. Have both sides engage that criterion specifically. Write it to stand on its own: describe the results in prose, name no papers, and give no reading list — assume a reader who knows the territory but has nothing to look up.
