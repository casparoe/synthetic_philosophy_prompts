I run a monthly reading group on minds and machines — a mixed room of about a dozen machine-learning engineers, two cognitive neuroscientists, and one philosopher of language who has been threatening to resign ever since we did panpsychism — and next month's session is mine to lead. The brief I've been handed is 'machine sentience', but I refuse to run it as the usual is-a-chatbot-conscious debate, which I have now watched produce an hour of heat and no light on three separate occasions. What I actually want to teach is the question underneath it: that when we argue about whether some system is sentient we are seldom asking an empirical question about a fact waiting to be discovered, but making an attribution — deciding, usually without noticing that we are deciding, which computations are the ones that matter morally, and then checking whether the system performs them. I have seventy-five minutes and a whiteboard, and I want a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself: what has to be in place before the attribution question even parses for this room, what depends on what, and where in the sequence it is likely to come apart.

The device I intend to build the session around, if you judge that it will bear the weight, is a pair of systems I have been calling behavioural twins: two artefacts with identical input–output profiles — they answer the same questions, flinch at the same stimuli, and score the same on every test we can devise — but different internal computations, one that computes the relevant function through a recurrent, self-referential loop and one that computes it feedforward. I want to teach with what I have been calling the swap test: hold the behaviour fixed, vary the computation, and see whether your verdict moves; if it moves, you have shown that it was the computation you cared about all along, and not the behaviour. Tell me what this example must have and what it must lack in order to carry the whole session, whether it can carry it at all or whether I should anchor instead on a biological case — an octopus, a bee — and hold the twins back until the end, and precisely where in the sequence the twins should first appear.

Then map the misconceptions for me, in the order they will actually surface in a room like this one, and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each off. My guesses, for you to confirm, reject, or reorder: that more computation means more sentience, so scale settles the matter; that sentience is a property of the material rather than the organisation; that the question is really empirical and we are merely waiting for a better test; that sentience, intelligence and agency are one and the same thing; and that any answer must smuggle in a particular theory of consciousness by the back door. Finish with a single question I can put to the room at the close that would show me they have grasped the attribution problem rather than merely learned to recite the words, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I would far rather say 'that is a different session' than bluff my way through the hard problem in the last ten minutes.
