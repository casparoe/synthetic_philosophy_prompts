So I've fallen down a rabbithole on pain asymbolia — the condition where someone reports that yes, the pain is there, they can point right at it, they can tell you how intense it is, but it just doesn't bother them. It doesn't hurt in the way that makes you want it to stop. Some of them laugh during the exam. The sensory side seems intact and the "ouch" side seems gone.

What I want from you is the most important implications of cases like that — and I'll throw in a hypothetical to sharpen it: imagine a drug that produces the same profile cleanly, leaves the burning and the location and the intensity fully intact, strips out the part where the person minds. A company wants to sell it as a painkiller.

Please don't give me the standard "so the sensory and affective dimensions of pain are dissociable" line, everyone says that in the first paragraph. I mean the downstream stuff. What does this do to hedonism, to the claim that pain is intrinsically bad for the one who has it? What should a doctor do with a patient who says "its there but I don't mind it" — treat them, reassure them, send them home? Does it change anything about whether we can say an animal or an infant is suffering when they can't report either way, and if so is that a reason to be more cautious or less?

And here's the wrinkle I actually care about. Suppose someone with asymbolia still wants the pain gone. Not because it feels bad, but because the sensation is distracting, because it means something is wrong with their body, because they can't concentrate or sleep with it running in the background. Is that person badly off or not? And whatever you say, what does it do to the theory that pain is bad because we desire its absence rather than because of how it feels?

Work through it in order of how much it actually matters, and flag anywhere you think the asymbolia cases get over-read.
