I have been asked to give the short faculty address at my department's graduation ceremony — the one that comes after the degrees are handed out and before everyone goes outside for photographs, in a marquee that will be hot by then. The graduands are mathematicians and computer scientists; perhaps a third of them have taken my course on inductive inference and will notice if I fudge a technical point, while their families will not know the difference and will mostly want something they can applaud. I want the address to be about what the Solomonoff–Kolmogorov picture of induction actually entitles us to hope, and there are three things I want it to honour rather than smooth over: that a claim that one explanation is simpler than another is meaningful only up to a constant that depends on the reference machine, so a small difference in description length means nothing on its own; that the universal prior is not computable, so every practical appeal to simplicity is an approximation with no guarantees attached; and that a hypothesis positing something magical is not excluded by the theory at all if it is computable — merely outweighed. Write the address: about seven hundred words, deliverable aloud, warm enough for the occasion, carrying one concrete example that the families can follow and the theorists will not wince at, and not trading on any reassurance I do not believe — in particular it must not tell them that the simplest explanation is the truest, or that the future will resemble the past.

Give me three sections, in this order:

1. 'The address' — about seven hundred words.
2. 'What the theory licensed' — about two hundred and fifty words, saying which reassurances I was entitled to give the graduands, which I had to trade away, and what I put in their place.
3. 'Cuts if the marquee is too hot' — about sixty words: the two sentences I should drop first, and why those are the dispensable ones.

Write it in British English.
