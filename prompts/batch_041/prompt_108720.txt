Compare Jain anekāntavāda with the Western idea of "reasonable pluralism" -- the thought, associated with political liberalism, that decent people will inevitably disagree about deep questions and that this disagreement is not a defect to be fixed but a permanent feature to be accommodated. Two friends of mine won't shut up about this one. One says anekāntavāda and reasonable pluralism are basically the same insight wearing different clothes: both say the truth has many sides, both say no single perspective captures it all, both tell you to be humble and tolerant. The other says that's a lazy conflation. Her line is that reasonable pluralism is about *people*, not *truth* -- it says we should tolerate disagreement because we can't expect everyone to converge, and it stays neutral on whether there's a fact of the matter. Anekāntavāda, she says, is a claim about *reality itself*: that things genuinely have infinite aspects and that every assertion is only true "in some sense" (syāt). So one is political modus vivendi and the other is metaphysics, and treating them as the same thing is exactly the mistake the Jains would have wanted you to notice -- you're saying they're "the same" only in some sense, and forgetting the sense. He fires back that she's making anekāntavāda sound like it's just relativism with extra steps, and that if every assertion is only true "in some sense," then so is the claim that anekāntavāda is a metaphysics of reality, so her neat contrast between the two views is itself only "in some sense" true, which she can't have it both ways on. She says that's cheap -- self-application doesn't refute a view, it just means the view has to be stated carefully, and the Jains were perfectly aware of this (the doctrine of *nikṣepa*, the different standpoints from which a word can be used, is precisely their way of handling it). He says fine, but then she's admitted anekāntavāda needs a whole extra apparatus to stop it from eating itself, whereas reasonable pluralism doesn't, and that's a point in *his* favour, not hers. Neither will move.

Can you sort this out for me? Go through it move by move -- where he's right, where she's right, and where they're talking past each other -- and then say who has the better of it overall. Please write in British English.
