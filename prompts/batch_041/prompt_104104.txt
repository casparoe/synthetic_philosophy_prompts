I have been trying to find a name for a conversational pattern and keep coming up empty. A question is asked; the answer given is true and does answer the question, but it is plainly not the answer that was wanted, and both parties understand that the real message lies in what was left unsaid. A landlord asked for a reference for a departing tenant writes that the tenant always paid the rent on time; a department asked about a job candidate's teaching is told that the candidate never missed a class. Nothing false is asserted, and the writer can, if challenged, insist that the sentence is true and that nothing more was meant. The hearer, meanwhile, treats the omission as the message, reasoning that the stronger and more relevant statement was withheld because it could not honestly be made. I realize that we often communicate more than we literally state, and that saying less than one could is one of the ways this happens. What I am after is the specific mechanism by which the message is carried by the hearer's recognition that a stronger statement was available and was withheld, together with the speaker's ability to retreat afterwards to the literal content without having lied. Is there an established term for this, or a small family of terms for its parts? I want the boundaries drawn and not just a label: how this differs from the ordinary business of meaning more than one says, from lying, from insinuation, and from a bare hint; whether the hearer is ever entitled to the inference or whether the speaker is right that nothing was meant; and whether it matters that the speaker can retreat precisely because what was said was true. A name would be welcome, but the carving is what I am really after.
