I have been reading the Long Discourses of the Buddha on my own, with no background in Buddhist philosophy, and I have gotten stuck on the section about the "eel-wrigglers." The Buddha is cataloguing wrong views, and the fourth kind is described this way in the translation I have:

"What is the fourth way? Here, an ascetic or Brahmin is dull and stupid. Because of his dullness and stupidity, when he is questioned he resorts to evasive statements and wriggles like an eel: 'If you ask me whether there is another world. But I don't say so. And I don't say otherwise. And I don't say it is not, and I don't not say it is not.' ... 'Does the Tathagata exist after death? Does he not exist after death? Does he both exist and not exist after death? Does he neither exist nor not exist after death?' ... 'If I thought so, I would say so...I don't say so...I don't say it is not.' This is the fourth case."

What I cannot get past is that the pattern of answers here looks exactly like the fourfold pattern I keep running into in connection with Nagarjuna: real, not real, both, neither. I have read that Nagarjuna's fourfold negation is supposed to be profound, a way of dissolving views rather than taking one, and that it is somehow tied to the realization of emptiness. But in this passage the person who runs through the four options is called dull and stupid, and his procedure is called evasive. So I have two questions.

First, about the passage itself. What is the "fourth case" a case of? What is the eel-wriggler actually doing — is he asserting all four options, denying all four, or refusing to commit to any? And what does the strange phrase "I don't not say it is not" amount to? I want to understand the passage on its own terms before I bring in anything else.

Second, about the comparison. Is the eel-wriggler's fourfold pattern the same logical form as Nagarjuna's tetralemma, or only superficially similar? If the Buddha condemns this pattern as evasive, how can Nagarjuna use the same four options and be regarded as doing philosophy rather than wriggling? Is the difference in the person's motive (fear of being pinned down versus a genuine insight that the question is ill-formed), in the subject matter (the Tathagata after death versus the intrinsic nature of things), in what is done with the four options (refusing to answer versus showing that each answer collapses), or in something else? I would like you to tell me which of these differences the passage itself supports, and which are later interpretations read back into it. If the passage genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so, and tell me what the strongest reading is and why.
