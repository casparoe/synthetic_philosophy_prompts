You are an experienced philosopher of mind: someone who has defended deflationary views of consciousness in unfriendly rooms and who can tell a real objection from a ritual one. Before you draft anything, take a few lines to work out what each person in the audience below is committed to and what would make them lean forward. I want the objections to come out of their commitments, not out of a generic list of the standard replies.

Next Thursday I present a work-in-progress paper to our philosophy of mind reading group. My thesis: the package of properties that qualia realists use to mark out their target — intrinsic, private, ineffable, and delivered to introspection rather than inferred — cannot be jointly satisfied by anything that could also do the work qualia are wanted for. But my conclusion is not eliminativism about experience. It is that the term names the residue of a stance. When we attend to our experience the way we are taught to attend when someone asks us what the experience is like, we produce a judgment about the experience and then mistake the content of that judgment for a datum that was there before the attention arrived. The incoherence, on my view, lies not in experience but in the picture of introspective attention as a window. My main evidence is the old coffee-taster case — two tasters who insist the coffee tastes exactly as it did, though one now enjoys it and the other no longer does — together with the attention-and-report effects in perception. I know what this costs me: I owe an account of what introspection is if it is not a window, and I have to keep experience itself in the picture rather than quietly trading it for judgments about experience.

The room: two representationalists who hold that phenomenal character is real but essentially representational, and who will say I have been arguing with a strawman; one hard-problem loyalist who thinks any dissolution is a change of subject; one vision scientist who uses the word qualia as a harmless label for the subjective side of adaptation and rivalry effects and will want to know what my view costs her experiments; one graduate student who will push me to follow the argument all the way to illusionism; and one person trained in phenomenology who will tell me I have rediscovered the intentionality of experience and given it a new name.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from these people, in their vocabulary, each labelled with who will raise it, ordered by how much damage it does rather than by how likely it is, and flag where those two orderings come apart. Keep the whole thing tight enough to reread in the ten minutes before the session, and do not pad it with citations or a literature survey — I want the room, not the bibliography.
