I'm in the second year of an MA in philosophy, and two things this term have pushed me toward the essay I want you to write. The first is that I adopted an eleven-year-old ex-racing greyhound in September; she is anxious, she leans against my legs in the kitchen, she has learned the jingle of my keys from two rooms away, and she sulks when I leave in a way that is either a mood or an extraordinarily elaborate piece of clockwork, and I genuinely cannot tell which. My flatmate, a Cartesian by temperament, keeps insisting that on Descartes' view there is no fact of the matter beyond the clockwork. The second is that I am taking a seminar on early modern philosophy of mind while my reading group works through Kafka's Metamorphosis next month, and the more I think about it the more I suspect that the story is a far better test case for Descartes on animal minds than the usual menagerie of parrots, magpies and garden statues. I'd like you to write me a reading of Metamorphosis through Descartes' account of animal minds. Write it as a philosopher of mind who is also a close reader of fiction — someone who thinks their way into a text rather than summarizing it — and please spend a few sentences at the start planning: list the scenes you intend to use and the Cartesian claims you intend to test them against, then write the essay proper. Assume a reader who knows the story well; I don't need page references, and please don't quote the text from memory, since I'd rather have your paraphrase of a scene than a misremembered sentence.

Take Descartes seriously as a philosopher rather than a cartoon villain, and keep three things in view. First, the two tests he offers in the Discourse — genuine language, meaning the ability to produce novel utterances that declare one's thoughts, and general adaptability of conduct across situations — together with his insistence that parrots and magpies can make the noises without meeting the test, and his comparison of animal bodies to the hydraulic figures in the gardens at Saint-Germain. Second, the distinction, which I'm told some commentators press, between thought and sensation: in his correspondence with Henry More in the late 1640s he seems to deny animals thought while allowing them sensation in the strict sense, insofar as sensation is for him a bodily affair, which makes the popular image of Descartes as the man who denied animals any feeling at all look like a caricature. Third, the asymmetry that does the real work: for Descartes I know my own mind with more certainty than I know my body, while I know that anyone else has a mind only by inference from behavior and speech — an inference underwritten, in his system, by the assumption that the other is the same kind of thing as me. Gregor is no longer the same kind of thing, and everything the family does follows from that.

What I want from the essay is a genuinely two-directional reading. Show me where the story illuminates Descartes: the reader's privileged access to Gregor's inner life against the family's behavioral evidence, the scene in which Grete argues that this creature cannot be Gregor precisely because Gregor would have understood and removed himself, the family's gradual slide into calling him a thing to be disposed of, the failure of the language test at exactly the point where comprehension is perfect and only production is gone. Then show me where the story resists the Cartesian frame and the honest critic has to concede it: the mind that seems to travel with the body, the new tastes and the ceiling-crawling, the wish to have the room stripped bare, the fact that Gregor's own account of himself is not the serene Cartesian first-person at all. I'd also like a paragraph on the moral aftermath, and on whether the story suggests that the beast-machine doctrine licenses cruelty or whether the family's cruelty would have happened anyway. End with a committed verdict, not a survey: is Kafka dramatizing Descartes' method while refuting his zoology, or exposing the test as a fact about us rather than about them, or something else entirely? Tell me what the story adds to the debate that the arguments alone cannot, and where the reading strains — I'd rather have a thesis I can argue with in seminar than a balanced inventory of possibilities. Roughly 1,500 words.
