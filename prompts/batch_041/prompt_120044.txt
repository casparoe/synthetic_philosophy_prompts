Here's my opening move, and I think it's a decent one, so please don't just wave it away. I'll grant you the entire genealogy. Fine: selection shaped our moral psychology, and it did so because certain dispositions made our ancestors better at cooperating, not because those dispositions were latching onto moral facts. But look at *which* beliefs that story actually explains. It explains why we think you shouldn't betray a friend, that you owe something to someone who helped you, that gratuitous cruelty is wrong, that fairness matters, that cheaters deserve punishment. Those are the beliefs that basically every moral theory in the room already agrees about. The ones we actually fight over -- about animals, about abortion, about whether you may let one person die to save five, about whether the state may execute anyone -- are not the ones the selection story reaches. And there's a reason for that: those are recent, weird, large-scale problems that our ancestors' environments would have put no pressure on, so if anything selection should be *least* involved in shaping them. So it looks like your debunking lands squarely on the bits of morality that were never in dispute, and says nothing about the bits where all the doubt and disagreement actually lives.
