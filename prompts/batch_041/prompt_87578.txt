So a few of us have been running a tiny online reading group on pessimism and antinatalism for the last six months or so -- started with Schopenhauer, got completely sidetracked, and somehow ended up spending like four straight sessions on Benatar's asymmetry: the thing where the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even if there's nobody around to enjoy that absence, but the absence of pleasure isn't bad unless there's somebody who's being deprived of it. None of us are students, nobody has a philosophy degree, we just yell at each other on a discord call every Sunday. Next month I'm hosting what I'm grandly calling our "final" -- one written question, everyone answers it, I read them all, then we compare notes -- so I need you to be the exam writer for me.

I want one essay question that a smart person who's been following along could write a decent answer to in about an hour, but that they can't get through just by reciting those four asymmetries back at me. The thing I keep getting stuck on, and the thing I want the question to force people to actually deal with, is that the asymmetry seems to need two different kinds of comparison at the same time. When it says the absence of pain is good even if nobody enjoys that absence, it's treating a state of affairs as good sort of "from nowhere", like a fact about the world can be a good thing without anyone being better off. But when it says the absence of pleasure isn't bad unless someone's deprived, it flips to being person-relative -- no subject, no harm. Fine, that's the trick that makes the conclusion come out (coming into existence is always a harm, or at the very least never a benefit). But then I came up with this case while walking the dog and I can't shake it: take a real person, alive right now, who's genuinely glad to be alive. The world where they were never conceived has, going by the asymmetry, a big absence-of-pain feature that counts as good (nobody suffers) plus an absence-of-pleasure that doesn't count as bad (nobody's deprived) -- so the never-conceived world scores better, ok. But now take that same person and instead of never being conceived, imagine them dying painlessly in their sleep tonight. The world after their death also has an absence of their future pain and an absence of their future pleasures. By the letters of the asymmetry, shouldn't the post-death world also be an improvement, at least in the same bookkeeping sense? Benatar obviously doesn't want that -- he treats death as a harm and coming-to-be as a harm too -- so I want the question to make people work out what exactly he's using to keep those two apart, and then say whether it's a real distinction or just a patch. Please don't let the question be answerable by "he says death harms an existing person and non-existence doesn't", because that's the exact thing I want them to interrogate rather than state.

And I want one extra constraint that I think will trip up the shallow answers: make them do it as a "swap test", where they take the asymmetry and swap the words pain and pleasure throughout, say what the resulting view would look like and what it would imply, and then explain whether the original asymmetry can give a reason for rejecting the swapped version other than just "well, the swapped version is obviously wrong". If all they say is that it's absurd, that should cost them marks.

So, from me to you: write the exam question itself (worded so it actually sounds like a question and not like a list of instructions), then a model answer of roughly 800 words, then a marking scheme. For the scheme I'd like a small number of must-hit points -- let's say five -- with marks attached to each (total 25), and for each point one line on the specific way people typically fluff it. Also please flag in the scheme the one move you think separates the really good answers from the merely competent ones (I have a hunch it's the bit about whether "good from nowhere" and "harm to a subject" can even live in the same theory), so I know how to weight it. Thanks, and sorry for the ramble.
