I'm a philosopher, though my patch is philosophy of biology and I haven't done any serious modal metaphysics since my PhD, so I'm hoping you can get me unstuck. A colleague in our reading group brought in an argument that he says shows the whole "you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg" line is a dead end, and it's been bugging me all week, mainly because I'm fairly sure the conclusion is too strong but I can't put my finger on which move is the dodgy one. Here it is, laid out the way he laid it out. (1) A thing's origin is essential to it — if x originates from y, then x could not have originated from anything other than y; he insists this is meant as a completely general truth, artefacts and organisms alike. (2) I originated from the fusion of a particular sperm and a particular egg — s and e are, in the relevant sense, my originating matter. (3) So there's no possible world in which I'm the product of a different sperm or egg. (4) But my coming to be also depended on a particular night, a particular meal my mother ate, the temperature of the room, and so on; take any of those away and I wouldn't have been conceived. (5) By (1), if my origin is essential then so is everything without which I wouldn't have come to be — so the meal and the room temperature are essential to me too. (6) Hence in any world where my mother ate something different that evening, the resulting child isn't me. (7) But that's plainly absurd — I could have been conceived a minute later and still have been me. (8) Therefore origin essentialism is false, and there's no special reason to think I couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg after all.

What I'd like from you is a proper diagnosis: which step to give up, why it looked fine in the first place, and what giving it up costs me. I suspect the trouble is somewhere in the middle, but I couldn't tell you where. Suppose I want to dig my heels in and keep (1) — then what exactly do I have to say about the difference between a thing's origin and its mere causal background, and does that leave the gametic claim (3) standing, or does it leave the whole question undecided? And if I instead abandon (1), what do I lose elsewhere? I'd also ask you to be quite careful to mark off what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. I don't want a confident survey that pretends there's a consensus on the gamete question when I'm pretty sure there isn't one, so please flag where the real disagreements lie and don't dress a live dispute up as the recieved view. If it matters, I'm inclined to think (3) is false but (1) is true, so I'm definately not after the easy answer.
