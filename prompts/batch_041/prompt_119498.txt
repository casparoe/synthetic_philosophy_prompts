Ok, I've been going back and fourth on this and I want the actual conceptual issue explained properly, not a summary of what a bunch of philosophers have said about it.

Here's the puzzle. Take three paintings. (1) A perfect forgery of an old master — someone today paints a canvas that's atom-for-atom identical to the original, same pigments, same hairline cracks in the varnish, and suppose the tech is good enough that no test we could ever run tells them apart. We still say it's not the original and it's worth basically nothing. (2) The actual original, except it's been restored so aggressively over four hundred years that essentially none of the original paint is left — it's modern paint on an old canvas, in the original composition. We still call it the original and museums fight over it. (3) The original artist, forty years after finishing the first version, paints a second version with their own hands that's visually identical to the first.

So case 2 is materially mostly a copy but we call it the original; case 1 is materially identical but we call it a fake; and case 3 is made by the same hand but it's a different object. I want you to explain what the value of being the original actually consists in, given all three. Is it the material stuff, the causal history (this paint was laid down by that person at that time), the fact that it's the product of an act of creation rather than an act of copying, or is originality doing several different jobs that we've lazily bundled under one word? Walk through each of the three cases and say which account handles it and which one has to fudge it, then tell me whether there's any single account that gets all three right, or whether we should just admit that "the original" picks out different things in different contexts.

And then the part I actually care about: if there's literally nothing left over — the duplicate is indistinguishable now and under any future test we could ever devise — does that mean our attachment to originals is just sentiment or a status game? I want the machinery of the argument, not a verdict about what museums should do.
