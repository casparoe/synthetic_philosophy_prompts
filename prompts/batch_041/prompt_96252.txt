I am a backend engineer, mostly distributed systems, with no formal training in philosophy; this is a question that has been accumulating on me for a couple of years and I would like to settle it.

The pattern is this. There are practices whose point is that the people inside them act for a certain reason, and the point is defeated if that reason is consulted at the moment of acting. The clearest case is a support team I worked with. We rewrote the playbook so that its first line said the goal was to make the customer feel heard, then trained people on that line and asked them to keep it in view during calls. The calls got worse: agents became audibly scripted, and customers could tell they were being managed. Nothing about the agents changed except that they now knew, and were thinking about, what the behavior was for. Second case: I visit a friend in the hospital. If I go because that is what the friendship calls for, the visit is a good one; if I pause to check that visiting is warranted by the friendship, the visit becomes an obligation I am discharging, and it shows. Third: a doctor tells a patient that recovery goes better if the patient believes it will, and the belief will not form on those grounds. My colleague's summary of all this is that you cannot do a thing well while holding the reason for doing it up in front of you, but that seems both too broad, since holding the reason up seems fine when I am filling out a tax return, and too narrow, since in my cases the damage is done even when the person has already decided and is not deliberating at all. The deciding is over; the awareness alone is corrosive.

I have tried three nearby things and none of them fit exactly. Goodhart's law covers the case where a measure becomes a target, and my support example has that flavor, but the damage in my cases does not require a measurement, and the friendship case has no metric in it anywhere. The paradox of hedonism is closest in spirit, but it is about one particular state, happiness, being self-defeating, whereas I am after the general shape, including cases where what is defeated is not a state of the agent but something relational, like being trusted. A colleague said there is something in ethics about utilitarianism being unable to be thought about while you act, and another recalled a case about a man choosing whom to save, where the complaint was that the theory demanded one thought more than the situation warranted; I could not get a name out of either of them, and in any case my cases are not dilemmas, since nothing is at stake, there is no conflict to resolve, and the trouble starts even when the right action is obvious.

So: does this have a name? If it does, I want the boundaries rather than the label, which of my cases it covers and which it does not, and why. If there is a family of names rather than one, tell me which member is closest and where the seams are, and in particular whether there is a term for the version in which the harm comes not from the person's psychology but from the practice of writing the point of a rule down where the people who follow it will read it, since that is the version I keep running into at work. If nothing fits, coin something and give me the definition plus two cases that fall just outside it. Two further cases I would like you to place, because I genuinely cannot tell whether they belong: a coach telling a player not to think about the mechanics of a stroke, and a manager who asks an employee to explain why they did a kind thing, after which the kind thing no longer counts for either of them. Please lead with a one-sentence answer and keep the whole reply under 600 words.
