Our reading group has been stuck on the Hart–Devlin thing for months now — the question whether the criminal law may enforce a shared morality just as such, over and above stopping anyone from getting harmed. I think I can state both sides. Devlin: a society's morality is all of a piece, you can't carve off the private bits, a society is entitled to defend its own existence, and the standard is what ordinary people actually feel — the ordinary person's outrage, not what a philosopher can justify from first principles. Hart: that's a licence for majority moralism, the claim that society disintegrates once its shared morality erodes is an empirical prediction nobody has ever produced evidence for, and the burden sits with whoever wants to criminalise. Six meetings, no movement. I don't want a verdict on who's right, I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is.

My own hypothesis, and please treat it as a hypothesis rather than something to agree with: the real disagreement isn't about whether society may enforce morality, it's about whether the thing being protected can be harmed at all, and if so whether it's the sort of thing that gets used up. Which is why I'd like you to think with this rather than just report on it — model a shared morality as a common-pool resource, like a fishery: depletable, costly to replenish, each act of non-conformity a small draw on the stock. Does that fit? Is shared morality rivalrous in the way the model needs (does my not believing something cost you anything)? Is it excludable? And if it doesn't fit, what happens to Devlin's move from the claim that you can't pick and choose which bits of the morality to enforce to the conclusion that the whole thing is at risk? There's also a direction-of-causation thing hiding in there — if the criminal law is part of what makes people hold the morality in the first place, then enforcement manufactures the stock instead of conserving it, and the arguement runs backwards. Say whether that's a real problem for Devlin or a cheap shot.

Then the diagnosis proper: substantive moral disagreement, disagreement about whether there's a harm in the picture at all, disagreement about where the burden of proof sits, or two people answering different questions under one heading (Devlin a juridical question about a community's authority to bind its members, Hart a political-moral question about when coercion is justified)? Pick one, say what each side would have to concede for the other's answer to become relevant, and be honest about whether anything could count as progress — evidence, a distinction, a change of question — or whether this disagreemnt should just be left to die.
