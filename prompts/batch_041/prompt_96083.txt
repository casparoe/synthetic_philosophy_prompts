I teach a graduate seminar in the history of philosophy, and the session I dread is the one on progress narratives — on what our historians call Whig history: the practice of telling the past as an approach to the present, sorting the dead into those who advanced the present consensus and those who held it back, and pricing a thinker's interest by how nearly he anticipated us. My students can state the objection in a sentence; they cannot feel it, and a description of the vice is too easy to nod along to. What I need is a piece of sustained, deadpan satire that shows the machinery working at full strength and never names itself.

Please write a mock referee's report on a manuscript in the history of philosophy. The journal, the author, and the subject are all fictional; I want no real names anywhere — no scholars living or dead, no journals, no titles of real works — and no citations at all. Where the referee feels the pull of the literature, he should gesture at it vaguely and with regret.

The referee is not a fool and not a villain. He is a courteous senior scholar, generous with praise, sincerely convinced that he is helping the author, and entirely unaware of what his demands add up to. The words Whig, presentism, anachronism and teleology must not appear, nor any of their synonyms used as terms of art; every comment, read on its own, should sound like ordinary good editorial advice. Stay inside the report's frame throughout, and step outside it only in the final section described below.

The submission he is reading is a contextualist study of Caspar Melander, a fictional seventeenth-century physician in Danzig. Treat the following as fixed. Melander's governing problem was to reconcile a thoroughly mechanical account of perception — impressions traced on the brain by the animal spirits — with his conviction that the senses are trustworthy witnesses to a providentially ordered world; his obsessive case was whether the imagination can mislead in matters of faith, on which he fought a twenty-year and now wholly forgotten controversy with a colleague, in a vocabulary that has no counterpart in ours. He held that matter cannot think, since whatever is composed of parts lacks the unity a thought requires; he held several other things we would call false, and argued for some of them well; and in one sentence of one letter he remarks that an idea may be less a picture than a disposition to respond, a thought he never develops. The manuscript reconstructs the controversy, declines to say whether Melander was right, and treats his theological commitments as premises of his arguments rather than as the residue of his century.

The report should, taken as a whole, do the following work without ever naming what it is doing:

- require the author to say how far Melander came toward our view and where he fell short, on the understanding that the present is the standard of assessment;
- take the single sentence about dispositions as what Melander "really meant", promote it to the centre of the paper, and let the rest of the system become the husk from which that sentence is to be extracted;
- find the twenty-year controversy interesting but marginal — not philosophy, of antiquarian interest — and propose that a paragraph in a footnote would do;
- explain the theological commitments away, kindly, as the constraints of his time;
- ask the author to stop reporting arguments and start adjudicating them, so that the reader is told which of Melander's arguments still stand;
- ask for a sentence on his critics as men who held the field back, or at least on why they were wrong;
- express surprise that anyone still takes seriously a question no one asks, and ask the author to establish its relevance to a current debate.

Two further requirements. First, the report must contain one comment of genuine philosophical force — I suggest the point that a history which refuses all evaluation cannot explain why we should care whether Melander's arguments are any good, and so threatens to turn the past into a museum of the inscrutable — and it must not be marked out as the reasonable one; the reader should have to find it. Second, the referee's demands must be mutually reinforcing in a way he cannot see: he should ask, say, that Melander's vocabulary be rendered in ours so that readers can follow it, and then complain that Melander is imprecise for failing to draw the distinctions the translation imports. Read end to end, the report should make Melander's actual question vanish, leaving a score.

Keep the register flat. No winks, no signposts of irony, no exclamation marks, no jokes at the expense of peer review as an institution, no material about referees being lazy or overworked: the comedy has to come entirely from the accuracy of the type, so that a colleague who works on the seventeenth century recognises the referee and winces rather than laughs at a caricature. I would also rather the praise were real; he should mean it.

Format, with rough lengths:

- "Recommendation": one or two lines in the standard formula, about 30 words.
- "Summary of the submission": the referee's neutral précis, about 80 words.
- "Major comments": numbered, about 450 words, the bulk of the piece.
- "Minor comments": numbered, about 120 words, mixing one pedantry and one correction that is simply right.
- "Note to me": after the report, in your own voice rather than the referee's, about 180 words, identifying which of his comments carries genuine philosophical force and which are the vices the piece exists to expose, and naming what the report as a whole does to Melander's question.
