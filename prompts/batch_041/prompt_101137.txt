I'm the anaesthesia lead at a veterinary referral hospital and I need an actual bedside procedure, not a lecture, for the question my residents keep getting wrong: is this animal experiencing something right now, and how sure do I have to be before I turn the analgesia up?

Some background on why it's hard. Most of the time we read pain off behaviour — the dog that won't bear weight, the cat with its ears flat. But a lot of our patients can't tell us anything and can't even move. We use neuromuscular blockers in some orthopaedic and eye cases, so the patient is conscious or only lightly under but completely paralysed, and the only channels left are autonomic: heart rate, blood pressure, pupil size, tears, sweating. And with an animal you obviously can't ask, and the species gap means the usual move — it acts the way I would if it hurt, so it probably hurts — is shakier than it is for another person. Whatever we do also gets written in the record and could end up in front of a disciplinary board, so I want something we can point to.

What I want: a procedure a resident can run at the bedside in about two minutes, in order, that decides (a) whether to treat this as a being that is currently experiencing pain and (b) how confident they have to be. It should say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a firm no (monitor only, don't treat), where it stops with a firm yes, and where it has to hand the call to me or to the owner because its turned into a value question — how much analgesia is too much, what we're willing to spend, whether this animal's life is worth living. Be honest about which steps are just judgement dressed up as measurement.

Ground it in what philosophy actually knows about the problem of other minds: that you never reach the other being's experience directly, only its body and its behaviour; the old argument-from-analogy move and why it's weak; the idea that some behaviour is a criterion for pain rather than just evidence for it; and the point that in practice we don't infer other minds at all, we respond to them, and blanket doubt isn't a stance anyone can actually live in. I'd also like you to take seriously the awkward consequence of the criterion idea for my situation — if pain behaviour is partly what makes the concept of pain apply, then chemically switching the behaviour off should leave you with nothing to go on, and yet clinically we clearly aren't left with nothing. Work out what to do with that.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a dog under a blocker for a cruciate repair, heart rate up about 20%, everything else normal; (2) a cat in the ER with a smashed pelvis, sitting quietly and purring; (3) a horse with colic that keeps looking at its flank but whose heart rate is normal. At least one of these should come out in a way that annoys me — if none does, you've made the procedure too soft.

And tell me what the procedure can't do: the cases where I should stop pretending there's a method and just make a call and own it.
