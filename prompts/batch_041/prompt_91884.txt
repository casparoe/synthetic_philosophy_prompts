I retired a few years ago (thirty-one seasons counting geese on the tundra, if you can believe it) and I've spent the retirement reading philosophy, which I'm sure I'm doing all wrong but I'm doing it anyway. I keep circling the free will problem, and especially that phrase about the varieties of free will worth wanting — Dennett's, I'm fairly sure, the subtitle of his book Elbow Room. The point being that there's no single thing called free will, and the real question is which of the several things people slap that name on is actually worth having.

I want to try the whole business out on a film instead of an argument, because I trust my instincts about stories more than my instincts about arguments. The film is Groundhog Day. You know the setup: Phil Connors, the sour weatherman, is trapped in Punxsutawney, and every morning it's February 2 again, and no matter what he does the day resets at six the next morning. He gorges, he seduces, he steals, he tries to kill himself a few different ways, he learns piano, he learns ice sculpture, he tries to save a homeless old man and fails no matter how many times he tries, and eventually he becomes — well, decent, generous, and by the end he wakes up and it's February 3.

So take the role of someone who knows the contemporary free will literature cold — the compatibilists, the libertarians, the skeptics, all of them — and is also a decent film critic, and think this through carefully before you commit to a line. Start by brainstorming the possible readings before you pick one, so I can see the roads not taken.

What I want is a real reading of the film through that "worth wanting" question, faithful in both directions. Does the movie dramatize something the argument-only debate misses — Phil doesn't get out by becoming uncaused, he gets out by becoming the author of his own day while the day stays exactly as fixed as it ever was; is that compatibilism made vivid, or is the film quietly cheating? Is the repeating day even a good picture of determinism at all, given that causes still produce effects and a determinism doesn't have to repeat itself? Which scenes carry the weight for you — I'd guess the suicide montage, the failed rescue of the old man, and the last perfect day — and where does the film resist the reading, where does the honest critic have to say the movie is trading on something the philosophy doesn't sanction? And does Phil actually want the freedom he ends up with, or does he just stop minding?

Then the part I actually care about, and I want you to take it seriously rather than pat me on the head, and then tell me where it breaks. From ecology I got into the habit of thinking about any system in terms of degrees of freedom — the nominal ones, the dimensions of the state space it's got on paper, versus the usable ones, the directions it can actually move in before some constraint clamps down on it. My instinct is that Phil's freedom grows across the film not because the loop breaks but because his usable share of a fixed state space keeps going up — he maps more and more of the day, and the day never changes. Is that a genuine distinction that does work in this debate, or am I just relabeling compatibilism in the language of my old trade? Where does it earn its keep — and where does it collapse, given that a thermostat has degrees of freedom and we don't call it free, and given that usable capacity says nothing about who authored the choice or whether anyone's on the hook for it. Tell me straight whether the degrees-of-freedom frame adds anything to the "worth wanting" question or just dresses it up.
