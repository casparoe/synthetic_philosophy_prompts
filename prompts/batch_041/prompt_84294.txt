I work on distributed commit protocols — two-phase commit, consensus, quorums, leases, crash-fault models — and I know the philosophical literature on collective intentionality only casually. Restate that debate in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: render Margaret Gilbert's joint commitment and plural subject, Michael Bratman's account of shared intention as interlocking plans plus mutual responsiveness, and John Searle's insistence that collective intentionality is a primitive not reducible to individual intentions plus common knowledge, each as a protocol or system design. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of each view survive translation cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? I especially want to know whether the normativity Gilbert builds into joint commitment — the thought that one party cannot simply walk away, and that rescission takes the concurrence of the others — is something my protocols genuinely possess, something they merely simulate with locks and timeouts, or something they cannot express. Finally, tell me whether the residue is a defect of the systems vocabulary or a sign that the philosophers are trading on a distinction that does no real work, and commit to a verdict on which of the three accounts, once translated, best describes what is actually happening when two people carry a table up a flight of stairs together.
