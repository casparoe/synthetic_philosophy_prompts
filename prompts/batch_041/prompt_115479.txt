A friend of mine is deep in the sex-work debate -- she's read up on the Nordic model (selling legal, buying criminal), the German and Dutch setups where it's legal and regulated, and New Zeland where they just decriminalized the whole thing -- and she handed me this argument for the Nordic model. I think the conclusion is right, but I also think the argument eats itself alive. Tell me where it breaks.

(1) Buying sex should be a crime, because the buyer is what makes the market: no buyers, no brothels, no trafficking pipeline to feed them.
(2) The person selling should not be punished. She's the one in the worse spot, and fining her or jailing her just piles more harm on the person already carrying it.
(3) So the principled line is: criminalize the buyer, decriminalize the seller. Punish the party responsible for the harm, protect the party who bears it.
(4) Now ask why the buyer is the responsible one. It can't be that he did something she didn't do -- they did the exact same thing, money changed hands for sex. He's the responsible one because he had a choice and she didn't. He could walk away at no cost; she was there because the rent was due.
(5) So the principle underneath (3) is: in a transaction that is harmful, the side whose participation is unconstrained is the criminal side, and the side whose participation is forced by need gets decriminalized.
(6) Take (5) seriously. The man who buys strawberries picked by a migrant paid a wage so low she sleeps in a shed. The man who buys a kidney from someone selling one to pay for his kid's surgery. The guy who orders delivery at 11pm knowing the rider is on hour thirteen because the app pays so little. In every one of these the buyer is free and the seller is cornered.
(7) By (5), every one of those buyers should be a criminal and every one of those sellers should walk free.
(8) That's absurd. I don't believe it, nobody believes it, and a principle that turns half the economy into a crime scene isn't a principle, it's a mood.
(9) So (5) is false. And if (5) is false then (3) has no backbone, and the Nordic model is just a legally enforced distaste for the transaction itself.

Here's my problem. I'd like to keep (3). And honestly (4) seems to me just true -- he really does have a choice and she really doesn't. But I can't see how to drop (5) and keep (3) without just asserting that sex is special, which is exactly the thing I was trying to avoid having to assert. Is there a move I'm missing? Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up actually cost me -- does it cost me the Nordic model, or just the tidy way I stated it?
