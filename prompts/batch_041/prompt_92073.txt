I teach civics at a secondary school, 17-18 year olds, and I've got a double period coming up — 80 minutes, 19 kids. I want to do something on the "which country is more liberal, Sweden or Norway" argument, the kind of thing where everyone has a gut answer in two seconds and nobody can say what would prove it either way. I've no philosophy training myself, I did history at uni, so I'm definately going to need the whole thing spelled out: rules, materials, timings, and the exact questions I ask at the end. What I want them to walk out with is the feeling that they spent half an hour arguing about a fact and it turned out to be an argument about a definition, and that the definition wasn't hiding somewhere waiting to be found.

Here's the shape I'm imagining, tell me if there's something better. Four or five groups, each playing a "national statistics office" that has to publish a verdict on which of the two countries is more liberal. Every group gets the same dossier — say twelve cards, each with one indicator on it: hours worked per week, whether shops can open on Sundays, prison numbers per head, top rate of tax, share of women in parliament, whether the state owns the oil, share who say religion matters to them, how hard it is to fire someone, union membership, whether a parent can be prosecuted for smacking a child, plus a couple that point the opposite way from the rest so nothing lines up neatly. I'll make the cards myself from whatever figures I can dig up, so the numbers don't need to be right in your answer, just plausible and a bit contradictory. Then the twist: each group also gets a sealed envelope. Inside, one slip defines "liberal" differently for each group — one gets freedom from the state, one gets social permissiveness and openness to outsiders, one gets individual rights and legal protection, one gets the European sense of liberal which is basically free markets, one gets the American sense which is close to the opposite. A second slip gives each group a different rule for adding it up: one has to let the single worst indicator decide, one just counts how many indicators each country wins, one weights each indicator by how much it matters to an ordinary person living there. They rank, they present, then they open the envelopes and see the definitions were handed out, not discovered. Then everyone votes out loud for one country as the more liberal, no abstaining, no saying it depends. That last bit is the whole point I think — the second you make them choose, the concept has to do some work.

Then the debrief, which is where I need the most help. I want to map what just happened onto the real thing: the moment where "more liberal" stops being one question and becomes a bundle of questions that come apart, so one country can win on freedom from the state and lose on how you're treated if you're an outsider; the fact that any single league table, the democracy ones, the economic freedom ones, has already made those trade-offs for you in the weighting and just doesn't tell you; and the move where somebody says just average all the indicators, which is also a choice. I'd like them to leave with the question of what would settle it, so at the end I want to ask each of them to name one thing they could find out that would change their mind, and I want you to tell me what's likely to happen when I do that. Be straight with me about what I'll get wrong the first time (my guess: the groups finish at wildly different times and one of them just picks the country with more oil money). Also tell me the one thing this activity can't do and that I'll have to say out loud myself. Don't give me a menu of five options — pick a design and commit to it, and then give me your own verdict at the end: which of the two countries is actually more liberal, or if you think that's an empty question, say so plainly and tell me what you'd ask instead.
