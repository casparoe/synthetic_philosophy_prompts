I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on minds and machines, and I need one essay question for the take-home final, on Turing's proposal that we build a child machine and educate it rather than try to simulate an adult mind. Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20. The question must not be answerable by reciting the imitation game.

Candidates should have to do three things. First, reconstruct the child-machine proposal as a claim about where the difficulty of machine intelligence actually lies: in arranging an education rather than in engineering adult competence, with the human child as the model of an initially unorganised system that is organised by training. Second, bring out a tension inside Turing's own account: his willingness to treat the search for a good child machine and a good education as a largely blind search over initial structures and curricula, set against his insistence that a teacher neither foresees the pupil's discoveries nor owns them, so that education cannot be reduced to a programme of instruction. Third, use the contrast between discipline and initiative — an untrained mind must acquire reliable routines but also the capacity to originate behaviour that is not merely the execution of its training — to judge whether the educational framing solves the problem of machine intelligence or only relocates it, for instance by pushing the explanatory burden onto whatever initial structure makes a machine educable in the first place.

The marking scheme should name the specific moves an A answer must make and a B answer can miss, assign marks to each, and flag two or three mistakes that should cost marks: treating the child machine as an implementation detail of the Turing test; reading the analogy as one between a machine and an already-formed adult who is reprogrammed; or arguing that because the teacher supplies the training, the pupil's later achievements are really the teacher's, so the proposal collapses.
