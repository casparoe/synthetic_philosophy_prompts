I'm on the curatorial committee of a small municipal museum that is redesigning its permanent galleries, and we have reached an impasse over the labels for a run of rooms that currently move from the medieval to the early modern. A colleague — a historian of science — has been arguing that terms such as the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are not discoveries about the past but administrative devices: chrononyms, as she calls them, names that do not merely pick out a stretch of time but help to constitute the historical object they appear to describe. On her view, the periods are less like contours on a map of the past than like the boundaries of a fire district — tools of temporal zoning whose justification is pragmatic through and through, and which can be redrawn without the world having been misdescribed.

I am sceptical, but I cannot articulate what follows from her position and what does not. Please set out the most important implications of taking that constructivist line seriously, ranked from most to least consequential, and not only for museum labelling but for the causal and comparative claims historians make. What happens, for instance, to the claim that the Scientific Revolution was a revolution, or that the Renaissance was a rebirth of antiquity, if the periods are later inventions? Does the constructivist view make cross-period comparison impossible, or merely require a different justification? And what are the most important implications for how we should decide when a periodisation is a mistake rather than simply a convenience? You need not cite any sources; I want the argument, not a reading list. Please write your answer in British English.
