Last night I lost an argument with a friend about creativity, and I would rather settle it properly than have it again. He is a cognitive scientist by training, and he pressed the Hofstadter line on me: that the crux of creativity is not invention from nothing but the making of variations on a theme, and that this is not a loose metaphor but very nearly the whole story. In the version he gave me, the picture has four parts. First, a theme is not a fixed object but something identified by a skeleton or core that survives being varied, so that two things count as the same theme just in case they share the right skeleton. Second, a theme can be represented as a point in a space of possibilities, or as a bundle of knobs — pitch, tempo, register, dynamics, instrumentation, and so on — that can be turned more or less independently of one another. Third, a creative act is a turn of one or more of those knobs: a small turn is routine, a large or unexpected turn is creative. Fourth, the genuinely imaginative cases are the higher-level moves, where one does not turn a knob but changes which dimensions are variable at all, or reshapes the skeleton while keeping the theme recognisable. He calls the whole business knob-twiddling, and he thinks it accounts for music completely — that what a jazz musician is doing when improvising is exactly this and nothing more.

I have played jazz for fifteen years and I do not believe him. But I do not trust my own objection either, since my reasons may be nothing more than the vanity of a practitioner. I have a conservatory theory background: I can talk about functional harmony, voice leading, form, reharmonisation, substitution and modal interchange at the level of someone who uses them daily, though I am not a theorist. Treat me as fluent in the vocabulary of the bandstand and the practice room, and in the standard theoretical vocabulary that goes with it.

What I want is a test, not another round of the argument.

Take the four parts above and restate each of them, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use — the head, the changes, comping, reharmonisation, substitution, modal interchange, motivic transformation, trading fours, contrafacts, playing the changes versus ignoring them, and whatever else the music supplies. Where the picture is vague, do not fill it in on my friend's behalf; if you must choose a reading, say which one you chose and why.

Then give me the residue report, one part at a time. For each of the four, tell me what translates cleanly into how musicians talk and think, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no counterpart at all. The third category is the part I care about, so do not hurry past it, and do not count as residue anything that is merely the absence of a word: I am asking about things the vocabulary cannot express, not things it happens not to name. I also do not want the residue to arrive as the usual list of things music is said to have and theories to lack — embodiment, emotion, sociality — unless you can show that each item bears on one specific part of the picture and would change the verdict on it.

Finally, my own suspicion, which I would like tested rather than endorsed. The picture strikes me as retrospective and spectatorial: it locates creativity in a relation between an act and a theme, both of which can be inspected after the fact. Playing feels like the opposite — it happens forward, in time, in response to what the drummer just did, and there is no moment at which a knob can be untwisted. Is that a real gap in the picture, or only a fact about performance rather than about creativity? Whichever way you come down, say whether the residue you found indicts the picture or indicts my vocabulary — whether the model cannot say something about music, or whether musicians cannot say something about creativity. And tell me which of the four parts is load-bearing: which one, if it fell, would bring the rest down with it.

I do not need references or a reading list; I want the translation and the diagnosis. If my friend has flattened the original into something cruder than it is, say so briefly, then work with the version I have given you anyway, since that is the one I have to answer.
