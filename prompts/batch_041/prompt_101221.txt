I host a monthly discussion group at a community center. The group is mixed: a few people have read some philosophy, most have not. We meet for a single ninety-minute session, and there are about twelve participants. I have been thinking on my own about a condition in which a person reports pain but does not find it bad. The person will say that it hurts—sometimes intensely—yet is not distressed by it, does not dread it, and may even decline pain relief because the sensation does not bother them. What interests me is not the medical rarity but the conceptual separation it seems to reveal: between pain as a sensation with an intensity and pain as something that is bad for the one who has it. I want the group to feel the force of that separation, not merely hear it described.

Design a runnable ninety-minute activity for twelve people that does this. The activity should not open with a lecture about the condition, and it should not name the condition until the debrief. Instead, the rules, materials, and incentives should arrange things so that participants themselves produce the separation between intensity and badness—so that they find themselves ranking, trading, or choosing in ways that make the two come apart before anyone tells them they can. Use cheap materials: index cards, tokens, a whiteboard, a timer. I would like a clear sequence: setup, rules, what participants actually do, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual distinction. The debrief should also address the harder question the split raises: whether the badness of pain is a separate component added to the sensation, or whether it is a matter of what the sensation does to the person—its motivational grip, its demand for relief, its role in practical reasoning. I would like participants to feel both why someone might say that pain can be present without being bad and why someone might resist that description.

Please include an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle those failures without turning the session into a lecture. Also tell me which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce—what it will inevitably flatten or leave out—and what I, as the facilitator, will have to supply in the debrief.
