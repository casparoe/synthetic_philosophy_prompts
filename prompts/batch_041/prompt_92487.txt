I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar that sits between rational-choice theory and democratic theory, and I am drafting the take-home final. I want a single essay question on the paradox of voting, and I do not want a question that a student can answer by reciting the familiar observation that one vote is astronomically unlikely to decide an election. The question should force students to separate things that are routinely run together, and to evaluate an argument rather than summarise a literature.

Please build the question around the following piece of reasoning, which I want reproduced in the question itself as the position students must assess, attributed only to a colleague in our reading group and not to any published source. The colleague argues that the turnout puzzle is an error in the unit of analysis. The standard puzzle, she says, asks what difference my single vote makes; but voting is not a solitary act with a solitary effect, it is a move inside a practice that only functions if enough others make the same move. Ask instead what happens when people situated as I am, deciding as I decide, vote or stay home, and a reason to vote reappears. She concludes that the paradox therefore dissolves rather than being solved.

The question should require students to do four things. First, reconstruct the standard argument precisely enough to identify which premise the colleague's reframing actually attacks, and make clear that the argument does not claim a vote has no effect but that its expected effect is swamped, so that the conclusion depends on how the value of the outcome is specified and on the size of the cost. Second, distinguish the turnout puzzle from the other problem that is also called the paradox of voting, and say why treating them as one thing is a mistake. Third, assess whether the colleague's move dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it: students should locate the inferential gap between what people in my position would bring about and what I have reason to do, and decide whether closing that gap requires an independent normative premise about participating in a practice, in which case the puzzle is conceded rather than dissolved. Fourth, say what the whole exercise implies for the ethics of voting, in particular whether there can be a duty to vote that is not grounded in the expected consequences of one's own ballot, and whether any such duty is conditional on the character of the practice.

Then give me a model answer of about seven hundred words and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should consist of five must-hit points worth five marks each, twenty-five in total, and for each point a single line on how candidates typically lose it. An A answer should be separated from a B answer by whether it keeps dissolution and relocation distinct and by whether it makes a normative account of voting do real work rather than invoking it as a label.

Everything you write for me, including the marking scheme, must be continuous prose. No headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic for emphasis, and no tables. Write it the way one would set it out in a long letter to a colleague.
