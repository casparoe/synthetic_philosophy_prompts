A friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about whether a nation can be guilty, and specifically about what Jaspers's fourfold division of guilt is actually doing — whether political guilt is a genuine species of guilt or just a polite name for liability and consequences, and whether anything in his framework can reach people who were not yet born when the crimes were committed. We did not settle it. I mention this because I am now marking a batch of examination scripts on precisely that question, and one answer has unsettled me: it reads well, it is confident, and it says something close to what my friend was arguing, which is exactly why I do not trust my first impression of it. I would like a second opinion that is not coloured by the argument I have just had.

The paper is a final-year undergraduate paper in political philosophy. The question was: Does Jaspers's distinction between criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt show that a nation can be guilty, or does it show only that individuals can be? Candidates were told to address the generational case — whether guilt of any kind can attach to those who came after — and to mark clearly where they were reconstructing Jaspers and where they were arguing on their own account. Answers are marked out of 100, with 70 and above a first, 60 to 69 an upper second, 50 to 59 a lower second, 40 to 49 a third, and below 40 a fail. Scripts are double-marked, so please be explicit enough that a second marker can see what you are rewarding and penalising.

The answer reads as follows:

Jaspers's account of guilt in the aftermath of the Second World War is best understood as an attempt to distinguish four senses in which a German might be said to be guilty. Criminal guilt attaches to those who broke specific laws and were tried in the courts; it is individual and it is the business of the judiciary. Political guilt attaches to all citizens of a state, because the state acts in the name of its citizens and they must bear the consequences of what it does; it is therefore collective. Moral guilt is what a person has before their own conscience for the acts they performed or failed to prevent. Metaphysical guilt is guilt before God, arising from the fact that one is still alive while others were killed, and from the impossibility of remaining wholly innocent once such things have happened.

The crucial point is that Jaspers denies collective guilt only in the strict moral sense. He accepts that all Germans were morally guilty in the political sense, since they had allowed themselves to be governed by a criminal regime, and this is what entitled the Allies to impose conditions on Germany afterwards. Moral guilt properly so called is individual and cannot be transferred: a son cannot be guilty of his father's crime, and Jaspers is clear that the guilt of the Nazi generation died with that generation. It follows that those born after the war have no share in the guilt of the Nazi period, and therefore that present-day Germans have no special obligations arising from that history — no obligation to fund memorials, for instance, or to compensate victims. So the answer to whether a nation can be guilty is that it can be politically guilty but not morally guilty; and since political guilt is only a matter of liability and consequences, the word guilt is in that context doing very little work.
