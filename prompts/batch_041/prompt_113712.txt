Last night I got into an unusually long argument with a friend — a philosopher who works on social choice — about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and we went in circles until the small hours without either of us giving ground. His position is that the theorem is one of the deepest results in moral philosophy: if each individual's preferences and the society's preferences all satisfy the axioms of expected utility theory, and if the social preference satisfies a suitably strong Pareto condition, then the social welfare function must be a positive weighted sum of individual utilities; and that once one adds the veil of ignorance — behind which a rational chooser regards herself as equally likely to occupy any position in society — the weights must come out equal, so that the theorem amounts to a rigorous derivation of (average) utilitarianism rather than a mere stipulation of it. My position is that this proves much less than he thinks. The axioms on their own deliver only a weighted sum, with the weights left entirely free; everything hinges on the equiprobability assumption at the veil, which is not a theorem but a substantive and contestable claim about how an impartial chooser should decide under uncertainty. A chooser who assigned unequal probabilities to being the various people, or who reasoned with a maximin rule rather than maximizing expected utility, or who cared about the distribution of outcomes rather than about her own prospect, need not be irrational in any way the theorem exposes. He countered that the theorem's real content is the claim that social rationality has the same expected-utility structure as individual rationality, and that rejecting that structure simply begs the question; I said that this is precisely the move that needs defending rather than assuming.

We kept tripping over two things in particular. First, the sure-thing principle: he treats its application to the social preference as innocuous, while I suspect it is doing most of the work and quietly rules out the very forms of concern for the worst-off that motivate the whole disagreement. Second, the difference between evaluating a policy ex ante (by the expected utility it gives each person) and ex post (by the utilities in each outcome): the theorem seems committed to the former, but it is not obvious that this is the right level at which to compare a society's options, and a friend of ours who dropped by suggested that if the chooser behind the veil were allowed to weight the positions unequally — as someone with a prioritarian concern for the worst-off might — the theorem would simply hand back a weighted sum with unequal weights, which makes it look less like an argument for utilitarianism than a neutral framework for choosing the weights.

What I want from you is a dialogue between my friend and me, written so that each side is presented at its strongest and neither is a strawman. Let him mount the best available defense of the theorem — including why he thinks the expected-utility structure of social preference is a constraint of rationality rather than a parochial assumption, and why ex ante evaluation is the correct level of assessment — and let me press the objections sketched above, each of us responding to what the other has actually said rather than talking past each other. I would especially like us to work through a concrete case (a small policy choice involving a lottery over who ends up well or badly off) so that the abstract disagreement about weights, probabilities, and the timing of evaluation becomes visible. Neither of us should concede too easily; if the exchange ends in a standoff, that is fine, but it should be a standoff in which both positions have been sharpened rather than blurred.
