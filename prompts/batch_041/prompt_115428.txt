I am a novelist, and I have been reading Hegel's Phenomenology of Mind in the old Baillie translation, in the section on lordship and bondage. I have reached this paragraph, and I cannot make it yield:

"This sublation in a double sense of its otherness in a double sense is at the same time a return in a double sense into its self. For, firstly, through sublation, it gets back itself, because it becomes one with itself again through the cancelling of its otherness; but secondly, it likewise gives otherness back again to the other self-consciousness, for it was aware of being in the other, it cancels this its own being in the other and thus lets the other again go free."

What I need is a patient reading of this paragraph, and then an account of what it is doing in the larger movement. Specifically: what are the two senses of sublation and the two senses of return here, and how do they relate to the two self-consciousnesses? Is the master's letting the other go free a moment of recognition, or is it precisely the failure of recognition that produces the master–slave relation? And how does this paragraph connect to the later claim that the bondsman, through labour and the formative activity of work, arrives at a self-consciousness that the master, in his enjoyment, does not?

I am trying to write a novel about a captor and his captive, and I want to know what Hegel is actually claiming about the structure of domination: whether he is saying that domination is self-undermining because it cannot secure the recognition it seeks, or whether he is saying something more precise and stranger — that the master's triumph consists in a kind of letting-go that is also a loss, and that the slave's subjection contains the seed of a freedom the master cannot have.

Please separate what is settled in the scholarly literature from what is genuinely contested among people who know the text well. In particular, I do not want a contested reading presented as the consensus: if the idea that the slave's labour is the engine of the dialectic is one reading among others, or if the symmetry of recognition is disputed, or if the historical versus logical status of the whole section is up for grabs, say so. I would rather have a careful account that marks its own uncertainties than a confident summary that flattens the disagreements.
