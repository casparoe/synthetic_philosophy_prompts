I am writing a novel whose central relationship is between a provincial official in a country I will leave unnamed and the businessman who has, over twenty years, paid for his daughter's schooling, stood surety on his brother's loan, and received in return not envelopes of cash but a series of introductions, permits, and one extremely lucrative blind eye. My editor will want to know whether this man is corrupt, and I have been trying to answer that question honestly rather than decoratively.

At a residency last spring I put the problem to a political scientist, and she disposed of it with a sentence I have not been able to stop turning over: that one person's gift is another person's bribe. She meant, I think, to warn me against imposing a foreign moral vocabulary on the material, and several people at the table nodded as though a question had been settled. It was not settled for me, partly because I cannot tell what the sentence is actually claiming.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that line might be expressing, ranging from the nearly empty to the quite radical? I am interested in the difference between the claim that the word corruption is applied unevenly across societies, the claim that the practice in question is not corrupt under the norms of the society that practices it, the claim that there is no fact of the matter because the norms are the only facts there are, and any further reading you think the sentence can bear. For each, I would like to know whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and what would be required to establish it. Then tell me which of these readings a speaker is most likely to be relying on when she uses the line to close down a discussion of a novel's moral framing, and whether that reading actually does the work she needs it to do. I would also like to know how the standard donor-and-NGO formula — corruption as the misuse of entrusted authority for private gain — fares under each reading, since that definition is what most of my readers will carry into the book, and the novel has to survive their judgment as well as hers.
