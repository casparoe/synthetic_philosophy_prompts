I'm a novelist, third book, and I've written myself into a corner. It's a comedy of manners set inside the privacy-compliance industry and there's a scene where my protagonist, a mid-level data-protection officer, is half-reading the advice column in her company's internal newsletter while putting off an actual decision. I need you to write that column for me, because every version I try comes out too clever and it needs to be deadpan.

Setup, so it doesn't come out generic: in my book there's a fictional regulator called the Office of Proportional Disclosure — don't sweat the details, it's basically an alt-universe mashup of the German, American and Chinese instincts about what citizens owe the state and what the state owes them in the way of not looking. The column is by the company's in-house agony aunt. I've been calling her Dr. Redact. She answers employees' letters about how much they personally should be hiding — from the employer, from the state, from each other — and she is relentlessly reasonable and never once breaks character. The joke, which I want you to never explain, is that she handles every letter by applying one of the three logics with perfect consistency, so the reader has to work out for themselves which logic she's picked and what it does to the person who asked.

Give me three or four letters and her replies. The letters should sit on real, recongnizable tensions, the kind someone in the field would nod at: consent fatigue and whether clicking accept a thousand times can mean anything; the "I've got nothing to hide" line and what's actually wrong with it; whether a personal trustworthiness score is unfair if it happens to be accurate; whether a right to be forgotten can coexist with someone else's right to remember; data minimisation versus the state occasionally genuinely needing to know. She should be even-handed to the point of being unsettling — no winking, no stating the critique, no "of course, this raises concerns."

Voice notes: she's a little vain, a little tired, fond of her own turns of phrase, and she signs off with the same small motto every time (make one up). Work in a running bit where she grades each writer's "disclosure hygiene" out of ten. Don't do a listicle, it's a column, so let her ramble and bury the lede. Around 600-700 words. No real companies, no statistics you'd have to invent, and please don't have her mention my fictional regulator so often that it reads like a worldbuilding dump — it's an in-world newsletter, so a bit of bueracracy is fine and even good.
