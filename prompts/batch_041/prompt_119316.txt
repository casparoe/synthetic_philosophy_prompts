I have no academic training in philosophy or economics; I read popular books on this sort of thing and then think about them on my own, so please forgive any terminology I get slightly wrong. I have been chewing over a lottery puzzle for some months and have ended up with a set of views that I suspect cannot all be held at once. I would like an audit of them rather than an introduction to the subject.

The puzzle, in the version I have been working with — my own tidied-up version of something I have seen called the Platonia dilemma, with a lottery attached that I think is called the luring lottery, so the details may be slightly off:

A prize of £1,000,000 is to be shared out among the entries received by a certain date. An entry is a postcard. Anyone may send in as many postcards as they like, or none at all. The prize is divided evenly among the postcards that arrive, so that if K postcards arrive in total each postcard is worth £1,000,000 divided by K, and whoever sent a postcard collects that amount for it; a person who sends m postcards therefore collects m times £1,000,000 divided by K. If no postcards arrive, nothing is paid to anyone. Everyone is in the same position: same information, same prize, same rules, and everyone knows this.

Here is where I have landed.

1. The right thing for me to do is to send exactly one postcard — not none, and not two.
2. The way to settle what to do in a case like this is to ask what would happen if everyone in my position did the same as me, and to choose the act that comes out best on that assumption.
3. If I were ever in a prisoner's dilemma against a perfect copy of myself — someone who will reason his way to exactly the conclusion I reach — I would cooperate, because there is no point in my taking the option that is better only if I am the only one taking it. Against an ordinary stranger, whose reasoning I cannot predict, I would look after myself and defect, because nothing I do changes what he does.
4. In Newcomb's problem I take both boxes. What is in the boxes was settled before I chose, and taking the extra box can only add to what I get.
5. The general principle I take from (3) and (4) is that what makes an act the right one is what it brings about, not what it would show about me or about how other people are likely to behave. Someone else's past decision is not an effect of my present choice, so it is not something my choice answers to.
6. If I discovered that everyone else had already sent in a hundred postcards each, I would send in a hundred and one rather than one.

What I would like from you:

- Say which of these pull against each other, which only appear to, and which are simply independent. I would much rather you erred towards saying that something only appears to be a tension than that you flag everything as a conflict.
- Order the genuine tensions by how badly they damage the package, and for each one say which item would have to give, what would be lost by giving it up, and what the remaining views would then commit me to.
- Where the answer turns on the arithmetic of the lottery, please set the arithmetic out explicitly rather than gesturing at it; I want to know whether my picture of the incentives is even correct, and in particular what my universalising rule in (2) does and does not pick out.
- Finish by naming the single change that would clear up the largest number of problems at once, and say what I would be taking on, and what I would be giving up, if I made it.

Please do not spend the answer telling me where these ideas come from or what other people have said about them. I only want to know whether this particular set of views can stand together, and where it cracks.
