Act as a historian of twentieth-century philosophy of science who knows the Vienna Circle in detail, and who also knows the long dispute over whether a centrally planned economy can allocate resources rationally. I want a considered reconstruction from you, not a survey, and I want it to end in a verdict.

The question concerns an episode Carnap did not live to see. In 1971, the year after his death, the Allende government in Chile began building a system designed by Stafford Beer that connected state enterprises by telex to a single mainframe in Santiago: factories transmitted daily production indices, statistical software flagged deviations from predicted ranges in near-real time, an economic simulator projected the effects of interventions, and an operations room displayed the results so that managers could send directives back out. Since then the ambition has been restated in stronger and more general terms — that machine learning, big data, and real-time optimization make it possible to run an economy in kind, setting quantities directly or computing shadow prices by iterating over a full model of production and demand, and that this dissolves the old calculation problem, the objection that without market prices there is no way to know the opportunity cost of resources or to discover which techniques, tastes, and local circumstances matter. The standard replies are that such systems either presuppose market prices in the data they learn from, or reproduce the knowledge problem in new clothing, and that proposals to plan in labour hours or physical quantities only relocate the difficulty. Suppose Carnap had lived long enough to read all of this.

What would he have thought? Address at least these three things. First, would he have treated the feasibility question — can in-kind computational allocation work? — as a theoretical question with a determinate answer, or would his distinction between questions internal to a framework and external, practical questions about which framework to adopt have led him to regard part of the dispute as a matter of decision rather than truth? And does that analogy even survive the fact that an economic order, unlike a language, has causal consequences in the world? Second, how would the rest of his apparatus cut: his physicalism and the ideal of a unified, quantitative science, which might encourage confidence that all the relevant facts are measurable and formally representable; his non-cognitivism about value judgments, given that the choice of an economic order is saturated with valuation; his principle of tolerance and his later pluralism; and his habit of treating philosophical perplexity as the residue of inadequate conceptual tools. Third, would he have been more Neurathian than Neurath or more cautious than Neurath — where exactly would he have parted company with the enthusiasts and with the Austrians? Weigh against all of this the fact that his explicit political writing is concentrated in his early years and that after his emigration he wrote very little about politics, so that much of what you say will be reconstruction from his philosophical commitments rather than report of his stated views.

Give me a definite verdict, with the reasoning that gets you there. Do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me, and do not hedge into "one could argue either way" — a firm answer that is wrong in a discoverable way is more useful to me than a balanced survey. Think it through before you start writing: spend a moment laying out the strongest Carnapian case for optimism about computational planning and the strongest Carnapian case against it, decide which is stronger and why, and then write roughly a thousand words of continuous prose defending your conclusion. Where you are reconstructing rather than reporting, say so in a sentence, but do not let that qualification dilute the verdict.
