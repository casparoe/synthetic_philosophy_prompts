Write a dialogue between two theorists of concepts, each fully competent and neither a strawman for the other. The occasion is their joint examination of a deliberately narrow microdomain: letter-string analogies of the form "abc is to abd as ijk is to what?", together with a small family of variants in which the strings are reversed, in which a run of identical letters stands in for a single letter, and in which the problem to be solved arrives only after a stretch of earlier, unrelated problems.

The first theorist holds that the concepts doing the work in this microdomain — "letter", "successor", "the same", "change the last one" — have no fixed core. On this view a concept is constituted on the fly by the analogies the reasoner has just drawn and by the pressure of the surrounding problems; the same string can therefore be read under different concepts in different contexts, and there is no context-independent fact about which completion is correct. The second theorist holds that the apparent fluidity is an artifact of a particular kind of implementation — stochastic, parallel, governed by something like a temperature — and not a feature of concepts as such; on this view there is a stable core to be isolated, and the work is to state it.

Both agree that the discriminating case is whether a run of prior problems can change the answer to a later one. Under the first view, priming by a stretch of reversal problems ought to shift how the reasoner reads "change the last letter" when it finally arrives; under the second, the earlier problems are irrelevant to the later answer and any shift is noise to be explained away by the implementation. Have them press this case against each other and see how far each will go: the first must say whether any reading is ever wrong, and the second must say where the core is written if not in the run of problems. Each should respond to the other's actual strongest move rather than to a caricature of it.

Keep the dialogue to the two positions and the strings. Do not name any real program, paper, or author, and do not cite anything; the argument must stand on the examples alone. End with the two of them agreeing on one further case that would settle the matter, and disagreeing about what it would show.
