OK so I got into an argument at a department lunch and it's been bugging me for a week. A collegue of mine (maths, not philosophy) reckons the Condorcet jury theorem basically settles how groups should make decisions, and he used it to argue we should scrap expert panels entirely. I wrote his case out and I'm pretty sure it proves way too much, but I can't put my finger on where it breaks. Here it is:

(1) If a group votes on a yes/no question, and each member is independently more likely to get it right than wrong, then the majority is more likely to be right than any single member is, and the more members you add, the closer the majority's probability of being right gets to 1.
(2) Our faculty has to decide whether a grant proposal is worth funding. Suppose each of us, reading it cold for half an hour, is right 51% of the time — barely better than a coin, but better.
(3) Then if we just get enough of us in a room — say 400 of us, each at 51% and each making up their own mind — the majority verdict will be right with probability indistinguishable from certainty.
(4) So it's actually epistemically irresponsible to use a small panel of real specialists; a big enough room of barely-above-chance readers beats them.
(5) Which is obviously nuts. Three people who've worked in this area for a decade and spent a week on the proposal are not less reliable than 400 of us skimming it over coffee.
(6) So either (1) is wrong or I'm applying it wrong.

Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If it helps: I'd much rather keep (1) than keep (4) — it's a real theorem and I don't want to be the guy who denies maths — but I'll take the hit if I have to. I also honestly couldn't tell you whether "51%" is even the right kind of thing to plug in there, or whether "each making up their own mind" is what the theorem actually needs.

One thing I'd ask: seperate the bits that are genuinely settled among people who know this theorem from the bits that are actually contested, and don't sell me a contested reading as if it were the consensus. I don't know this literature well enough to tell the difference myself.
