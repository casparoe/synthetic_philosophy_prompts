I teach a grad seminar on interpretability and AI safety, and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on mechanistic anomaly detection, 60 minutes, closed book. Trouble is, every version I've drafted can be passed by reciting the formula — anomaly means the model computed the answer with a mechanism you didn't anticipate — and then gesturing at "compare it to the mechanism you did anticipate." The actual hard part, which my students have definately never had to defend, is the identity side of it: "a mechanism you didn't anticipate" only means anything if you already have a story about when two mechanisms count as the same one. So the question has to force them to state that story and then live with it.

Here's the setup I'm putting on the exam sheet. Please reuse it, I want the question to be self-contained so nobody has to have read any particular paper:

Suppose you've audited a model on some task and you have a verified account of the mechanism it uses — a chain of components that carries information left to right, call it M. Now three cases come up. (1) Same behavior, different insides: on new inputs the outputs match what M predicts, but your probes show the answer being computed along a different route. (2) Same insides, different system: you retrain from scratch and find something that, at the level of description you used for M, has the same parts in the same arrangement — but the weights are all different and it came out of a separate training run. (3) Same behavior, same insides, different level: every probe you can run that respects your chosen units (heads, features, directions in activation space) comes out the same, but a colleague re-describes the same computation in finer-grained units and it stops looking like one mechanism.

And I'll hand them three candidate criteria to pick from: (a) two mechanisms are the same when they compute the same input-output map on the distribution that matters; (b) they're the same when they respond identically to a declared set of interventions; (c) they're the same when they have the same causal history, i.e. came out of the same process of formation.

The question: pick a criterion, defend it against the other two, and use it to say which of cases 1–3 is a mechanistic anomaly and which isn't. Then the part I actually care about — say whether, on their criterion, "same mechanism" is a claim about the world or a claim about what the overseer happened to know, and show that their answer doesn't make mechanistic anomaly detection either trivial (everything is an anomaly, since no two runs are identical) or vacuous (nothing is, since behaviorally equivalent things get lumped together). If their criterion does collapse into one of those, they should say so and say what the least-bad repair is. I also want them to name one case where the criterion gives an answer we shouldn't trust.

Then write me three things: the question as it would appear on the exam sheet, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (20 total), each point with a one-line note on how students typically lose it. The scheme has to actually separate an A from a B — name the specific move only the A answers make, not just "shows understanding." American English please, it's a US program.

One last thing: the students won't have web access during the exam and I dont want the question hinging on any particular paper, so don't build it around citations or the names of specific methods. The setup above is the whole world of the question.
