Act as a philosopher of mind and an ethicist of animal welfare, with a solid working knowledge of invertebrate neurobiology and of the methodological debates over how sentience is detected. Think carefully before you answer, and begin with a brief brainstorming pass in which you list the candidate readings you are considering before you commit to any verdicts.

I sit on a small working group drafting welfare guidance for invertebrate farming. One member keeps ending arguments with a single sentence: sentience comes in degrees, so any line you draw is arbitrary. His use of it is a slippery-slope move. If we take insect sentience seriously, he says, then on any graded scale something with feedback loops and internal states — a thermostat, or the control system of a thermostat-like device — scores above zero as well; so either we admit the thermostat into the moral community or we concede that our insect threshold was a matter of taste. His conclusion is that we should leave the line where it is and not regulate insects at all. I think the sentence is doing several jobs at once and that at least one of them is a fallacy, but I cannot yet say which.

Give me a menu, not a single reading. Separate out the distinct precise claims that the sentence could be making, label them, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, along with what would decide it. At minimum, I want the following distinctions handled:

- degrees of the phenomenal property itself, versus degrees of richness or determinacy in its content, versus degrees of our confidence about it;
- a graded underlying capacity versus a graded moral status — and whether the second follows from the first at all;
- the several senses of arbitrary: unprincipled, conventional but principled (as with an age of majority), and genuinely no-fact-of-the-matter;
- a boundary that is sharp in the world but unknowable in practice, versus a boundary that does not exist;
- the sorites structure of the slide to thermostats, and whether the colleague's argument needs an actual vagueness premise or only an epistemic one;
- the possibility of graded moral weight combined with a threshold below which nothing registers, and whether that combination is stable.

Then tell me which reading his argument actually requires, whether that reading is true, and whether it gets him his conclusion. If some reading of the slogan cuts against him — licensing precaution rather than indifference — say so and explain why. Close with one paragraph I can read aloud at the next meeting, stating what the slogan does and does not entitle anyone to conclude.

Work from the concepts themselves; do not pad the answer with literature references, and do not retreat into the observation that the matter is complicated.
