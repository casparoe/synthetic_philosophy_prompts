For the past several months a running argument among my friends has been whether consciousness is best conceived of as a kind of cinema. One of them, who builds virtual-reality displays for a living, is adamant that the brain is essentially a projector and a screen: information is rendered, then presented at a fixed rate — he has some figure in mind of a dozen or so showings a second — and what we call experience is simply whatever happens to be on screen at the moment of showing, so that there is always a fact of the matter about which frame is currently "now". The other, who has read a little about the philosopher Daniel Dennett's attack on what he calls the Cartesian theatre, takes the same picture in the opposite direction. Since there is no screen, no auditorium and no single place where everything is at last displayed to an audience of one, she concludes that consciousness as we ordinarily conceive of it is an illusion, a story the brain tells about a performance that never took place. They cannot both be right, and my suspicion is that both are leaning on the same comparison rather harder than it will bear.

What I have managed to gather about the alternative — the multiple drafts model — is that it denies any finish line in the brain at which the order in which things arrive becomes the order in which they are experienced. On that account perception is a swarm of parallel processes, each of which becomes reportable, or leaves its mark on memory and behaviour, at its own time; there is no completed print and no single showing, and something's becoming conscious is supposed to be less like being displayed than like becoming famous. I have also read somewhere about an experiment in which a green spot and a red spot flashed in quick succession are seen as one spot travelling across and changing colour part-way — and that nobody can say when, exactly, the colour change is experienced, because there seems to be no moment at which the "finished" version is delivered. That case strikes me as the crux of the matter, and I would like the cinema picture audited against it.

So: please lay out how far the cinema analogy actually holds. Set out, in prose, what is supposed to correspond to what — the projector, the screen, the audience, the frame, the running time, the completed print — and say which of those correspondences survive contact with the multiple drafts model and which are quietly imported by the analogy without any warrant. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the two uses my friends are making of it from the ones that are merely decorative, tell me what each of them smuggles in (a viewer, a single moment of presentation, simultaneity, a privileged place), and say whether either conclusion — that consciousness has a datable location in the stream, or that there is no consciousness at all — follows from anything left standing once the mapping is corrected. I am also curious whether my engineer friend's variant, in which the brain is likened to a headset that renders late and then retroactively warps the image so that the delay is never noticed, is a genuinely better analogy or merely the same mistake in modern dress, and what a better comparison would be if there is one. Please write it as continuous prose, the way one would set it out in a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and in British English.
