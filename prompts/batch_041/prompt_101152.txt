I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I've been circling a piece about how historians actually explain things, and I keep getting snagged on a distinction a philosopher friend of mine keeps pushing over drinks. She says an explanation can work in one of two ways: it can subsume the event under a general law (the old Hempel covering-law picture, where you explain a revolution or the collapse of a currency the way you'd explain a metal bar expanding -- by showing that, given the laws and the initial conditions, it more or less had to happen), or it can tell a story, where the explanation just *is* the narrative and the explanatory work gets done by the ordering of events, each one setting up the next so that by the end you can see how it all hangs together. I roughly get the contrast, but two things genuinely puzzle me. First, why should the narrative sort count as an explanation at all rather than a well-told description that leaves the reader satisfied without ever showing *why* anything had to occur? And second, why do narrativists insist the covering-law picture isn't merely too demanding but actually misdescribes what historians are up to? So could you lay the conceptual issue out properly for me: what each model thinks an explanation is *for*, what the covering-law model has to say about the awkward fact that historians hardly ever state any laws, and what a narrativist means when they claim the chronology itself does explanatory work rather than just decorating it.

And there's a third strand I'd like folded in, because it's the bit that keeps derailing the piece. A different friend -- a novelist, so take it with a grain of salt -- reckons historical explanation is really more like the solution to a detective story. On his account the point isn't a law and it isn't even the sequence: it's the moment of reversal where you suddenly re-see events you'd already been told about, so that the clue dropped in chapter two turns out to have meant something else entirely, and the whole thing clicks into place retroactively. Is that just a colourful restatement of the narrative view, or is it picking out something the narrativists haven't quite got a name for -- some backwards-looking, re-descriptive element that neither the law model nor the plain story model captures? Tell me what it gets right that the other two miss, and then where the analogy breaks down, because I suspect it breaks somewhere interesting.
