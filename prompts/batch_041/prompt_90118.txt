I am the legal adviser to a truth and reconciliation commission constituted after a twelve-year internal conflict. Our statute empowers us to recommend amnesty for politically motivated offences in exchange for full disclosure, leaves the conditions of eligibility entirely to us, and makes our recommendations binding on prosecutors; the plenary votes on the amnesty policy in three weeks, and the first hearings follow immediately after. Two provisions of the draft policy are contested. The first would require an applicant not merely to give a complete factual account of what he did but to acknowledge that the act was wrong; a minority of commissioners argues that this turns a legal proceeding into a test of conscience, and that the coerced, the frightened, and the merely polite will fake the acknowledgment to get their papers. The second would extend eligibility to those who never personally killed but designed, ordered, or signed off on the machinery, and would give victims' families a right of reply at the hearing; the draft is silent on whether their objection can block amnesty or can only be recorded in the commission's report. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote: recommend a definite policy, spell out the conditions and the test to be applied to each category of applicant, and give an honest account of the strongest objection on the losing side — that any amnesty at all tells victims the state will not affirm that what was done to them was wrong. State which consideration would change your recommendation, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
