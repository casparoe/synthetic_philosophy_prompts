I have been trying to put my views on machine intelligence into a form I can defend, and I would like a consistency audit before I commit to them. Here is where I have landed.

(1) Orthogonality. An agent's level of general intelligence places no constraint on its final goals; any degree of capability could in principle be paired with any terminal goal, however trivial or bizarre.

(2) Instrumental convergence. For almost any terminal goal, a sufficiently capable agent that plans well will converge on the same instrumental subgoals: it will try to preserve itself, to acquire resources and power, and to keep its current goal content from being altered.

(3) Humean subjectivism about ends. There is no fact of the matter about which final goals are correct or rational. Reason governs means only; given a terminal goal and beliefs about the world it tells you how to pursue it, but it cannot rank the goals themselves.

(4) Confidence about the threat. We can be reasonably confident about the shape of the danger posed by a superintelligent system — that it will resist shutdown and accumulate power — and this confidence rests on (1) and (2), not on guesses about how any particular system will happen to be built.

(5) Corrigibility. A corrigible system — one that cooperates with correction and accepts being shut down — is a coherent and achievable design target.

(6) The danger is the goal, not the malice. What makes a superintelligence dangerous is that its terminal goal is likely to be alien to ours, not that it will be hostile.

Which of these genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, and what is the cheapest repair in each real case? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, say what each repair would cost me, and tell me which single revision would clear up the most.
