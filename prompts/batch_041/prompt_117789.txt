I host a small podcast on decision theory and epistemology — mostly an excuse to talk at length with people whose work I have been arguing with in comment threads for a decade — and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career trying to retire the Dutch book argument for probabilism. Her position, as I reconstruct it, has three planks. First, that the synchronic Dutch book theorem is at bottom a theorem about the consistency of a preference ordering over acts: anyone who has already accepted expected-utility theory learns nothing from it, and anyone who hasn't is being handed an argument that quietly assumes what it set out to prove. Second, that the diachronic versions of the argument — the ones meant to force conditionalization, or a principle of reflection — are invalid, because they require a bookie whose strategy is fixed in advance in order to exploit a foreseeable lapse of rationality, and an agent who has been told the strategy has thereby acquired evidence that ought to move the very credences the strategy is designed to punish. Third, that the criterion we should put in its place is what she calls adversarial robustness: your credences are acceptable just in case no rational and equally well-informed adversary can assemble a set of bets you would accept that guarantees you a loss. I have been reading rationalist blogs since well before the Sequences were finished, and I have lost more hours than I care to admit to forum threads in which somebody declares that a view is Dutch-bookable as though that ended the discussion, so I am broadly on her side, but I do not think I understand her position well enough to push on it.

What I want from you is nine to eleven interview questions, in the order I should actually ask them, that force her to defend each plank instead of restating it, plus for each question one or two conditional follow-ups keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — if she says the information asymmetry is doing no work, ask X; if she concedes the point, ask Y. I want the questions to press where the technical pressure is genuinely highest, so please make sure the set covers the following: whether her first plank leaves her with any argument for probabilism that is neither question-begging nor merely prudential, and whether she accepts that consequence; the gap between being exploitable and being irrational, including what her view says about an agent who simply refuses all bets, or who is risk-averse about stakes, or who is indifferent to money; the objection that the guaranteed loss attaches to the whole package of bets rather than to any single bet she would individually reject, and whether her criterion can even be stated without assuming the package principle; why the deliberative move in her second plank does not prove too much, since if learning the bookie's strategy legitimately shifts your credences, it seems to immunize you against synchronic Dutch books as well — if it does not, I want the question that makes her say what distinguishes the two cases; whether adversarial robustness is weaker than probabilism, taking as a test case an agent whose credences are only finitely additive, the kind who can assign probability one to each of infinitely many mutually exclusive possibilities so long as nothing forces those to sum to one; whether the clause requiring the adversary to be equally well informed collapses her criterion into the reasoning behind no-trade results in economics, where the asymmetry of information rather than any defect in the agent is what generates the sure loss; and whether the adversary can be the agent herself at a later time, since in that case the asymmetry disappears and the argument's force returns. Close with a question about what evidence or proof would actually make her abandon the adversarial robustness criterion, and one about what she takes the practical upshot to be for how people in my audience talk about well-calibrated systems.

Please keep each question short enough to say aloud and do not ask her to reproduce a proof — a sketch is fine, and she can always expand. Do not ask her for reading recommendations, paper titles, or where in the literature a point was first made; this is a conversation, not a syllabus, and I have read her work. Nothing about how she came to philosophy, and nothing that lets her answer by defining her own terms a fourth time.
