I have been teaching the axiology of Principia Ethica and find myself drawn to something close to Moore's own position: that the greatest intrinsic goods are certain complex wholes — what he calls the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects — and that these wholes are organic, so that a constituent may have little or no value in isolation and yet be indispensable to the value of the whole. I want a map of this position's exposure: which arguments, cases, or formal results would genuinely count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a clear line between what Moore himself should concede and what only a critic would press.

I am especially concerned with four pressure points. First, the method of absolute isolation: if its verdicts turn out to be unstable, or to conflict with Moore's own claim that the mere existence of beauty is negligible beside the consciousness of it, how much of the view survives, and is that magnitude comparison something isolation can actually deliver? Second, the aesthetic whole seems to require a robust realism about beauty; how much of the view stands if aesthetic value is response-dependent, so that the object's beauty is not a constituent feature of the world? Third, on friendship: making the beloved's actual existence and good qualities constituents of the valuable whole appears to commit Moore to the claim that affection for the unworthy is worth little, and that the value of a friendship varies with the friend's merit. If that is unacceptable, is there a repair that is not merely ad hoc? Fourth, what becomes of the view if goods Moore treats as subordinate — knowledge, achievement, moral virtue, the relief of suffering — turn out to be ultimate goods in their own right, or if the two great goods collapse into one?

Finally, tell me honestly whether the doctrine of organic unities insulates the position from counterexample, and whether that insulation is a defect in a theory of this kind.
