Two of my colleagues collided at a faculty meeting last week over a student's essay defending the claim that the pyramids were built by a lost civilisation, and over whether we should have called the essay pseudohistory in our comments rather than simply badly argued. I was asked to referee, declined, and would now like you to do it. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

The historian: The label is circular. We decide who is outside the club and then dress the decision up as method. And the lost-civilisation thesis is perfectly testable — it has been tested, by radiocarbon and stratigraphy, and it failed. Unfalsifiability is not what is wrong with it, so calling it pseudohistory adds nothing to the ordinary charge of being wrong.

The philosopher: Failure is not the issue; every research programme has anomalies. What marks pseudohistory is how anomalies are handled — each one is absorbed by another ad hoc move (the civilisation was more advanced than we thought, its traces were destroyed, the dating is corrupt), and the thesis never generates a novel prediction that survives testing. That is Lakatos's distinction between a progressive and a degenerating programme, and it is a claim about method, not about who is in the club.

The historian: Then your criterion reduces to handles evidence badly, which describes plenty of ordinary history that nobody calls pseudohistory. And the specific charge — that the thesis cannot be refuted — is false. Its defenders state clearly what would refute them: undisturbed strata at the relevant dates. What they then do is explain the evidence away, which is a vice, not a different kind of inquiry.

The philosopher: Explaining evidence away is precisely the different kind of inquiry. The absence of the expected ruins is read as evidence that the ruins were destroyed; the silence of the record becomes a premise. A programme that can convert any result into a confirmation is self-sealing, and self-sealing is an epistemic property, not a sociological one.

The historian: Every programme is self-sealing at the margins, and the ones we call respectable stay respectable because their practitioners hold the chairs, control the journals and train the next generation. This is the old objection that the demarcation problem is itself a pseudo-problem — that pseudoscience is a term of politics rather than epistemology — and nothing you have said answers it.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five moves, say how much of it is right and where it goes wrong, then say who has the better of the overall dispute and what both of them are missing.
