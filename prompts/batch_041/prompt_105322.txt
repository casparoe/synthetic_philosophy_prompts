I write about science and ideas, and for the past several months I've been reporting a piece on a claim that keeps surfacing in the decision theory I read for it: that to be rational is, at bottom, to maximize expected utility, and that this is not one stylistic option among rival decision rules but a requirement of coherence. A philosopher I interviewed for the article, who holds the strong version of the claim, gave me the following argument as a proof rather than a persuasion, and I've reproduced it as he laid it out. (1) A rational agent will not knowingly enter a finite sequence of transactions whose net effect is that she ends up certainly worse off than she started; call this the no-sure-loss condition. (2) For any agent whose preferences over options fail the expected-utility axioms — whose rankings are intransitive, or who treats some pairs of options as incomparable, or whose choices flout the independence condition — there exists a finite sequence of transactions, each of which she prefers to the position she occupies when it is offered to her, whose net effect is that she ends up certainly worse off. (3) Therefore the preferences of a rational agent must satisfy the expected-utility axioms. (4) By the standard representation results, a preference ranking satisfies those axioms just in case it can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to some probability and utility assignment. (5) Therefore a rational agent must maximize expected utility. He illustrates (2) with the familiar sort of pump — an agent who prefers a bicycle to a piano, a piano to a horse, and a horse to a bicycle, together with a broker who charges a small fee each time the agent trades up the cycle — and he takes the whole construction to refute anyone who thinks a rational agent may hold incomplete preferences, may be averse to risk in ways that depart from expected-utility maximization, or may simply decline to assign a number to everything.

I find myself unable to say where, if anywhere, the argument goes wrong, and I would rather not publish the strong claim if the argument meant to underwrite it is doing something illicit. What I most want to keep are (1) and (2): the sure-loss intuition strikes me as the firmest thing in the neighborhood, and the pump construction itself looks like a genuine theorem about the preferences it covers. So I'm asking you to name the premise or the inferential step I should give up, to explain why it looked so fine, and to price the candidate repairs — including what is lost if I narrow (2) to the cases it actually establishes rather than the wider class it advertises, and whether the conclusion can survive as a claim about how an agent's choices may be represented while being abandoned as a claim about how she must deliberate, or whether that distinction is just a way of keeping the conclusion while pretending to have dropped it. Be specific about which of (1) through (5) you are sacrificing, and about what else that forces overboard.
