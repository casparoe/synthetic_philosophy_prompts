I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a scene in my book. One character is a dying man's caretaker, and she tells him a lie to keep his spirits up—says his son is on the way when she has no idea if that's true. The other character is her brother, a lapsed seminarian who keeps quoting Augustine at her. I'm trying to figure out exactly what Augustine is saying in this passage from On Lying:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false."

Can you help me unpack it? I think I get that he's not saying you lie just because what you say is false—he's saying the lie is in the mismatch between what's in your mind and what you put out into the world. But then what's the double heart exactly? Is it two beleifs at once, or one belief plus one intention to deceive? And if my caretaker tells the dying man something she thinks is false but it turns out to be true (the son really is coming), does Augustine still call that a lie? That seems like the kind of thing my lapsed seminarian would pounce on. Also, does Augustine care at all about whether the lie helps or hurts? I need to know how to write the brother's argument without making him a cardboard absolutist. Thanks.
