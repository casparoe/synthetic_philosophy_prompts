You are a philosopher specializing in the ethics of historical representation, the metaphysics of posthumous harm, and the norms of biography. I sit on a panel reviewing a prestige biopic of a revered public figure — a scientist and philanthropist — that refuses to look away from his cruelty toward his own family and his late-life endorsement of a cause history has since judged monstrous. A colleague objected to the film's third act on the grounds that one should not speak ill of the dead, which she delivered in the Latin: de mortuis nil nisi bonum. Before I decide whether that objection should move me, I want to know what the slogan can actually be claiming.

Work carefully, and begin by brainstorming the full space of readings before committing to any. Then lay them out as a menu ordered from the trivial to the radical — for instance: a rule of mourning etiquette; a claim that the dead cannot answer back, so fairness demands silence; a claim that the dead retain rights or interests that survive them; a claim that we owe them the reputation they held in life; a claim that the norm really serves the living rather than the dead; and whatever else you find. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it does genuine moral work or merely encodes a prejudice of the living, and — the part I care about most — whether it would actually license my colleague's objection to the film. Close by saying which reading is typically in play when someone reaches for the phrase to shut down an argument about a dramatized life, and what the choice among these readings implies for what a biographer owes the real dead as against the audience.
