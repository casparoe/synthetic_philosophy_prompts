I am putting together a two-page research proposal for a small interdisciplinary workshop on commitment devices — economists, a few legal theorists, and two or three philosophers, with each of us given forty minutes and the organisers asking for a written plan a fortnight in advance. My assigned topic is constitutional precommitment, and I have a half-formed idea that I would like turned into a proper proposal.

The idea is this. Discussions of constitutional entrenchment nearly always lean on the individual case: an agent who fears her future self binds that self in advance, as Ulysses did with the rope and the oarsmen. I have come to think the two cases get run together because precommitment is treated as a single mechanism when it is at least two. Some devices work by removing an option — the boats are burned, the cigarettes are thrown away, the rope is tied. Call these removal-commitments. Others leave the option in place and change what it costs: the penalty clause, the deposit that is forfeited, the reputational stake. Call these payoff-commitments. A removal-commitment never works by itself; it works because some second party — call her the guardian — holds the line and has no reason to release the agent at the moment of weakness. Ulysses is bound because the oarsmen cannot hear him and would not untie him if they could. A payoff-commitment needs no guardian, but it needs something else instead: the agent must still care, at the later time, about the costs the device imposes.

My suspicion is that constitutional precommitment can only ever be of the second kind, and that the familiar objections are circling this point without quite landing on it. A constitution cannot burn boats. Any entrenched rule, however rigid, comes bundled with a procedure for its own amendment or replacement, and whoever applies that procedure — a court, a legislature, a constituent assembly — is a guardian whose incentives are fixed inside the very system she is meant to police. The rope is tied by the person it holds.

I do not want a demolition job. I want a proposal that asks, precisely, what would have to be true for a constitution of the second kind to do the work that a Ulysses contract of the first kind does, and whether those conditions can be met. Please write the proposal so that it contains the following.

1. The question, stated in a single paragraph, sharply enough that a reader can tell what would count as settling it, and not merely as "does the analogy hold?".

2. Why the existing answers leave it open. There is the standard defence of the analogy and there is the sceptical objection that the bound are not the binders and that the real problem is disagreement rather than weakness of will. Say why neither, as usually put, addresses the distinction above — and be specific about which premise each leaves untouched.

3. The method: what I would actually do in the time available, and why that rather than the obvious alternatives (surveying the literature, building a formal model, importing empirical work on commitment devices). I expect the work to be conceptual, carried through a single worked example: take one entrenched provision and ask what would have to be true of the guardian's incentives for the entrenchment to bind. I have not chosen the example; pick one and describe it in general terms rather than depending on its exact wording. Say why one example is enough and what it would show.

4. What a result looks like: what I would be able to say at the end that I cannot say now, and what would count as the project failing. I would rather be told in advance what would show me I was wrong than be told the project is promising.

5. Scope: two pages, roughly 900 words, and about three weeks of real work before the workshop. Be honest about what has to be dropped and whether the question survives the cut.

Two constraints on the writing. Do not cite anything: refer to positions and arguments by description and I will insert the references myself, and do not assume I can look anything up. And write it in British English, in the register of a proposal rather than an essay — it is a plan for work not yet done, and it has to persuade a mixed audience of economists and lawyers that the philosophy earns its forty minutes.
