For the last four years I've run a monthly discussion circle at the local library — about a dozen adults, no academic training among us, though two or three read a great deal. Next month the session is mine to lead, and I want to spend it on the thought behind the old rhyme about the nail, the shoe, the horse and the kingdom: the claim that large historical outcomes can turn on trivial accidents. I have no formal background in history or philosophy; this is something I've been chewing over on my own, mainly because I keep noticing that the same sort of story gets used to prove opposite conclusions — that history is a chain of accidents all the way down, and that the large outcomes were coming regardless of the small stuff. I want the group to leave able to tell those apart.

Don't give me the explanation. Give me a plan for delivering it, in ninety minutes, with a whiteboard and no preparation expected of anyone.

What the plan has to cover:

- The order the ideas must come in, with the dependencies made explicit: for each step, what it rests on and what specifically misfires if I put it earlier. I want the reasoning, not just the list.
- The one example that carries the whole session, and the constraints on it. It should be either something the room can reproduce at the table with nothing but paper, a coin and a steady hand, or something every person in the room already holds a firm opinion about. Tell me what the example must have, what it must conspicuously lack, and whether the rhyme's own lost-nail story works as the carrying example or has to be demoted to a closing joke.
- The distinction I want to land, which in my notes I've been calling amplification, openness and robustness. Amplification: the rules are fixed and nothing is undetermined, but minute differences in where things start swell into large differences in where they end. Openness: the outcome was not settled by anything that preceded it. Robustness: many different routes arrive at the same coarse outcome, so no single small thing was required. The session should make it impossible to collapse these three into one another, and you should tell me which pair the group will inevitably fuse, and at what moment.
- The misconceptions, in the order they will actually form, each with the step of the sequence where it surfaces and the precise move or question I should use to head it off there. I'm already expecting some version of "sensitivity means randomness", "if it's sensitive then nothing was inevitable", and "finding a small cause turns the outcome into an accident". Assume there are others I haven't anticipated, including at least one that only appears after the group believes it has understood.
- The check. One thing to do in the last ten minutes that shows whether they can apply the distinction to a case we never discussed, with what a pass looks like and what a polite nodding failure looks like.
- What to refuse. Tell me what a session at this level should openly decline to settle — determinism versus indeterminism in physics, whether counterfactuals about the past have truth values, whatever else is load-bearing but out of reach — and the one-line reason to give when someone pushes.
- Where I'm likely to lose the room: the moments where my own enthusiasm will tempt me to over-explain, or to let the discussion drift into a general argument about free will, and the fallback for each.

Anything you invoke has to be reconstructible from the session itself; the group will have read nothing, and I don't want a reading list.

Give me all of this as a single JSON object and nothing else, with these fields:

- "thesis": one sentence the group should be able to say at the end, in ordinary words rather than jargon.
- "sequence": an array of objects, each with "step" (integer), "idea" (the concept introduced, one sentence), "depends_on" (the step numbers it requires, and why), "why_not_earlier" (what misfires if it comes sooner), "minutes" (planned time).
- "carrying_example": an object with "case", "why_it_carries", "what_it_must_lack", "rhyme_verdict" (whether the lost-nail story serves as the carrying example, and why).
- "misconceptions": an array of objects, each with "misconception", "appears_at_step" (integer), "what_it_gets_confused_with", "how_to_head_it_off" (the exact question or move).
- "check_for_understanding": an object with "task", "pass_looks_like", "failure_looks_like".
- "leave_out": an array of strings, each naming something to refuse and the one-line reason.
- "tripwires": an array of strings, each a moment where the session is likely to go wrong and the fallback.
