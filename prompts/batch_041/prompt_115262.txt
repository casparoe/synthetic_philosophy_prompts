Please tell me whether its central objection is sound, flag anything in it that is factually or logically wrong, and say which single part of it is worth keeping in a revision. Essay: "Changing the Rules"

The MU-puzzle, which I read about in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, begins with the string MI and four rules. Rule one: a string ending in I may have a U added to its end. Rule two: from a string beginning with M, everything after the M may be repeated, so that Mx becomes Mxx. Rule three: any III may be replaced by a U. Rule four: any UU may be deleted. The question is whether MU can be derived. Hofstadter says it cannot, and that one has to step outside the system to see why. The proof from outside is that the number of I's is never divisible by three, whereas MU has no I at all.

My objection is that this proof is really a proof about the list of rules rather than about MU. Nobody wrote a rule that removes the last I, and that is the entire reason MU is out of reach. If we add one more rule to the list, MU becomes reachable in three steps: MI becomes MIU by rule one, MIU becomes MIII by the new rule, and MIII becomes MU by rule three. The new rule simply reverses rule three, allowing a U to be replaced by III. So the impossibility was only ever an artefact of a particular four-rule list, and since we are always free to add a rule whenever a derivation fails, nothing is ever really unprovable. Jumping out of the system, on this view, does not teach us something about the system; it only records our decision to stop playing by the old rules. I therefore think the puzzle is shallower than it is usually presented as being.

One detail from my own working: rule two doubles the number of I's, while rules one and four leave that number untouched, so the number of I's in any string you can reach is a power of two, which is why zero is unreachable.
