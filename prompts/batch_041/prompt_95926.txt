I teach 11th grade US history at a big public high school and I got voluntold onto the district's curriculumn comittee, which now eats two hours of my life every other Thursday. Last month we were arguing about a draft unit on Reconstruction and someone ended the whole discussion with, basically, well, history is written by the victors. Heads nodded, we moved on to the next agenda item. Two weeks later, same table, someone else said the real problem with American textbooks is that for about a hundred years the losers wrote them — the Lost Cause crowd won the narrative war and the textbooks followed along — so actually history is written by the losers. Heads nodded again. And I've been reading up on the fights in Japan over how textbooks describe the colonial period and Korea, where it isn't even clear who the victor is supposed to be, and the whole line starts to feel like a magic trick.

I'm a history teacher, not a philosopher, so I'd like you to do the philosophy for me. Take the role of someone who works on both historiography and the philosophy of language — the sort of person who is annoying about what a sentence actually commits you to — and think carefully before you answer. Start with a short brainstorm: get every distinct thing that sentence could be claiming onto the page, including the boring readings and the crazy ones, before you organize anything. Then organize it.

What I want in the end is a menu, not a verdict on one reading. For each reading, state the precise claim, say what would have to be true in the world for it to hold, and grade it: platitude, contentious but defensible, or false (or just unfalsifiable hand-waving). I want the full spread from trivial to radical — readings that turn on what counts as writing, on who counts as the victors, on whether the claim is about the archive that survives, about the content of what gets taught, about the intentions of the people doing the writing, or about what we should believe. For each one, tell me what evidence would actually settle it, and don't be shy about the ones that sound cynical but turn out to be empty.

Then: which reading did the first person at that meeting most likely mean, and does that reading actually support what they used it for — treating our draft unit as not worth arguing about? Same question for the second person, since their line looks like the slogan flipped. Then the part I really want: check the readings against the two other cases, the Lost Cause textbook era and the Japan/Korea dispute, and tell me which readings make the slogan and its mirror image compatible and which make them flatly contradict. And is there a reading on which the slogan undercuts whoever is using it — because if the winners write history, what exactly does a curriculum committee think it's doing?

Finish with a version of the menu I can read out loud at the next meeting, one line per reading, plus one sentence on which reading I should be pushing back against. No citations, no further reading — I'll be in a windowless room and I can't look anything up anyway, so just reason it out.
