You are a social epistemologist with a taste for conceptual engineering, and I would like you to work deliberately rather than quickly. Begin with a short brainstorm: inventory the phenomena that live in the neighborhood of the topic below, sort them into clusters, and say which cluster you think is the central one. Only then write the requirements list. I would rather have five well-motivated criteria with their cases than fifteen thin ones, and I would rather you flag a difficulty than paper over it.

What I want from you is the desiderata for a theory of what it is for a person or a community to be in an epistemic bubble or an echo chamber — the specification, not the theory. Assume the familiar distinction is roughly this: a bubble is a deficit of exposure, where the relevant information simply never arrives; an echo chamber is a deficit of trust, where outside sources are present but have been pre-emptively discredited, so that disconfirmation gets absorbed and strengthens the belief. Treat that as a starting point to be interrogated, not as a fixed frame; if you think the distinction should be redrawn or abandoned, say so and give me the requirements for whatever replaces it.

Here are the cases I keep tripping over. Each should earn its place in the list, or be explained away.

1. A closed community whose signature belief is true, well-evidenced, and checkable by its members, and which still runs a full discrediting apparatus against outsiders. Does the apparatus make it an echo chamber even when it is right?
2. A community constituted largely by the belief that other communities are bubbles, whose members apply the concept exclusively outward. I am asking partly because I suspect I live in this one, and I want a theory with something to say about its own users.
3. A group with no false beliefs to speak of but a systematically distorted picture of what the disagreement is about — everyone agrees, and agrees against a strawman. Is this a bubble, an echo chamber, a third thing, or nothing?
4. Deliberate closure by a group for whom open inquiry has been costly, e.g. a persecuted minority or a support group. Closure here looks instrumentally rational. Does the theory have to call it a pathology, and if not, what distinguishes it from case 1?
5. Two groups with the same exclusion mechanism, one of which dissolves on contact with outsiders and one of which hardens. The asymmetry seems to be the whole ballgame, and I want the theory to be answerable for it.
6. Partial membership: people who read the outside sources, distrust them, and still shift a little. If the phenomenon is graded, the theory needs a severity scale rather than a predicate.

At minimum, I expect the list to say something about: whether the theory can identify a bubble without presupposing that the disputed belief is false, since otherwise it is circular; whether the pathology is a property of individuals, of the group, or of the network, and whether a population can be bubbled while every member is individually well calibrated; the difference between holding a false belief and arriving at it by a bad route; the self-application case; what counts as the mechanism, and who is doing the discrediting, by what means; the causes, distinguishing bottom-up homophily from top-down curation and institutional pressure; and what follows for intervention, including whether mere exposure is ever sufficient.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of one camp. Then tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — in particular, whether non-circularity, the demand that the theory condemn the obvious cases, and the demand that it not condemn every community with shared norms of trust can all be met at once. That tension is the part I most want to see. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to eliminate candidate theories, and say in a few sentences where the exposure-based view, the discrediting-based view, the truth-tracking view, and the marketplace-of-ideas view each fail against the list. Keep the scoring brief; the criteria are the deliverable.

A table is fine for the requirements. Define your terms as you use them, and do not cite any literature, name any authors, or refer me to any reading — I want the argument, not the apparatus. If a requirement rests on an empirical claim, label it as empirical and say what would falsify it.
