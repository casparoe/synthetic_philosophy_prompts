I'm revising the lecture notes for the segment of my decision theory course on Bertrand's paradox, and I want to get the moral right before I commit it to the syllabus. The three standard constructions — choosing a chord's endpoints at random on the circumference, choosing its midpoint at random along a fixed radius, and choosing its midpoint at random over the disk — yield three different values, 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, for one and the same proposition, namely that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Since that proposition is either true or false, the principle of indifference seems to be licensing incompatible probabilities for a single event, which would make it not merely vague but inconsistent, and that is how I have been teaching it. A colleague proposes a patch: restrict indifference to quantities on which the symmetry group of the figure acts transitively, observing that the circle's rotations do act transitively on the angular position of the chord's midpoint. Does that patch actually restore a single determinate answer, or does the indeterminacy simply reappear somewhere else? And if the principle really is inconsistent, should I be telling students to fall back on relative frequencies instead?
