This was the essay question on the midterm:

Can a theory be right but early? Answer using Malthus's principle of population as your case study. Your answer should (a) state as precisely as you can what Malthus claimed and what he predicted, (b) explain how a defender of Malthus could argue that the non-occurrence of the predicted catastrophe does not refute the theory, and (c) say whether that defense leaves the theory unfalsifiable, and what follows for how we should describe Malthus's theory today. 600-800 words. Here's the student's answer, copied over as-is:

Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population (1798) rests on two ratios: population, if unchecked, grows geometrically, while the means of subsistence can at best grow arithmetically. Since the first ratio is larger, any society that lets population grow freely will push against the food supply until something gives. What gives are the checks: positive checks, which raise the death rate (famine, epidemic disease, war), and preventive checks, which lower the birth rate. Malthus wrote the Essay as a reply to the radical perfectibilism of Godwin and Marx, who thought human institutions were the only obstacle to indefinite improvement. If the real obstacle is a biological ratio, no political reform can remove it. He drew the policy conclusion that the poor laws were counterproductive, since relieving the poor let them marry earlier and have more children, which pushed wages back down and food prices up, so the relief helped the people it aimed at only in the short run.

Now the empirical record. For most of history the Malthusian model looks right: in pre-industrial Europe and Asia, real wages and living standards hovered near subsistence for centuries, and whenever population rose, mortality rose with it. That is the pattern the model predicts, and it is not a trivial prediction; it is the kind of thing you could have been wrong about. So the core mechanism has a lot of support for the period Malthus was actually theorizing about.

Where he looks wrong is the future. Since roughly 1800 in the West, and later in most of the world, both ratios moved. Agricultural productivity exploded (fertilizers, mechanization, then the Green Revolution), and fertility fell as incomes rose and women's education and employment rose. The result is the demographic transition, in which the preventive check finally did the work and people had fewer children by choice, though through contraception and changing preferences rather than the late marriage and celibacy Malthus himself recommended. So the catastrophe never arrived, and food supply grew faster than population in most places.

Does that refute him? A defender will say no, because the theory is implicitly conditional. What Malthus is really claiming is: holding technology and preferences fixed, population growth runs ahead of subsistence until a check operates. In the last two centuries the antecedent was not satisfied, since technology did not stay fixed and preferences changed, so the observation is not a counterexample to the conditional. It is a case where the antecedent fails. That is a legitimate move, not just a dodge, and it explains why the theory still gets used: it is the default model for pre-industrial economies, and people worry about a Malthusian future in cases where the escape mechanisms stall.

But this is where I think the defense goes too far. If any outcome can be absorbed by saying the ceteris paribus clause did not hold, then the theory has no falsifiable content left. An unfalsifiable theory does not tell us anything about the world, which is Popper's point. So the apparent success of the Malthusian model in describing pre-industrial Europe is really an artifact: the model is loose enough that any pattern of population and wages can be read as consistent with it. Lakatos would say the research program has become degenerating, adding epicycles instead of new predictions. On this view Malthus was not right but early; he was unfalsifiable all along, and modern Malthusians who claim vindication are just protecting the core.

The way out, I think, is to ask what the amended theory forbids. If we state it as a conditional with specified antecedents, no change in technology and no change in fertility preferences, then it makes a claim we could test by finding a society where the antecedents held and the prediction failed. That is a real empirical claim, and the pre-industrial record is evidence for it. So I would say Malthus was partly right but early: right about the mechanism, wrong about how long it could be held off, and the interesting question is whether the escape was a one-time lucky break or something the model should have anticipated.
