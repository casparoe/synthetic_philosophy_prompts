I have no background in philosophy — I studied accounting and have been reading about minds and selves on my own for a couple of years — and I've gotten stuck on a thought experiment that a friend in my book club raised and that I've since seen described in a couple of popular books. The idea, as she told it and as I understand it, is that if you watch an ant colony closely you find that its behavior can be made sense of only by speaking of the colony itself as wanting things, believing things, and working things out — it does something clever that no single ant could be doing or could even be aware of. Not one of the ants has the faintest idea what the colony is up to, and the colony's mind, whatever it amounts to, is not sitting in any particular ant. The analogy she drew was to a brain: a single neuron doesn't think, and yet somehow the whole thing does.

Here is where I keep getting stuck, and I suspect I've set the problem up badly without being able to see how. It seems to me there are only two things that could possibly be going on. Either the colony genuinely has a mind, in which case there has to be something there — some extra ingredient, a group mind hovering over the ants the way I used to assume my mind hovers over my neurons — or else there is nothing in the colony but ants following simple rules, plus a human observer who finds it convenient to say that the colony wants this or fears that, in which case the mind is something we are putting into the colony, the way we put a face into a cloud, and every statement about what the colony believes is really a statement about us. Since the whole point of the example seems to be that the ants are just ants, I assume it has to be the second option, and that disturbs me more than I expected, because the same reasoning appears to apply to me. If my mind is nothing over and above my neurons, and a single neuron has no mind, then by the very same logic my mind is only a handy way of talking that someone else is using — and it would wink out of existence if nobody were around to interpret me.

What I want to know is which of those two horns I'm actually supposed to be on, and if the honest answer is neither, then what the third possibility is, and why it isn't merely a way of having it both ways — calling the colony's mind real when you want it to count for something and observer-dependent when you want to stay modest about it. I'm asking in plain language because I don't know the technical vocabulary; if you have to bring some in, please explain it as you go, and don't assume I've read the surrounding literature. Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you would write a long letter to someone who is interested but untrained, and please lead with your verdict on the either/or rather than easing into it.
