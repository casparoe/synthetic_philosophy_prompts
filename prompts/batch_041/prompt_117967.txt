At a reading group I sit in on — a mix of cognitive science and philosophy people, mostly grad students, plus one visitor from an economics department — two of the regulars spent most of last week's session on satisficing, and the argument ran five rounds without either of them conceding a point. What made it frustrating is that by the end I couldn't tell whether they were disagreeing about how minds work, about how to use the word "optimize," or about what a good explanation is. I've tried to set out their exchange as close to verbatim as I can. I'm reporting it, not defending either side; I genuinely don't know who came off better.

Nadia opened: satisficing is a redescription, not a rival to optimizing. Give her any procedure that stops at "good enough" and she will write down an optimization problem whose solution it is — either put the cost of searching into the objective, or constrain the search budget and optimize within it. So the alleged alternative to optimization is a solution to an optimization problem. Ravi said that proves too much: you can redescribe *any* rule, including flipping a coin, as optimal for some objective, and the existence of a redescription tells you nothing about whether it is the right explanation. The bounded agent doesn't have Nadia's objective function; it has an aspiration level it adapts in light of experience, and it stops when the level is met. If explaining the behavior requires imputing a utility function the agent never computes, you have stopped doing psychology. Nadia countered that "good enough" has to be good enough *relative to something* — an aspiration level without a scale is not a stopping rule, and a scale with a stopping point is a threshold on a value function. Either the agent is optimizing with a threshold, or it is stopping arbitrarily. Ravi said the level is set by past outcomes and by the environment and it moves — the same person who accepts one salary in a slack market holds out for twice as much in a tight one — and a threshold that moves in response to outcomes isn't a fixed value function with a free parameter, it's a feedback loop. Nadia said fine: then the aspiration level is part of the objective, and satisficing is maximizing the probability of reaching it, which makes it a special case of optimization rather than an alternative.

Ravi then brought in the empirical literature: in small-sample environments, crude rules that ignore most of the available information often predict better than linear models fitted to the same data, and he took that as evidence that the interesting unit of analysis is the match between a rule and an environment, not a general preference for optimality. Nadia said that is just the bias–variance tradeoff, that such a rule is a regularizer, and that regularized estimation is optimization with a penalty term — his own example shows the value of constraints, not the failure of optimization. Ravi replied that this concedes his real claim and merely renames the phenomenon: "regularizer" is the new word for "heuristic." Nadia said no, because "regularizer" tells you what to tune and why it helps, whereas "heuristic" tells you only that the rule is simple; one of those is a research program and the other is a label. Ravi's last move was that this makes her framework unfalsifiable — if every procedure is optimal for something, "the agent is optimizing" explains nothing and predicts nothing, and every time a heuristic wins she can say the optimization was over the wrong space, and every time a model wins he can be told the rule was crude. Nadia asked what would count as evidence for him; he said a prediction not derivable from an optimization model with a free parameter, and added, needling her, that the people who derive heuristics as optimal under resource constraints seem to think they are vindicating heuristics, not refuting them. Ravi closed by saying that is because they quietly dropped the original critique rather than answering it, so that "bounded rationality" now names optimization with small numbers.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than giving me a general essay on bounded rationality. For each distinct move — the redescription argument, the "good enough relative to what" objection, the adaptive-aspiration reply, the absorption of satisficing into expected-utility-with-a-threshold, the bias–variance exchange, the unfalsifiability charge, and the closing charge that the critique has been renamed rather than met — tell me whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-overstated, with a short justification. Then separate the questions I suspect they were running together: whether a redescription of a procedure as optimal is always available; whether the redescription is explanatory; whether satisficing is normatively defensible; and whether "optimization" names a formal framework or a psychological hypothesis. Say which of those each of them was actually addressing when they thought they were addressing the others, identify what each is right about that the other never grants, and tell me what both are missing — including whether any part of the dispute is verbal and, if so, what substantive disagreement survives once the terminology is fixed. Finally, give me your verdict on who is more nearly right overall, and say plainly if the question as they posed it does not have a determinate answer.
