Suppose William James could be brought up to date on the modern notion of sphexishness: the phenomenon, often illustrated by the digger wasp, in which an animal performs an apparently purposeful sequence of actions that, when interrupted, simply restarts from the beginning rather than adjusting to the new circumstances, raising the question of whether such rigidity betrays a wholly mechanical inner life and whether human flexibility is different in kind. What position would James have taken on the issue of whether sphexishness supports a deterministic picture of human action, or instead sharpens the contrast between instinctual routine and the voluntary attention he treated as central to free will? Reconstruct his likely view as carefully as you can, separating what follows from his psychology of habit, instinct and attention from what is more speculative. State how confident you are in that reconstruction and identify the argument or consideration that would most likely lead you to revise it. Answer in British English.
