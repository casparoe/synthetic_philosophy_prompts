I teach a grad seminar that spends a few weeks on cause prioritisation, and I keep catching myself defending the importance / tractability / neglectedness framework -- you rank causes by how much is at stake, how much a marginal bit of work would buy, and how few people are already on it, and you treat the product as a rough ranking. My students take it as common sense. I've started to suspect its popularity is a symptom rather than a vindication. So play the part of someone trying to talk me out of it: what would actually count against the framework, sorted by how decisive each thing would be?

I don't want the familiar line that neglect might itself be a warning sign, and I don't want a reading list -- I want the objections that bite even when the framework is read charitably, as a heuristic rather than a law. Three that I keep circling. (a) A colleague insists the three factors can't be independant, because a cause tends to stay neglected exactly when the people who look closely conclude it's intractable, so multiplying the three double-counts the same information. Is that right, and how badly does it break things? (b) Importance is the only term carrying any moral weight, and it's the term we can least quantify or compare across value theories and across different kinds of beings, so doesn't the multiplication manufacture a precision that makes a contested axiological judgement look like an engineering calculation? (c) Acting on the framework changes its own inputs: if neglect is a reason to enter, success dissolves the reason, and a criterion that erases itself on use seems like it ought to worry me. Is that a defect, or just what any competitive domain looks like?

I'd also like to know whether the framework has quietly been qualified into unfalsifiability. When someone says the factors are just a way of estimating marginal value under uncertainty, have they given up any claim that could turn out wrong -- and if so, is that a problem for a tool like this, or the honest way to hold it? Which of the objections should a defender concede rather than deflect? I'd rather know what I'm giving up than be told the framework survives intact. And I'd like a verdict on whether the framework even has a determinate output, given that a cause isn't a natural kind and two people can agree on all the numbers and still carve the space differently. If you can produce a case where the framework and a straight marginal cost-effectiveness calculation come apart, use it -- that's the sort of thing that moves me.
