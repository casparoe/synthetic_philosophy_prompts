I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in condensed-matter research, now emeritus — who has spent the last several years reading philosophy in earnest, and I have recently been asked to join the advisory board of a small foundation that funds conceptual work in AI safety. Our board commands a modest budget, enough to support perhaps a dozen researchers for two years, and we have decided to concentrate it on a single cluster of questions surrounding low-impact AI: whether "avoiding side effects," or "acting with low impact," can be given a precise operational meaning without first settling the full specification of value that the field keeps deferring. I have read enough to be dangerous and not enough to be useful, and I keep stumbling on what looks like a circularity. An impact measure tells you how much the world diverges from some baseline, but the baseline itself seems to require a judgment about what the world would have been, or should have been, absent the agent — and that judgment appears to smuggle in the very values we were trying to avoid assuming. I have also been toying with a distinction I cannot yet make precise: on one reading, a side effect is simply an effect the agent did not intend, which is value-free but seems useless for safety, since an unintended catastrophe is still a catastrophe; on another reading, a side effect is an effect that is bad under the true values, which is useful for safety but plainly not value-free. If that dichotomy is real, it may be the crux of the whole program, but I would not trust my own framing of it.

What I want from you is an agenda-setting exercise, not a survey and certainly not a reading list. Suppose the two-year pot is real and must be divided: which open questions in this area most deserve that effort, and in what order? I care far less about the inventory than about the reasoning that orders it. For each candidate, I want to know whether it is genuinely upstream of the others — whether settling it would make the rest easier, or whether it is merely adjacent — how tractable it is for philosophers and mathematically inclined theorists rather than for empirical ML researchers, and what kind of argument or result over the next couple of years would move it up or down our list. I am not wedded to the framing above. If you think the live questions are better organized around, say, the choice of counterfactual baseline, the contrast between the agent's own objective and some external standard, or the difference between realized and merely possible impact, then say so and argue for the reorganization. I am equally happy to be told that a question I take to be central is a distraction, provided you tell me why.

Please render your answer as a table with the following columns, one row per question, ordered by your recommended priority: rank; question (the open question stated as precisely as you can, in one or two sentences); upstream_of (what other questions or lines of work depend on its answer, and why); tractability (how far a philosopher or theorist without a laboratory can get, and with what tools); disconfirming_evidence (what finding or argument within the next two years would lower its priority); and notes (the strongest objection to your ranking it where you did). After the table, close with two or three sentences naming the single question you would fund first and the one you would drop, and state the criteria you used to break ties.
