Okay, I need a Q&A prep sheet and I'm on a deadline. In three weeks I'm giving a talk at a small two-day workshop -- it's a weird mix of animal-cognition people and machine-mind people, which is exactly why I want to get this right.

Here's my line. Everybody, me included until recently, trots out Dennett's four kinds of creature and treats it as a ladder: the ones whose behavior is basically hardwired by natural selection, the ones that can also learn by trial and error over their own lifetime, the ones that can run the trial in their head and let the hypothesis die instead of them, and the ones that think with words and other borrowed tools they picked up from the culture around them. Each rung is supposed to contain the one below it. My claim is that the ladder breaks at the top rung. A word-and-tool user is not just a smarter head-simulator; part of its thinking lives outside its skull, in the language, the instruments, and the other minds it borrows from. And then the punchline, which is why the ML people are even in the room: an LLM is the limiting case of this. It's assembled out of the written residue of millions of other creatures' in-the-head trials, but there's no simulator of its own underneath all that residue. I've been calling them library creatures -- they live in the fossil record of other minds' experiments. And if that's right, then being a word-and-tool creature stops being evidence of the kind of inner understanding we care about when we ask whether something matters morally.

The room is about 25 people. Roughly: eight comparative psychologists who work on corvids and cephalopods and think the whole taxonomy is armchair stuff you cant operationalize; four or five philosophy-of-mind people who are basically Dennett loyalists and will defend the ladder as a ladder; three ML researchers who think "kinds of minds" is anthropocentric hand-waving and that I'm smuggling consciousness talk in through the back door; one cognitive ethologist who is going to hammer me on the ethics of keeping clever animals in captivity; and a few grad students who'll just want to talk about octopuses.
