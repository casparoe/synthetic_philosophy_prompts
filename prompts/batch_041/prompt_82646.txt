I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I have to defend a working position on self-deception in a seminar next week, so I'd like an audit of it before I go in. Here is the position, stated as flatly as I can manage.

(1) Belief is not under the will. I cannot believe that p by deciding to; belief answers to evidence rather than to wanting.

(2) Self-deception is real, and it is not merely motivated error. The subject's epistemic life is organised around the unwelcome proposition — the avoidance is too content-specific, and too coordinated across different parts of her behaviour, to be written off as an attention bias. The slow-accommodation case is one of these: over years, someone quietly stops reading a certain kind of news, stops returning a friend's calls, and re-describes a failing project as still finding its footing, with no episode of deciding anything.

(3) So the regulation is directive-like: a standing state whose content is roughly "keep this out of view." It is not an intention to believe, and it is not something the subject adopts; it is a policy of not looking.

(4) That directive is subintentional. It operates below the level of the agent's intentions and decisions, and the arrangement is never chosen.

(5) A constraint I have been imposing on candidate accounts: the story must run identically whether the unwelcome truth is about the subject herself — her competence, her marriage — or about the world, for instance a colleague's mediocrity. No special first-personal machinery, and no privileged self-knowledge doing explanatory work.

(6) Retention: the unwelcome proposition has to be there, held at bay. If the subject never had the evidence, or has genuinely lost it, there is no deception to speak of.

(7) Evaluatively, self-deception is an epistemic failing but not a moral one; I don't take the self-deceiver to be blameworthy for it.

I don't need pointers to the literature, I need to know where this comes apart. Please give me, in this order:

- Commitments, restated: one sentence each, so I can see whether you've read the set the way I intend it (~100 words).
- Genuine tensions, ranked: most severe first. For each, name the two commitments, say what exactly commits me to what, which of the two would have to give, and how much that costs me elsewhere (~500 words).
- Tensions that dissolve: pairs that look inconsistent but aren't, with an explanation of why the appearance is misleading (~200 words).
- Cheapest repair: the single revision that buys the most consistency, and what it costs (~200 words).
- Bottom line: three sentences at most.
