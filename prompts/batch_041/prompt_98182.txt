I'm in a graduate seminar on rational choice, and we've spent the last two weeks on the centipede game and the backward induction paradox. I've drafted a short position piece to submit as my response paper, but before I do I want it attacked. I would much rather hear where it is wrong, or where it sounds deep but is actually trivial, than be told it is well organized.

Here is the piece. The centipede game is a finite sequence of decision nodes: at each node the player to move can either take a payoff for herself and end the game, or pass, which enlarges the pot and hands the decision to the other player. Passing is better for both players if the game runs long enough, but at every single node taking beats what you would get by passing and then having the other player take. Backward induction therefore says the first player takes immediately, and the strong intuitive pull of the opposite prediction — that real or idealized players would cooperate down the tree for a while — is the paradox. My claim is that this is not a paradox about rationality but a symptom of asking one theory to do two different jobs. Call them the predictive job and the deliberative job. In the predictive job, a theory of rationality tells us what a rational agent will in fact do; in the deliberative job, it tells an agent what she ought to do. In most finite games the two jobs coincide, so we never notice that they are distinct. The centipede is the case where they come apart, and backward induction is exactly the point of divergence. The backward induction argument, as usually stated, establishes only a conditional: if it is common knowledge that both players are rational and that both reason all the way to the end of the tree, then the first player takes at the first node. The antecedent carries all the weight. The trouble is that we then read the conclusion as an unconditional prediction about what will happen, when all we are entitled to is a conditional about what follows from an idealization. Common knowledge of rationality, in the strength the argument needs, is not a fact about the world; it is a modeling assumption that makes the players see the whole tree and know each other's conclusions.

So I want to propose what I have been calling the idealization-alarm reading. When a model predicts that an agent does something no plausible theory of rationality would recommend anyone to expect — or, in the limit, predicts a kind of universal defection — that prediction should be treated the way physicists treat a divergent quantity: as an alarm that the idealization has been pushed past its domain, not as a discovery about the subject matter. The common-knowledge assumption is a parameter, and the paradox is what appears when the parameter is driven to infinity. On this reading, the right response to the backward induction result is not to revise our theory of rationality but to say that the model has left the region where it is informative, and then to ask what happens at finite, realistic strengths of the common-knowledge assumption. I think this dissolves the paradox rather than solving it, and I want to know whether that is a real move or a dodge.

What I would like from you: tell me where the essay is factually or formally wrong — especially whether the predictive/deliberative split is doing any real work, or whether a standard solution concept already handles the case without it. Tell me whether the idealization-alarm reading is genuinely distinct from the familiar reply that the paradox merely shows common knowledge of rationality to be unrealistic, or whether I have dressed up a truism. And tell me the single strongest objection to the piece, plus the one place where I am overclaiming. I don't need a reading list; I need the argument pressure-tested.
