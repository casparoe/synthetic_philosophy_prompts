I have been reading around the literature on acausal threats and the proposal, sometimes called a surrogate goal, that an agent can protect what it genuinely values from blackmail by adopting a decoy objective that a threatener can harm cheaply, so that the threatener's threats get redirected onto the decoy rather than onto the thing the agent actually cares about. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want help with an argument that a colleague in my decision theory reading group says shows the proposal is either empty or incoherent, and I cannot decide whether he has found a real problem or merely shuffled the labels. Here is the argument as he gave it: (1) A threat against you is effective only if the threatener would carry it out were you not to comply. (2) A threatener carries out a threat only if doing so is better for them, given your non-compliance, than not carrying it out. (3) The surrogate-goal proposal says that by caring about a decoy, you can make a threatener's threat against your real goal non-credible, because the threatener now has a cheaper target in the decoy. (4) But for the threatener's threat against the decoy to be credible, the threatener must be willing to harm the decoy; harming the decoy costs the threatener something and benefits them only through your compliance. (5) So the threat against the decoy is credible only if it in fact moves you to comply. (6) If it moves you to comply, you are still paying the threatener — you are just paying to protect the decoy instead of the original goal. (7) If it does not move you to comply, the threatener will not bother making it and will return to threatening the original goal. (8) Either way, you pay to protect something you care about, or you face the original threat. (9) Therefore surrogate goals cannot reduce the price of blackmail; they can only change the description of what is being bought off.

My colleague concludes that the surrogate-goal literature has confused a change in the threatener's menu of options with a change in the threatener's willingness to follow through, and that the whole idea collapses. I cannot find the flaw, and I am not even sure there is one, but I would rather keep the idea that surrogate goals can sometimes help than abandon it. Please tell me which numbered step I should reject, explain why it looked so plausible, and then price the rejection: what does the surrogate-goal view have to say in its own defense, and what does that defense cost it? If the argument is actually sound and the proposal really is empty, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a rescue, and tell me which of the usual motivations for surrogate goals it undermines. I would also like you to flag any place where the argument depends on a reading of credibility or willingness that a defender could reasonably reject, since that seems to be where the action is.
