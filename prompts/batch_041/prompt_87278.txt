I share a Thursday lunch with a colleague from the medical school — I do the philosophy half of our master's course on health and disease, she does the clinical half — and we've fallen into the habit of trading verdicts on cases over the sandwiches. Hers have been remarkably stable all term: congenital red–green colour blindness in a young man who isn't troubled by it and isn't limited by it counts as a disease, if a mild one; deep grief a year after a bereavement, the man otherwise functioning perfectly well, doesn't; situs inversus totalis found by accident on a scan, every organ doing its job, doesn't; the thinning hair of a 57-year-old who isn't bothered, and whose friends treat it as nothing more than ageing, doesn't; and type 1 diabetes, obviously, does. I'm fairly sure that set is consistent with more than one rule, and I want you to tell me what they are. (I've kept the textbook cases out on purpose — I'd rather the rules were reconstructed from these five than recited.)

Next week we're on whether medicine just medicalises ordinary life, and I want to put this to her: a man in his sixties in a hill village where almost every adult's teeth are rotten — his ache constantly, he's lost several, he can barely chew anything hard — and the local health workers shrug and say that's just what teeth are like round here. Disease or not, on her view? Then give me the rule she's actually running, the other rules that fit all five verdicts just as well, your best guess between them and why, and the one further case — not one of the five above — that would do most to pull the rules apart, saying what each of them predicts about it. I've a suspiscion this turns on how the reference class gets fixed, and if it does, say so rather than fudging it. Under 400 words, in British English, please — it's going into a seperate file with my seminar notes and I don't want to be tidying your commas.
