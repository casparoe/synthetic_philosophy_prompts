After forty years as a physiologist I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of mind, and I have just finished a popular book on consciousness whose chapter on Daniel Dennett I would like you to audit, since I do not trust my footing here.

As the book tells it, Dennett's claim is that qualia — the raw, private feels of experience, the way coffee tastes to you and to no one else — simply do not exist; they are a fiction bequeathed to us by ordinary language and careless philosophy. The argument turns on a thought experiment about two veteran coffee tasters whose tastes change in opposite directions: one reports that the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and that he has merely come to dislike it, while the other reports that he likes it as much as ever but that it no longer tastes the same. Since neither report can be checked against anything beyond the report itself, the book infers that there is no fact of the matter about what either man experiences, and that this is precisely what is wrong with the notion of qualia: a supposed property that makes no difference to anything observable is not a property at all. Once neuroscience has catalogued what we can discriminate and what we are disposed to say about it, the author concludes, nothing is left for a private inner feel to explain, and talk of what an experience is like is a placeholder that a mature science will simply retire. She grants that Dennett does not literally deny that we have experiences, but reads him as holding that we are zombies — normal in behavior and function, empty within — and she offers this as a reductio of his position rather than a consequence of it.

Which parts of this would Dennett, and a careful functionalist more generally, accept, which would they wince at, and which are simply wrong? I am temperamentally sympathetic — a career in physiology left me with little patience for raw feels treated as explanatory posits — so I would rather learn where my sympathy is purchasing a distortion. In particular: does the coffee-taster example really establish that there is no fact of the matter about experience, or does it do something more modest; is the principle that a difference making no observable difference is no difference at all one that Dennett is entitled to invoke, or is the book smuggling in a cruder verificationism than he holds; and does anything in the accurate portion of the summary actually support the closing claim that experience itself is an illusion that neuroscience will dissolve?
