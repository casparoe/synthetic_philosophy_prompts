A five-member committee must decide whether to make a job offer, an offer being warranted only if the candidate is technically strong (A), a good fit for the team (B), and likely to accept (C). Each member holds an individually consistent judgment set over A, B, C and their conjunction, yet on the profile before them majorities affirm A, B, and C separately while a majority rejects the conjunction, so premise-wise and conclusion-wise majority voting return opposite verdicts. To break such deadlocks the committee adopts a standing rule: whenever the two majorities diverge, it holds a second vote on the single question, "should this decision follow the premise-wise or the conclusion-wise result?", and the winner governs. Give me your credence that this rule is a genuine resolution of the discursive dilemma — that it yields a verdict the committee is right to adopt, not merely one it has decided to adopt. Decompose the number: how much turns on whether there is a procedure-independent fact about what a group ought to judge; on whether the second vote tracks such a fact rather than adding one more arbitrary cut; on whether the logic of the decision rule itself settles the premise/conclusion choice; and on whether a purely procedural resolution was all that could ever have been on offer. Give a rough probability for each, show how they combine, and tell me which term your number is most sensitive to and what would move it most.
