I've been chewing this over on my own for months and I cant get it to sit still, so apologies in advance for not having the words for it.

The one thing I'm sure of: a belief is a bet on how things are, and the only thing that makes it good *as a belief* is how close it lands to the truth. Not whether it's comforting, not whether it's useful, not whether I could defend it to anyone.

Here's my gut on a pile of cases. I want to know if one rule gets all of them, and if so which of my gut calls I have to give up.

- My aunt believes her son is fine. He isn't. It keeps her going. Bad belief. Bad as a belief, I mean — I'm not saying anyone should tell her.
- A bloke believes his wife is faithful because a handwriting expert and a dream told him so. False, and the reasons were hopeless. Still: bad belief. But so is the version where the reasons were excellent and it's still false. Reasons don't seem to be the thing that makes it good.
- Fair coin, before the toss. Me at 50/50 on heads: nothing to fix. Me at 90/10: a mess. Now suppose the coin is actually bent and heads comes up 90 times in 100 — me at 90/10 on heads is doing great, and that's true even before a single toss happens. That one feels like it's telling me something.
- Same 50/50 person, but on everything. Spying, his wife, the weather, the moon being cheese — 50/50 across the board. He's never badly wrong about any single thing, and he's the worst believer I know. So being close to the truth question-by-question doesn't add up to being good, and I can't see what the extra ingredient is.
- Two 90s: I'm 90% sure I locked the door and 90% sure I didn't. Either one on its own is fine, a bit of wobble. The pair is nonsense. So the whole set is doing something the parts aren't.
- 90% sure of a true thing beats 60% sure of the same true thing. Fine. But 99% sure of some true fact, versus 99% sure that 2+2=4 — the second bloke is close to the truth too, but only because there was no way for him to miss. Feels like he's getting credit for nothing, though I can't say what he's actually done wrong.
- I'm 100% sure 2+2=4 and 100% sure my team wins on Saturday. Both certain, both (say) true, and the second one feels like a defect in me that the first one isn't. The only difference I can find is that the second one could have gone the other way.
- If a fairy offered to nudge all my opinions a bit closer to how things really are, I'd take it every time, no hesitation. But if she offered to make me certain that my opinions right now are the best opinions I could possibly have — better than what I'd think tomorrow, better than anyone else's — I'd say no, thats a bug, not a gift. And somehow those feel like the same offer.
- 'Somethings going to happen this week' is much safer than 'it'll rain Thursday'. Not the better belief though.
- If I'm 70% on something and then something happens that ought to push me to 80%, and I don't budge — my number hasn't changed, but I feel like I got worse. Which is odd, because by the closeness-to-the-truth line, nothing about me changed at all.

Is there one rule behind all this? If it's 'closeness to the truth is the only thing that counts, and you should do whatever makes that as high as it can be', fine — but then tell me straight which of my calls above it kills, because I can't get the 50/50-on-everything bloke and the two contradictory 90s to sit under it. And tell me what it says about a case I haven't put down: the perfectly calibrated man, 60% confident of ten things with exactly six of them true, against the man who's 90% confident of ten things with nine true. Who's the better believer, and does your rule agree with you?

Start with your answer in the first sentence, then the reasons — no preamble, and please don't repeat my cases back to me.
