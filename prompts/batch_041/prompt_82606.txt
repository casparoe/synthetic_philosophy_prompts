I am a high school student preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club, and I have run into a problem in Nyāya epistemology that I cannot resolve on my own. Nyāya is said to accept four pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — and a pramāṇa is a valid means of knowledge. Our club recently discussed an incident at our school: a classmate was accused of copying an essay from a website. The case against him rested on three pramāṇas, as I counted them: our teacher saw the website open on his laptop (perception), another student said she watched him paste the text in (testimony), and the essay's sentences matched the website almost word for word (inference). The case for him rested on one pramāṇa: his own statement that he wrote it himself, which is also testimony. Since a pramāṇa is a valid means of knowledge, and the accusation had three of them against his one, I argued that Nyāya commits us to treating the accusation as knowledge and the boy as guilty. A friend told me that this is a basic misunderstanding of what a pramāṇa is, and that the four pramāṇas are not four kinds of evidence to be counted up, but I cannot see why not. If each pramāṇa is a separate valid means of knowledge, then more of them should mean more knowledge; and if testimony is one of them, then a person's own denial should count for as much as a witness's report. Could you explain where my reasoning goes wrong, and what a Nyāya philosopher would actually say about a case of conflicting reports like this? Please give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
