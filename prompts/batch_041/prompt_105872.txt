I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and in a few weeks I'm giving a talk to the jurisprudence reading group at a law school where a friend teaches. I need a Q&A prep sheet for the question period.

Here is my thesis. The self-amendment paradoxes that show up in the game Nomic — where a rule authorizes its own revision, or two rules each claim to override the other, or a change to the rule of change turns out to depend on the rule it is changing — are not a curiosity about a parlor game. They are the same structural problem that every legal system has with its own machinery for changing rules. Once you see that, the interesting question is never whether a system has an unamendable kernel; it always does. The interesting questions are where that kernel sits and how it is protected. On this view, the familiar dispute between reading a constitution as fixed text and reading it as a living instrument is not a disagreement about whether there is a fixed point at all but about where the fixed point is located, and anyone who insists their system has no fixed point has either hidden one or is quietly running on a regress. I will also argue that the design choices a software engineer is forced to make when building a self-modifying rules engine — what to hard-code, what to let the rules touch, how to break a tie when rules conflict about their own priority — are the same choices a constitution-maker is forced to make, whether or not they notice.

I have built a small self-modifying rules engine and run Nomic games with friends on it, so I can speak concretely about what actually breaks when a rule edits itself and what I had to freeze to stop the system from eating itself. Use that as the sketch of my argument.

The audience is mostly legal philosophers: two or three Hart-style legal positivists, one or two constitutional theorists who lean originalist, and a legal realist or two. They care a great deal about the distinction between a rule's validity and its amendment, about how a legal system identifies which rules count at all, and about whether interpretive theories are descriptive or normative. They will know the legal-theory landscape cold but will not have heard of Nomic and will suspect I am smuggling the constitutive rules of a game into a discussion of genuine legal norms. Also in the room, because I work in a computer science department, are two colleagues who do programming languages and type theory; they will care about self-reference, termination, and whether my engine actually halts.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones a logician would raise in the abstract. Give me the eight or so most likely, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a legal philosopher in this audience would phrase it. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should refuse to concede even under pressure, and finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
