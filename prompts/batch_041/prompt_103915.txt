Im writing a novel set on a Mars base, so I've been reading up on the rules real space agencies actually use, and theres one thing I can't square. On the one hand they go to absurd lengths to keep Earth microbes off Mars — they bake spacecraft, they steer landers away from the places where there might be water, missions get sorted into categories by how likely they are to contaminate something. On the other hand the whole point is to put people there, and a human body can't be sterilized at all, so the framework gets awkward exactly where the mission matters most. And then the Moon: the first landing site is treated like a monument nobody should disturb, but theres nothing alive up there to protect, so what's being protected is our own stuff and our own memory of it. I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I don't have the vocabulary for this, but: what do these practices actually assume? What view of value do you have to hold for it to make sense to sterilize a probe and then fly a person to the same planet — that other worlds have value in themselves, or only that they have value for as long as we haven't touched them yet? Those seem like different claims to me, and the second one feels like it collapses into 'we value the untouched' rather than 'we value Mars.' Which parts of the practice really need a particular theory behind them, and which would be licensed by almost any theory you picked? And what would actually have to change in the rules if you swapped the implicit view out for a flatly anthropocentric one, or for the opposite, a Mars-has-value-in-itself one? I keep feeling theres a coherent position hiding in there and I want to know whether it's really coherent or just a pile of habits.
