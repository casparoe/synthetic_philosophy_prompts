The question was this:

Three interpretations of probability are usually distinguished: the frequency interpretation, the subjective (personalist) interpretation, and the propensity interpretation. (a) For each, say what probability is supposed to be, and what it demands of an agent's credences. (b) Say how each of the three handles the reference class problem, using the probability that a particular radium atom decays within the next hour as your test case. (c) Say how each accounts for the connection between objective chance and rational credence, and argue for whichever of the three you think gives the best account.

And their answer:

(a) The frequency interpretation, in von Mises's version, defines the probability of an attribute as its limiting relative frequency within a collective. A collective is an infinite sequence of trials with a limiting relative frequency for each attribute, satisfying the axiom of randomness: no place selection can pick out a subsequence with a different limit. So on this view probability attaches to sequences of events and not to individual events. The subjective interpretation, in the tradition of Ramsey and de Finetti, treats probability as a degree of belief. Its only constraint is coherence: an agent whose credences violate the probability calculus can be exploited by a Dutch book, a set of bets that guarantees them a loss whatever happens. The propensity interpretation, in Popper's version, treats probability as a real dispositional property of a repeatable experimental arrangement. Like frequency it is objective; unlike frequency it attaches to the single case, so an individual atom can have a propensity to decay even though it only decays once.

(b) The reference class problem is that any event belongs to indefinitely many classes with different relative frequencies, so a frequency probability is undefined until a class is chosen. This is the standard objection to the frequency interpretation. The subjectivist is in the worst position here, because a credence is always a credence relative to a body of evidence, and different partitions of that evidence give different numbers. The frequentist escapes the problem because the collective fixes the reference class once and for all, and the axiom of randomness guarantees that every admissible subsequence has the same limit. For the radium atom, the frequentist says that its probability of decaying within the hour is the limit of the relative frequency of decay among radium atoms in the relevant collective, while the propensity theorist says the atom itself has a tendency to decay. So the propensity interpretation is really a version of the frequency interpretation, with the reference class fixed in advance by the experimental arrangement rather than by the collective. De Finetti's exchangeability condition is what saves the subjectivist: an exchangeable sequence is one in which the world really has no preference for order, and this lets him recover objective-looking probabilities without ever positing an objective chance.

(c) All three interpretations agree that rational credence should match objective chance when the chance is known; what they disagree about is what does the matching. The subjectivist reads this as a constraint on the agent's credence function, whereas the propensity theorist reads it as a fact about the world, since the propensity is what makes the credence correct rather than merely convenient. This is why the propensity interpretation gives the best account of the relation: it is the only one of the three that is both objective and applicable to single cases, which is exactly what the radium example requires. And since Kolmogorov's axioms say nothing about interpretation, it follows that the three interpretations must agree on the numerical value assigned to every event and disagree only about what those numbers refer to. The best overall view is therefore a pluralism on which frequency, propensity and credence are three legitimate notions answering three different questions.
