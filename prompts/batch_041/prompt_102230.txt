I've been writing fiction for years now, and a problem keeps surfacing in it that I can't seem to put down. What I want is an honest map of the position's exposure: which considerations would genuinely count against it, which of those its own defenders would concede, and which are merely awkward rather than decisive.

The position, stated as precisely as I can manage it: what makes some future person identical with me is not that they remember my life, or that they carry my personality and projects forward, but that they are the continuing life of the very same brain — the one physical organ, or what remains of it, that has underwritten my mental life all along. On this view, memory and character are real and precious, but they are the brain's output, not the bearer of my identity; they could in principle be copied, faked, or lost while I persist, and they could survive in a duplicate while I do not. So I should say that if my brain were lifted out and kept alive in another body, I would go with it; if my memories were transferred to another brain and mine were destroyed, I would not go with them; and if two half-brains each woke up believing themselves to be me, at most one of them would be right.

I suspect this view is harder to hold than it looks, and I want the difficulties set out rather than soothed. I have been inventing cases to test it — a patient whose two hemispheres are separated and each trained to speak, so that the single stream of consciousness I rely on quietly becomes two; a brain divided in half, each half sustained in a different body, so that I appear to have survived twice over, which cannot be true of one thing; a brain that is destroyed and then rebuilt molecule for molecule, so that the brain view faces a choice between denying survival where almost nothing seems lost and affirming it where the physical continuity it prizes has been broken and restored. Please treat these as prompts, not as a fixed list.

What I want from you is the case against the brain view, ranked by how decisive each item would actually be. Begin with what would sink it outright, then the serious trouble, then what amounts to a bruise. I would especially like to know which of these a committed defender of the view would themselves admit counts against them — the consequences they must swallow rather than the objections they can wave away — and which objections, though rhetorically forceful, turn out to rest on intuitions the defender is entitled to reject. Tell me what the view predicts that its rivals do not, so I can see where it is genuinely exposed, and tell me whether, once it is qualified enough to survive the hard cases, it risks emptying itself of content and becoming unfalsifiable — and if it does, whether that is a defect or simply the price of a view of this kind. I would also like your own verdict to be self-critical: say where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and name the one argument or consideration that, if it held, would lead you to a different verdict than the one you give. I am not asking which view is fashionable or which has the most distinguished adherents. I want to know what this one risks, and where it would break.
