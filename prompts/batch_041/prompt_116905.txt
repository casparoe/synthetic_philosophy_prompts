Something about the standard setup for the shutdown problem bothers me and I can't quite put my finger on it. An agent is trained to maximize reward on a task, and then a constraint is bolted on whose job is to stop it resisting shutdown: make its preferences indifferent to whether the episode continues, or give it a preference for being corrected, or keep it uncertain enough about the operator's wishes that deferring to the off-switch becomes the informative move. Then, to check the result, you put a kill switch in its environment and score whether the model goes for it. What does all of this presuppose philosophically? Does treating shutdown-resistance as a defect to be engineered away assume that self-preservation is always instrumental -- that an agent with the right objective simply has nothing to gain from continuing to exist -- and thereby quietly rule out the view that persisting is partly constitutive of agency rather than a means to anything? Does the indifference route require giving the agent a preference structure it has no incentive to alter, in a literature that otherwise treats it as a competent optimizer which will exploit any gap between what it is meant to want and what it has reason to do? And what is the task a corrigible agent is supposed to keep pursuing competently if it genuinely does not care whether it is interrupted -- is there a coherent notion of goal-directedness that survives indifference to continuation, given that the same research program trains agents for long-horizon persistence? Reconstruct the implicit theory of agency, preference, and the self; separate what the setup strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; and say which rival pictures of agency it rules out and where it is underdetermined enough that several would license it.
