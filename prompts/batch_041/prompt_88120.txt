Four months ago I was appointed to the board of a small land trust, and at my third meeting two trustees who have served far longer than I have fell into an argument that I have not been able to settle in my own mind since. I have no philosophical training, and I am now one of the people who has to vote on deals of exactly the kind they were arguing about, so I would like this worked through properly rather than smoothed over.

The background: the trust owns a forty-acre marsh. A developer wanted to build a warehouse on the parcel next to it, which would have degraded the marsh badly. Rather than fight the project to the end, the trust negotiated a settlement. The developer scaled the building down, agreed to place a conservation easement on two hundred acres it owns elsewhere, and committed $1.2 million to a stewardship fund; in return the trust agreed not to oppose the project. The board approved the settlement. Ines voted for it, Ray voted against, and at the meeting they went at each other roughly as follows.

(1) Ines said the settlement was a plain improvement and that no theory was needed to see it. Under the alternative — no settlement — the warehouse goes up roughly as first proposed and the trust gets nothing. Under the settlement, the developer is worse off than it wanted to be but better off than in a fight, and the trust is better off than in a fight on every dimension it cares about: less habitat destroyed, more land protected, money for stewardship. Two parties who rank the possible outcomes differently can almost always find a split that both prefer to the fight, she said; that is all a moral compromise is — gains from trade, where the traded goods are whatever each side happens to care about.

(2) Ray said that picture assumes both sides are ranking the same set of options on a single scale, so that 'better for both sides' has a definite meaning. But the things at stake here are not commensurable. The marsh is not one item on a list standing next to $1.2 million; it is the kind of thing whose value is not measured in that currency at all. And once you have accepted a price for twelve acres, you have said that there is a price, which changes what the marsh is — both for the trust and for the next developer who comes asking.

(3) Ines replied that refusing to price the marsh does not make it priceless; it only means the bulldozers come, the marsh is gone, and there is no fund. The refusal is a gesture, and the marsh pays for the gesture.

(4) Ray said he was not asking for a world without trade-offs. He was saying that some trade-offs should not be made, and that others should not be made legible as trades. Letting a thing be exchanged for money does not merely rank it lower on one occasion; it moves it into a different category, and the category is what people are responding to when such deals strike them as disgusting. He added that he did not think that disgust was a bug. He also said the settlement does not merely divide up a fixed loss — it teaches everyone that opposition can be bought, which raises the price of every future fight. And he said he could not get past the fact that the trust was not choosing between the deal and the marsh; it was choosing between the deal and the marsh's destruction, and a gain measured from a threat is not a gain.

(5) Ines said that last point proved too much: every negotiation happens under some threat, so if a threat disqualified the baseline, no settlement in any conflict could ever count as an improvement. She added that Ray seemed to want a deal in which none of the trust's values were traded off, but every choice trades one value against another, including the choice to fight; the only question is which trade. As for the disgust, she said that if a visceral reaction counts as a reason, then every taboo counts as a reason, and she did not accept that; some reactions are just squeamishness. If the settlement protects more habitat and more species than the alternative, she said, her own discomfort at the price tag is not an argument about the marsh, it is an argument about her.

They asked me to break the tie and I could not, and I still cannot.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, tell me whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-incomplete, and say briefly why. Then tell me what each of them is missing that neither stated, and who has the better of the argument overall — not just about whether this particular settlement should have been signed, but about what a compromise of this kind is actually doing. I would rather you reason about the arguments themselves than report what any particular philosopher has said about compromise, and I would like you to be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who study it and which parts are genuinely contested, so that I do not mistake a live disagreement for a consensus.
