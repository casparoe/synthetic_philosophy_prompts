I am a high school student who has been thinking about the philosophical problem of vagueness, and I would like you to write a dialogue that helps me work through a disagreement I have not been able to settle on my own.

As I understand the two positions I am interested in, the epistemicist holds that vague predicates — words such as 'tall', 'heap', or 'bald' — actually have perfectly sharp boundaries, so that there is a fact of the matter about exactly how many hairs separate the bald from the not-bald, and our inability to locate that boundary reflects a limit on our knowledge rather than any indeterminacy in the world. The supervaluationist denies that any single sharp boundary exists and instead treats a vague term as admitting many equally admissible ways of being made precise; on this view a sentence containing a vague term is true when it comes out true under every admissible precisification, false when it comes out false under every one, and neither true nor false otherwise.

I have tried to test these two pictures against a pair of cases I invented, and I would like the dialogue to move through both of them.

In the first case, a city council passes an ordinance declaring that a building counts as a skyscraper if and only if it is at least N metres tall, where N is a specific number written on a slip of paper sealed in a vault and never disclosed. Everyone agrees that the ordinance has a definite content and that each building either satisfies it or does not, whether or not we can ever discover which.

In the second case, a community begins using a newly coined word, say 'zaf', for objects whose colour resembles a particular swatch of teal. The community applies the word with complete confidence to the clear cases and has simply never had occasion to decide what to say about the borderline shades.

Please write a dialogue between a committed epistemicist and a committed supervaluationist in which each tries to establish whether these two cases are, or are not, fundamentally the same kind of case. I would like the epistemicist to argue that the community's settled usage, together with the facts about colours, fixes a sharp boundary just as surely as the ordinance does, and the supervaluationist to argue that the second case is precisely the kind of case in which there is no fact of the matter at all. Neither participant should be a strawman: each should be given the strongest version of their position, and each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. Toward the end, I would also like them to confront two worries of mine. First, if the supervaluationist insists that the boundary is vague rather than sharp, is the boundary between the definitely-zaf and the borderline-zaf itself any less vague, or does the problem simply recur one level up? Second, if the epistemicist insists that there is a sharp boundary even in the second case, what exactly is supposed to fix where it falls, given that no one has ever decided and nothing was ever stipulated?
