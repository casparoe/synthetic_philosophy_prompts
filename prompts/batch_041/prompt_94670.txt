I've been following the decision theory fight that grew out of Newcomb's problem for a while now, specifically the branch of it that says the real choice isn't the box, it's the rule you're running: pick the policy or algorithm you'd have wanted locked in before you knew which situation you'd end up in, and act as if your decision were a fixed fact about you that predictors and other agents can read off. People call this the updateless or timeless thing. The people who think this way say two-boxing is just an error. The causalists say the predictor's accuracy is a correlation you should ignore once you're in the room, or that the case is incoherent anyway. Evidentialists one-box here too, but get hammered on the cases where their reasoning misfires. Years of posts and papers, and honestly its gotten to the point where nobody moves.

Here's my hypothesis, tell me if it's wrong. I don't think they're disagreeing about the cases. When the causalist says the money is already in the box or it isn't so take both, they're treating the choice as an act with a causal footprint -- something that happens at a time and can only affect what's downstream of it. When the policy person says the rule that two-boxes gets a thousand and the rule that one-boxes gets a million, they're treating the choice as a logical fact about what kind of thing you are, with no time index in it at all. Those are different objects, so it isn't even the same case for the two sides, and both arguments can be airtight at once. Same shape in the case where you should hand a stranger money because of what a predictor would have done if a coin had landed the other way -- causalist: the coin already landed, paying is just setting cash on fire; policy person: the rule that pays is the rule that gets paid.

But maybe that diagnosis is too cheap, and the real disagreement is about what a decision theory is even for -- telling an agent what to do here and now, or telling a designer which agent to build. Or it's about what counts as a well-posed case: the causalist wants a mechanism for how the predictor knows, the policy person thinks the correlation is just given, and there's alot of smart people on each side talking past each other.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually -- verbal, substantive, a clash of standards about what counts as a case, or two questions sharing one name? And is the same stall sitting inside the policy view itself, because if your decision is computed by a fixed rule, learning that you're running that rule looks like information you should update on, which is the external problem again in miniature? Given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress -- a formal result, a distinction that makes one side's cases untranslatable into the other's, or an argument that we should stop asking? Diagnosis and reasons, not a survey of who says what.
