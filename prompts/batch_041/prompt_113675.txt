I am a software engineer with no formal training in logic or philosophy, and I have agreed to give a short internal talk on what Gödel's incompleteness theorems do and do not establish outside of mathematics. The theorems get invoked constantly in discussions of AI safety, static analysis, and formal verification, and my impression is that most of those invocations are either a loose analogy or a plain misunderstanding. What I want from you is not a summary of the theorems but a calibration exercise on a specific list of claims.

Here are eight claims that have been put to me, at various times, as things the incompleteness theorems establish or support. I have not attributed them to anyone.

1. Any consistent formal system strong enough to express basic arithmetic contains statements that it can neither prove nor disprove.
2. There are mathematical truths that no formal system can ever prove.
3. The second theorem shows that we can never know that arithmetic is consistent.
4. Hilbert's program of proving the consistency of mathematics by elementary, unquestionable means cannot be carried out in the way he intended.
5. Any tool that analyzes programs must be incomplete: for every sound analyzer there are programs whose behavior it cannot determine, and this follows from Gödel's work.
6. Since a formal system cannot prove its own consistency, no AI system can verify its own correctness.
7. If a person can see that a sentence is true even though a given formal system cannot prove it, then human mathematical reasoning cannot be captured by any machine or formal system.
8. Because every consistent system leaves some sentence undecided, and that sentence can be added as a new axiom and the process repeated, no formal system captures all arithmetical truths.

For each claim, give a score from 0 to 10 for how well the incompleteness theorems, as they are standardly stated, support the claim as written. Use these anchors: 10 means the claim is what the theorems establish, allowing for paraphrase; 7 to 9 means the theorems support it once their scope conditions are respected; 4 to 6 means the conclusion may be defensible but the theorems carry you only part of the way; 1 to 3 means the claim really rests on a different result, or on an extra premise the theorems do not supply; 0 means there is no real connection. Do not spread the scores out artificially to fill the range, and say so if two claims deserve the same score for the same reason.

Present the result as a table, ordered from best supported to least well supported, with one row per claim and four columns: the claim number; the score; a single sentence of justification; and, if the claim needs something more to go through, the missing premise in a few words, or the word none if nothing further is needed.

Then answer four short questions:

(a) Which claims have a conclusion that is defensible, or even true, but arrive at it through the wrong result? For each, name the result that would actually do the work.

(b) Which two claims in the list are most likely to be confused with one another, and what distinction separates them?

(c) Which claim is most sensitive to a condition the theorems place on the systems they apply to, and what is that condition?

(d) Which claim would you expect a technically competent reader to rate most differently from a lay reader, and why?

I do not need references, reading suggestions, or a history of the theorems. I want your reasoning about the claims as I have written them.
