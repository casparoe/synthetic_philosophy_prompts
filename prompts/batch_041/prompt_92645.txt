At a departmental workshop last week, two colleagues got into an argument about Hume's standard of taste that I found myself unable to referee. I would like you to adjudicate it move by move. Neither is a strawman, and I think each made at least one point that the other never adequately answered. I will call them A and B.

A opened as follows. Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste begins from the premise that beauty is not a property of objects but a sentiment in the mind of the spectator. That is the premise that seems to make beauty non-objective. But then Hume asks for a standard of taste and locates it in the joint verdict of true judges. The true judges, however, are identified by five qualities: delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. Those qualities are not independently specifiable. We know someone has delicacy of taste because their judgments track the beautiful, and we know what is beautiful because the true judges say so. So the standard is circular: the true judge is the one with good taste, and good taste is what the true judge has. A conceded that Hume's essay is more subtle than this summary suggests, but said the circle is real.

B replied that the circle is not vicious. Hume's account is naturalistic, and delicacy of taste is a perceptual capacity, like a refined palate. It can be identified by its effects. B cited Hume's anecdote about the cask of wine in which one taster detects a leathery taste and another an iron taste, and both are vindicated when the cask is opened and found to contain a key with a leather thong. Practice and comparison are empirical procedures; the true judge is an idealized observer; and idealized observers are legitimate in any empirical theory of judgment. We define normal color vision by the verdicts of observers with healthy eyes, not by an independent realm of colors, and no one calls that circular.

A answered that the color analogy fails at the crucial point. In the color case we have independent access to the physical facts — wavelengths, photoreceptors — that explain why normal observers converge. In the taste case we have no independent access to beauty. The wine example actually proves A's point: the confirmation comes from a non-aesthetic fact, the presence of the key. That shows the tasters' palates were discriminating, not that their judgment of the wine's beauty was correct. Delicacy of palate is a capacity to detect, but what it detects in the aesthetic case is a sentiment, and a sentiment is not a fact to be detected. So the standard either has no object or has an object that is not independent of the judges.

B replied that this conflates two questions. Hume can consistently hold that beauty is a sentiment and that there is a standard for the correctness of judgments of taste. The standard is not a definition of beauty; it is a norm for judging. A judgment of taste is correct when it is the verdict of a spectator who has practiced, compared, shed prejudice, and has good sense. This is a normative standard, not a metaphysical one, and it is not circular because the five qualities can be described independently of the verdicts they produce. We can say what practice is, what comparison is, what prejudice is, without already knowing which works are beautiful. The standard is the verdict of the suitably prepared observer, and the preparation is specified independently of the verdict.

A then pressed the fifth condition. Good sense, A said, is not an aesthetic capacity; it is a capacity for reasoning. Hume requires the true judge to have good sense in order to correct the distortions of prejudice and to see the relations of parts to wholes. If the standard of taste depends on good sense, then it depends on a standard of reasoning, and that standard is not itself a matter of sentiment. So Hume's sentiment theory is either incomplete or inconsistent: the aesthetic standard turns out to be an epistemic standard. And if one says good sense is just another sentiment, one loses the normativity that the standard was supposed to provide.

B answered that Hume can accept that the standard of taste is not purely aesthetic. His project is naturalistic: taste is a sentiment, but the correction of sentiment requires reasoning about the object — about its structure, its genre, its historical context, its intended effects. That is why the true judge must have good sense. This is not an inconsistency; it is the recognition that aesthetic judgment is a hybrid of sentiment and cognition. The sentiment supplies the pleasure; the reasoning ensures that the pleasure is caused by the right features of the object rather than by prejudice or accident. The standard is the verdict of the observer whose sentiment is properly informed.

A closed with a dilemma. If the standard is the verdict of informed sentiment, then the question is whether informed sentiment converges. Hume admits that it does not always converge. Where the true judges disagree, he says the standard is missing. That means the standard is not a standard at all; it is a regulative ideal that may never be realized. And if it is never realized, then the objectivity of beauty is not established — it is postulated. B replied that a regulative ideal can still do normative work. It tells us that some judgments are better than others, that prejudice and inexperience are defects, and that criticism is possible. Hume never promised a decision procedure. The convergence he does find, across cultures and ages and among the qualified, is enough to distinguish the standard from mere preference.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. I want you to separate two questions that I suspect are being run together: first, the exegetical question of what Hume's essay actually commits him to, and second, the philosophical question of whether the position, once stated, is defensible. For each of the major moves — the circularity charge, the color-vision analogy, the distinction between a standard for beauty and a standard for judging, the role of good sense, and the dilemma about non-convergence — say which of the two colleagues has the better of it and why. Where a move is partly right and partly wrong, say which part is which. Then say what both of them are missing, and give your overall verdict on which one is more nearly right, with the caveat that a verdict on the exegetical question and a verdict on the philosophical question may come apart. Please do not simply survey the secondary literature; I want an argument, and I want you to be explicit about where Hume's text underdetermines the answer and where it does not.
