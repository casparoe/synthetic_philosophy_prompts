Zeno's paradoxes of motion — the Dichotomy, Achilles, the Arrow, and the Stadium — are usually met with a small repertoire of resolution strategies, each of which buys its success at a different cost. I would like you to rank those strategies, but not in the abstract. I am supplying a stress case, of my own construction, designed to press on the strategies in different ways, and I want the ranking to reflect how well each strategy survives it.

The stress case, which I will call the Anchored Arrow. Consider an object confined to a line on which two points, A and B, are marked. Over the closed time interval from t = 0 to t = 1 the object's position is stipulated as follows: at every rational time it is exactly at A, and at every irrational time it is exactly at B. The object therefore has exactly one definite position at every instant; it is never anywhere other than A or B; and it never passes through any of the points between them. Yet the instants at which it is at A are dense in the interval, as are the instants at which it is at B, so the position function is discontinuous at every instant. Did the object move? If so, did it move from A to B, or merely alternate in place? If not, what distinguishes its history from one in which it is at rest?

The candidate resolution strategies are as follows.

(i) The convergent-series strategy. The sub-tasks into which Zeno divides the motion form a geometric series with a finite sum, so the fact that there are infinitely many of them is no obstacle to completing them all in a finite time; the paradox rests on a confusion between an infinite number of terms and an infinite total.

(ii) The at-at strategy. Motion is not a further ingredient added to an object's occupying different positions at different times; there is no extra fact of passage or becoming to be located at any instant, so Zeno's demand that we identify a moment at which the arrow changes place is misconceived.

(iii) The constructivist strategy. The continuum is not an actually infinite collection of determinate points lying in wait; the paradox trades on treating a potentially infinite or merely ideal construction as a completed totality, and it dissolves once that assumption is dropped.

(iv) The dialetheist strategy. At the critical boundary the description is genuinely contradictory — the runner both has and has not arrived — and this is a true contradiction to be accepted rather than a reductio of the scenario.

(v) The quietist strategy. The paradox is a symptom of language idling; the questions Zeno raises are not well-formed questions about the world, and the proper response is to decline to answer them rather than to construct a theory that does.

(vi) The causal-finitist strategy. Infinite sequences of causally connected steps completed within a finite time are metaphysically impossible; the scenarios are impossible rather than paradoxical, and so they tell us nothing about the nature of motion.

Rank (i) through (vi) from most to least adequate, where adequacy is measured by how well the strategy (a) resolves the classical paradoxes and (b) accommodates the Anchored Arrow without either ignoring it or denying a commitment we have strong independent reason to keep. Give each strategy a score from 0 to 10 on this composite measure, together with a one- to two-sentence justification, and state for each whether it must deny a datum, remain silent, or explain the case away. If you judge the Anchored Arrow not to be a legitimate test case, say briefly why, but still produce the ranking.
