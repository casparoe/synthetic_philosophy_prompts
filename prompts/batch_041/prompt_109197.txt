Leibniz’s identity of indiscernibles says that things sharing all their properties are identical. Black’s two spheres are supposed to be a counterexample: they share all their properties yet are numerically distinct. But I’ve also been told that the identity of indiscernibles is a logical truth, so Black’s scenario must be impossible. If that’s so, is the correct response to say that there is really only one sphere, or that identity is a primitive, bare numerical difference that cannot be reduced to properties? And doesn’t the usual reply—that the spheres are discernible because one is identical to sphere A and the other to sphere B—simply presuppose the distinctness it is meant to explain?
