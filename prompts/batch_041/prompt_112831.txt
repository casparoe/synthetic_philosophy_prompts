I'm in a grad seminar on the ethics of war, and I've got to write a short position piece for next week that's been bugging me. The thing I keep circling is a gap between how we legally treat soldiers and how we morally talk about them. Here's the practice: under the laws of armed conflict, any soldier in a regular army has the same licence to kill enemy soldiers and the same immunity from prosecution for doing it, regardless of which side he's on or whether his side's war is just. A conscript in an army fighting a war of conquest and a conscript in the army defending against it can shoot at each other, and in the eyes of the law they're doing the same thing: both lawful combatants, neither a murderer. But the same body of law calls the leaders who launched the unjust war criminals, and ordinary criminal law wouldn't let a bank robber off because he was following orders from a bank-robbing state. And the revisionist critics of the traditional view say the soldier on the unjust side is doing something seriously wrong, just less culpable than the generals who sent him, and that the symmetrical immunity is a pragmatic fiction designed to stop war from becoming total. Also one guy in my seminar keeps insisting the whole doctrine only makes sense if you think the soldier's act isn't really his own, that he's an instrument of the state, and I'm pretty sure that's wrong but I can't articulate why.

So what I want from you is the implicit theory. What would have to be true about moral responsibility, or about what makes it permissible to kill someone, for that symmetrical immunity to be more than a convention? Does the practice actually presuppose that combatants can't know whether their cause is just, or only that we can't fairly blame them for not knowing — and are those different claims? Is the immunity a claim about the soldier's act not being attributable to him, or about the act being attributable but excused, or about there being no wrong in the first place? Which rival theories does the practice quietly rule out — does it need the traditional view, or could a revisionist view that's just more generous about what counts as a just cause, or a purely institutional account of when coercion excuses, license the same rules? Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it's underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would each generate the same legal regime. Then, briefly: if we replaced the presupposed view with the revisionist one, what would actually have to change — trials for unjust combatants, a different doctrine of duress, the collapse of the distinction between soldier and murderer — and is that a reductio or just a bullet to bite? Also say something about my classmate's instrument-of-the-state idea, because I suspect it either proves too much or proves nothing.

Keep it under 600 words, as continuous prose, no headings and no bullet lists — I want to see the argument move, not a taxonomy.
