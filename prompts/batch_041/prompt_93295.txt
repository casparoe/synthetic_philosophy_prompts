Earlier this year I was issued a civil citation for failing to act on something I could see from my own window — I watched, I did nothing, and the city now classifies me as one of the causes of the resulting damage. In the months since, I have learned that the theory behind that citation is not a clerical mistake but a fairly literal application of the counterfactual account of causation by omission, which is to say a view I can no longer dismiss as absurd and cannot comfortably live under either. I would like you to write a satire of that view and of the administrative world it would generate: a deadpan Notice to Residents, roughly 550–650 words, issued by a fictional municipal Office of Omission Liability, explaining to citizens how their liability for unperformed acts is computed, why a failure to prevent some distant harm counts as its cause, and what an appeal would look like. The humor must come from getting the philosophy right rather than from inventing absurdities — the collapse of the doing/allowing distinction, the regress that opens once not-preventing is counted as causing (my file should be enormous, and so, it turns out, should everyone's), the but-for test's inability to draw any principled line, the nagging worry that an omission is not an event at all and so not the sort of thing that could be a cause in the first place, and the role a pre-existing duty to act is supposed to play in marking off which omissions actually count. Keep the voice flat, procedural, and unironic throughout, with no winking at the reader, and make the piece fair enough that someone who works on this debate would wince in recognition rather than in embarrassment.
