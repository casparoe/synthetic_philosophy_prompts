A national transport agency appraises major infrastructure projects using a standard cost-benefit analysis. It monetizes travel-time savings, accident reductions, and carbon emissions; assigns a fixed monetary value to a statistical life; discounts future benefits and costs at a single social discount rate; sums the monetized benefits and costs across all affected people; and ranks projects by net present value. Distributional effects are reported in a separate appendix, but they do not enter the ranking. The agency uses the same monetary values for every citizen regardless of income, and it treats a dollar of benefit to a rich commuter and a dollar of benefit to a poor commuter as equally weighted in the final total. The agency's guidance says that this procedure is justified because it measures changes in individual welfare and aggregates them in a way that respects each person's preferences, but the same guidance also insists that the agency does not make interpersonal comparisons of utility and relies only on market prices and willingness-to-pay estimates.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about welfare, interpersonal comparison, and social aggregation? Reconstruct the implicit theory. In particular, does the practice presuppose something like Harsanyi's aggregation theorem—roughly, that if individual preferences and the social preference satisfy expected-utility axioms and the social preference respects unanimous individual preferences, then the social preference can be represented as a weighted sum of individual utilities—or does it need only a weaker welfarist-consequentialist framework? Which of Harsanyi's premises are doing real work here, and which are left implicit or quietly violated? Does the practice require full interpersonal comparability of utility, or only comparability of money, and is the difference philosophically significant? Does it presuppose a veil-of-ignorance justification, or can it be defended without one? Which rival views of social welfare does it rule out—Rawlsian maximin, prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, a capability approach, or a deliberative conception that refuses to aggregate preferences? Is the practice genuinely underdetermined between a utilitarian rule and a weighted utilitarian rule, or does its use of equal monetary values already commit it to one?

Then say what would have to change if the agency adopted a prioritarian social welfare function, or if it introduced distributional weights that gave extra weight to benefits to the worse off. Would that be a modification of the same underlying theory, or a rejection of it? Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, identify the point precisely, and say what further feature of the practice or what additional normative premise would settle it.
