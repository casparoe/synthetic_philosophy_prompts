I chair the charter-review commission in a mid-sized city — about 400,000 residents, a fifteen-member council elected citywide every four years with no term limits, a mayor with a veto, and a small professional staff. Three proposals have been formally filed with us: a two-term cap on council service; a switch from at-large to district elections; and a civic jury of, say, forty residents drawn by lot and given binding authority over a fixed list of land-use and budget ordinances. Our hearings have been useless so far, because every one of these collapses into a yes-or-no question about whether politicians are corrupt, and I keep finding that two commissioners who both say yes to term limits disagree about everything that actually matters. I need a document I can put in front of the commission — people who are not political theorists — that separates the underlying commitments rather than the slogans.

So what I'd like from you is a decision tree rather than a prose essay. It should begin with a node a commissioner can answer without knowing any of the labels: what you take the actual defect of the present arrangement to be — distorted incentives and selection, unrepresentative composition, poor information, short time horizons, or something else entirely — and then branch, node by node, to a named institutional position. Every branch node must be a concrete case or claim that someone can answer about a described situation (a specific long-serving councillor whose committee oversees an industry she has a stake in; a specific ordinance the jury would have vetoed; a specific neighborhood whose voters would lose their only competitive race), not a general opinion question; if a node could be answered by saying 'I support sortition', rewrite it. Please make sure the tree's leaves include at least: a single allotted chamber with full legislative authority; a mixed arrangement with an elected chamber and an allotted revising chamber; a binding citizens' jury confined to a fixed set of questions; term limits; rotation of offices by class; sortition used only inside parties, to set candidate lists or ballot order; and a no-institutional-change position that puts the weight on rebuilding parties and local press. Add whatever other leaves the logical space demands. The distinctions I care about most are these: whether legitimacy is supposed to come from consent and authorization or from statistical resemblance to the population; whether deliberation is meant to transform preferences or only to aggregate them; whether accountability must be individual and retrospective (recall, rotation, review) or can be collective and prospective; whether serving is an obligation that may be imposed on people or a burden that must be compensated and made attractive; and whether a reform is meant to change who decides or only what the deciders know.

Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone actually holds, don't quietly drop it — say which combination it is and diagnose why it's empty (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Where two leaves come out behaviorally indistinguishable on your tree — I suspect this happens with term limits and rotation, and possibly with the allotted chamber and the jury at different scales — flag the pair instead of pretending the tree separates them.

Format it as follows: 'What the tree is for' (100–150 words, addressed to commissioners who will read nothing else); 'The nodes' (the tree itself, six to eight branch nodes, each a numbered question with two or three answers and an arrow to the next node or to a leaf, plus one line on why the answers genuinely come apart at that node); 'The leaves' (eight to twelve named positions, each with one sentence on the commitment that will embarrass its holder); 'Empty combinations' (short, a paragraph or a list); 'Pairs this tree cannot separate' (short); and 'How to read a respondent's path' (150–200 words, telling a commissioner how to trace a colleague's answers to a leaf and what to do when a path dead-ends in an empty branch). Total under 2,000 words.
