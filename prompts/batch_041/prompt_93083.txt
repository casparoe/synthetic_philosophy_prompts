I am a retired physiologist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy of biology, and next month I am presenting a paper to a small departmental reading group on a topic I have been circling for a while: the standoff between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. My thesis is that function claims are best understood as answers to contrastive why-questions — to specify a trait's function is to specify the contrast the questioner is asking about — and that once the contrast class is made explicit, the etiological account (what a trait was selected for) and the causal-role account (what a part contributes to a system's capacity) turn out not to be rivals at all but answers to different questions. I expect the interesting cases to be exaptations, vestigial structures, pathologies, and the parts of synthetic biology where nothing was selected for anything. I would like you to act as a philosopher of biology who knows this literature inside out and has watched these arguments run for decades, and to think carefully before writing.

The audience is not uniform, and I want the objections I will actually get from this room, not the generic ones. Two of them are committed etiologists of the Millikan-and-Neander stripe; one works in the Cummins tradition and thinks functions are contributions to a capacity; one is a philosopher of medicine who needs function talk to underwrite a distinction between disease and design; one is a historian of biology who suspects the whole notion is a relic; and one graduate student works on teleosemantics and will press me on whether my account can deliver determinate content. Several will suspect that a physiologist is quietly smuggling design back in, and at least one will suspect the opposite — that I have dissolved the concept rather than analyzed it.

Please prepare me a question-period prep sheet, in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — write it the way you would write me a long letter. Start by brainstorming where the fault lines in this audience actually run, then give me the objections ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where the reply is weakest. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
