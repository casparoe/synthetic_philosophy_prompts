I run a small manufacturing firm, and a former supplier is suing me over a contract I terminated. The claim is for $200,000. I can absorb a loss of that size — it would set the business back a couple of years, but it would not sink it — and I am not especially anxious about money on this scale, so I would rather the decision not turn on my appetite for risk.

My lawyer will not give me a single probability of losing. She gives me two. The first comes from a database of comparable contract disputes: cases with features like mine went against the defendant in somewhere between twenty and thirty-five per cent of instances. The second is her own reading of the merits — the clause, the correspondence, the witnesses — which she puts at forty to sixty per cent. She refuses to merge them into one range, on the grounds that there is no number between thirty-five and forty per cent that she would defend, and she says the two are answers to different questions rather than two readings of a single quantity, so that averaging them would be a mistake. The litigation manager at my insurer, who handles hundreds of such matters, told me she would have said "about one in three," though she added that she would not want to be held to it — a figure that sits almost exactly at the point, thirty per cent by my arithmetic, where settling and going to trial cost me the same.

If the case goes to trial, I will spend a further $60,000 on fees and expenses whatever the outcome, and $200,000 more if I lose. The plaintiff has offered to settle for $120,000, all in, and the offer stands until Friday.

I have done the arithmetic for single numbers. If my chance of losing is twenty per cent, trial is plainly the better course; if it is sixty per cent, settlement plainly is. My lawyer says that the fact I can run the same calculation twice and reach opposite answers is itself the thing to be thought about, and not a nuisance to be averaged away. I do not see what else there is to do with a range except pick a number inside it, and I suspect that picking the middle would only launder a guess into a calculation.

There is one further complication. For a month's work and $15,000 I could commission a mock trial — a focus group run as a rehearsal of the case — which would tell me considerably more about how a jury is likely to see it. Taking that route means letting Friday's offer lapse, since I cannot do both. A friend of mine says the decision is easy, because more information can only ever help. My lawyer is not so sure, at least if I decide in the way she is urging me to decide. I do not understand how learning more could possibly leave me worse off.

What should I do about the settlement, and how should I be reasoning about her two ranges in the meantime? I would rather have a recommendation than a survey of positions, though I would like to understand the reasoning well enough to stand behind it.
