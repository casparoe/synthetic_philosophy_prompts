I live in a housing co-op — 24 adults in an old hotel we bought together, a seven-person board, most things decided by show of hands at the monthly meeting, with lease changes and big spends going to the whole membership at the AGM. I've ended up as the person who writes up the decision rules, mostly becuase I was the only one who volunteered, and I've hit something I can't write my way out of. Our rulebook has two clauses that everybody loves and that I'm now fairly sure can't both be true. Clause one: on certain questions the person it's about decides, full stop — whether your name appears on any public list of members, what you do inside your own unit, whether you put your own signature on anything. Clause two: if everyone would be at least as well off with option X as with option Y, and at least one person strictly better off, we take X without further debate. Someone pointed me at Sen's liberal paradox and now I see it everywhere: cases where clause two and clause one point in opposite directions, and if you honour both you get a loop — the group prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — at which point the chair just picks whichever he happens to like and we all pretend it was a decision. What I want from you is not an explanation of the theorem; I've read enough summaries and they all trot out the same two-readers-and-a-dirty-book example and stop there. I want a procedure the chair can actually run, live, at the table. The chair rotates monthly, so assume zero philosophical training; assume five minutes per item, a whiteboard, eight items on the agenda, and a couple of times a year something blows up badly enough that a household leaves. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each so we can move on, the point at which the procedure simply stops with a no, and the points where it has to hand the thing to a human because what's left is a value question rather than a fact question. Tell me which distinctions you had to keep to make it work and which ones you threw away, and be honest about which steps are just judgement wearing a lab coat.

Things it has to survive. Our members' preferences are not always about their own business — we have people who care a great deal about what other members do inside their own units, and I don't think we can legislate that away, so the procedure has to keep working when someone's ranking over the options is partly about somebody else's choices. (A friend told me that if everyone only cared about their own business the whole paradox evaporates. I don't know if that's right, but it doesn't matter, since we're plainly not that community.) One member wants to scrap the private-sphere clause entirely and make everything a straight vote; another wants to make it absolute, so it can never be traded away for anything at all. I need the procedure to show me what each of those would actually cost us, rather than quietly picking one. And it has to be usable by someone who is irritated and holding a pen.

Then run it on three items from our actual backlog. (1) We're applying for a garden grant that requires a public list of member households. Priya, who founded the place, doesn't want to be on it. Dan, who joined last year, has been quite tiresome about it. I asked them to rank the three options — nobody listed, only Priya listed, only Dan listed — and this is what I got. Priya: nobody, then only herself, then only Dan. Dan: only Priya, then only himself, then nobody. (2) The lease renewal requires every adult member to sign a personal guarantee, and Ravi won't, on principle, because his own finances are his own business. Without his signature we either lose the building or the rent jumps for everyone else. (3) A proposal to make Saturday work days mandatory, which one member says is his own time and nobody's business. For each, tell me what the procedure does, including whether it gets past the first question at all, and flag the one where the honest answer is that the procedure can't help and a person has to decide. Finish with what the procedure can't do — I'd rather find the holes now than at the AGM in front of everyone.
