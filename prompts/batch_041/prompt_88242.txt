I'm a philosophy graduate student, and I sit in on a weekly ethics reading group that has lately turned into a running argument between two of its members — a first-year named Dana and a fourth-year named Elliot — about what is sometimes called the paradox of deontology. Dana has been pressing that the position is internally unstable, while Elliot insists the instability is an illusion produced by a bad premise. Last week they went at it for the better part of an hour, and I'd like help sorting out who has the better of each exchange rather than merely who "won." Here is how it went, as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Dana opened by laying out the paradox as she understands it: deontology tells us it is wrong to kill one innocent person even when doing so is the only way to save five, and the usual defense of that constraint is that it protects individuals — it refuses to let any one person be sacrificed for the greater good. But notice what the constraint actually does: by forbidding the killing, it lets five people die. So a rule that is justified by its protection of individuals ends up protecting fewer individuals than the alternative would. The justification and the effect of the rule pull in opposite directions, and that, she said, is the paradox. (2) Elliot replied that this only looks paradoxical if you smuggle in a consequentialist premise — namely that "protecting individuals" is something you measure by counting how many individuals come out protected. Deontology rejects exactly that. The constraint is not a device for maximizing the number of people shielded; it is the expression of a thought about what may be done to a person, that a person may not be used as a mere means. Counting heads is the very move the deontologist denies, so there is no contradiction here, only a refused premise.

(3) Dana came back: but the deontologist still owes us an account of why the individual is protected at all, and any such account will appeal to something about individuals — their interests, their dignity, the fact that their lives matter. And the moment you concede that individuals matter, you have let the camel's nose into the tent: why does it stop mattering that more of them get to live? If the reason I may not be sacrificed is that my life matters, then five lives mattering more should outweigh. So the deontologist either counts what matters (and becomes a consequentialist) or refuses to count (and then "protection" is empty — it protects no one in particular, only an abstract rule). (4) Elliot answered that there is a third option: the constraint protects the agent, not merely the patient. It is about what I may do — about the integrity of my own agency — and not about the tally of who survives. The question "why not kill one to save five?" is answered, at least in part, by "because I would be the one doing it, and I may not treat another person as a tool even for excellent ends." The paradox only bites, he said, if you insist that the constraint must be justified by the outcomes it produces for those it forbids us to harm. (5) Dana closed by saying that this relocates the mystery rather than dissolving it: why should my integrity outweigh five lives? Said plainly, refusing to save five people in order to keep my own hands clean sounds monstrous, and if "integrity" is not about anyone's interests then it is hard to see what it is worth. And the doing/allowing line this rests on looks unstable in any case — the constraint is supposed to bind whether I pull a trigger or merely redirect a trolley.

I have reported this as evenly as I could. What I want is a point-by-point adjudication: for each of the five moves, tell me whether it is sound, unsound, or somewhere in between, and why; where Dana or Elliot is attacking a position the other does not actually hold; and what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the paradox is really supposed to show.
