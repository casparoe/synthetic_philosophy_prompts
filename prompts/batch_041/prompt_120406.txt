I have no academic training in philosophy or economics, but I have been reading Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic possibilities for our grandchildren and thinking about it on my own, and I have gotten stuck on a problem I would like you to work through carefully with me.

Keynes divides human needs into two classes. Absolute needs are those we feel whatever the situation of our fellow human beings; relative needs are those we feel only insofar as their satisfaction lifts us above, or makes us feel superior to, others. He predicts that within a century the absolute needs of everyone in the developed world will be within easy reach, that the working week will shrink drastically, and that the real problem will no longer be economic but how to use the resulting freedom — how to live well, to enjoy leisure, to cultivate the arts of life rather than to be consumed by the pursuit of more. He also insists, though, that for a while longer we must keep avarice, usury, and precaution as our gods, because only these can carry us through the last of the tunnel of economic necessity into the open daylight beyond it; once we are out, we can abandon them and once again value ends above means.

What I cannot make cohere is the two-stage structure. Three things bother me, and I would like you to tell me which of them is the real problem, whether Keynes has a way out, and what the strongest version of his position would be.

First, his own taxonomy seems to predict that the tunnel has no exit. If relative needs are positional — if we feel them only when satisfaction puts us above others — then general abundance cannot satisfy them, because abundance lifts everyone at once and leaves the comparison unchanged. The economic problem then is not a quantity that can be solved once; it is a treadmill. Keynes seems to treat the economic problem as solvable anyway. Is he entitled to? Can he treat relative needs as not truly needs, but as a kind of semi-pathological appetite that satiation will cure — or does his own account of them undercut his prediction?

Second, I have a layman's sense that some things cannot be adopted on purpose for their good effects without the adoption spoiling the effect. Sincerity, faith, and being in love seem to be like this. If avarice, usury, and precaution are like this too, then the transitional stage is unstable in a deeper way: held as mere instruments they may not motivate, and held genuinely they may not be discardable at will. Is Keynes asking a society to feel something it cannot feel on command, or to stop feeling something it cannot stop feeling? What is the best response available to him?

Third, there is a problem of the last generation. The people who must keep their shoulder to the wheel during the tunnel are not the people who will stand in the daylight. Keynes seems to think this is acceptable because the transition is short and the payoff enormous, but I want to know what is owed to the transitional generation and what the good life is supposed to be for them. This looks to me structurally like the problem in Marx where the transitional state is supposed to wither away after doing its job, but has no internal mechanism guaranteeing it will; I would like you to say whether that analogy is apt or misleading.

There is also a composition problem I cannot get past, and it may be the sharpest version of the whole difficulty. Keynes's picture of the good life — friendship, art, beauty, travel, conversation, the enjoyment of leisure — is quite specific, and it looks like the life of a cultivated and leisured class. If abundance really is general and the working week really shrinks, who does the work that makes that life possible, and does the good life he describes survive being made available to everyone? Is his vision of flourishing dependent on the very scarcity it predicts will disappear?

Please explain the conceptual issue rather than summarizing the essay; I have read it and do not need its argument restated. What I want is a precise statement of what exactly the tension is and what would count as resolving it, the strongest reconstruction of Keynes's position, and then your own committed verdict. Tell me which of these worries, if any, is fatal to his two-stage ethics, which are merely difficulties he could absorb, and what the good life looks like on the resolution you would defend. Do not give me a survey of possible readings and leave the choice to me — I want you to take a definite position and defend it, including against the objections you think are strongest. And please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I will not be able to check citations and do not need them.
