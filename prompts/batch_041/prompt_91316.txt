I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and I want a poem to close our last session. Please write a pantoum of five quatrains — twenty lines, no rhyme required, the usual interlocking rule in force, so that each quatrain's second and fourth lines become the next quatrain's first and third, and the last quatrain returns the poem's opening lines. The form should do the philosophical work: the lines that keep coming back should be the propositions that hold fast while everything around them moves — the ones that are neither known nor doubted, that sit outside the space in which knowing and doubting get their sense at all, that we are trained into rather than argued into, and that can drift from the riverbed into the current and back again as the picture around them shifts. So the same sentence should read as bedrock in one position and as a bare empirical claim in another, and the closing quatrain, in returning the opening lines, should make them sound like something inherited rather than something established. Give me one quatrain in which someone is taught rather than convinced, and let the poem end where the demand for further reasons runs out — not in a special kind of knowing, but in doing. Don't name Wittgenstein and don't use the word hinge; the recurrence itself should carry the idea, so that someone who has read the book recognizes it without being told. Keep the diction plain, since the flatness of the repeated lines is part of the point, and give me the poem only.
