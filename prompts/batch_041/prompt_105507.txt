At a lunch last month I ended up the only silent person at a table where two colleagues — Dana, an economist who builds demand models for a living, and Ravi, a philosopher of social science — spent an hour arguing about a concrete case and then about what the case shows. I'd like you to referee the exchange as it actually went, not to tell me which famous position is correct.

The case that started it: a city pilot offers small advances against your next paycheck through an app, for a flat fee. Take-up is heavy, and many users renew month after month. When the pilot adds an optional feature — a "freeze" that lets you lock your own account for thirty days — roughly a third of active users turn it on; most of them let it run past its expiry, and in follow-up calls most say they are glad they did. A smaller group phones in to have the freeze lifted early, some of them angry about it.

Here is the exchange, condensed but faithful. Adjudicate it move by move.

1. **Dana:** Preference just is choice. To say she prefers the advance to the fee is to say she takes the advance when both are on offer; utility is a bookkeeping device that represents a consistent choice ranking. There is no further fact about what she "really" wants for the theory to be right or wrong about. If her choices satisfy the usual consistency conditions, a utility function represents them, and that function is the entire welfare story.

2. **Ravi:** Then you are committed to saying that each of her fourteen renewals made her better off. A framework that cannot distinguish "this product serves her" from "this product has been engineered to capture her" has stopped doing the work we need a welfare notion to do. You have defined utility as whatever the market produced and called it science.

3. **Dana:** The theory is falsifiable — the consistency conditions are empirical, and they fail often. Give me the alternative. If you have a welfare metric that isn't choice-based, tell me how we would measure it and how any claim about it could be shown false.

4. **Ravi:** We measure it in the obvious place: the freeze. The people who take the freeze are choosing now to constrain their later choices. Your framework has to read that as a preference over menus and then weigh it against the preference for the advance — and the theory itself supplies no exchange rate between the two. So at exactly the point where the interesting question lives, whether this app should exist in this form, your theory is either silent or has quietly assumed an answer. Either way the paternalism is doing the work, not the utility.

5. **Dana:** Menu preferences are part of the theory, not an embarrassment to it. Combining them does require a weighting, and that weighting is a value judgment — openly so. I would rather have the value judgment sitting in the open as a normative premise than hidden inside a phrase like "her true preference." Because that is what you have: a preference she would have under idealized conditions. Which conditions? You choose them, and that choice is as normative as anything I do. We are in the same boat; I just label the boat.

6. **Ravi:** Then stop saying the data settle it. And here is the technical problem with your rescue. Your representation theorem needs her choices to be consistent. But the renewals and the freeze are not consistent with a single ranking over outcomes — she takes the advance, and she takes the lock, and the lock is precisely a choice against the advance. So the representation does not exist. You have to select some subset of her behavior to rationalize, and the selection is exactly where the exploitation question lives. You cannot have it both ways.

7. **Dana:** The object of preference is not "the advance." It is the whole consumption plan, including the constraints she imposes on herself. On the enlarged space the choices are consistent. And every model selects; my selection is disciplined by fit and out-of-sample prediction, which is more discipline than your idealization has.

8. **Ravi:** If you enlarge the space whenever coherence fails, then any behavior at all can be made coherent — you can always posit a preference over plans that generates it. A framework that can rationalize anything cannot underwrite the policy judgments you want to make with it. "Revealed preference" becomes a way of laundering whatever happened into "what she wanted."

9. **Ravi:** And it follows from your view that you cannot even say a defrauded buyer was harmed. That seems like a reductio.

10. **Dana:** No. Fraud is a case of false belief — the thing received is not the thing chosen — so the theory locates the harm exactly where it belongs. The cases that are genuinely hard for you are the ones with no misrepresentation at all, where a person says afterwards that she was harmed and you still want to insist that she was. Tell me what fact about her makes that true, and how you would know it, without appealing to an idealization you selected.

Go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right but aimed at the wrong target, and briefly justify the rating — in particular, flag any move that is correct as a point about one thing but being used to settle a different thing. Then tell me which of the two is more nearly right overall, and what the one who is more nearly right still has to concede. I am not asking for a survey of what has been written on this; I want the merits of these ten moves, argued out. Take a definite position rather than laying the considerations side by side and leaving the verdict to me.
