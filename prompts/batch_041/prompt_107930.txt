I run a small philosophy discussion group out of my living room — I never went to uni, we're all just people who read too much — and each month I write up one question for everyone to answer in writing before we meet, then we mark each other's. I need next month's on Gödel's theorems and what they do and dont show outside of maths, because that's the thing everyone in the group has a strong half-opinion about and nobody can actually argue for.

So please write me three things: one exam-style essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme. It should be something a person can write in about 45 minutes.

What I want the question to actually test: people in the group keep saying Gödel proved that any system of rules has gaps, so there's always a legal question with a right answer no judge can reach, or that it proves a mind can't be a machine because we can see the sentence is true while the machine can't. I don't beleive that's what it says, but I want the question to force someone to show me exactly where the slide happens, step by step, instead of just saying "that's not what it says".

So put a specific argument in front of them and make them take it apart. Make up the argument yourself, in its strongest form — something concrete, like someone insisting a tax code or a legal code is "incomplete" in Gödel's sense, or the mind-vs-machine one — and make it good enough that the answer can't just call it a strawman. Then the question should ask (a) what would have to be true of that system for the theorems to apply at all, (b) which of those conditions fail, and (c) whether any version of the argument survives, because I don't want it smugly dismissed either — I want to know what the theorems legitimately do tell us outside maths, if anything.

Things the model answer has to get right rather than hand-wave, because these are where we always fall apart:

- "unprovable" is always unprovable-in-this-system; there's no plain unprovable, and bolting the sentence on as a new axiom doesn't fix anything
- the sentence being "true" is doing real work and depends on what you mean by true — and I half remember the original theorem needed a stronger assumption than plain consistency and that this got patched later, so get that straight and don't fudge it
- the second theorem, and the symmetry: the machine can't prove its own consistency, but neither can we prove ours, so it isn't the free lunch people think
- the move where you say "but we can always step outside and see it" — what that costs you, and why a machine could be built to do the same thing over and over
- one place outside maths where something like this genuinely bites, versus one place where it's just vibes (the real one doesn't have to be exotic — undecidability turning up in some physical or computational setting counts, as long as it's real)

For the marking scheme: 20 marks total, five must-hit points with the marks for each, a one-line note on how people usually get each one wrong, and a clear line between a B (knows the two statements, can recite them, maybe gets the mind thing half right) and an A (can do the scope conditions and handle the strongest version of the argument without either swallowing it or dismissing it). Keep the model answer honest — if a version of the argument isn't actually dead, say so, and flag any step that's a judgment call rather than a theorem. And no citations or reading lists, we're not doing that, a model without internet shouldn't have to guess at them.
