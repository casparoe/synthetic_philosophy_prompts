I'm a second-year cognitive science PhD student trying to shape a dissertation on the neural correlates of visual awareness, and I've gotten stuck on a philosophical problem that I suspect has real methodological consequences. The view I'm worried about is epiphenomenalism — the claim that conscious experiences are caused by brain processes but do not themselves cause anything, so that my experience of red is a byproduct of the visual machinery rather than a contributor to what I say or do next. What bothers me is the puzzle that follows, which I've seen called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if that view is right, then my judgment that I am having an experience, and my report of it, cannot be caused by the experience itself; they must be produced by whatever physical state the experience accompanies. If so, the reports I would use as data are generated by the substrate rather than by the experience, and it is unclear what evidential weight they could carry for claims about experience. I'd like you to set out the most important implications of this position — for the practice of consciousness science, for how we should treat systems whose verbal reports we can't trust (animals, and now language models), and for the ethics of caring about consciousness at all — and rank them.

I don't want a survey or a reading list; I want to know which two or three of these implications are genuinely load-bearing and why, and which are overstated or don't actually follow from the position. Several concrete claims have been put to me, and I'd rather you engage with them than talk around them. A labmate insists that if epiphenomenalism were true, no experiment could ever distinguish a system that has experiences from one that merely processes information, because the report would be generated by the processing either way. A friend proposed a variant she called split epiphenomenalism, on which the experience itself is a byproduct but its content is not, because content is carried by representational states that do real causal work, while the experience is simply a byproduct that happens to be about the same thing. My advisor thinks the whole puzzle is a reductio of epiphenomenalism rather than a discovery about evidence. For each of these, say whether it holds up, what it would change in practice if it did, and how it interacts with the others.

Finally, tell me what would move an implication up or down your ranking: what empirical result or conceptual development would make one of these matter more, or less, than it does now. I'm trying to decide where to spend the next three years, so the ranking should be defended in terms of what actually changes for research and for how we treat uncertain minds, not just in terms of which point is most discussed. Please write in American English.
