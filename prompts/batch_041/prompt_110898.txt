Some colleagues and I have been running a long informal seminar on the ethics of historical memory, and two of us have been going at each other for months. One — call her the ranker — thinks it's not just permissible but often obligatory to say that some mass atrocities were worse than others; she thinks refusing to compare is a way of insulating comfortable intuitions, and that we already rank implicitly every time we decide which anniversary gets state funding, which atrocity gets taught in schools, which survivors get priority in a reparations scheme. The other — call him the incommensurabilist — thinks the whole project is either incoherent or obscene: that assigning comparative magnitudes to industrial murder mistakes a moral horror for a quantity, and that the urge to rank is usually a way of quietly demoting someone else's dead. They've been talking past each other for a year and I'm sick of it.

I want the joint statement they could actually both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. To keep it from turning into mush, I'd like the comparison question broken down instead of treated as one yes/no. Something like: separate (a) dominance verdicts — A is worse than B on every dimension both parties already accept; (b) trade-off verdicts — A is worse on some dimensions and better on others, once you stipulate a weighting; and (c) the residual cases where no common dimension is agreed on at all. Maybe that breakdown is wrong — if so, say so and fix it, but don't just collapse the levels into "ranking: pro or con."

The deliverable should have a numbered section of agreed claims (about what comparison even is, about what we're doing when we commemorate, about what's at stake in refusing to compare, about the difference between a scholarly comparison and a political one); then a table of the remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording that its own side would accept; then the cruxes. I suspect one crux is whether a moral "dimension" has to be something the victims themselves could recognize or endorse, and another is whether the legitimacy of a comparison depends on who's asking and why (a reparations board vs. a dinner-table arguement). For each crux, name a concrete case or arguement that both would agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts we'd find or conclude — if one side predicts the case will be a wash, say that too. And if part of their fight turns out to be verbal — e.g., they mean seperate things by "worse," or one is ranking event-types and the other the totality of suffering — say which part and why. Don't worry about references or reading lists; we know the literature, we just want the document. And please don't adjudicate who's right — the point is a text both of them could put their names to, with whatever disagreement is left made exact.
