I am a postdoctoral fellow in history and archival studies, and next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop hosted by a university center for archival research. The audience is roughly half practicing archivists and records managers, a third historians of colonialism and labor, and a small remainder digital humanities researchers who build text-mining and entity-resolution tools for digitized collections. The archivists tend to care about professional standards, provenance, and the practical ethics of description; the historians want to know what evidentiary claims the surviving record can actually support; the digital humanities people are enthusiastic about computational recovery of marginalized voices and are inclined to see metadata as a technical problem. My thesis is that the silences of an archive are not merely gaps to be filled or symptoms of power to be denounced, but are constitutive of the archive’s evidentiary authority: an archive becomes authoritative not despite its silences but through them, because exclusion and selection are what allow the remaining records to appear as a coherent series. I call this the constitutive-silence thesis. My positive proposal is a practice I call silence-forward description, in which cataloging and metadata record not only what is present but the shape of what is absent, and in which the archivist’s own acts of selection are treated as part of the record rather than as neutral background. I sketch this with a hypothetical case: a municipal archive whose nineteenth-century intake registers for a poorhouse survive, but the corresponding case files were destroyed in a fire, and the registers’ evidentiary value now depends on the missing case files, because the registers’ categories only make sense as a grid for files that no longer exist. I argue that a similar structure holds for more ordinary archives, including ones that have never suffered a dramatic loss.
