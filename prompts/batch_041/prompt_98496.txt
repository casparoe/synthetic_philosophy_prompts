I have now taught a graduate seminar on persistence twice, and on both occasions the endurantists and the perdurantists in the room arrived, by roughly the sixth week, at a point where each side could restate the other's position accurately and neither could be moved by any argument the other offered. The familiar materials were all on the table early — the argument from temporary intrinsics, the problem of coinciding objects, the appeal to vagueness in composition — and the remaining weeks consisted almost entirely of each side redescribing the same cases in its own idiom and declaring the redescription decisive. I am writing because I want a diagnosis of that impasse rather than another defense of either view, and I would like you to treat the situation as a puzzle about the structure of the disagreement.

To be concrete about what stalls: the endurantist holds that an ordinary object is wholly present at each moment of its existence and is strictly identical across those moments, so that a poker is one and the same thing when hot and when cold; the perdurantist holds that such an object is a four-dimensional aggregate whose temporal parts are what exist at each moment, so that the poker is only derivatively present at any one time. The endurantist answers the problem of change by relativizing the having of properties to times; the perdurantist answers it by relativizing the subject of the properties to parts. Each then accuses the other of failing to capture genuine change — the perdurantist says the endurantist has merely renamed the difficulty, the endurantist says the perdurantist has replaced change with variation among distinct things — and neither charge lands, because each presupposes the very framework the other rejects. A colleague in semantics insists the whole dispute is downstream of a question about logical form, namely whether the temporal qualifier in a sentence like 'the poker was hot' functions as a sentential operator that time-indexes the entire proposition or as a predicate modifier that turns the property into a time-involving relation; he thinks the metaphysics is hostage to the semantics and that the two camps have simply chosen different regimentations.

My own hypothesis, which I hold loosely and would like you to test rather than assume, is that the disagreement is not about what there is but about what a thing is doing when it is said to have a property at a time — whether having a property is a two-place relation between a thing and a property, or a three-place relation among a thing, a property, and a time — and that if this is the crux, the two sides are not disagreeing about the furniture of the world but about the correct regimentation of ordinary temporal discourse. I am uncertain whether that makes the dispute verbal, or whether a residual substantive question survives underneath it about what must be true of a thing for it to change. There is a further wrinkle I cannot resolve on my own: one of my students proposed that the two views can be reconciled by treating parthood itself as time-relative, so that an object has a temporal part at a time without being composed of temporal parts simpliciter, and I cannot tell whether this genuinely dissolves the distinction between the views, collapses one into the other, or merely relocates the disagreement into the semantics of the parthood predicate.

Please diagnose this. Is the impasse substantive, verbal, or a case of two sides answering different questions under a single heading? If some hidden question is doing the work — about the logical form of temporal predication, about the time-relativity of parthood, about which cases are canonical, or about standards of what counts as an explanation of change — name it and say why it is the load-bearing one. If you think my hypothesis about regimentation is right, say what follows for whether either side can be refuted; if you think it is wrong, say what the real crux is instead. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about how time-indexing interacts with parthood, a principled decision about which cases are to be treated as canonical, or a demonstration that the two frameworks are intertranslatable in a way that makes the dispute idle. And if the honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected here, tell me that plainly and explain why the question remains worth asking anyway.
