Then I bought the Enchiridion itself, the Epictetus, and the opening pages are just not the same philosophy I was being sold. The bit I'm stuck on:

"Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions."

...and a few lines down: "The things in our control are by nature free, unrestrained, unhindered; but those not in our control are weak, slavish, restrained, belonging to others." And then: "You will do nothing against your will. No one will hurt you, you will have no enemies."

Then three chapters later he says: "If you kiss your child, or your wife, say that you only kiss things which are human, and thus you will not be disturbed if either of them dies."

I've got a 4 year old and that line honestly turned my stomach. So what I want from you is a reading, not a survey. Pick an interpretation, defend it, and tell me which of my confusions are just me being a bad reader.

Here's what I can't square. (a) "In our control" obviously can't mean "I can make it happen" — I can't just decide to stop wanting things. But he files desire and aversion on the "ours" side and my own body and my good name on the other side. What's the actual test he's using to sort things into the two piles? (b) The modern stuff keeps telling me you can't control your feelings, only your reaction to them — but Epictetus says opinion, desire and aversion are precisely what's up to me. Is the modern version just wrong about him, and does the difference matter? (c) The modern books also sneak in a third bucket — things you can't control but can influence, like my wife's health, or how a meeting goes. Is that a fair reading of the passage, or a way of dodging it? Because if influence counts, then everything is "in our control" to some degree and the whole line dissolves. (d) If I'm supposed to be able to loose my wife or my kid and stay undisturbed, how is that not just... a monster? And what is he getting at a page later when he says to pursue things "with gentleness and reservation"?

So: give me a verdict. What he actually means, whether the modern revival has dumbed him down, and whether the "kiss your child" advice really follows from the first bit or is a step too far. Commit to something. I'd rather have an interpretation I can argue with than a menu of options.
