A colleague is finishing a book in which she works through a long series of cases, ruling in each on whether a first-person report may be trusted as evidence about the state it purports to report. Her rulings so far run as follows. She treats as trustworthy: a report that the patch before the subject looks orange; a report that the subject is right now straining to recall a name; a report of the felt sharpness of a pain on a numbered scale; and a report that a word feels familiar before it can be retrieved. She treats as untrustworthy: a report, given just after a choice between two identical pairs of stockings, that the chosen pair was the better made; a report that a hiring decision turned on the candidate's qualifications when the candidate's accent actually drove it; and a report that listing reasons for gratitude lifted the subject's mood because the reasons were compelling, when the lift came from the act of listing itself. The case now on her desk: a subject reports, of a thought she is having at this very moment, that she is the one producing it rather than its merely occurring to her, that is, a report of the sense of agency over her own current thinking. Tell me which rules her rulings are jointly consistent with, what each rule predicts for this new case, which prediction I should expect her to give and why, and which further case would do the most to separate the rules. There is no need to cite anyone; I will add references myself. Give the response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
