I'm a novelist, and I host a small podcast on philosophy and fiction. Next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a specific account of the wrong-kind/right-kind reason distinction: roughly, she holds that a reason is right-kind for an attitude just when it bears on the attitude's constitutive aim, and that wrong-kind reasons are really reasons to want to have the attitude rather than reasons for it. My current novel concerns a literary critic asked to review a close friend's book; he has a reason to praise it because it will spare the friend's feelings, but he also suspects the friendship has made him more alert to the book's genuine merits. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond restating her view. At least one should press whether a reason that is wrong-kind in its origin (e.g., friendship) can become right-kind through its causal role in making the critic more sensitive to aesthetic features, and what her account says about that. Another should ask how her account handles a single consideration that seems to count both for and against the attitude, as when the friend's need for praise both biases the critic and gives him a reason to look harder. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Please avoid asking her to survey the literature or name names; keep the focus on the structure of the problem and the case.
