I direct a residential treatment program for substance use disorders, and I want to understand the philosophical commitments built into our clinical practice. Our protocol combines two strands that a philosopher might find in tension.

The first is the disease strand. We teach residents that addiction is a chronic brain disease, that cravings are neurobiological events, that relapse is often a symptom rather than a decision, and that medication-assisted treatment is not cheating. We use contingency management: clean urine tests earn vouchers. We train staff to identify triggers and to treat a relapse as a predictable consequence of exposure, stress, and sleep loss.

The second is the responsibility strand. Every resident must keep what we call a choice journal. In it they narrate each relapse in the first person active: not that the craving took over them, but that they chose to use. They must complete a bad faith inventory, listing occasions on which they deceived themselves about their own intentions—times they told themselves they were only going to the corner store, or that they could stop after one, or that they were keeping the pills for a friend. Staff are instructed never to accept an appeal to inability as a final answer; the official line is that denial is not a symptom to be waited out but a project the resident is running. Residents who will not take responsibility are discharged for failure to engage, even though their files also describe a chronic relapsing brain disease.

What philosophical view of freedom and self-deception does this practice presuppose? Does it require something like Sartre's radical freedom—the claim that a person is at every moment the author of their situation, that facticity is always surpassed by transcendence, and that bad faith is a flight from a freedom one cannot escape? Or can the same practice be made sense of under a compatibilist view, or under a divided-self view on which the addict is not one agent but a conflicted bundle? Which parts of our protocol strictly presuppose radical freedom, which parts merely fit it, and which parts are indifferent among several theories? Is the practice internally consistent, or does the disease model contradict the responsibility rituals? If the practice were rebuilt on a thoroughgoing psychiatric determinism, or on an explicitly Sartrean view, what exactly would have to change in our intake forms, our group sessions, our discharge criteria, and our language? I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I want the implicit theory reconstructed and tested.

Please structure your response under the following headings, in this order:

Summary verdict (100–150 words): state what the practice presupposes and whether it is coherent.

The implicit theory of freedom (400–500 words): reconstruct the view of agency, choice, and the self that the practice requires.

The implicit theory of self-deception (300–400 words): say what bad faith must be for the choice journal and the bad faith inventory to make sense.

Strict presuppositions versus mere fits (300–400 words): distinguish what the practice cannot do without from what it merely suits.

Rival theories that would also license the practice (300–400 words): consider compatibilism, divided-self views, and the disease model, among others.

Internal tensions (300–400 words): identify where the practice pulls in inconsistent directions.

What would change under a replacement view (300–400 words): say what would have to change if the program adopted a thoroughgoing determinism or a full Sartrean view.

Bottom line (100–150 words): give your overall assessment.
