I spent forty years as a natural scientist and have lately retired into philosophy, and I keep returning to a finding that seems to be read in opposite ways by everyone who cites it: in the countries that score highest on gender equality, the differences between men's and women's occupational preferences and willingness to take risks appear to be at their widest rather than their narrowest. I can see at least three readings of it — that the underlying dispositions are innate and simply less constrained where equality is greater; that the equality indices measure the wrong things, or that the comparison is an artefact of how the underlying distributions are summarised; and that richer societies are in fact more gendered in their expectations, not less. Each seems to me to fit the same evidence, which makes me suspect that the finding, as usually stated, barely discriminates between them.

Please treat this as an agenda-setting problem rather than a literature review. Suppose I have two years, half-time, no sociological training, but a reasonable grasp of quantitative reasoning. Which few open questions should I take up if the aim is to turn this from something that can be read either way into something that genuinely discriminates between the readings? Defend the ordering: which questions are upstream of which, which are answerable from existing data or by conceptual analysis alone, which are probably unanswerable, and what would have to be discovered for the ranking to change. Please answer in British English.
