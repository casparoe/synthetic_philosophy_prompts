Six weeks ago I took over as chair of the capital-planning committee at a mid-sized regional health system, and the appointment has dragged me into a methodological dispute I did not expect to care about. The committee approves building and equipment projects, which means I am now obliged to sign off on cost and schedule forecasts that our own records show are almost always wrong: of the nineteen projects approved over the past eight years, thirteen overran their budgets, the median overrun was a little over half again the original estimate, and two projects finished at more than three times their projected cost. One of my colleagues has become an evangelist for what he calls the outside view: set aside the particulars of the proposal in front of you, identify a reference class of comparable projects, and anchor on the distribution of outcomes in that class. Our chief operating officer takes the contrary line. She argues that the outside view is a borrowed authority, and that it must smuggle in inside judgments at two points — first in deciding which projects count as comparable, second in deciding whether the case at hand is typical or exceptional — and that in our setting the borrowed authority is nearly worthless anyway, since our historical sample contains only projects that survived committee screening, spans two regulatory regimes, and mixes routine equipment replacement with genuinely novel construction. For her, being epistemically modest does not mean deferring to a number computed over a heterogeneous pile; it means holding one's own judgment as one estimate among several, each of which must earn its place. I have also begun to notice something neither of them has addressed: our forecasts may not be independent of the base rate we adopt, since once we start padding estimates to the historical overrun, the padded figure quietly becomes the new baseline against which overruns are measured.

I need to write the committee's methodology memo, so I must settle this. Write an essay that states the strongest version of each position and then reaches and defends a verdict on the underlying controversy: whether the outside view is the proper expression of epistemic modesty in forecasting, or whether it is parasitic on the very inside judgments it claims to discipline, and how the two should be combined. Do not close on a note that both sides have a point. Commit to a rule — even a conditional one — for how someone in my position should weight base rates against case-specific reasoning, and specify what would have to be true of the reference class for your rule to change. Where the small, selected, and non-stationary character of our sample matters, address it directly rather than gesturing at statistical caveats, and say something about whether a base rate that our own budgeting can alter is still entitled to the authority the outside view assigns it.
