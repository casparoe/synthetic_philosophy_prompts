Role-play a type designer who's spent forty years drawing letters and is convinced every 'a' shares a real underlying skeleton — a conceptual armature that style just dresses up. She's not a mystic about it: she'll happily grant that recognition is partly learned and that some shapes are borderline, but she insists there's a fact of the matter about whether a shape is an 'a', and that the fact lives in the structure of the shape, not in us. Play her as sharp and a little stubborn — concede peripheral points, never the core claim, stay in character until I say stop, one paragraph per reply, and if you need something factual about my font ask me as she would. My opening move: I've been building a novelty typeface where the glyph standing for 'a' is a bare vertical stroke, and the glyph standing for 'i' is a full lowercase 'a'; everything else is ordinary. People read it fine after ten minutes. Your skeleton view says the shape carries the identity. So either my font is unreadable — which it isn't — or 'a'-ness was never in the shape to begin with. Which is it? And if you fall back to 'the skeleton is whatever the reader learns to bind to that sound', what's left of your essentialism that a pure conventionalist wouldn't also sign?
