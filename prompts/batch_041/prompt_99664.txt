I have been reading a trade book on Indian philosophy — the author is a comparative philosopher rather than a specialist in Jainism — and before I lean on its central chapter in a seminar paper I would like an accuracy audit of it. Here, as faithfully as I can reproduce it, is the book's account of anekāntavāda:

"Jainism's great contribution to philosophy is anekāntavāda, the doctrine that reality is many-sided: every object has infinitely many aspects, and every knower grasps only a few of them, so no statement about anything can be wholly true. To give this doctrine a logic, the Jains built syādvāda, the seven-limbed system in which every claim is prefixed with syāt — 'perhaps,' or 'it depends' — yielding the seven possibilities: perhaps it is, perhaps it is not, perhaps it both is and is not, perhaps it is inexpressible, and so on. In this the Jains anticipated paraconsistent logic by two millennia; like the dialetheists, they hold that some contradictions are true, which is what allows them to affirm that a thing both is and is not. Nāgārjuna's tetralemma is the Buddhist cousin of the same move, and the tradition's favorite illustration is the parable of the blind men and the elephant, each of whom mistakes the part he can feel for the whole — the moral being that the blind men are all equally right, and therefore that all views are equally true. Anekāntavāda is thus the ancient world's statement of epistemic humility: a refusal to claim that one's own perspective is the whole truth, and the intellectual root of the Jain commitment to non-violence and tolerance."

The chapter closes by drawing what it takes to be the consequence: because every view is true from some standpoint, a Jain must hold that in some sense the earth is round and in some sense flat, and this is why Jainism is uniquely suited to pluralist societies.

I am inclined to think the chapter is broadly right — it agrees with the impression I have from other secondary sources — but two things nag at me. First, I have seen syādvāda and nayavāda used as though they were simply other names for anekāntavāda, and I cannot tell from the chapter whether they name the same doctrine or three distinct ones. Second, the comparison with dialetheism feels to me like a rhetorical flourish rather than a thesis, though I would not know how to say why.

Please work through the summary sentence by sentence. Tell me what a specialist in Jain thought would accept without demur, what would make them wince as a popular simplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong; where a technical term is being used in its everyday sense rather than its technical one, say so explicitly. Then tell me whether the chapter's closing inference — that a Jain must regard 'the earth is round' and 'the earth is flat' as each true in some sense, and that this underwrites a general relativism — follows from anything accurate in the summary, or whether it is produced by the summary's own mistakes. Finally, if the summary has to be repaired, what is the most defensible core that survives the repair, and what does even that core still assert that a modern reader might reasonably resist?
