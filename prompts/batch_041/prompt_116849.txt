I am twenty years old and in the second year of an engineering degree at a technical university in a country that sits near the top of every international ranking of gender equality: parental leave is generous and shared, corporate boards are quota-regulated, and my university runs a well-funded program to bring women into fields where they are scarce. This last fact matters only because of an argument that has been making me miserable for a month.

My department has offered me a scholarship reserved for women. It would cover the rest of my degree, and it comes with obligations: I would be expected to appear at recruitment events, be photographed, and serve as an example. I want it, and I am ashamed of wanting it, though I cannot say exactly why. My aunt, who reads a great deal of social science and has no patience for euphemism, says I should take it without a second thought, and adds the claim I cannot shake: the countries that have done the most to remove obstacles to women's choices are precisely the ones in which men and women end up choosing most differently, so the differences that remain cannot be the residue of injustice, and anyone who treats my preference for the lab over the seminar room as anything less than fully mine is patronizing me. My mentor in the department, who is a woman and has been generous with her time, says nearly the opposite: because we are steeped in expectations from the moment we can speak, a preference like mine is not yet evidence about what I would have wanted had the obstacles truly been gone, and the honest course is to hold my own preferences at arm's length until I can see which of them survive scrutiny. My roommate, who is studying philosophy and enjoys being difficult, says that both of them are inventing a person who does not exist, because there is no fact of the matter about what I would want under conditions that have never obtained; there is only the wanting I actually have, and the only interesting question is what to do with it.

For what it is worth, my mother wanted to be a physicist in this same country when it was less equal than it is now; she was told, more or less, that this was not for girls, and she became a pharmacist and says she has no regrets that she can find. I do not know whether that counts for my aunt or for my mentor.

All three of these people are talking about me rather than to me, and each of their arguments makes me feel like a case rather than a person. I would like advice, and I would like it to be advice about my situation rather than about the topic in general.

First, what should I do about the scholarship: take it, decline it, or take it while refusing the terms in which it is offered? I do not want a formula; I want to know what the decision actually turns on, and in particular whether accepting it commits me to a claim about why it exists. Second, and this is the question that keeps me awake: what would it even mean for a preference to be mine rather than something done to me? Can that question be answered without appealing to a version of me that has been stripped of every social influence — and if my mentor is right that no such version exists, does the distinction between a preference that is genuinely mine and one that is merely imposed collapse? If it collapses, tell me what follows and what does not. Third, I have a younger sister who is being told by our father that her dislike of mathematics is a fact about her nature and that she should stop fighting it. I would like to know what I owe her, and whether the answer depends on how the first two questions come out.

You may tell me that my aunt's empirical claim is contested, but only if you can also tell me what turns on whether it is true: if the pattern held exactly as she describes it, what would follow about the fairness of the remaining differences, and what would not? I do not need to be told to follow my passion, to trust myself, or that the question is complicated.
