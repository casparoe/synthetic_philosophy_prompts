A reading group I run on Indian epistemology has been stuck for over a year on anekāntavāda, and I would like your diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on the doctrine. One camp reads the syādvāda — the seven modes of qualified predication, running through is, is not, both, and the inexpressible, each predication qualified by syāt — as a semantic theory: reality is many-sided, our predicates capture it only partially, and the seven modes map the ways an assertion can hold of a complexly qualified thing. The other camp treats the same material as a dialectical and soteriological discipline: each assertion is assigned to a standpoint (naya) from which it is true, and the point is to dissolve disputes rather than to state truths. Each side has a standing objection to the other — that the semantic reading collapses into relativism, that the dialectical reading forfeits the epistemic work the doctrine is asked to do — and neither has moved. My hypothesis, held loosely, is that the two camps are answering different questions under one name: what makes a predication true of a many-sided object, as against how disputants should proceed. If that is right, say what would count as progress; if not, say what substantive question remains — for instance whether the many-sidedness belongs to the world or to our descriptions of it. Tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and identify the point you are least sure of.
