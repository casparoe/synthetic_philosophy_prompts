Ok so I'm a novelist — short fiction, mostly — and I've been circling a story I can't quite land, so I'm hoping you'll just write a version of it for me. The thing I'm after is the old Epicurean move, that death can't be bad for the person who dies because there's nobody left to be harmed, and the standard reply that death is bad precisely because it deprives you of the good things you would otherwise have had (Nagel and co.). I do not want a story where two people sit in a cafe and debate this. I want the idea to be load-bearing, so you feel it in your gut rather than hear it in dialogue.

Here's the seed I've been carrying around — use it or bend it, I don't mind. Near-future bureaucracy: a Bureau of Posthumous Accounts, where clerks audit every death and work out what the dead person was deprived of — not money, but life-years, experiences, the whole counterfactual — and then the state pays that deficit out to a named beneficiary. So death becomes, quite literally and administratively, a harm that gets priced. My protagonist is a clerk who's very good at it. Then a case lands on her desk: a man who, while alive, filed a satiety declaration — a legal statement that he'd gotten everything he wanted from his life, that he was complete. He dies young. Under the bureau's own rules, a complete life has a deprivation of zero, so the state owes nothing. But his daughter is contesting it, because she insists he was robbed — of grandchildren, of a retirement, of all the ordinary things he didn't yet know he wanted. The clerk has to decide, and I need the story to make both the Epicurean view and the deprivation view feel true at once, not just have her pick a side and explain herself.

I'd like it to end somewhere that doesn't resolve the philosophy but drops the reader inside the timing puzzle — when exactly was this man harmed, if he was? Before he died he wasn't deprived yet; after he died there was no him to be deprived. And I want the daughter to be right about something the philosophy hasn't accounted for, even if she can't state it cleanly. Maybe 2500 words. Don't let anyone give a lecture — I'd rather the concept come through the paperwork and the daughter's specifics, the little objects and dates she brings in. And for gods sake don't end it with someone staring at the sea.
