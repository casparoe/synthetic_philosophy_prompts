Hey — I'm putting together a humor piece for a blog I run with some friends from my game-theory reading group, and I want to try something I dont see done well. Everyone writes the same Moloch riffs, but I want an in-universe document instead: a shareholder letter. Not a real company — invent one, some unlucky mattress or meal-delivery or hospital-staffing outfit — and write the annual letter from its CEO to investors, maybe 700–900 words, deadpan the whole way through. The gag is that this CEO has read just enough about multipolar traps (the thing where a bunch of actors each doing the locally sensible thing keeps everyone stuck in an outcome none of them wanted, with no central authority to make them stop — arms races, ad wars, regulatory races to the bottom, that whole family) to see his own business clearly, and has decided, sincerely, that the trap is a law of nature. Every year the company cuts a corner, shaves the product, undercuts a rival, and the letter reports each one as a triumph of discipline. He's not a villain and he's not winking — he's a guy who genuinely believes that because nobody can unilaterally stop, the race to the bottom is just the shape of the world, and that keeping on running it is the responsible thing to do.

The jokes have to come from the game theory, though — a friend of mine who actually does mechanism design should laugh, not wince. So let him get the real structure right: why the "let's all just agree to stop" plan keeps collapsing for want of any enforcement, why each individual defection is locally rational, why breaking the whole thing needs a third party or a binding shared standard. But here's the part I actually care about. Somewhere in the letter, have him describe one situation that is really just a coordination problem — a case where everyone could simply settle on a common standard, or sign an assurance contract, and be done — as though it were the same inescapable trap as everything else. The reader should be able to tell that this one isn't a trap, and the letter should never admit it; he should just fold it into the same weary "we have no choice" refrain. Maybe give the company a recurring slogan it uses to make all this sound noble, and a made-up internal award for whichever department cuts the most.

Keep the vocabulary light enough that someone who's never heard the word "multipolar" still follows it, and dont let it turn into a lecture — I want it funny on the first read and worse (or better?) on the second, when you realize he's wrong about exactly one thing.
