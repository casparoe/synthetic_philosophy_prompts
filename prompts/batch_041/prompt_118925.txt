I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to work out what I actually think about duties of rescue. Singer's drowning-child example, together with the principle that we ought to prevent something bad when we can do so without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, reads to me as either plainly correct or wildly too demanding, and I can't tell which — partly because the situations I actually face look very little like the pond. So I've written down six situations from my own working life. Please sort them into tiers by the strength of the duty to intervene, where tier 1 means not acting would be clearly wrong and tier 5 means there is no duty here at all, with one sentence of justification for each placement.

1. On my walk to work I pass a shallow pond in which a small child is drowning. Wading in would ruin a $300 pair of shoes and make me late to a meeting.
2. I am the only person with the credentials to revert a colleague's misconfiguration that will, within a few hours, take down the appointment system of a hospital in another country. Doing it tonight means missing my sister's wedding rehearsal.
3. My employer converts each hour of overtime I log into a $50 donation to a charity of my choosing, and I understand that a few dollars covers treatment for a child with a parasitic infection. I could log forty hours.
4. I have spotted a billing bug that will overcharge twenty thousand users by three dollars each next month. Fixing it would cost me a day; nobody else has noticed it; and my manager has told me to deprioritize it.
5. A stranger on an internet forum says they intend to kill themselves tonight. I could stay up talking with them, but I cannot tell how serious they are, I have a presentation in the morning, and I doubt I would help much.
6. A design decision I am about to make will leave a future team with a codebase roughly ten percent harder to maintain for the next five years. Spending two extra days now would avoid it.

Once the tiers are settled, tell me which single feature of these cases is doing the most work in your ranking — physical distance, the size of the sacrifice, the certainty of the harm, the number of people affected, whether the victims are identifiable, whether the harm would be something I do rather than something I merely fail to prevent, or how many hands my help passes through on its way to the person it reaches — and then name a pair of these cases that would come apart if I forced you to choose between that feature and its closest rival.
