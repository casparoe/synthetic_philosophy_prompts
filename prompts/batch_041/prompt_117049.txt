I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on decision theory and its critics, and I'm writing a term paper on the fanaticism problem for expected value maximization — the way a large enough payoff at a small enough probability seems to swamp every ordinary consideration. I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher of decision theory who works on this material and can argue from first principles rather than reciting positions. Please open with a short brainstorming pass before committing to any verdicts: state what you take the problem to consist in, and correct my framing if it is off. My framing is that the problem has three distinguishable faces. First, the swamping face: a lottery with a minuscule chance of an astronomically large payoff outranks every ordinary certainty, and continues to do so no matter how good the ordinary option gets. Second, the probability-insensitivity face: at extreme magnitudes the ranking stops responding to probability at all, so a one-in-ten-to-the-hundredth chance of an enormous good becomes interchangeable with a large certainty. Third, the infinite-value face: where an option carries any chance of an infinite payoff, the rule either cannot rank the options or makes them all tie. If these are not three faces of one problem, or if I have collapsed two things that need keeping apart, say so and redraw the map before going further.

Anchor the ranking to this case. Option A is a sure gain of 100 units of value. Option B costs you 1 unit for certain and, with probability 10^-100, delivers 10^200 units; otherwise nothing. Option C, with probability 10^-100, delivers an outcome of infinite value; otherwise nothing. I am not asking whether to take B or C — I am asking how the responses below handle a case of that shape.

Here are nine responses. The glosses are mine, written so that we are talking about the same thing; where a label is standard I have still tried to state the content rather than lean on the label. (1) Probability cutoff: treat any probability below a fixed threshold, say 10^-9, as zero, or as capped at the threshold. (2) Bounded value: no outcome, however described, may contribute more than some finite ceiling. (3) Diminishing marginal value: value grows sublinearly in the quantity of good, as utility does in wealth, so 10^200 units is only a modest multiple of an ordinary life. (4) Credence skepticism: block the conclusion epistemically, holding that in cases of this shape your credence in the enormous payoff should be near zero because the claim arrives with no track record, so the problem never gets started. (5) Small-probability discounting: treat small probabilities as smaller than they are, or require a minimum probability before a prospect counts at all — and tell me whether it matters that the best-known descriptive finding about how people weight small probabilities runs the other way, overweighting them. (6) Lexicographic priority: some goods or harms are lexically prior, so no probability-weighted quantity of lesser good can outweigh a certainty about a protected category. (7) Finitism about value: reject infinite or unbounded value, so the setups generating the worst cases are ruled out as ill-formed rather than answered. (8) Mine, the actionability constraint: a prospect enters the calculation only if you can specify the mechanism by which the payoff would actually be delivered, so offers whose payoffs are specified purely by description are excluded from the option set rather than discounted. (9) Mine, stakes-scaled credence: reduce your effective credence in an outcome in proportion to how far its magnitude exceeds the largest magnitude you have ever seen realized, on the grounds that extreme magnitudes are epistemically suspect.

What I want from you:

First, for each response, say which face or faces of the problem it actually addresses, and score it from 0 to 10 on a single dimension: how much of the rationale for expected value maximization it preserves. A 10 means someone who accepts the rule for ordinary cases could adopt the response without feeling they have abandoned the underlying argument that outcomes should be weighed by their value and their probability; a 0 means the response is a rejection of the rule wearing its name. Show your reasoning where the score is contestable.

Second, arrange all nine into a tier list, S through D, on that score, with a sentence of justification for each placement, and flag any ties you regard as not being genuine ties.

Third, if there is a tenth response I should have listed, add it, place it in the tiers, and say what made me miss it.

Fourth, tell me whether either of my two proposals is incoherent, question-begging, or merely a redescription of one of the others.

Fifth, for the highest-ranked response, tell me what it costs: what someone who adopted it would have to give up, and what the most serious objection to it is.

Finally, say how confident you are in each of these claims — a rough level is fine, as long as it is honest — and single out the one point where you are least sure of yourself.
