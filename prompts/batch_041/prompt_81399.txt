I am a novelist finishing a book that braids two stories: a woman who signs an irrevocable private contract with a custodian to keep her from abandoning a manuscript, and a small parliamentary republic that entrenches a fiscal rule in its constitution. Both are commitment devices; both belong to the family of Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment; and the standard treatments I have encountered give me the familiar cases without giving me the specification I need. Before I decide what my characters can honestly do, I want the desiderata any adequate theory of precommitment -- personal and constitutional, treated together and separately -- must satisfy.

Give me the requirements list. For each, state the requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it, and whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. I want the cases a theory must get right, including at least these: the classical case of a person who binds her future self to avoid anticipated weakness of will; the case of a future self who has changed her values and does not consent to the binding; the case of a commitment that is rational ex ante but catastrophic ex post because of information unavailable at signing; the case of a constitution that cannot be amended, or whose amendment procedure is itself entrenched, or that contains a clause forbidding any future amendment to that clause; the case of a generation that binds its successors; the case of a precommitment used to entrench a morally odious end, such as a constitution that protects a hereditary caste system or a personal contract that enforces an addiction; and the case of a commitment device whose enforcer is a third party with interests of its own. I also want you to consider some less familiar structures I am using in the novel: a sunset clause that renews by default unless the bound party performs a costly public act of revocation; a two-tier arrangement in which the bound self may petition an independent custodian for release, but the custodian's decision is final and unreviewable; a self-binding contract that requires the bound self to write a public justification before any revocation; and a constitutional provision that entrenches not a policy but the procedure by which policies are entrenched. Treat these as test cases, not curiosities.

The desiderata should include at least: what it is for a commitment to be binding rather than merely costly to break; how a theory distinguishes precommitment from ordinary preference change, from coercion, and from paternalism; what normative status the later, dissenting self's preferences have; whether the legitimacy of a precommitment can be separated from its effectiveness; how a theory handles the dead hand of the past without making all constitutional entrenchment illegitimate; how it accounts for the difference between a person and a polity as the subject of precommitment -- for example, the absence of a single unified preference, the presence of successive generations, and the fact that the enforcer is often the same collective agent that is bound; and how it reconciles the rigidity needed for a commitment to do any work with the flexibility needed to correct for error and changed circumstance. Add any requirement you think I have missed, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular theories.

Finally, mark any pair or subset of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. If the list itself implies that no theory can have everything -- that some commitments must be either illegitimate or ineffective, or that the same device cannot be both self-imposed and other-enforced -- say so plainly. That result is more useful to me than a scorecard. If you do score the main families of view against the list -- the prudential self-control account, the consent-based account, the sovereignty or constituent-power account, and the narrative-identity account -- keep it brief and subordinate to the criteria. I am not asking for a bibliography or for names and dates; I want the specification.
