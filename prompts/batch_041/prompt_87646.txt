I'm not a lawyer, never went to law school, but I've fallen down a rabbit hole of court podcasts and now I have alot of opinions at dinner parties. The thing that keeps bugging me is how judges decide what a law actually means. Everyone says "just read the text" or "just figure out what they were trying to do," and those two sound like they agree until they don't, and then everything falls apart. I want you to write me an essay that actually picks a side on this, not one of those "both have merit" things that ends by saying it depends.

Here's the made-up case I keep using to test my own views, and I want you to use it too. My state has a law that says it's a crime to "store or keep a hazardous substance within 500 feet of any school." The state keeps an official list of what counts as hazardous, and the list includes ordinary bleach, I guess because in big enough amounts it's dangerous. There's a hardware store about 400 feet from an elementary school, and it sells bleach by the gallon like every hardware store does. Nobody actually thinks the bleach is a danger, it's the kind of stuff under every sink. The law was passed after a chemical plant fire near a school, and the whole debate at the time was about explosions and toxic clouds, not cleaning supplies. So the words cover the hardware store but the point of the law clearly doesn't. And to make it worse, the only real evidence of what they were "trying to do" is a floor speech by one of the sponsors that maybe three other legislators even heard.

So please write the essay on textualism vs purposivism — reading the words as written vs following what the law was for — using this hardware store as the running example, and reach an actual verdict about which way judges should go when the two clash, or a principled rule for mixing them if you think that's the honest answer. Be hard on yourself: tell me the strongest objection to whatever you land on and say why it doesn't sink it. I don't need a reading list or case names, I can't check those anyway, I just want the argument.
