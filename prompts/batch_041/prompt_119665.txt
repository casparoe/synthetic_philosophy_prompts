I am assembling materials for a technical reading group on proposals for extracting truthful answers from AI systems whose outputs we cannot directly verify, and I want a decision tree that maps the positions on one such proposal: adversarial debate, in which two instances of a system argue for and against a claim before a judge that is weaker or more resource-bounded than the debaters, and the judge awards the win to whichever side it finds more convincing. The proposal's appeal is that the judge need never solve the problem itself; its most discussed vulnerability is obfuscation, in which a debater defending a false answer supplies an argument too long, intricate, or locally plausible for its flaw to be located within the judge's budget, so the honest side loses while being right.

Build the tree from the logical structure of the proposal rather than from a list of papers. Begin at the assumptions it needs in order to work: that a correct claim admits a defense the honest debater can actually produce; that the honest debater can locate the flaw in a dishonest argument within the judge's budget; that the judge's verdict reliably tracks the better argument; and that the question admits a determinate answer that can be argued for or against. Each internal node should be the question that turns on relaxing exactly one of these assumptions, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. Each leaf should name the position if one exists and give one sentence on its most awkward commitment; mark combinations that no one occupies as unnamed, and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

I want two distinctions kept explicit throughout. First, whether the failure is attributed to the judge's limited resources, to the intrinsic difficulty of finding the flaw, or to the debaters optimizing adversarially against the judge. Second, whether the proposed remedy is to strengthen the judge, to alter the protocol (additional rounds, cross-examination, requiring the honest side to supply a short certificate of its claim), or to abandon debate for a different method. Include a branch on the case in which the correct answer has no short certificate, so that the honest side is right but cannot prove it briefly.

Aim for eight to twelve leaves, pitched at readers who know the proposal but disagree about its prospects. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and say why.
