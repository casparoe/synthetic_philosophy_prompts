I teach a 90-minute seminar on 19th-century philosophy, 18 undergrads, and I want to run Kierkegaard on the press and the public -- levelling, the public as a phantom that nobody actually belongs to, chatter, all that -- as an activity instead of a lecture. Here's my rough sketch, redesign it as much as you like: everyone gets slips of paper and drops anonymous one-line reactions to a provocation I put up (a minor scandal, some local outrage), I collect them, read them back, and we assemble a "newspaper" stating the group's opinion on the matter. Then I ask each of them privately, on paper, whether they actually hold that opinion, and I'm hoping almost nobody does. Then a second round where the group has to decide something with small real stakes (someone gets publicly blamed, or we have to act on it) while everyone is still anonymous. What I want them to walk out seeing: that "the public" is something no one is, that levelling flattens whoever tries to stand out or say something sincere, and that anonymity is what makes the whole thing both possible and completely irresponsible. Give me the rules, roles, materials, timings, and a debrief that maps what happened onto levelling, the public, chatter and the press as its vehicle. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle that. And be upfront about which part of his view the activity can't reach and that I'll have to supply myself. Finally, say what would change your mind -- what would make you conclude the activity misses his point, or that I've got his view wrong -- and where your reading of him is most likely to be off.
