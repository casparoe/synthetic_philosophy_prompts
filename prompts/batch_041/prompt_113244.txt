Until recently I regarded the problem of evil as a seminar exercise, but after a death in my family this year the question of whether this could be the best of all possible worlds — Leibniz's claim that a perfectly good and all-powerful creator, constrained only by the compossibility of perfections, must have actualized the maximum of harmony available, so that every evil we encounter is somehow required by the greater good of the whole — has stopped being academic. What I keep meeting, when I try to raise it with friends who are religious, is an analogy rather than an argument: the world, they say, is like a tapestry, or a painting, or a mosaic. Up close, from where we stand, we see only the tangled knots and loose threads, the drab and clashing patches; it is only from a distance, or from the weaver's side, that the whole resolves into a coherent and even beautiful design. The analogy is doing real work for them. It is meant to license the conclusion that we are in no position to judge the world's evils, that the apparent chaos is an artifact of our limited vantage point, and that the whole is beautiful even where the parts look ugly, so we should trust the design we cannot see.

I would like you to audit that analogy rather than simply restate the theodicy it is meant to support. Begin by making the correspondence explicit — what plays the role of the weaver, the threads, the knots, the front and back of the cloth, the vantage point, and the beholder — and then tell me where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the use the analogy is being put to from the ones that are merely incidental. I am especially concerned about two things. First, the ugly stretches of a tapestry are not themselves aware of being ugly, nor of being compensated elsewhere in the pattern, whereas the apparent evils in this world are felt by creatures who suffer them; I want to know how much of the analogy's force depends on treating the parts of the world as though they were threads rather than subjects. Second, the analogy seems to presuppose that value accrues to the whole rather than to the parts — that a world can be the best even if every individual life in it is wretched — and I want to know whether that holistic premise is something the analogy establishes or merely assumes. Finally, and most importantly: does the consolation the analogy was offered to give actually follow from it, or was the analogy silently carrying a conclusion that the underlying argument never reached? If it is a poor analogy, what would a better one look like, and what would the better analogy cost — what would it have to give up?
