I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums on emergence and reduction, and I've come to suspect that most of the disagreement dissolves the moment people say what they are actually demanding of a theory. So before I read another account of emergence, I want the specification first.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of emergence in science must satisfy. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp.

I expect the list to cover at least:

- The phenomena it must get right: renormalization-group universality, where distinct microphysics yield the same macro-laws; phase transitions that exist only in the thermodynamic limit; and coarse-grained variables that are predictively indispensable even though the microdescription in principle suffices.
- The distinctions it must not collapse: above all between emergence-for-us (a fact about our computational and cognitive limits) and emergence-in-principle (a claim about what the world's furniture actually is), and between a macro-law that is merely convenient and one that supports autonomous counterfactuals.
- The results it must stay consistent with: the causal closure of fundamental physics, the non-uniqueness of coarse-graining, and the gap between "derivable in principle" and "derivable in practice."

Two constructed cases I want the theory to adjudicate, because I suspect they pull the desiderata apart:

1. A deterministic cellular automaton whose microdynamics I specify, together with a macro-variable that (a) obeys an exact closed-form update, (b) supports counterfactuals that match the microdynamics, but (c) can be computed from the microstate only by a procedure no physically realizable machine could run. Is this emergent? Which desideratum decides it?
2. A macro-variable that is predictively useful for forecasting but whose counterfactuals come apart from the microdynamics under intervention — the macro level misrepresents what would happen if you intervened. Must a theory of emergence exclude this, or can it count as a species of emergence?

Assume the purpose is metaphysical: I want to know what is in the world, not merely what is useful for modeling. Say explicitly whether that purpose drops any of the requirements from the list, or changes which are negotiable.

Then flag any pair of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — I am especially interested in whether explanatory autonomy for higher-level laws can be had without either causal overdetermination or epiphenomenalism. Finally, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and say why. Keep any scoring of named approaches brief; the criteria are what I am after.
