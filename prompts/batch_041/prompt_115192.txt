I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether Marx's two headline charges against capitalist production — that it exploits the worker and that it estranges her — are genuinely two charges or one relation described twice. Her line was that they stand or fall together: the same arrangement that lets someone else appropriate what the worker produces is the arrangement that puts her at a distance from the product, from the activity of producing, from her species-being, and from the people she works alongside. Mine was that this conflates two things which can in principle come apart, and that the interesting question is which way they come apart. The easy direction is alienation without exploitation — the independent producer who owns her tools, sells into an anonymous market, and is estranged from the fate of what she makes without anyone extracting a surplus from her. What I want from you is the hard direction, built as a designed case rather than asserted, because the point is to test whether the two diagnoses are conceptually independent or whether one is quietly smuggling in the other. What the case is for fixes what counts as cheating: the exploitation verdict has to fall out of Marx's own account of surplus value, and the non-alienation has to fall out of how the work is organised, not out of the worker's feelings about it.

So construct that case, in a single paragraph of scenario, under the following constraints. It must be a real sort of workplace, contemporary or historical — nothing science-fictional, no demons, no perfect institutions. The worker must not be deceived: she knows what she is paid, what she produces, and who keeps the difference, and the arrangement survives her full understanding of it. She must not be described as cheerful, grateful, or ideologically reconciled; if the only route to non-alienation is that she fails to grasp her position, or has been brought to love it, the case has failed. Her non-alienation must be structural — a matter of her relation to the means of production, to the design and pace of the process, to the destination of the product, and to her co-workers — and it must be non-alienation on all four of the dimensions Marx distinguishes, not three out of four. And avoid the examples everyone reaches for: no artisan who owns his own tools, no worker cooperative, no small craft business with a beloved owner.

Then do the check. For each of the four dimensions, say whether the case genuinely delivers non-alienation or whether I have quietly redefined the dimension to make room for it, and show that the exploitation verdict is generated rather than stipulated. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work — my own hunch is that it is not ownership of the means of production but whether the worker has any standing in the decision about what the product is for, and I would rather have that hunch tested than flattered. Then remove that feature and say whether the case collapses back into ordinary alienation, and whether the collapse is caused by Marx's theory or by my description. Finally, give me the strongest case for the other side: what would someone who holds that exploitation and alienation are one structure under two names say is wrong with my scenario — that it is not really exploitation, that it is not really non-alienation, or that Marx never allowed them to come apart in the first place? And if the case does hold up, what does it show about whether alienation attaches to wage labour specifically or to commodity production more generally?
