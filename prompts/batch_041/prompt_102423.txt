So I just got roped onto the ethics committee at the hospital where I work — I'm the token philosopher, apparently — and I've been reading through the last few years of our advance-directive cases to get a feel for how we actually decide, since the written policy is uselessly vauge. Three that seem to have set the tone. First, a man with a progressive neurological illness had signed a directive years earlier refusing to be put on a ventilator; when the respiratory crisis finally came he could no longer make decisions, and we honored the directive and let him go. Second, a woman's directive refused artificial nutrition and hydration, and shortly before she lost capacity she wrote a note confirming she still meant it; when the moment came we honored it. Third, a directive refusing resuscitation that had been signed in the days right after a suicide attempt, while the patient was in acute crisis; when he later arrested for an unrelated reason we overrode the directive and resuscitated him, on the grounds that whoever signed that thing wasn't really in a position to be binding anybody.

Here's what's bugging me. I can't tell whether this committee is running the view that an advance directive is basically a prediction — our best guess at what the patient would want once the moment arrived, authoritative only for as long as the patient can't speak for themselves — or the view that it's a self-command, something the earlier self deliberately imposes on the later self precisely because it knows the later self may wobble, the way somebody signs a contract with a future addict. Both readings seem to fit all three cases: in none of them did the patient regain capacity and contradict the directive, and the third falls out either way because the signer arguably wasn't competent when he signed.

Now we've got the case that actually tests it. A woman in her sixties signed a directive refusing dialysis, back when she watched her father die slowly on it and was adamant about never going that way. She now has early kidney failure, is fully capacitated, and has changed her mind — a grandchild was born, she says she wants the years, and her nephrologist thinks she'd do well on dialysis. She isn't depressed, isn't being pressured, and can recite the risks back to us. Do we honor the directive or honor her? I want your best guess at what we'd do if we were being consistent with ourselves, which of the two readings — or some third one, say that a directive only binds a self that's still "the same person" — we'd actually be committing to either way, and, since I suspect the two readings come apart somewhere else entirely, the next case that would really tell them apart. And tell me how confident you are in all this, and which piece of it you're least sure about.
