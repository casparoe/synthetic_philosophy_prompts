I run a grad seminar that straddles philosophy of science and AI safety, and last week one of my students — a CS type with a couple of security internships under his belt — threw an analogy at me that I still haven't worked out how to answer properly. We'd been working through the "control" literature, the stuff that says: fine, assume the model might be actively scheming against you; stop trying to make it good and start trying to extract useful work out of it anyway — untrusted models paired against trusted ones, monitors that read every output, narrow interfaces so the model can only touch a handful of things. His line was that this is definately nothing new. It's just running untrusted code. You don't audit a binary for good intentions; you sandbox it, you watch what syscalls it makes, you keep the trusted computing base small and the blast radius smaller, and you get on with your day. The conclusion he wanted was cheerfully deflationary: AI control is standard security engineering in a new coat of paint, the alignment crowd is chasing a non-problem, and the only genuinely hard parts are the boring engineering ones — latency, false positive rates, that sort of thing. I don't want to just tell him he's wrong; I want the actual audit, and I'd like it to be the kind of thing I could hand him afterwards.

So: give me the correspondence table first (what plays the role of the sandbox, the syscalls, the trusted computing base, the attacker, the exploit, the patch, and so on), then tell me where the analogy earns its keep, then where it breaks — and be careful to seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for his deflationary conclusion from the ones that are just interesting trivia about binaries and language models. Then I want to know what the sandbox picture quietly imports that the AI case doesn't have; I suspect it's something about who is allowed to read the adversary's output, but you tell me. Finish by saying whether the deflationary conclusion survives once all that is on the table, and if it doesn't, what weaker thing the analogy does support.

Sections, in this order: "The mapping" — a table of correspondences, six to eight rows, one clause per cell. "Where it earns its keep" — 150–200 words. "Where it breaks" — the disanalogies, with a clear line drawn between the ones that matter for his conclusion and the ones that don't, 400–500 words. "What the picture smuggles in" — 150–200 words. "Verdict" — 200–300 words, saying what survives. "A better analogy" — one paragraph, and say plainly if you don't think there is one.
