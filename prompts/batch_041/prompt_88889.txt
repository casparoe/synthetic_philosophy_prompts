I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the years since my retirement reading my way into philosophy of science and philosophy of mind, and I have reached a puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. Please act as an expert in the philosophy of representation and in the theory of machine learning, and help me think it through with real care. I would like you to brainstorm the space of positions first — lay out the main ways the question can be answered and what turns on each — and only then give me your considered explanation.

The puzzle concerns the interpretability literature on artificial neural networks. Researchers train sparse autoencoders, fit linear probes, visualize features, trace circuits, and then speak as though they have identified the representations the network "uses." I keep asking what licenses the move from "we can decode this information from the activations" to "the network represents it."

Let me sharpen the question with a distinction I have been using, and I want you to tell me whether it is a good one or whether it collapses under pressure. Call a state a spectator representation when its content is recoverable by an external observer — a probe can be trained to read it off — and an actor representation when the system itself routes that content into its downstream computation, so that the content makes a difference to what the system does. My instinct as a scientist is that only the second notion could bear explanatory weight, and that the first is nearly free: any sufficiently rich activation vector will let you decode any number of things, including things the network has no business representing. But then I worry that the actor notion is circular, because to say the system "routes" or "uses" the content seems to presuppose the very representation I am trying to establish. Explain to me where the circularity worry is genuine and where it is an illusion, and say what an interpretability finding would have to show in order to count as evidence for actor rather than spectator representations.

I would also like you to address two further points that I believe any adequate answer must get right but that I have not seen stated clearly. First, the role of intervention: if the strongest evidence for use is that ablating or steering a feature changes behavior, what does that evidence actually establish, given that a system can change its behavior when you poke it without representing the thing you poked? Second, whether realism about representations is even the right target — whether this dispute is really about the world (what the network does) or about our explanatory practices (whether representational talk earns its keep) — and how one would tell these two readings apart.

Throughout, be as precise as you can about your terms, and do not smooth over the places where the argument is genuinely contested. At the end, state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, with your reason for the doubt.
