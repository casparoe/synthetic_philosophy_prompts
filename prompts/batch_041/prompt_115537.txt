Next month's topic at our little LessWrong-adjacent reading group is self-deception, and I want to hand out a decision tree instead of a reading list — we're all a bit allergic to "summarise the literature" exercises and half of us have no signal anyway.

What I keep noticing is that everyone agrees self-deception happens, but when you push them they mean wildly different things, and most of the arguing turns out to be people answering different questions. So build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. I'd start at the node where you have to say whether the self-decieved person really holds the beleif they seem to deny — and what "really holds" would have to mean — then branch on whether the process is intentional or only motivated, whether it needs a divided mind or runs on a single one with attention and avowal doing the work, and whether it's a distinct phenomenon at all rather than ordinary motivated belief plus avoidance. I do want a leaf for the view that the point of deceiving yourself is to deceive other people better, since that changes everything downstream.

Every node has to be a concrete case someone can answer without already knowing the labels: the partner who is somehow the last to know, the doctor who smokes, the investor who won't open the statements, the person loudly declaring a belief their behaviour flatly contradicts, and the one where someone seems to half-know — I've never managed to make that last case precise, so see if your questions can. Leaves get a standard name where there is one and a coined one where there isn't, plus a sentence each on the case that makes that position most uncomfortable. Flag any two leaves the tree can't really separate, and any combination of branch answers that comes out empty, saying whether that's because it's incoherent, unliveable, or just unexplored.

Two sides of A4 max, and please write it in British English — I'm printing it and I can't stand "ize".
