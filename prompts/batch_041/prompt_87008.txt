For the satire issue of my department's student magazine I want to try something more demanding than the usual headscarf jokes, and I have settled on a fake internal document: the seventh edition of the Handbook for School Administrators, issued by a fictional regulator — call it the Bureau of Republican Neutrality and Reasonable Accommodation — that has been handed the impossible brief of running a single school system under two doctrines at once. On one side stands the French inheritance: it is the state, not society, that must be neutral; public servants owe strict neutrality while pupils and the users of a public service keep their freedom of conscience; religious signs in school are sorted into conspicuous ones, which are forbidden, and discreet ones, which are not; and the state's own calendar of holidays is treated as though it had no author. On the other side stands the Anglo-American inheritance: the state does not police what people wear, but must accommodate individual religious practice unless doing so imposes an undue hardship, an inquiry conducted case by case, turning on the sincerity of the belief and on the staff time the accommodation costs. The Bureau I have in mind fuses the two: neutrality is a duty of the institution and a virtue of its employees; the same person is a user of the service in the morning and an agent of it after lunch; every burden is placed on an undue-hardship scale; and the Bureau's declared aim is not to accommodate difference but to integrate it, on the theory that the majority's culture is one of the parties at the table rather than the table itself.

Write the handbook: eight to twelve numbered entries, each a question from a school administrator — of the kind that actually lands on a head teacher's desk, about a swimming class, a school meal, a beard, a memorial, a parent who volunteers in the canteen — followed by the Bureau's deadpan ruling, 800-1000 words in all, in the voice of a body sincerely persuaded that it is being helpful. The rulings are the jokes, and each should exaggerate something real about one doctrine or the other: the threshold between conspicuous and discreet, the sincerity test that asks the state to weigh what it claims it cannot judge, the difference between the neutrality of the state and the absence of religion from public space, the way a granted accommodation hardens into a precedent the next administrator dare not refuse. At least three entries should reach a result that neither tradition would accept on its own, and at least one should turn entirely on a change in a person's legal status rather than on anything the person did or wore. Nowhere should the objections be stated: the reader should infer them from watching the Bureau answer the next question as though the previous one had not been asked. Keep the target to doctrines and paperwork — no real politicians or parties, and no faith held up as the joke, since the comedy should come from the categories. After the document, add a short note on where the piece takes liberties with the two doctrines or leans on a contested reading of one of them, which ruling is nearest to being defensible and what would have to be true of the doctrine for it to be defensible, and which entry you would cut first if you decided the satire had been unfair.
