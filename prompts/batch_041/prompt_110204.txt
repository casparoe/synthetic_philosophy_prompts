I am a graduate student in philosophy, and last week I watched two colleagues of mine -- both of whom work on social epistemology -- get into a long, increasingly heated argument about a real situation in my building's tenants' association. I stayed out of it, but I have agreed to write a neutral memo summarizing the exchange for our seminar, and I genuinely cannot decide who has the better of it. I would like you, as someone who knows the philosophy of testimony and social epistemology well, to adjudicate the dispute move by move. Please reason carefully before committing to a verdict; arguments of this shape are easy to judge by whichever side happens to be stated last, and I want a considered assessment rather than a summary. For clarity I am paraphrasing both of them, though the technical vocabulary is theirs.

Here is the case. A tenant I will call Mara repeatedly complained to the association that her landlord kept letting himself into her apartment without notice, ostensibly to check the boiler, and that this made her feel unsafe. She could not put it more sharply than that it felt as though he were entitled to be in her home whenever he liked. The chair of the association brushed her off as oversensitive. Some weeks later a male tenant, Devin, described the very same practice as "a standing boundary violation that creates a low-grade threat environment," and the association immediately took it seriously and started drafting a policy. My two colleagues then disagreed about how to characterize what had gone wrong. Colleague A said: this is a textbook case of testimonial injustice -- Mara's testimony was given less credibility than it deserved, and the operative reason was her identity (young, female, a renter) rather than anything about what she said; Devin said the same thing and was believed; the wrong is the credibility deficit, full stop. Colleague B replied that the credibility deficit is real but is not the heart of it -- the deeper problem is hermeneutical: neither the association nor, crucially, Mara herself had the shared concept (boundary violation, threat environment) needed to render her experience intelligible, and Devin did not merely enjoy more credibility but supplied the interpretive resource everyone had been lacking; that is a different wrong, calling for a different remedy (concept-building rather than simply believing victims). Colleague A countered that the two cannot be cleanly separated: the concept was missing in the first place precisely because the people whose experience it names have no standing to coin it, and Devin's coinage was accepted precisely because he had the credibility Mara lacked, so the hermeneutical gap is downstream of the same identity prejudice and not a separate wrong. Colleague B answered that this may be how it happened here, but the connection is contingent rather than conceptual -- hermeneutical injustice can occur with no credibility deficit at all (everyone might believe you completely and still be unable to understand what you are telling them) -- and that A's move collapses two distinct wrongs into one, which will misdirect the remedy.

Please adjudicate this point by point. Is A right that the credibility deficit is the whole of the wrong? Is B right that the hermeneutical wrong is conceptually distinct and separable from the testimonial one? Is A right that in this case the hermeneutical gap is downstream of identity prejudice? Does B's contingent-versus-conceptual point actually tell against A's position, or is it a distinction without a difference for this case? And step back: should the injustice be located in the moment of dismissal itself, or in the background distribution of interpretive power that made the dismissal possible? Finally, say which colleague has the better overall position and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate the question.
