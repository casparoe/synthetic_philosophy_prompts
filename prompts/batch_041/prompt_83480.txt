I keep noticing that the law sides with one temporal slice of a person against another, and that it does not always pick the same slice. Four familiar rules make the pattern vivid. A consumer who buys at a distance — online, by phone, at the door — ordinarily gets a short window in which to cancel for no reason at all, though the contract was perfectly valid when made: the later self is permitted to repudiate what the earlier self agreed to. A person who puts themselves on a gambling self-exclusion register is in the opposite position: once listed, operators must turn them away, and a later change of heart does not by itself release them, so the earlier self governs the later. The default rule of contract law otherwise runs the same way — a change of mind is no excuse, and the earlier self's promise binds the later. Bankruptcy discharge runs the other way again, letting a later self shed many obligations that the earlier self freely and competently incurred.

What does this pattern presuppose? I want the implicit theory of the person across time, and of diachronic rationality, reconstructed. Does it require treating time-inconsistent preferences as a rational defect of the kind the money-pump and Dutch-book arguments are meant to expose — as a structure that leaves you exploitable by a sequence of trades you would accept one at a time — or can the whole pattern be licensed by a purely descriptive story about preference drift plus ordinary paternalism about welfare? Does it commit the law to there being a fact of the matter about which time-slice is really the agent, or is that a fiction the law finds convenient? Which of the four rules are strictly presupposed by a single underlying view, and which merely fit it comfortably? Is the pattern coherent — is there one principle, whether vulnerability, informedness, who engineered the situation, or who bears the reliance cost, that generates all four rules — or is it a heap of unrelated doctrines pointing in different directions, in which case which of them should be revised?

Finally, tell me what the practice would look like under two rival pictures: one on which the earlier self always binds the later, commitments sacrosanct, regret never a reason, precommitment devices fully enforceable; and one on which the later self is a genuinely different agent whose preferences are simply to be weighed against the earlier self's. Say which rules survive under each picture and which parts of the current practice would have to be abandoned. No citations or literature review needed; I want the presuppositions reconstructed, the underdetermination mapped, and the inconsistency either resolved or located precisely.
