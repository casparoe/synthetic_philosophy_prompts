I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and for three years I have been reporting on a quarrel in a town that grew up around a single textile mill: how the town should commemorate a strike that ended in 1911 with the deaths of several workers. My reporting has taken me through what survives of the record — the union's minute book, the local newspaper, transcripts of interviews with the strikers' children recorded in the 1970s — and through what does not. The company's personnel files were destroyed when the works closed; the newspaper was owned by the mill's largest shareholder; the death toll rests on memories gathered half a century after the fact. A descendant has recently found a box of photographs with pencilled captions in an unidentified hand, several of which are demonstrably wrong.

The council has at last agreed on a plaque, and because my reporting became part of the argument over its wording, I have been asked to give the address at the unveiling. The audience will be mixed and will not be neutral: descendants of the strikers, descendants of the men who kept working, the mayor, a school group, and a few professional historians who will notice every overstatement. The long argument about historical objectivity — Ranke's ambition to recover the past as it essentially was, Carr's insistence that historical facts are constituted by the questions the present puts to them, White's account of the narrative form historians impose on the record — will be the substance of the occasion whether or not I mention it.

Please write that address: about six minutes as delivered, roughly eight hundred words. It must say enough to justify a plaque without pretending that the record is complete or disinterested, and it must not slide into the view that because the evidence is partial, nothing can responsibly be asserted. It should resist the audience's appetite for a verdict on blame without leaving them with a shrug. Do not let it become a lecture, and do not name-drop the scholars above unless a single passing reference genuinely helps; the philosophical work should be done by the speech rather than announced in it. The address should not make my own reporting its subject. Close with one sentence — the strongest claim about what happened that the surviving record licenses — which I intend to deliver as my final line and may later quote in print.
