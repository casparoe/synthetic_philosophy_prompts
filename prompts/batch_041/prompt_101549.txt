After an argument with a friend that ran most of an evening, I want to write up my side of it as an essay, and I want the essay to reach a verdict rather than split the difference. The question is whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. I'd like it organized around the two hinges our argument kept circling: whether "ought" implies "can" in a sense strong enough to exclude obligations that cannot be jointly discharged, and whether obligations agglomerate — whether from "I ought to do A" and "I ought to do B" it follows that I ought to bring about both. My own view, which the essay should defend, is that a dilemma is genuine only when an agent would be doing wrong whichever of two incompatible acts she performs, and merely apparent when a better-informed or better-placed agent would see that one of the supposed obligations was never binding at all. So the essay should keep those two cases distinct, argue that the first kind is sometimes actual, and take seriously the strongest objection against me: that every apparent dilemma is really just a case in which the weaker obligation is overridden rather than violated, so that talk of a "genuine" dilemma merely confuses what is required with what we believe is required. Include a symmetric case of your own devising, in which neither obligation is weaker and no resolution leaves the agent without fault, and let that case test the verdict. End with a definite conclusion and the reasons that support it.
