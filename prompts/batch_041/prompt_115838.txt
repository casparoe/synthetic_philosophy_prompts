My verdicts about art will not settle under any single principle I have tried, and I would like you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually captures them, and which of the verdicts I ought to give up.

A lullaby a mother sings to a child who has already fallen asleep, which no one else ever hears: art, and good art. A funeral lament recorded by an anthropologist, in a language I do not understand, sung by people whose lives I know nothing about: art, and it moves me to tears. A virtuoso piece of instrumental music I have heard many times and admire without ever having been moved by it: great art, though I cannot say it does anything to me at all. An advertising jingle I cannot get out of my head, which reliably makes me feel something every time it surfaces: not art. A film made to move a nation to war, which did in fact move me when I was young and which I now regard as a lie: not art. A child's drawing made with real feeling and shown to no one: art, and better art than most of what hangs in galleries. A portrait executed to professional standard by a studio painter who did not care about the sitter: not art. A hymn sung by a congregation whose beliefs I reject, which moves me every time I hear it: art, and good art, even though I think the feeling it transmits is false. A poem I love in a translation I am told is unfaithful to the original: art, and good art.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the virtuoso piece and the jingle. The first moves no one and I call it great art; the second moves me and I call it nothing. If art is the transmission of feeling, my second verdict is wrong. If it is something else — mastery, or a certain relation between maker and work — then my first one is.

I have been reading Tolstoy's What Is Art?, and the theory I would like to hold is roughly his: that a work is art when it transmits to others a feeling the maker actually had, and that its value lies in the sincerity and individuality of that feeling and in its being communicable to anyone, not only to the cultivated. I can see that this theory will not save all my verdicts, and I suspect that the repair I want — some condition on the maker's relation to the work that is independent of its effect on me — is either doing all the work or is empty.

So: state the principle that comes closest, clause by clause, naming for each clause the verdict that motivates it. Say what it implies about cases I have not listed: a piece of music I admire and then learn was generated by a machine that had no feeling to transmit; a joke that is funny only to the six people who were there; a proof or a chess combination whose beauty only someone with years of training can even perceive; a song written for a nation that no longer exists; and the case I keep going back and forth on, a novel I think is great and cannot stand. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, which I should abandon, and whether the result is still recognizably Tolstoy's view or a different view wearing its clothes. Where it overrules Tolstoy's own taste, say so plainly.
