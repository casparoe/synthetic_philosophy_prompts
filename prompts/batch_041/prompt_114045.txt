I'm a philosophy grad student and I've got a draft essay on the cluelessness objection to consequentialism that I'd like you to comment on. I care most about whether the central move actually works, but also whether the whole thing is interesting or just me rediscovering something everyone already knows.

The gist: the cluelessness objection says a consequentialist has to know an act's consequences to know whether it's right, but the consequences of even a trivial act ramify forever and can't be traced, so the theory can't guide action.

I think that objection usually runs two different ignorances together and only one of them is a real threat. Call the first reach-cluelessness: I can't know whether the long downstream effects of giving this five dollars to a charity are net positive or net negative, because they fan out, get swamped, recombine with a million other causes. Call the second weight-cluelessness: I don't know how to trade a tiny probability of an astronomically large effect against a large probability of a small one (the Pascal's mugging type of case).

Claim one: weight-cluelessness isn't a moral-epistemology problem at all. Its a general decision-theory problem about astronomical and infinite stakes, and it bites any theory that's sensitive to probabilities of outcomes, ordinary prudence included. So it shouldn't get filed under consequentialism is unworkable.

Claim two, the one I actually care about: reach-cluelessness is symmetric, so it doesn't favor the non-consequentialist. Whatever rule a deontologist says I should follow (don't lie, keep your promises) has downstream effects that are exactly as untraceable as the consequentialist's. So if cluelessness means the consequentialist can't know her act was right, it means the deontologist can't know her act was right either, unless she wants to say the rightness of following the rule has nothing to do with what happens. And if she says that, she hasn't escaped the objection, she's changed the subject. Cluelessness looks like a problem for action-guiding theories in general, not a reason to prefer one family of them.

So: is the two-way distinction doing real work or is it just relabeling? Is the symmetry argument sound, or is there a way for the non-consequentialist to slip out that I'm missing? And is claim two too strong as stated, or does it need to be restricted to theories that say anything at all about how the world should go?

Give me comments on accuracy and on whether it lands, please. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, and where my reasoning is most likely to be wrong. American English.
