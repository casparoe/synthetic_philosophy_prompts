I'm a retired geochemist — thirty-odd years of isotope work, mostly on hydrothermal systems — and since I stopped running a lab I've been reading moral philosophy, which I've found much harder than the chemistry, and I mean that as a compliment. For the last two years I've chaired the research-integrity committee at my old institute: five of us, mostly retired, we meet once a month for ninety minutes, and we get maybe fifteen complaints a year, everything from authorship squabbles to outright data fabrication. The wall we keep hitting is this. Somebody files a complaint, and the person on the other end says, in effect, look who's talking. The group whose record-keeping has been a shambles for years complains that another group's notebooks are a shambles. A professor who has quietly handed out gift authorships to his own students complains about a colleague padding an author list. A postdoc who retracted a paper for duplicated image panels complains about someone else's duplicated panels. Right now the committee does one of two things depending on who showed up that day: we treat the "look who's talking" as a full stop and dismiss the complaint, or we wave it away as a distraction and never mention it in the report. Both feel wrong, and I suspect the reason is that I've been conflating two different things — whether the complaint is true, and whether this particular person gets to be the one pressing it. I've run into the vocabulary for this in my reading (standing, warrant, the idea that blame might have a gate as well as a content), but the actual arguments are scattered and I can't tell which of them a committee like ours is supposed to be using, or whether the whole "you have no standing" move is just a debating trick that I should stop dignifying. Note that our committee itself is not innocent here — we sat on a very similar case about eight years ago and did nothing — so I'd like the procedure to say something about whether an institution can lose standing too, not just a person.

What I want from you is a decision procedure we can actually run in one sitting, not a lecture. Turn whatever the philosophy of blame and hypocrisy actually knows about standing into a sequence of questions, in order, that a committee of retired scientists can work through with a complaint in front of them and a whiteboard behind them: what to ask first, what counts as an answer to each question, where the procedure stops with a dismissal, where it says "the substance is fine but this complainant can't be the one to carry it" (and what that would even look like operationally — do they become an anonymous source? does the committee take the case up on its own motion?), and where it hands the thing back to us because the remaining question is one of values and not of fact. It has to distinguish, if the distinction survives contact with reality, between a complainant who is a hypocrite and a complainant who is simply not a saint; between hypocrisy that bears on the specific charge and hypocrisy that's just an unrelated stain; and between an individual complainant and an institutional one, since half our cases come in as "the department feels that..." and I genuinely don't know what standing means for a body rather than a person. Keep in mind that four of the five of us have no philosophy at all, that whatever we write down has to survive being read by the institute's legal office if anyone appeals, and that the chair — me — signs the report, so the procedure needs to be honest about which calls are mine to make. I'm not asking for a reading list or for you to tell me whose book to consult; most of us don't have library access and anyway that's not the deliverable. Just the machinery.

Please give it to me in these sections, in this order: first, "The procedure at a glance" — the whole thing as a numbered list I could fit on one whiteboard, ten lines or so, no explanation; then "Step by step", the bulk of the answer, walking through each question, what a sufficient answer looks like, where it terminates, and where it hands off, a page or two; then "Three cases run through it" — use my notebook-shambles case, the gift-authorship case, and one more you invent, ideally one where the procedure tells us to proceed with the complaint and I'd rather it didn't, and say so; then "Where this is judgment in a lab coat" — the steps that look mechanical but aren't, a paragraph or a short list; and finally "What this can't do" — five lines, honest about the cases where it will misfire or produce a result nobody in the room will like. Write it plainly; if you have to choose between precision and something the other four will understand, pick the second one, but flag what got lost.
