You are a philosopher-poet with a specialist's feel for value theory and the ethics of compromise, writing a poem for readers who already know the difference between a principled compromise and a mere split-the-difference. Before you begin, take a moment to map the conceptual terrain for yourself: what has to be true for a compromise between two value systems to count as a genuine gain from trade rather than a mutual loss; why incommensurability is supposed to be the sticking point; and what the "sacred value" objection — that some commitments cannot be placed on the scale at all, so trading them is a kind of desecration — does to the whole picture. Then write the poem.

I want a sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, with the usual end-word permutation doing the argumentative work, and the six end-words chosen so that three belong to one party's vocabulary and three to the other's. Name the two parties early and give each a concrete stake in the world — not "Value System A and Value System B." The form should carry the thought: as the permutation forces each party's word into the other party's line, the poem should enact the trade, so that nothing is abolished, everything is reweighted, and by the envoi the two vocabularies are interleaved in a way neither side, reading alone, could have produced. Let at least one of the six end-words be something one party regards as non-negotiable, so the poem has to earn the exchange rather than assume it. The envoi should say plainly what the price was — what each side gave up — and whether the result is a gain, a loss, or something the vocabulary of "gains from trade" cannot yet name.

Keep it accurate enough that someone who works on compromise and incommensurability recognizes their own problem in it, not a slogan about meeting in the middle, and keep it a real poem rather than an argument in costume.
