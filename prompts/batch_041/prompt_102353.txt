Hey, I'm putting together a short explainer for a little reading group I run on hedonism -- the ethical view, not the eat-drink-and-be-merry stereotype -- and I keep getting stuck on this "heterogeneity of pleasure" objection. The version I've absorbed goes: the pleasure of a hot shower, the pleasure of finally getting a proof to click, and the pleasure of seeing a close friend after a long time have basically nothing in common as experiences -- no shared feeling-tone, no one sensation that's present in all of them -- so "pleasure" doesn't pick out a single kind of thing at all. And I take the punchline to be that hedonism is just refuted: you can't claim pleasure is the only intrinsic good and that we should maximize it if there's no one thing called pleasure to maximize. That seems airtight to me, which is exactly why it bugs me that philosophers still treat hedonism as a live option and keep writing about it as if nothing happened.

A friend in the group said I've got the target wrong -- that the objection only really lands on one particular theory of what pleasure *is*, and that you can stay a hedonist while happily agreeing that pleasures are wildly different from each other. I honeslty can't tell if she's right or just being clever. So could you sort me out: is the heterogeneity point supposed to refute hedonism outright, and if not, what exactly is it an objection to? What, if anything, would a hedonist have to give up in order to live with it, and where is the real bite of the worry (if there is one) as opposed to the version I've been repeating to people? No need for a lit review or a reading list, I just want to see where I've overreached.
