Two years into retirement from experimental physics, I have joined a reading group on the philosophy of probability, in which I am by some distance the least prepared member. One of the others, a former mathematical physicist and the most consistent reasoner in the room, has over several sessions given me her verdicts on a series of problems, and I should like to anticipate her verdict on one more, and to understand what she is in fact committed to.

Her verdicts so far are these. On a fair six-sided die about to be rolled, she assigns probability 1/6 to each face. On a thoroughly shuffled standard deck, she assigns probability 1/4 to the top card being a spade. On the problem of a chord said to be drawn at random in a circle, where the question is the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, she declines to give any number, holding the question ill-posed: one can argue for 1/3, for 1/4, or for 1/2, according as the random chord is generated from random endpoints, from a random midpoint, or from a random distance from the centre, and nothing in the statement privileges one of these. On a factory producing cubes whose edge lengths lie between 0 and 2 metres, where the question is the probability that a cube's edge is at most 1 metre, she declines for the same reason, since spreading credence uniformly over edge length yields 1/2, over face area 1/4, and over volume 1/8.

The problem I now intend to raise at the next meeting, and which she has not yet seen, is this. A meteorite strikes a spherical asteroid at a single point, and the only thing we are told is that no direction is favoured. What is the probability that the impact point lies within 60 degrees of the north pole?

I should like you to predict her verdict on this problem; to set out the two or three general rules that fit her four earlier verdicts equally well; to say what each of those rules predicts here; to give me your best guess, with your reasons for preferring it; and to name a further problem that would separate whichever of the rules remain tied. I care less about the number than about the rules, and I would rather be told honestly that her record underdetermines the new case than be handed a confident answer that papers over the gap.
