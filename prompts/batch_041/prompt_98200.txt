I am advising a national statistics office that has been urged to make climbing the annual international happiness league tables a policy goal, and it wants the specification before it commits. Before anyone designs an index, write the desiderata: what any adequate national measure of wellbeing would have to deliver for it to settle the question of whether a country should try to top such a ranking. Treat the purpose as fixed — an instrument good enough to justify adopting a rank position as a national target — and let the requirements follow from that, rather than from what existing indices happen to report.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say explicitly whether it is a genuine requirement or merely the preference of one camp. The following matters should all be addressed, though you should decide which of them survive as requirements and which dissolve on inspection:

- what the measure is a measure of — how someone evaluates her life as a whole, how she feels from moment to moment, or whether her life is going well in a more objective sense — motivated by the case of a person who rates her life highly but reports frequent negative emotion, and the person whose life is meaningful but not pleasant;
- adaptation and shifting standards, motivated by the case of someone who reports high satisfaction with circumstances an observer would call deprived, and by the person with expensive tastes who reports low satisfaction in enviable circumstances;
- whether scores can be compared across persons and across cultures at all, motivated by the possibility that two people or two societies use the same scale to mean different things;
- robustness to context, framing, and question order, including the tendency to judge one's life against salient comparisons rather than in the abstract;
- sensitivity to the conditions policy can actually change, as against stable traits the policy cannot touch;
- aggregation and legibility — whether a single number is meaningful, how dimensions are weighted, and what trade-offs the index licenses;
- what becomes of the measure once it is a target, and whether it can be gamed;
- the boundary between measuring wellbeing and measuring its causes, so that the index does not smuggle in a policy programme under the guise of a reading.

I especially want the part where the list turns on itself: identify any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, if a measure sensitive enough to guide policy is also the one most vulnerable to adaptation and framing, or if cross-cultural comparability can only be bought at the price of disregarding how people set their own standards — and explain what that impossibility means for anyone who wants the ranking to settle the policy question. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to rule out the current league tables, and briefly, which of the remaining requirements the most defensible indices meet and which they fail.
