I TA a second-year formal epistemology unit (about 18 students, one 90-minute session, and yes the projector is held together with tape), and there's a thing I've been trying to get them to *feel* rather than learn: that when you get a message of the form "if A, then probably B" and it's given a number, your credences are genuinely underdetermined by it, and the obvious fixes all pull apart. Every year they nod at this and then, on the problem sheet, half of them condition on the material conditional as if "if...then" were just a disjunction and the other half quietly assume the message says nothing about A at all, and nobody notices they've made different choices. What I want is a session where they're forced to write down an actual number, discover their neighbours wrote different numbers for equally good reasons, and can't get the message itself to adjudicate. Here's the seed I want it built on, feel free to bend it: a drone operator is searching for a stranded hiker in a park divided into a North sector and a South sector, each half the area; within each sector there's a smaller "likely zone" that the operator initially thinks she's 50/50 to be in, so her prior is flat at a quarter on each of the four cells (North-zone, North-rest, South-zone, South-rest). Then the radio comes in: "if she's in the North sector, the chance she's in the North likely-zone is 4 in 5." She has to reallocate. Nobody can tell her what her new credence that she's in the North should be — or rather, everybody thinks they can.

Design me the runnable session. I want rules, materials (I'm happy to print slips and hand out tokens), a scoring scheme with small stakes — I'd rather it be a proper scoring rule so that honestly reporting your credences is the thing that pays, and I don't want to have to do the arithmetic live, so give me a printed table if it comes to that — plus a minute-by-minute timeline, any roles, and what I write on the board when. Crucially the structure should make them collide with the problem themselves before I name anything: they should commit to numbers, see the spread across the room, and only then start defending their rule. If you can build in a second round where the same information arrives phrased differently (say, as a claim about the conditional itself versus as a plain probability that some "if...then" sentence is true), or a round where a converse conditional comes in, so the direction of the conditional and the two readings of "if" both get exercised, all the better. And I want them to notice that the disagreement isn't incoherence — that two people can hold perfectly coherent, non-Dutch-bookable credences that differ, so no amount of betting will settle it.

Then the debrief, which is where I most need help: map the moments in the activity onto the actual moving parts of the problem, and give me a table of the candidate update rules a smart student might be reaching for — conditioning on the material conditional, holding the prior on the North fixed, the "move as little as possible" or closest-distribution idea, and whatever else deserves a seat — with the number each one spits out for the probability of being in the North in this exact scenario, and one line on what's attractive and what's fishy about each. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (I'd bet money on at least one group treating the 4-in-5 as a fact they can just plug in) and how to handle it in the moment, and be straight with me about which part of the puzzle the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply from the front of the room.
