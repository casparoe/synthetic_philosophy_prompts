I'm a high school student working on a project about the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials, and I keep running into two camps that argue past each other. The first holds that the trials were legitimate: the defendants' conduct was already criminal under customary international law and under ordinary morality, so the objection about retroactivity is a technicality, and some measure of victors' justice was the unavoidable price of holding anyone to account at all. The second holds that the trials were the winners writing law to fit the losers: the offenses — above all the charge of waging aggressive war — were defined after the acts, the judges were the victors, and the tu quoque objection (that the victors had done the same things) was never really answered.

Write the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered section of claims both accept; then the exact points of disagreement, each phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording; then the cruxes; and for each crux a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If part of the quarrel turns out to be verbal — one side arguing about the morality of the acts, the other about the form of the law — say which part and why. I don't need the history of either tribunal or any sources; I care about the argument.

To keep this from turning into a rehearsal of the usual talking points, please also work through a case neither side has an answer ready for: a future tribunal charging a leader with the deliberate destruction of an entire country's environment as a weapon of war, an offense that was not a crime when the acts were committed. Treat it as a crux, say what each side predicts, and say whether the two objections — retroactivity and victors' justice — come apart there.
