I've been defending the following position and want an honest map of its exposure. The doing/allowing distinction is not a matter of intention, of bodily movement, or of the familiar counterfactual (would the harm have occurred anyway?); it is a matter of causal process ownership. An agent does harm when the harm is the terminus of a causal process she initiates or sustains; she merely allows it when the harm is the terminus of a process already underway that she neither initiates nor sustains. Sustaining covers continuous action (holding a head under water) and also iterated non-action that is a necessary input at each stage (a landlord who each month renews nothing while a tenant's housing lapses). I want to extend this to institutional settings by treating an institution as a process to which an agent can be a designated input.

What would count against it? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they are. I want cases where the account yields a verdict we should reject; the argument that 'the same process' is doing all the work and cannot be specified non-circularly; rival accounts that cover the same cases with fewer commitments; and, the one I worry about most, whether the extension to institutions and iterated omissions costs the account the very distinction it was built to draw, since at some point sustaining a process may be indistinguishable from failing to interrupt one. Tell me what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether the qualifications I've built in have made it unfalsifiable -- and if so, whether that is a defect here or a sign that I have misdescribed what the account is for.
