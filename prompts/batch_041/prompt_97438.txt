I spent thirty-four years as a bench experimentalist — the last twenty of them on protein crystallography — and since retiring I have been trying, with more enthusiasm than discipline, to acquire a philosophical education. By a route too accidental to recount, that has landed me on the advisory committee of a small foundation that has earmarked funds to support something like eight researchers for three years on the conceptual foundations of scalable oversight, with an emphasis on debate-style protocols: the theory, not the engineering. The committee has asked me, as its resident non-specialist, to write the memo arguing for where the money should go, and I would rather not simply ratify the preferences of the two members who actually work in the field. What I want from you is a defended agenda rather than a survey. Among the open conceptual questions in this area — whether a judge weaker than the advocates it is judging can in principle extract a reliable verdict from their exchange; whether a system trained to win debates retains any incentive toward honesty once it has learned what actually persuades; what the target of the procedure is supposed to be when, for the questions we care most about, no human may be able to check the answer; whether the protocol's guarantees decay gracefully or catastrophically as the gap between judge and advocate widens; and whatever I have failed to think of — which are upstream of the others, which are tractable for people who work with arguments rather than gradients, and which I should deprioritize on the ground that settling them would not change much downstream?

Two further considerations, which I offer as provocations rather than as settled framing, since they are my own and I cannot tell whether they are deep or merely confused. The first is a distinction I have not seen drawn cleanly: oversight of what a system believes, and oversight of what it will do. A debate can in principle establish facts about the world, but what we actually require is confidence in a system's behaviour in circumstances no one has yet described, and it is not obvious that assurance of the first kind transfers to the second. The second is that the judge's limitation may be informational rather than computational — it is asked to adjudicate claims about situations it has never encountered and may never encounter — so that any guarantee we extract is conditional on a distributional assumption about the judge's competence which is itself unverifiable at precisely the moment it matters. If either of these is the right way to carve the problem, it ought to reorder the priorities, and I would like you to say so explicitly.

Please close by telling me what would move an item up or down this list over the next two or three years — what a result would have to look like for me to advise the board that the money should be redirected — and be candid about which of my candidate questions is not really a question at all. I should add that the researchers will be philosophers and theorists with no laboratory access to frontier models; they can read and argue, and occasionally run small experiments through collaborators, but they cannot train anything at scale.
