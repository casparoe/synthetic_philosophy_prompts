Two friends of mine in a philosophy of science reading group spent most of last week arguing about how to reason concerning how many observers a world contains, and they have asked me to referee because I said I would think it over. Neither of them has read up on the subject; they are working it out from scratch, so please judge the arguments as they stand rather than by which established camp they happen to land in. The case they were arguing about is this. You come to with no memories in a numbered room, and you know only the following: before you woke, a fair coin was tossed; heads means the building you are in contains exactly ten rooms, tails means it contains exactly one million rooms; and, whichever it was, you were assigned uniformly at random to one of them. You look at the door and see the number 7. Neither of them disputes the setup; they disagree only about what follows from it.

Nadia says the number 7 is overwhelming evidence that the coin came up heads. If there were a million rooms, the chance that a randomly placed occupant such as herself would be in room 7 is one in a million; if there were ten, it is one in ten; so she should now be almost certain of the ten-room building. She takes the general lesson to be that finding oneself at a low rank, or early in a sequence, or in any small slot, is evidence that the set of slots is small, and she accepts the consequence that she should expect humanity to be short-lived, since she finds herself among the first hundred billion or so humans rather than somewhere in the middle of a long future. Omar says her reasoning is backwards. Before the coin is even tossed, he says, we must ask how likely it is that someone like him — this observer, not merely some observer or other — should exist under each hypothesis. A million rooms give a million times as many positions for him to have occupied, and therefore a million times as many ways for the world to contain him; ten rooms give only ten. So his existence itself is a million times more likely on the million-room hypothesis, which exactly cancels the one-in-ten versus one-in-a-million difference, leaving the room number with no evidential force at all: he should still say fifty-fifty. He likewise accepts the consequence that we should be confident the world contains vastly more people than we would otherwise have thought, since a hypothesis that posits more observers multiplies the ways for us to be here — and he says he would hold to this even when the many-observers hypothesis is, on independent grounds, far less plausible.

Nadia's reply is that Omar is treating her existence as though it were evidence, which she says is a mistake: on either hypothesis someone was going to wake up in this building having these experiences, so there was never any chance that she would fail to exist, and the only thing she could have learned differently is which room she is in. What Omar calls a million ways for the world to contain him are, on her view, merely a million ways for the world to contain someone, and only one of those someones is her. Omar answers that this is precisely the point at issue — that she is treating him as a placeholder for whoever happens to exist, when the thing needing explanation is that it is he, this particular observer, who exists — and he adds that her way of reasoning has the further cost of letting an arbitrary coincidence of position swamp everything else she knows about how buildings get built, so that the first fact she learns about her place in the world is decisive. Nadia replies that his alternative is worse: if the sheer number of observers could raise a hypothesis's credibility, one could make any world as likely as one pleased simply by positing more inhabitants. Omar's rejoinder is that the mirror-image absurdity on her side is that she must treat her own birth rank as strong evidence that the species is near its end, letting her read the whole human future off the fact that she happens to be early. They went around this loop for an hour without either yielding, and I could not tell which of them was making the mistake, or whether each was right about something different. Please work through the exchange move by move — Nadia's initial inference from the room number, Omar's cancellation argument, Nadia's claim that she could not have failed to exist, Omar's charge that the room number cannot swamp everything else, and each of the two reductios — and say who is right about which specific step, what each of them is missing, and whether there is a defensible position that preserves some of what each is after.
