I am the analyst attached to the appraisal panel of a regional health board — the body that decides whether to fund changes to how services are configured — and we are stuck in a loop. Whenever a proposal is contentious, someone moves that we defer and commission more evidence first: a pilot at two sites, a modelling exercise, a patient survey, a further audit of the existing service. The motion is almost always defended by an appeal to Good's theorem — the claim that the expected value of information is never negative, so that we cannot lose by looking before we decide. I have read enough to be fairly sure that this is a loose application, but I do not have a better rule, and the deferrals are costing us: two of them have pushed changes back by more than a year, and at least one service has got worse in the meantime. We meet monthly, we have nobody trained in decision analysis, the chair has the casting vote, and our finance director — who has to sign off anything above £50,000, and who has become allergic to pilots — will be reading this too.

Please write us a procedure we can run in ten minutes, at the table, on each request to gather more evidence before deciding: the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and tells us to decide now on what we have, and where it has to hand the question to a person because the answer turns on values rather than facts. If a step needs numbers we do not have, say what to do instead. Be explicit about exactly what is being operationalised. As I understand it, the theorem is a claim about the expected value of a free observation to a single expected-utility maximiser with a fixed prior and fixed preferences, who will then act on what they learn; the non-negativity is a property of the expectation taken before looking, not a promise that any particular result leaves us better off than ignorance would have. I would like the procedure to mark clearly the three or four places where getting from that statement to a public body's decision is a substantive step rather than a deduction — the panel is not a single agent with one prior; commissioning evidence is itself an act with consequences, since a consultation or an audit changes what the service does and what people believe while it runs; the panel does not always act on what it learns; and value here means expected utility, which is precisely what is in dispute. Then run it on three cases: a proposal to pilot a new triage model at two sites before regional roll-out; a proposal to survey patients before deciding whether to close a small unit; and a proposal to defer the reconfiguration of stroke services pending further modelling. I suspect the third will come out against the cautious option, which several of my colleagues will not enjoy hearing — better to have that argument now than in six months.

Throughout, keep clear which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it — the mathematics of the theorem is one thing; whether expected-utility language is the right frame for a public body at all, whether information has a value apart from the decision it feeds, and how much of the result survives when the decision-maker is a committee are quite another. Please do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus, and say plainly where a competent analyst could reasonably run the procedure differently from the way you have written it. Write in British English.
