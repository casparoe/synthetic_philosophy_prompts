I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of intellectual property, and I keep tripping over an inconsistency among the regimes that govern ideas at my own university. Copyright leaves ideas entirely free — only expression is owned — so I may reimplement a colleague's algorithm the day after reading her paper. Patent law says nearly the opposite: the invention, the method itself, is the thing owned, for a term, provided it is novel and non-obvious. Research integrity policy takes a third line: anyone's ideas may be used freely, but they must be attributed, and failing to attribute a substantive idea counts as misconduct even if I arrived at it independently. My integrity office only pursues idea-plagiarism, as opposed to text-plagiarism, when the idea was non-obvious to a competent researcher in the field and central to the later work — a filter that looks borrowed from patent law but does different work there. Then the technology transfer office treats those same ideas as trade secrets that may not be disclosed at all. All four regimes sit in one building and bind the same people.

Please reconstruct what each practice presupposes about ideas. What conception of an idea — as property, as a discoverable fact, as a social artifact, as someone's labour — does each regime need in order to be coherent, and which rival conceptions does each quietly rule out? Which elements are strictly presupposed and which are merely well served? I suspect the idea/expression line is really about administrability and could survive almost any metaphysics of ideas, whereas the attribution norm needs something more; tell me whether that's right. Where are these regimes genuinely inconsistent, and where do they only look inconsistent because each answers a different question? Finally, if we replaced the whole patchwork with a single view — ideas are never owned, only attributed — what would have to change in each practice to make that workable?
