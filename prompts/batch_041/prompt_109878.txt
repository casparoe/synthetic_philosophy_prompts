I'm an ICU nurse and I sit on the ethics committee at my hospital, which is rewriting our death-determination policy (we use neurologic criteria for some patients and circulatory criteria for others, depending on whether we're withdrawing support). Two things I keep running into while reading up on this, and they wont leave me alone.

First, people draw a line between a function being permanent and it being irreversible -- permanent meaning it won't come back given what we can actually do right now, irreversible meaning it can't come back no matter what. Our policy, as far as I can tell, leans on the first sense without ever saying so, and I'm not sure anyone on the committee has noticed that this is a choice.

Second, I've started to think the word "death" in our policy is doing two jobs at once: naming a biological state, and marking the point where it's ok to stop and where it's ok to take organs. Those might not be the same point, and it bothers me that we write it as if they obviously are.

What are the most important implications of that permanence/irreversibility distinction for how we should actually write the thing -- especially for donation after circulatory death, where we stand back and let the heart stay stopped for a few minutes and then proceed? I'd also like to know what follows from the second point, if anything does. Please be upfront about which parts of this are settled among people who genuinely work on it and which are contested, because I don't want to hand the committee a contested position dressed up as the consensus.
