I keep seeing "Whiggish" thrown around in historiography seminars like its a slur you can just aim at a book, and I want a case that shows it's a property of the telling rather than of any false claim. So take this as the definition for the exercise: an account is Whiggish to the degree that the prominence it gives each episode — space, emphasis, where it sits in the arc — is monotone in how much that episode contributes to the outcome we now care about. Call that the prominence rule and use it throughout. Now build me a thought experiment: two historians working from the exact same archive, making exactly the same factual claims and exactly the same causal claims, both getting every one of them right, and yet one comes out Whiggish under the rule and the other doesn't. Use a small episode — invent the details if you need to, I care about the structure, not the archive. Constraints: neither historian is ignorant or careless, nothing either asserts is false, and the difference mustn't come from one of them suppressing evidence; it has to fall out of how they weight things. Then do the check on the case: lay out the facts, the causal claims, and the prominence each one assigns, and show why the rule gives the verdict it does for each. Finish with which feature is doing the work and what happens if you take it away — if prominence is made to track evidential weight rather than causal contribution, does the case collapse into a plain disagreement about the evidence? And tell me how a defender of the Whiggish one pushes back, because I suspect the reply will be that her weighting is the one the evidence forces.
