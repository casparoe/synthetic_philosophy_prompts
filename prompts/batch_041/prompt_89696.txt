I am enrolled in a graduate seminar on theories of truth, and I am trying to get clear on a disagreement that I suspect is more than a technical squabble between two ways of handling the Liar. The first family of views treats the truth predicate as only partially defined: one starts from a base of sentences whose status is antecedently settled and iterates the rules governing the logical connectives and the truth predicate until nothing further is decided, so that sentences whose status is never settled — most famously the self-referential Liar — come out as neither true nor false rather than as bearing some exotic value. The second family, associated with Herzberger, Gupta, and Belnap, admits no such gap: one begins with a classical hypothesis about which sentences are true, applies the rule that a sentence is true just in case what it says obtains, and thereby revises the hypothesis, with some sentences settling down and others flipping indefinitely. Truth is then identified not with the extension that some privileged fixed point would assign, but with the rule of revision itself. I would like you to compare these two approaches as rival theories of one and the same phenomenon, explaining in your own words — no need to survey the literature or supply references — what each is committed to about (i) what precisely goes wrong with the Liar sentence, (ii) whether truth is to be analysed as a predicate with a determinate extension or as a circularly defined concept whose content is given by a procedure for revising hypotheses, and (iii) whether the ordinary links between truth, assertion, and the biconditional that says a sentence is true just in case things are as it says they are survive intact or must be qualified.

Please then press the comparison with a series of test cases: the sentence that says of itself that it is false; the sentence that says of itself that it is not true, which by the lights of the first theory is indeed not true and therefore seems to be saying something correct, so that it threatens to come out true after all; and the truth-teller, which says of itself merely that it is true. Does the contrast between the Liar and the truth-teller come out the same way in both frameworks, or do the two theories sort these sentences differently? A related question is whether either theory can express, in the very language it is theorizing about, the notion it uses to explain the pathology — being ungrounded, or being not true, in the one case, and being stably true or true in every hypothesis on which the process settles, in the other. If it cannot, say what the resulting revenge construction looks like, and whether the two versions of revenge do equal damage or whether one is a merely technical nuisance.

Finally, I would like your verdict, together with the strongest case you can make for the side you reject. Which of the two theories is more defensible as an account of the concept we actually use, and is the disagreement substantive — a genuine conflict about what truth is — or is it largely a disagreement about which theoretical role we most want a theory of truth to serve, such as preserving classical reasoning, explaining why the Liar fails to say anything, or explaining why it fails to settle on anything? If you think a hybrid is available — for instance, treating truth as a circularly defined concept while treating groundedness as marking a real distinction between sentences that do and do not express a determinate proposition — describe what that hybrid would look like and what it would cost.
