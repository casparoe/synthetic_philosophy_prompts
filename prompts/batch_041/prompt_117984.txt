I am a sixth-form student writing up an extended project, and I want to produce the document that two members of our school's science and philosophy society should have written months ago instead of continuing to argue past one another. One is a behavioural geneticist who treats heritability estimates as well-defined and useful parameters — ratios of variance components within a given population at a given time — while the other is a developmental-systems critic who thinks the quantity is too model-dependent, too unstable across cohorts, and too easily read as a claim that what is genetic is fixed, and that the variance-decomposition framework distorts the biology by treating environment as the residual.

Draft their joint adversarial-collaboration statement.

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign: what heritability is a property of; whether a high estimate licenses any inference about whether an intervention could shift the trait; whether heritability can rise or fall with no change whatever in the causal architecture; what the thought experiment of genetically identical seeds planted in good and poor soil does and does not establish; and what the two of them jointly say about how the term should be used in public discussion.

2. The residual disagreements, each phrased the way the side holding it would phrase its own position and precise enough that the other side would recognise it as its opponent's real view rather than a caricature. Include at least: whether heritability within a population has any bearing on the causes of differences between populations; whether gene–environment interaction and gene–environment correlation undermine the estimates or merely complicate their interpretation; and whether polygenic scores that predict poorly across ancestry groups are evidence about biology or only about sampling and study design.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each, a concrete case, dataset or argument that both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, together with what each would accept as evidence against its own position. I want these as specific as, say, a natural experiment in which a region's incomes rose sharply and the heritability of some cognitive measure there fell while its mean rose; do not leave them at the level of a call for more research.

4. If any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — if they mean different things by environment, innate or genetic — say which part and why.

Please keep the tone flat and technical, and resist fudging everything into a both-sides-are-partly-right verdict. Where they genuinely agree that a widely repeated claim is simply false, say so plainly.
