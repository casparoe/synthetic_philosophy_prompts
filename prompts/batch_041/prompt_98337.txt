A puzzle I keep returning to is the surprise exam. A teacher announces that there will be an exam on one day next week, and that the students will not be able to know, on the evening before, that it is coming the next day. The students reason backwards: it cannot fall on the last day, because by the night before they would know it was the only day left; granted that, it cannot fall on the day before that; and so on until no day remains, so the announcement cannot be fulfilled at all. The teacher then sets the exam midweek and it is a genuine surprise.

What I want from you is not a solution to the paradox but a diagnosis of why the argument about it has stalled. The discussion has been going for decades, and the positions have hardened into at least three. One camp holds that the students' backward induction is valid and the announcement is therefore self-undermining, so that the puzzle is why the exam still surprises them. A second holds that the announcement is perfectly consistent and the flaw lies somewhere in the students' reasoning — perhaps in what they may assume about the teacher's reliability, or in how knowledge is carried backwards through the week. A third holds that the announcement can be true yet cannot be known in advance to be true, so that surprise and foreknowledge cannot both be secured, and the paradox lives in that gap. Each side has an arsenal of cases, and nobody moves.

My own hypothesis — and I want you to treat it strictly as a hypothesis, not a premise — is that the main camps are answering different questions under a single name: one is asking whether the announcement can be true, another whether the students can rationally rely on it, and a third what the teacher can coherently assert. I also suspect the announcement has the flavour of a Moore-paradoxical assertion, since its content is partly about what the students themselves will believe. If that is right, the dispute may be verbal or a case of crossed questions; if it is wrong, I want to know what the real disagreement is.

Diagnose it, using what each side actually says rather than a survey of who said what. Is this a substantive disagreement about the logic of knowledge, a verbal one about the words surprise and know, two groups answering different questions under one heading, or a hidden formal question in disguise? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a re-posed question — or argue that none should be expected and say why.

Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: which consideration, argument, or counterexample would move you toward a different diagnosis, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
