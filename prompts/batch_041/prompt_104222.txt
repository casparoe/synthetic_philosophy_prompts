I'm a science journalist working on a feature about the Anthropocene, and I keep banging my head against the same wall: everyone agrees it's a thing, nobody agrees what kind of thing it is. I need you to help me think it through. Take the role of a philosopher of science who also knows a lot of history and geology, and start by brainstorming out loud about what's actually at stake before you answer. Think carefully -- this is one of those cases where the obvious answer is the least interesting one. I'm not a philosopher, so keep the jargon to a minimum.

Here's the concrete practice I want you to interrogate. A natural history museum is building a permanent exhibit on the Anthropocene. The label committee has adopted a rule: a period can only begin at a point where you can point to a single physical marker -- a layer of sediment, an ice core, a coral skeleton -- that shows the change, and that marker has to be findable by someone who doesn't know the human story at all. The education team wants to start the exhibit at the Industrial Revolution, because that's when the story gets legible to visitors. The ethics panel says the whole exhibit is a political act and the start date should be chosen to make a point about responsibility. And the director has floated a compromise: a dual-clock room, where one wall shows stratigraphic time and the other shows human historical time, and the exhibit only counts if both clocks agree on a moment. I'm not making all of this up -- okay, I'm making the dual-clock room up, but the rest is the kind of fight that's happening in real committees.

What I want from you: what philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Not just that it assumes humans are a geological force -- that's the easy part. I mean: what theory of historical periods does the label rule presuppose? Does a period have to be discoverable from the rocks alone to be real, or is that a methodological rule that the committee is smuggling in as a metaphysical one? What view of the nature/culture split does the exhibit assume, and does the dual-clock compromise actually resolve it or just hide it? Does the practice presuppose that who decides is a scientific question, a political one, or both -- and if both, what does it assume about how they fit together? Also tell me which parts are strictly presupposed by the practice and which are just things it happens to fit well with, and where the practice is underdetermined -- i.e., what rival theories of historical periodization, causation, or agency would also license the same exhibit. Finally, if you replaced the implicit theory with a different one, what would have to change in the exhibit, the label rule, or the museum's public stance?

I definately don't want a reading list or citations -- I need the argument, not the bibliography. And don't just give me a list of isms. I want the reconstruction to be concrete enough that a curator could read it and say, oh, so that's what we were assuming.
