You are a philosopher of mind and a methodologist of cognitive science, with a good ear for the difference between a method's real liabilities and objections that merely restate a rival's commitments. Think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming the space of possible defeaters, then organize and rank them.

I hold that heterophenomenology — Dennett's proposal that a science of consciousness should treat a subject's utterances as data, bracket the question of whether they are accurate, build up the heterophenomenological world as a kind of neutral fiction about what the subject takes their experience to be, and then explain why the subject says and believes those things — is not merely a useful technique but the only methodologically respectable route for such a science. Tell me what would count against that. Which problems are self-undermining (does the method tacitly presuppose the very first-person facts it claims to bracket, and is its celebrated neutrality a genuine abstention or verificationism in disguise)? Which empirical developments tell against it (for instance paradigms that aim to measure perceptual awareness with the report stripped out altogether, or results suggesting that introspective reports are routinely confabulated or shaped by the reporting task itself)? And which rival methodologies plausibly reach the same target by other means (guided first-person elicitation and trained phenomenological description, or interactive, second-person experimental designs)?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, marking which ones a defender should concede, what a committed heterophenomenologist would reply, and whether that reply answers the objection or merely insulates the view. The evidence I would personally find hardest to resist is any case in which what a subject can report comes apart from what they perceptually register. End by saying candidly whether the position as I have stated it is falsifiable at all, and if it is not, whether that is a fatal defect or simply the price of any top-level methodological stance.
