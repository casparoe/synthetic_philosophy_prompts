About a year ago I went through a rough stretch — my dad got sick and I got laid off in the same month — and a friend gave me one of the popular Stoicism books, and honestly it helped more than I expected. But since then I've gone back to the older stuff (Epictetus, in whatever free translation I could find online) and I can't shake the feeling that the modern version and the ancient version are doing different things with the same words. The modern version tells you to seperate your life into two buckets, what you control and what you don't, and then spend your energy in the first one. When I actually try it, almost nothing lands in bucket one — my effort, my temper, my ability to pay attention on a bad day all seem to depend on sleep and who I ran into that morning. And the old text says the body isn't up to us, which can't mean I have no causal influence over my body, so the phrase has to be doing something else — something more like what's mine to answer for, what's properly my doing and my fault, rather than what I can nudge. I've also noticed the popular stuff quietly drops the bit where health and money are indifferent but still worth preferring, and that seems to change alot downstream.

Anyway, I host a monthly philosophy meetup in the back room of a bar — about 14 regulars, mixed ages, a few have actually read the primary texts and most have only read the bestsellers — and I've been asked to run the next session. 90 minutes, no projector, just a table, and I don't want to stand up and lecture. What I want is an activity where they run into this themselves: something where they have to sort things into up-to-me and not-up-to-me while something real is at stake, and they discover that the sorting won't hold, that the middle is where basically their whole life sits, and that the difference between what I can influence and what I'm answerable for is the actual crux. The group is smart and a couple of them will try to defuse the whole thing by saying everything is partly controllable, so the activity needs to survive that move and preferably make it feel like a dodge.

Please design the whole thing: rules, materials (assume index cards, sticky notes, a bag, poker chips, a die, a timer, no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline, and then the debrief, with the mapping from what happened in the game onto the doctrine spelled out — including the preferred-indifferents point and the business about aiming at an outcome while attaching a proviso to it. Don't let them hear the technical vocabulary until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how I should handle that in the moment, and be honest about which part of the ancient view a 90-minute game just can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.
