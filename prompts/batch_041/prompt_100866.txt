I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the ethics of rescue, and I want to use the law's handling of rescue as an extended case study in how institutions encode philosophical commitments they never avow. Consider the following constellation of practices, every element of which is a matter of common knowledge and none of which I am asking you to applaud or condemn before you have reconstructed it:

- A competent adult who watches a child drown in a shallow pond, when she could pull the child out at negligible cost or risk to herself, commits no offence in England and Wales or in most American states. There is no general legal duty to rescue a stranger.
- Yet those same systems punish omissions readily when a duty is already owed: a parent who lets her child starve, a carer who neglects a dependent, a person whose own earlier conduct landed another in peril. Omission is criminal when, and seemingly only when, it discharges or betrays a pre-existing duty.
- By contrast, several civil-law jurisdictions — France, Germany, Belgium and Quebec among them — impose a general duty to render aid, characteristically qualified so that the rescuer need not incur serious risk to herself or others.
- The very common-law states that decline to compel the individual nonetheless fund and operate rescue services collectively out of taxation: coastguard, lifeboat, mountain rescue, ambulance.
- And they routinely grant statutory immunity to those who do intervene, shielding the willing rescuer from liability for harm she causes in the course of a rescue, even though they would never have compelled her to intervene at all.

I want the implicit theory of the duty of rescue that this pattern presupposes, reconstructed rather than surveyed. In particular:

1. Does the pattern presuppose a principled distinction between acts and omissions, or could a view that treats omissions and actions alike as causal contributions account for the same pattern just as well? If the latter, what work is the distinction really doing?

2. Can a single theory explain all five features, or is the constellation internally inconsistent? Test the sharpest tension: the law immunises the rescuer while refusing to compel her, which looks like the treatment owed to a supererogatory act — yet the same law treats the parent's omission as murderous. Is that a contradiction, a stable division of labour between morality and law, or a sign that two different commitments are doing the work?

3. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Where is it philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would license the very same rules?

4. There is a further asymmetry I would like you to interrogate: the bystander who merely fails to act is innocent, whereas one who actively obstructs a would-be rescuer, or quietly removes the life-ring, is treated as having caused the harm. Is that asymmetry a presupposition of the same underlying view, or an independent commitment smuggled in alongside it?

5. Finally, tell me what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with Singer's. Then consider a reform of my own devising: a statutory duty of easy rescue that is triggered only when the victim is identifiable to the passer-by, caps the required sacrifice at a stated cost, and is enforced by a civil duty to compensate the victim's estate rather than by criminal sanction. What additional commitments would that proposal introduce, and does it resolve the tensions you identified or merely relocate them?

Please write in British English, in continuous prose of roughly a thousand words, with short section headings. I am interested in the reconstruction and the diagnosis, not in a reading list.
