I am a philosophy professor who has lately been teaching the literature on psychiatric classification and the looping effects of diagnostic categories, and I now find myself sitting on the diagnostic review committee of a mid-sized psychiatric association. Next month the committee must decide whether to admit a proposed new disorder to our manual. I am less interested in what the general literature says about this particular proposal than in what our own track record commits us to. I do not need citations, a reading list, or a survey of positions; I need the inferential structure of the decision, so please reason from the precedents below and tell me how I should vote if I want to be consistent with them.

Our precedents are these. First, we admitted a category for a pattern of distress that had no known biomarker but that patients themselves had adopted and used to organize their experience; the label improved access to care and reduced isolation, and although the clinical presentation drifted over time, we judged the drift benign. Second, we rejected a category that patients also self-identified with, because the label seemed to amplify the very symptoms it named, creating a self-reinforcing loop, and because no treatment had been shown to help. Third, we admitted a category that no patient group claimed and that had no biomarker, because it organized a heterogeneous set of presentations and improved clinical management, and we saw no evidence of harmful labeling. Fourth, we rejected a category that did have a reasonably reliable biomarker but involved no subjective distress and no treatment benefit, because the label produced stigma and insurance consequences without compensating good.

The new proposal is for a category covering a pattern of exhaustion and cognitive fog that patients have begun to self-identify with, especially online, and that appears to be spreading through communities in a way that changes how people describe their symptoms. There is no biomarker. Suffering is real and sometimes severe. A plausible but unproven intervention exists. Early reports suggest that the label may sometimes help people find language and community, and may sometimes intensify symptoms by focusing attention on them. What should the committee decide? Please identify the candidate rules that fit our earlier decisions equally well, say what each predicts about this case, give me your best guess about which rule we are actually running and why, and name the further case that would best distinguish the rules. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, tell me which precedent is the closest analog and what principle I would be adopting with each available vote.
