You are a philosopher of language with a specialist's grasp of Quine's radical translation, the indeterminacy of translation, and the inscrutability of reference, and of the later work on meaning, rule-following, and interpretation that grew out of them. Work carefully and from first principles, and show your reasoning. Begin by restating my cases in your own words. Then brainstorm two or three candidate principles before committing to one. Then give me a single principle, or a small set of them, stated precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically to a new case. I want the argument, not the literature: no citations, no reading lists, no survey of who said what. If a notion I lean on is confused, say so and repair it rather than dodging around it.

Here is the machinery I am working with, so we need not spend time on setup. In Quine's scenario a field linguist meets a language with no shared history and no bilingual interpreters, and constructs a manual that fits the totality of the natives' utterance and assent/dissent behaviour. Quine's claim is that rival manuals can fit exactly the same total behaviour while disagreeing about what a native word means; the stock example is whether gavagai, uttered as a rabbit scurries past, means rabbit, or undetached rabbit parts, or a momentary temporal slice of a rabbit, or is a holophrastic counterpart of an entire observation sentence. The divergence is supposed to be absorbable: disagreement about the reference of a term can be pushed into the interpretation of the surrounding vocabulary, so that the rival manuals agree on every sentence the natives would assent to or dissent from. There is also the permutation argument, on which any reshuffling of the domain of a theory can be offset by a compensating reinterpretation of its predicates, leaving all truth values intact; and the claim that the indeterminacy is not a shortage of evidence but the absence of anything to be right or wrong about.

Here is my running tally of verdicts, on whether there is a fact of the matter about what a word means.

- Gavagai. No fact of the matter. Each of the four manuals is correct in the only sense worth caring about.
- The arithmetic case. A speaker has only ever added small numbers, and someone proposes that their addition word might really denote a deviant function that agrees with addition on every case the speaker has ever entertained and diverges beyond that. Here I think there is a fact of the matter: the speaker means addition. Their training and habits fix it.
- Plain observation words. When the natives have a word for a scene right in front of them, a rabbit in full view or a colour patch, translation of that word strikes me as about as determinate as anything ever gets. That is the anchor.
- Coarser categories. Suppose the natives have one word covering everything we would split into blue and green, and are never in a position where the difference matters. I think there is a fact of the matter: the word means the coarser category, and a manual that renders it with our word for blue is simply wrong, even though no behaviour tells against it.
- Bilingual informants. If some of the tribe have learned English and tell me their word means rabbit, I think that settles it. They know what they mean.
- My own language. When my neighbour uses a word in ways I find baffling, I still think there is a fact of the matter about what she means by it, even when I cannot recover it. Indeterminacy looks like a feature of exotic cases, not of meaning as such.
- Dead languages. Given only texts and no speakers left, my instinct is that there is still a fact of the matter, and merely less evidence.

Two things I suspect but cannot square. My hunch has been this: where rival manuals agree on the truth value of every sentence, there is no fact of the matter, and where they disagree on some sentence, there is one. That makes gavagai indeterminate, which suits me. But I am told the same reasoning reapplies to the arithmetic case, since addition and the deviant function agree on every sentence the speaker has ever entertained, and that verdict I refuse to give up. So either the hunch is wrong or the arithmetic verdict is. Separately, a disposition-based principle — a fact of the matter is fixed by what speakers would do or say in counterfactual circumstances — saves the arithmetic case but looks like it should also dissolve gavagai, and the coarser-category case and the bilingual-informant case each seem to pull against my gavagai verdict in a different direction.

So: is there a principle, or a small set, that delivers my verdicts, and if not, which verdict should I surrender? For each clause of your principle, tell me which of my cases forces it. Then apply it to a case I have not decided: natives with a word that coincides, across every situation they ever discuss, with a biological kind, but which they treat and teach as a ritual category — is there a fact of the matter about which it means? Then invent one further case of your own that you think would embarrass your own principle, and say whether it defeats the principle or merely bounds it. Finish by naming the single one of my verdicts you are least confident I should keep, and say what would have to be true for me to be right to keep it.
