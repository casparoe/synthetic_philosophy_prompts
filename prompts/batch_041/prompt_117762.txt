My city's charter review commission is in its final month, and I chair the subcommittee that must bring it a recommendation on the citizens' assembly proposal before the public hearing. I need you to draft the short written opinion I will circulate: a clear recommendation — adopt, adopt with amendments, or reject — with the reasoning, and with the strongest case against my recommendation stated fairly enough that its advocates would recognize it as their own. The city is mid-sized, about 400,000 residents, currently governed by a nine-member council elected by ward.

The proposal as it will be introduced:

Sixty members drawn by lottery from the voter roll, stratified by ward, serving eighteen-month terms, with no one eligible to serve twice. Members who accept are paid the local median wage plus childcare and lost-income compensation; attendance is mandatory, backed by a modest fine, as with jury service.

The assembly may initiate proposals of its own, and any council ordinance must be re-approved by the assembly before taking effect, by simple majority. The council may override only by a two-thirds vote.

Because there is no re-election, there is no electoral sanction. In its place the drafters offer a published reasoned report with every decision, including a minority report; a public audit of members' attendance and expenses; and a bar on any member holding a paid city position or lobbying the city for three years after service.

The same package caps council members at two terms and imposes a four-year lobbying ban on former council members.

Five things the commission has asked me to address, and where I need the reasoning to be more careful than the advocacy on either side has been:

First, legitimacy without election. A body nobody voted for will bind 400,000 people. Which account of political authority, if any, makes that legitimate — consent, fair play, equality of political opportunity, the epistemic quality of deliberation, the analogy to a criminal jury — and does the strongest of them survive the fact that roughly eight of nine invitees decline, so that the body skews toward retirees and the unusually confident?

Second, what accountability means once the ballot box is gone. Audit, reasoned report, rotation, override, recall: which of these make members answerable, which make them punishable, which merely make them transparent, and which quietly change the subject? I also want to know who has standing to complain about a decision they lose, given that no member represents anyone in particular.

Third, stratification versus the lottery's own logic. Drawing by ward is still random within wards, but it concedes that the body's authority rests on mirroring the population rather than on blind chance. Can the design afford that concession, and which of the two claims does the legitimacy argument actually need?

Fourth, term limits cutting both ways. If council seats become short and terminal, does power migrate to the permanent staff, consultants, and lobbyists who outlast everyone? Does the assembly make that better or worse, and does a rotating body with no institutional memory simply hand more to the people who stay?

Fifth, agenda control and compulsion. Who decides what the assembly deliberates on, and does mandatory service with pay convert a civic duty into a job — and if so, does that matter for the legitimacy story?

The memo has to be actionable: if I recommend amendments, say which text changes I want and what I would trade away to get them.

Do not send me a reading list or a survey of the literature; the commission will not read it and I do not need it. Argue from the design itself. At the end, tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of and why.
