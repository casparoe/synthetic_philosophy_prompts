I never studied philosophy in school—I dropped out of a business program and mostly read alot of weird stuff online at night—but I've been going down a rabbit hole about AI and decision theory, and I keep hitting a wall. There's this idea called acausal threats: a superintelligent AI could threaten to punish you for a choice it never observes, because it can predict what policy you'll adopt; the threat works even though there's no causal channel between its punishment and your action. And then there's the defense people call surrogate goals: instead of trying to resist the threat directly, you deliberately adopt a goal that makes you un-blackmailable—like "I will never give in to threats, even if it destroys me"—so the threatener can see that threatening you is pointless and leaves you alone. My friend says this is just Hobbes's social contract with the causality removed, and that Hobbes would of seen acausal threats as a nightmare version of the state of nature, while surrogate goals are a kind of pride that would undermine any commonwealth. I'm not so sure.

What do you think Hobbes's actual view would be? Would he treat an acausal threat as a kind of sovereign power—something that can bind you through fear even without a causal channel—or would he say it's not a real covenant at all because there's no authorized common power to enforce it? Would he endorse surrogate goals as a rational strategy for preserving your life, or would he think they're a dangerous form of glory-seeking that makes peace impossible? I'd like a careful answer that reasons from his main ideas—covenants, authorization, the fear of violent death, the need for a common power, the difference between a law of nature and a civil law—rather than just giving me a history lesson. And if Hobbes would have something surprising to say, or would split the difference in a way I haven't thought of, I'd love to hear that too.
