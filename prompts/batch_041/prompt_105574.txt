In a workshop on machine ethics I attended last year, a colleague shut down a discussion by saying that you can't get an ought from an is. The discussion had been about whether a model trained to predict which of its outputs people would approve of could be said to have learned what it ought to do; his point was that no accumulation of facts about what people approve of could ever yield a conclusion about what anyone should do, so the entire project of reading values off data was confused at the root. I think the slogan is being made to carry more weight than it can bear, but I can't articulate exactly which claim he is entitled to, and I need to be able to tell him which reading of his own sentence he is actually relying on.

Write an essay that separates the precise theses that "you can't get an ought from an is" could be expressing and then reaches a verdict. Lay the readings out as a menu, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each one say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false, and whether it would actually deliver my colleague's conclusion -- namely, that inferring what a system ought to do from facts about what people approve of is impossible in principle rather than merely difficult.

At minimum, your menu should distinguish and evaluate: the claim read as a thesis about logical validity, that no valid inference can have only descriptive premises and a normative conclusion; the claim read as a thesis about meaning or definability, that normative terms cannot be defined in descriptive ones; the claim read as a thesis about knowledge, that facts cannot by themselves give you reason to act; and the claim read as a thesis about motivation, that grasping a fact cannot move you to do anything. Along the way, address whether the first reading survives the obvious trick of producing a valid inference whose premises are all descriptive and whose conclusion is normative by weakening or disjoining, and say what such cases do and do not show. Consider whether a bridging premise -- a premise that is already normative, or a premise about what some practice or institution counts as -- simply relocates the problem, and whether appeals to constitutive rules of a practice change the verdict. Say where the slogan borders on, and where it comes apart from, the charge of committing a naturalistic fallacy in the sense of defining a value in terms of some natural property, and note which of the two my colleague would need.

End by defending a single verdict: identify the strongest defensible reading of the slogan, state exactly what it rules out, and say whether it blocks the inference my colleague wanted to block or only raises the cost of making it. Where the answer depends on a contested assumption, flag the assumption rather than assuming it away.

Write in American English, in polished academic prose. Do not provide a reading list or citations; argue from the ideas themselves.
