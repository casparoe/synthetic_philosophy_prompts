I retired last year after thirty-five years in microbial ecology, and I have spent the past eighteen months reading philosophy of science with more enthusiasm than method. I keep returning to the underdetermination of theory by evidence, because it names something I lived with at the bench but never had to state precisely. I have in mind a handful of cases from my own work and from neighboring fields, and I would like you to help me see what principle, if any, underlies my verdicts about them. Please treat me as a serious amateur: I can follow formal distinctions, but I need them explained in plain language, and I am not looking for a reading list or citations.

My verdicts are these. First, when two models of bacterial chemotaxis — one saying the cell compares receptor occupancy over time, the other saying it senses spatial differences across its body — both fit the same capillary-assay trajectories from my old lab, I say the evidence underdetermines the choice, even though I know the spatial model is physically implausible for a cell that small. Second, when a magnetic-map model and an olfactory-map model of bird navigation both fit the same release-and-recapture data, I say the evidence underdetermines the choice, but only weakly, because I think a new experiment could break the tie in principle. Third, when a colleague presents a neutral model and a niche model of species-abundance distributions that fit the same plot, I say the evidence underdetermines the choice, and I am uneasy about that, because the two models seem to disagree about what would happen if I manipulated the environment. Fourth, when someone shows me two formulations of the same enzyme-kinetics model related by a mathematical reparameterization, I say the evidence does not underdetermine anything; there is just one model written twice. Fifth, when a model with extra free parameters is tuned to fit the same data as a simpler model, I say the evidence underdetermines the choice between them, but I also think the simpler model is better supported — and I cannot tell whether that is a contradiction or a distinction. A young colleague has proposed that we adopt an 'evidential symmetry audit': before accepting any model, list every rival that fits the same data and assign them equal credence until an intervention breaks the symmetry. I find this proposal both admirable and absurd, and I want to know which of my verdicts it would force me to give up.

Please act as a philosopher of science with expertise in underdetermination, scientific inference, and the epistemology of experiment. Think carefully about the cases before answering. You may brainstorm privately, but in your final answer give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no headings. Each item should be a sentence or two. In item 1, state the single best principle you can find that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could use it to classify a new case. In item 2, identify the case that motivates its central clause and explain why. In item 3, identify the case that forces you to add a qualification or a second clause. In item 4, say what your principle predicts for a case I have not mentioned: two models that fit the same data but differ in whether they posit a hidden entity that is, by hypothesis, permanently unobservable. In item 5, tell me which of my five verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should abandon that verdict or abandon the principle.
