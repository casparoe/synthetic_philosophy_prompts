Act as a philosopher of mind and philosophical psychologist specializing in self-deception. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the phenomena in the area before you commit to a list.

I want a specification of what any adequate theory of self-deception has to deliver. Give me the desiderata — the cases it must get right, each with the consideration behind it: someone who sincerely avows a belief their own evidence tells against; someone who manages that evidence by avoiding or reframing it rather than confronting it; a subject who seems to be at once the deceiver and the deceived; wishful thinking that falls short of self-deception; and a person who is self-deceived about whether they are self-deceived at all.

The list should also cover the distinctions the theory cannot collapse (self-deception versus weakness of will, self-control, other-deception, wishful thinking, and mere ignorance or error; believing something versus merely acting as if; the deceptive state versus the process that produces it), the two paradoxes it has to dissolve — the static one, that to deceive myself I must know the truth yet to be deceived I must not, and the dynamic one, that deception is an intentional project whose intended result is a state of which I am unaware — and any findings from empirical psychology it must not contradict.

Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive theories; and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of particular camps (intentionalist versus deflationary, conscious versus unconscious, gradable versus all-or-nothing).
