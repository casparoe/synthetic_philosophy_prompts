A group of us have been having a long-running argument, mostly over a group chat, about psychiatry and coercion. It started when one friend, who is a psychiatry resident, described a commitment hearing she had sat in on, and another friend, who reads a great deal of popular science and is rather more confident than his reading warrants, announced that the whole institution rests on a mistake, namely that mental illness does not exist. He says he assembled his view from a chapter in a history-of-psychiatry book and a lecture he only half remembers, and he has now written it up for the rest of us. His version runs roughly like this. Szasz was a twentieth-century psychiatrist who argued that mental illness is a myth: the mind is not a bodily organ, so there is nothing there to be diseased, and the entire vocabulary of psychiatric diagnosis is a metaphor that has been mistaken for a literal claim. What the people so labeled actually have are problems in living; they are unhappy or behave badly, and nothing is really wrong with them in the way that something is really wrong with a person who has cancer. Since the diagnosis is not a medical fact but a moral judgment dressed up as one, psychiatry is a branch of social control rather than medicine, and Szasz therefore opposed psychiatric treatment, together with the involuntary commitment that goes with it, on the view that a person diagnosed with schizophrenia is not genuinely suffering from anything but only from other people's disapproval. My friend's conclusion is that Szasz has been decisively refuted by the evidence that psychosis runs in families and responds to medication, and that his position was a political slogan rather than a serious contribution to the philosophy of medicine.

I suspect this is unfair in ways I cannot name. My impression is that Szasz was himself a practicing psychoanalyst and something of a libertarian, which makes me doubt that he opposed treatment as such, and I have a vague sense that he was making a claim about a concept rather than about patients. But I cannot reconstruct the argument, and I certainly cannot say which parts of the summary he would have accepted and which he would have regarded as a caricature. I would like you to audit the summary claim by claim: tell me what a careful reader of Szasz would accept, what he would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong, and then say whether my friend's conclusion follows from whatever survives correction. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually know the philosophy of psychiatry — for instance, whether a disease concept has to be grounded in a bodily lesion, whether his political conclusions follow from his conceptual one, and whether the notion of problems in living can bear the weight he places on it — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. I would rather have a calibrated answer that tells me where the crux lies than a verdict, since I expect to keep arguing about this for years.

Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the way one would write a long email to a friend who is wrong about something and worth correcting.
