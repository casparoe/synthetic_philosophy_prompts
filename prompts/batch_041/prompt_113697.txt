An argument about illusionism concerning phenomenal consciousness that I have been reconstructing, and that I cannot fault though I reject its conclusion:

(1) Illusionism asserts that phenomenal consciousness does not exist; what we take to be experiences with a phenomenal character are introspective representations that misrepresent their subject as possessing phenomenal properties.
(2) A state is an illusion only if, in that state, it seems to the subject that things are a certain way.
(3) Whenever it seems to a subject that things are a certain way, the subject is in a state possessing phenomenal character: there is something it is like to be in that state.
(4) Therefore the introspective states the illusionist calls illusions of phenomenal consciousness themselves possess phenomenal character.
(5) Every state possessing phenomenal character is a state of phenomenal consciousness.
(6) Therefore illusionism is committed to the existence of the very thing it denies, and is incoherent.

I am fairly confident the reasoning is valid, so one of (1) through (5) must be given up, and I would rather not surrender (3): I cannot see how anything could seem to a subject to be a certain way without its being like something for that subject. Yet rejecting (3) is the move I keep being pushed toward, and I do not understand how to make it work without quietly smuggling phenomenality back in. Identify the premise or step that should be rejected, explain why it looks compelling, and price the alternatives. In particular, can the illusionist unpack a state's seeming to its subject to involve a phenomenal property in a way that attributes no phenomenal character to the state itself, and if so, does that reading of (2) leave the argument valid or expose an equivocation?
