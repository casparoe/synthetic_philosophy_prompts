I spent forty years at the bench in plant physiology and my retirement reading philosophy, which is how I became the uneasy third party in a weekly seminar where two friends have now argued for a year about whether an omission can cause anything. I want them to produce the document a genuine adversarial collaboration would yield, and I am asking you to draft it.

One of them holds that causation is difference-making: an event or absence is a cause of an outcome just when the outcome counterfactually depends on it — had the drug been given, the patient would have lived — so a failure to act stands in the same causal relation to a death as a positive intervention does. The other holds that causation is production: a cause is a spatiotemporally located event that brings its effect about through a concrete process, and since absences have no location and do nothing, nothing can be caused by one; our readiness to say that an omission caused a death records what explains or what we may blame, not what produced what.

The document should contain, first, a numbered list of claims both could sign: about what stands in need of explanation, about which cases any account must classify, and about what each would count as a mistake in the other's position. Then the residual disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept, in a table whose third column holds the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. For every crux, the case or argument both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance, specific enough to be checked later.

The cases should be sorted under a shared taxonomy rather than taken one at a time: ordinary positive causation; the prevention of a process already under way; a bare omission; the case in which one event prevents a preventer, so that what looks like a cause has merely removed an obstacle; and a case in which two separate omissions are each on its own sufficient for the outcome, so that neither satisfies the strictest counterfactual test. My former field supplies two more that I care about. A sailor on a long voyage with no fruit develops scurvy, and the deficiency is a textbook cause — yet what goes wrong in the body can be described entirely in positive terms, and I cannot get the two of them to agree on whether that redescription refutes the first position or merely replaces one true causal claim with another. And a seedling grown in the dark becomes etiolated, pale and spindly: there the absence of a stimulus is standardly treated as the input to a developmental pathway.

I would also like the document to say whether part of this dispute is verbal — whether the two are describing one relation under two names or two relations under one name — and, if one of the positions has been qualified until nothing could count against it, to say so and to say whether that is a defect. I confess a lingering sympathy for the second position, since every effect I spent a career tracing was produced by something: a hormone, an enzyme, a photon. But I distrust that sympathy as a prejudice of method. Take definite positions throughout, including on the cases above; do not lay the options out and leave the choice to me, since that is what the seminar already does.
