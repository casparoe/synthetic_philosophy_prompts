Consider a spectrum of options, O1, O2, …, On, arranged so that each option differs from its immediate neighbours only slightly — for instance, a small increase in the number of people who exist alongside a small decrease in the quality of each life, or a tiny dilution of some good spread across a very large population. Suppose that, judged pairwise, each option seems at least as good as the next one along the spectrum, and yet the two endpoints seem clearly ordered the other way, with O1 better than On. This is the pattern that generates the most familiar arguments against the transitivity of "better than." Please give an intuitive explanation of why this pattern poses a genuine conceptual problem for transitivity, rather than merely reflecting our uncertainty about a long series of comparisons.

Your explanation should do four things. First, say why transitivity is normally treated as close to definitional for a comparative relation such as "better than," and what would be lost if it failed — for individual deliberation, for interpersonal or population aggregation, and for the very idea of "maximising." Second, distinguish at least three structurally different responses to the spectrum (for example: rejecting the pairwise judgements at each step; rejecting transitivity itself; or rejecting the comparability of the endpoints) and state, for each, which other commitment it forces us to abandon. Third, introduce and assess a distinction of your own between local transitivity (holding for triples whose members sit close together on the spectrum) and global transitivity (holding for the whole chain), and say honestly whether this distinction resolves the problem or merely restates it. Fourth, be explicit throughout about which formal properties of "better than" — irreflexivity, asymmetry, transitivity, completeness — each proposed fix would require us to give up, and whether the resulting relation could still support a defensible account of rational choice.

Write for a reader who is willing to follow a long chain of reasoning: reconstruct the argument so that it can be checked step by step, rather than surveying who has defended which position.
