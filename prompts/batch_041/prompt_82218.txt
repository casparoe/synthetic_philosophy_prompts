Im doing a piece — long-ish, for a magazine that shall remain nameless until they actually commission it — on Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust, and I want to build it around the Wannsee meeting rather than around the camps. Jan 20, 1942, Heydrich in the chair, Eichmann taking the minutes, a room full of ministry people and SS officers, and what survives reads like an interagency meeting about a resettlement program: numbers by country, transport capacity, who has jurisdiction over mixed marriages, what counts as a case that needs to be handled "evacuated to the east." Nobody in that room writes anything that sounds like a death sentence, and that's sort of the point.

Here's what I need, and it is not a summary of Bauman — I've read the book, I know the line about modernity producing the Holocaust rather than merely failing to prevent it, the bureaucratic rationality, the division of labour, the victims reduced to a category, the whole thing as an extreme case of a modern society trying to get rid of what doesnt fit (his garden-and-gardener thing, or am I misremembering that part?). What I want is the actual mapping onto this one episode, and then the places where the mapping fails.

So: what does his machinery predict about a meeting like Wannsee? What should we expect to find in that room, in that document, in the way the agenda is ordered, in who gets invited and who doesn't — and do we find it, or is the protocol doing something his theory didn't anticipate? Then the failures. Two things bother me. First, the shooting in the east was already well underway by the time they sat down in that villa, and it was done at close range, face to face, by men who could see exactly who they were killing. If moral distance and the invisibility of the victim are carrying the explanatory weight, what do we do with killers who had no distance at all? Second, ideology: does his account need antisemitism as an input, or is the bureaucracy supposed to generate the goal on its own? A theory that says any modern state with a big enough administrative apparatus could do this is saying something quite different from one that says this particular modern state had this particular fantasy about Jews, and I want to know which one he's actually holding.

And then the part I keep going back and forth on. Does Bauman's "normal" quietly put him on the comparability side of the Historikerstreit? Nolte's move was to make the Nazi crimes comparable — a reaction to something, continuous with other mass violence — and Habermas's reply was that the comparison functions as apologetics. Bauman says the Holocaust is a normal product of modernity, which sounds like the same move and I suspect isn't. Tell me what the difference is, if there is one, and whether his uniqueness claim (he does have one, I think) survives the comparison he's making. And tell me where the theory is just silent — features of Wannsee that Bauman's frame has nothing to say about and shouldn't be forced to.

Last thing: give me your verdict on which reading of him is right, and be explicit about what would change your mind — which argument or piece of evidence would move you off it, and where you think your reading is most likely to be wrong. And dont give me a bibliography, I can find the book.
