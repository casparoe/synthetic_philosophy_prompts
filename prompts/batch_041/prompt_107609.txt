OK, so, context: I'm a retired field biologist — thirty-odd years counting seabirds on various godforsaken islands — and since I hung up the binoculars I've been reading philosophy, which has been humbling. I'm trying to write something and I could use your help.

A friend put a distinction to me recently that I can't stop thinking about. He said some explanations are skyhooks: they do the heavy lifting without themselves ever having been lifted — the hard part just shows up already solved, no scaffolding underneath, nothing that had to be built first. And some explanations are cranes: the thing doing the lifting was itself assembled, step by step, out of bits that were already lying around, each bit doing a small honest job. Then he warned me about a third thing he called greedy reductionism — where you're so set on explaining some grand phenomenon in terms of small dumb parts that you end up explaining it away. You account for every piece and lose the pattern that made anyone care.

Here's what I want. A short story — a few pages, real characters, a real place, not an essay in a trenchcoat — that puts all three of these in play. Please don't have anybody in it explain the concepts; I want to watch them happen instead. So: somebody is staring at something genuinely impressive (a living thing, a machine, a mind, a society, a piece of behavior — your call) and the skyhook reading of it is seductive, maybe even a comfort. Somebody else keeps pointing at a crane. And a third person is the greedy reductionist, who thinks the crane story proves the impressive thing was never there to begin with. I don't want the ending to just declare the crane people the winners. I want the reader to feel why the skyhook pulls at us, and also to feel what the reductionist has thrown away.

Also, please don't reach for the obvious examples. No watch found on the heath, no eye, no beehive. Surprise me — set it somewhere I wouldn't have thought of, maybe even somewhere that isn't Earth.

Oh — and if you can make the greedy reductionist a little sympathetic, rather than a strawman, I'd definately like that. I was probably one for a lot of my career and didn't notice.
