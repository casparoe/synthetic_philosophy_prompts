My brother and I have been arguing for months about something that started out dumb and turned out not to be. I stopped opening a drawer of mail for about six months — bills, one letter from the hospital — and just didn't think about it. He says I was deceiving myself. I say I never believed anything false, I just never looked, and the not-looking was the whole thing. He says that's a dodge: if you arrange your life so you never have to find something out, you know it somewhere.

So write the joint statement of an adverserial collaboration between the two camps. Camp one: self-deception is real — a person can hold a belief their evidence is against, because they want it to be true. Camp two, the deflationists: that's an incoherent state, you can't both know and not know, so every genuine case is really something else — strategic avoidance of inquiry, plain ignorance, or a change of belief with no contradiction in it. Don't pick a winner; write the document both could sign.

I want numbered claims both sides accept: what the phenomenon is, what actually goes on in the disputed cases, what's true by definition versus substantive, and whether part of this fight is just about words (whether self-deception is a success term, say). Then the disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording. Then the cruxes. I think one is whether never-looking and confronted-with-it-and-still-denying are the same phenomenon, and whether there has to be something in the head that represents the unwelcome truth for it to count as self-deception at all. Another is whether the deflationist can explain why the avoidance is selective — why people manage not to look at exactly the things they'd rather not know — without smuggling in a motive that already knows what it's avoiding. For each crux give the case or test both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Use my mail drawer as one of the worked cases if it helps.
