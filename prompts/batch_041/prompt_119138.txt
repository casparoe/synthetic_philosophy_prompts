I'm assembling the icebreakers for a graduate reading group on decision theory under imperfect recall, and I need one joke about the absent-minded driver. The audience will know the problem, so a punchline that trades only on "and then he forgot again" will die on the table; the humour has to come out of the structure of the case. To fix the version I have in mind: the driver faces two intersections and cannot tell which of them he is at; exiting at the first earns him nothing, exiting at the second earns him four, and overshooting both earns him one. I already know the standard result — his best policy from before the trip is genuinely mixed, continuing with probability two-thirds for an expected payoff of four-thirds, strictly better than either pure policy — so please don't spend the joke re-deriving that or explaining it to me. What I want it to turn on is the awkwardness underneath: that this best-from-the-armchair policy is one he would repudiate on the spot if he ever learned where he was, since at the second intersection pressing on is plainly worse than leaving, and yet he can never use that fact, because his condition consists precisely in never being told whether the repudiation applies.

Write it as a short scene with dialogue, and give the driver someone to talk to: a passenger who is not himself absent-minded, so that the driver is not merely alone with his confusion. Let the passenger offer every sensible suggestion a helpful person would offer — that they have already passed one turn-off, that the next one is the one they want, that the driver should simply pick a rule and hold to it — and let the joke show why none of this is usable from the driver's seat, without turning him into a fool or the passenger into a sage. If it helps, the passenger can be the sort of person who has read the theory and keeps insisting on the mixing policy, which is exactly the advice the driver cannot act on in the moment; or the passenger's intact memory can simply dissolve the problem, in which case that dissolution is the joke. Either ending is fine, so long as the humour comes from the structure rather than from the mere fact of confusion.

The punchline should land on the gap between the policy that is best before the trip and the choice that is best at the wheel, not on forgetfulness as such; a narrated moral at the end would spoil it. A dozen lines or fewer, please, and no citations, no names of papers or authors, and no appeals to who first posed the problem — I want the joke to stand on the case alone, and to be funny to people who know the problem well.
