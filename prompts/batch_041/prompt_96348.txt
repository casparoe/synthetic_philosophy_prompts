I am a staff writer at a general-interest magazine that covers science and ideas, and I am commissioning the parody slot for our annual year-in-review issue. Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher of decision theory who has also written for consumer-product review outlets, and work deliberately: before drafting, spend a few sentences thinking in the open — the candidate positions, the standard objections to each, and which of them your piece will need — and then write the piece. The brainstorm is for my notes and should not appear in the copy.

What I want is a deadpan buyer's guide — the sober, testing-obsessed roundup format a review site publishes, with a headline, a deck, a short "how we chose" paragraph, an entry for each product tested, a runner-up, an upgrade pick, an "also consider" sidebar, and a closing "the bottom line" — except that the products under review are rival decision procedures, and each one is being assessed as a policy for what to do when a stranger makes an astronomically improbable threat or promise whose expected value, taken at face value, is enormous. The thought experiment being riffed on is the one usually called Pascal's mugging: a stranger threatens something terrible on a scale so vast, at a probability so small, that the product of the two dwarfs any ordinary cost of compliance, so that expected-value maximization appears to say: comply. The comedy should come from our test kitchen treating this as a consumer-electronics question, and it will only work if the underlying structure is exactly right.

Please build the review around five standardized muggers that the testing protocol ran against every candidate procedure. Name them as you like, but the five cases should be:

(a) the plain mugger — a finite but astronomical harm at a fantastically small probability;
(b) the infinite mugger — a harm that is genuinely infinite, or an eternity of something merely mildly unpleasant, where the expected value is infinite or undefined rather than merely huge; the piece must register that this is a different problem from (a), not a bigger version of it;
(c) the crowd of muggers — many mutually incompatible threats, each of which the same arithmetic says must be satisfied, so that complying with any one of them commits you to ruin;
(d) the reverse mugger — the same vanishing probability, but of an enormous reward rather than a harm, which is the wager-shaped version of the case;
(e) the unnamed mugger — a threat whose probability is never specified, only asserted to be nonzero, which raises whether "maximize expected value" delivers a verdict at all when no distribution is on the table.

Each entry should report how the procedure fared in the register of a review — "we found", "in our testing", "the flaw is forgivable" — and should be accurate about which objection it is illustrating. At least one entry must be the procedure of simply complying with the mugger: grade it the way a reviewer grades a gadget that performs exactly as advertised and is nonetheless unusable, and let the reader see why without your saying it. The candidates should span the real range of positions — refusing on principle, bounding or discounting utility, paying, and denying that the question is well-posed — though you need not announce which is which.

Three things have to land without any lecturing: why compliance is supposed to follow from expected-value maximization; why the crowd-of-muggers case turns that conclusion into a reductio, since the same reasoning licenses countless incompatible wagers; and why some theorists hold that the plain mugger's threat has no well-defined expected value, so the question is malformed rather than merely difficult. Include at least one customer testimonial from an invented person, one "flaw but forgivable" verdict, and one sidebar entry that treats the mugger's own motive as a product-spec question — why would a being capable of this need your wallet?

House rules: no citations, no reading list, no names of real living philosophers, no quotations from real texts, and no winking. The irony has to be carried entirely by the deadpan, so that a reader who knows the literature can tell the author understands it, while a reader who does not still follows every joke. Around 800 words.
