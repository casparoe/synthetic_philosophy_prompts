You are a philosopher of the Chinese tradition specializing in the Warring States period, with particular expertise in the Mencius–Xunzi controversy over human nature and in the interpretive debates surrounding the concept of xing. I would like you to help me think through a problem with real care. Please begin by brainstorming: before you commit to any verdict, lay out for yourself the full range of things the relevant claims could be taken to mean.

Here is the situation. I belong to an interdisciplinary reading group on human nature and institutions. One member, a political scientist, habitually compresses the Mencius–Xunzi disagreement into a pair of slogans: Mencius taught that human nature is good, Xunzi that it is bad. He then builds a policy argument on that pair. Because Mencius supposedly held that people are naturally disposed to care for one another, he argues, a generous welfare state can rest on spontaneous solidarity; Xunzi's darker estimate of our nature is supposed to justify instead an apparatus of incentives, laws, and deliberately manufactured norms. My suspicion is that the slogans are doing far less work than he takes them to do, and perhaps different work, but I cannot yet articulate exactly why.

I want from you a disambiguation laid out as a menu rather than a single exposition. For each of the two slogans — that human nature is good and that it is bad — enumerate the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. In doing so, attend to the several senses the key terms might carry: what xing is here said to be (innate desires, spontaneous tendencies, native capacities, or a developmental endowment); what good and bad are predicated of (actual conduct, motivational structure, unrealized potential, or the fit between our nature and the normative order); and whether the claim is meant as descriptive, predictive, or in part normative. For each reading, state whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and explain why. Where a reading turns on a contested interpretation, say so and give the strongest case on either side.

Then tell me which of these readings would actually license my colleague's policy inference and which would not, and if the inference requires a reading that neither thinker would have accepted, say so plainly. You need not supply a bibliography or citations; I want the argument, not the reading list, and where a matter is genuinely contested among interpreters, describe the disagreement in your own words rather than gesturing at sources. Precision matters more here than completeness, and you should flag any point at which you are uncertain rather than papering over it.
