I work on the infrastructure team at a company that builds clinical decision-support software, and I run a small reading group that meets after work. Two of the regulars have been arguing for about three months, and I would like to get their dispute into a shape where we can actually see what is at stake.

The subject is the old killing-versus-letting-die question, but it keeps getting concrete in a way that is specific to what we build: our product ships defaults. At some point somebody decides whether the system escalates or stays quiet, alerts or doesn't, blocks a prescription or lets it through. Years later a patient is harmed, the person who set the parameter has moved to another team, and nobody can agree on whether anyone killed anybody.

Here are the two positions, as fairly as I can put them.

The first, which I will call the causal-shape view, holds that the difference between doing and allowing is a fact about the causal structure of the situation rather than about anyone's duties. When an agent does something, the harm depends on her intervention: remove her and it does not happen. When she merely allows, the harm was already on its way and she declined to interpose. The attraction is that the distinction comes out objective and independent of who is judging; the cost is that it is hard to say why such a structural fact should carry moral weight, and the view seems forced to say that choosing a default is a doing, which turns "we only left it on the default" into a confession rather than a defense.

The second, the commitments view, holds that there is no interesting causal difference at all. Any doing can be redescribed as an allowing and the reverse, depending on the baseline you pick, and causing something by omission is as real as causing it by action. What distinguishes the cases is the agent's normative position: what she has taken on, what role she occupies, what she has led people to expect, what she owes. On this account the default cases are interesting only because defaults are where expectations and roles get frozen, not because a default alters the shape of anything. The cost here is that the killing/letting-die line becomes hostage to convention, and it is hard to say what is wrong with an agent who has taken on nothing.

I would like the two of them to produce the document an adversarial collaboration is supposed to produce, a joint statement both of them could sign. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both positions can sign. These need to be substantive, not "harm is bad" or "context matters." They should cover, at a minimum, what both sides can agree the default cases do and do not show, and which of the standard moves in this argument are dead ends.

2. The points on which they still disagree, each written in wording that the side it belongs to would accept as its own, and in a form that does not smuggle in the other side's assumptions. If part of the dispute turns out to be about words rather than substance, identify that part and say why.

3. The cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I expect a crux about baselines (what it means for the harm to have happened anyway) and one about whether causing by omission is a real relation or a conventional one, but do not let my guesses constrain the list.

4. For each crux, a case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance and, where it makes sense, the observation each side would count as telling against its own position. At least one crux should be tested against our default cases, and I want three of them kept distinct, because they have been running together in the argument: (a) a default that someone deliberately chose; (b) a default that nobody ever chose, where the behavior comes from whatever the code falls back to when the parameter is unset; and (c) a default set by one team and inherited, unexamined, by another. If both positions make the same prediction about all three, say so, and say what that shows.

Two constraints. First, no citations and no reading list: the group is offline, I have no philosophy training, and I would rather have the reasoning than the references, so nothing should lean on who said what. Second, resist hedging everything into "it depends"; where a side has to commit to a prediction in order to be interesting, make it commit. And if there is a point both of them should accept as decisive against one of the two positions, put it in, because I would rather find that out than preserve a false symmetry.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a section of disagreement statements, then a table with one row per crux: the crux, the shared case or argument, the prediction from the causal-shape side, and the prediction from the commitments side.
