Write a short story that stages the integrity objection to consequentialist morality — the charge, associated with Bernard Williams, that an agent moved by love or loyalty, who then appends a justifying thought to the effect that the act was, all things considered, for the best, has already betrayed the very commitment that moved them, because that justification is one thought too many. The story should make a reader feel the force of the objection rather than be told it.

The story must satisfy the following constraints.

1. Invent a concrete, non-generic situation. Do not reuse the standard rescue cases (the drowning spouse weighed against strangers, the coerced execution, the hospital that can save five by sacrificing one). Find a setting in which the arithmetic of the greater good genuinely runs against the person the protagonist loves, and in which the protagonist's act is costly, unrehearsed, and unglamorous.

2. The protagonist is not a philosopher and does not use the vocabulary of ethics. The objection must be carried by the plot, the pacing, and the prose, not by any character's commentary or by the narrator's judgment.

3. Include a frame that separates the deed from its justification. Some time after the act, the protagonist must give reasons for it to an institution — for example a claims office that reimburses emergency rescues, a review board, or a committee deciding whether to renew a licence. The institution should not be villainous or satirical; it should be procedurally reasonable, even humane. Its demand is what converts the protagonist's commitment into an item to be weighed.

4. The pivotal beat is the moment the justifying thought arrives unbidden — in the protagonist's own voice, at the moment of action or immediately after — and the protagonist notices themselves thinking it. Render that noticing as a small estrangement, not as guilt and not as a lesson. The reader should feel the thought arrive as an intruder that the protagonist cannot unthink.

5. Do not resolve the tension. No epiphany, no closing moral, no narrator who knows which attitude is correct. A reader should be able to reconstruct the objection from the events alone.

6. Aim for roughly 1,500–2,500 words.

After the story, add a short note of under 200 words that (a) identifies the exact sentence or beat where the objection is dramatized, and (b) states honestly what this story cannot capture — for instance, that the objection is ordinarily pressed as an argument about the structure of a moral theory, whereas a story can only show the experience of one person, and that the reader's residual sympathy for the reasonable institution is itself part of the difficulty.
