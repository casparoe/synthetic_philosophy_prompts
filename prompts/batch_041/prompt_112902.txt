I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for a long time — long enough that my instinct with any concept is to ask what work it does rather than what it is, and long enough to have a soft spot for the eighteenth-century version of that instinct. Bishop Berkeley's The Querist (1735) is my favourite unread book: an economic tract on Irish poverty, published anonymously in three parts, written entirely as a numbered list of questions put to the public, with not one of them answered. The doctrine underneath is bracing, and I think it is continuous with how the good posts now talk about money. Money is not wealth but a claim on wealth — Berkeley's phrase for its true idea is that it is "a ticket or counter"; the real source of wealth is the earth and men's labour in it; money earns its keep only by stirring up and circulating that labour; and what men actually want, underneath, is power, of which money is one instrument. His first query asks whether there ever was an industrious nation poor, or an idle rich. The whole thing is an argument conducted in interrogatives, and the form is not decoration: asking rather than asserting hands the conclusion to the reader, who can hardly refuse a question whose answer is obvious.

I want a poem built on that.

Write a sequence of twelve to eighteen numbered queries. Every sentence in the piece must be a question — no assertions anywhere, including the title, which should be the title of a pamphlet, in your own words rather than Berkeley's. Number the queries as though they had been lifted from a much longer run: begin well past 1, and stop in the middle of the sequence, so the reader feels the interrogation continuing off the page. Lean on the whether-clause, his signature cadence, but do not open every query the same way, and do not write fake-archaic pastiche — let the period live in the form and keep the diction alive.

The queries should be doing the work of the argument rather than illustrating it. Let them move roughly as his do: from what the coin is in itself, to what it merely stands for, to whose labour it commands, to who ends up holding very little of it — and end on the question the sequence cannot answer, the one about power. I would like the questions to hang together as a chain, each one getting its footing from the one after it, the way a ticket gets its value from something not present. A few should be answerable on the spot, so that the reader feels the pull to answer out loud; the last should be one nobody can. Someone who knows Berkeley's economics should recognize the ticket thesis, the labour theory of wealth, credit as circulation, and power as the real end; someone who has never heard of him should still find it a good poem. It should not read like a summary wearing question marks.

Then add a section titled "On the Queries", of about two hundred words, explaining the choices: how the sequence moves and where its turn is, what the numbering is doing, which bits are Berkeley and which are yours, and where you think the poem falls short of him.
