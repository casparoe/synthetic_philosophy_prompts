I'm a graduate student in a fortnightly seminar that pairs early modern metaphysics with the history of economic thought, and next month I'm the one opening our session on Berkeley's *The Querist*. I thought I had the book's monetary argument under control until a colleague remarked, more or less in passing, that if money is really a ticket or counter then Berkeley has no business treating an arbitrary change in the denomination of coin as a fraud: tickets can be reprinted at will, and a counter that has been relabelled is still a counter. I can see what she means, and I can also see that Berkeley seems to hold both claims at once. What I want from you is not a survey of the secondary literature, a reading list, or citations — I would rather you work from the passage itself — but a patient reading of the following queries and, above all, of the connections among them:

> 5. Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?
> 6. Whether any other means, equally conducing to excite and circulate the industry of mankind, may not be as useful as money.
> 20. Whether the creating of wants be not the likeliest way to produce industry in a people? And whether, if our peasants were accustomed to eat beef and wear shoes, they would not be more industrious?
> 21. Whether other things being given, as climate, soil, etc., the wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?
> 23. Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?
> 24. Whether the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty?
> 25. Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?
> 26. Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?
> 28. Whether arbitrary changing the denomination of coin be not a public cheat?

The knot I keep failing to untie is what the ticket-or-counter comparison is actually claiming. Is it a thesis about the essence of money, about the conditions under which money functions, or only about the unit of account? In query 25, what exactly is the referent of "such proportion" — is it the demand-and-plenty proportion of query 24, and if so, does calling the currency terms "exponents or denominations" of a proportion amount to a coherent position or merely to a metaphor that has been pushed too far? In query 21, credit appears to circulate independently of any particular mark or token, which makes money sound like one optional instrument among others; I would like to know whether queries 23 and 25 narrow that claim or merely restate it. And in query 26, once the bullion is gone, what is left to anchor the denominations, on Berkeley's own terms, such that their arbitrary alteration in query 28 can still be a "public cheat" rather than a harmless renaming — is the wrong done a deception, a breach of convention, an injury to creditors, or something else again? I would also like to know what independent work the excitation-of-industry strand in queries 5 and 6 is doing alongside the ticket metaphor, and whether the appeal to creating wants in query 20 fits that strand or quietly conflicts with it.

Finally, please flag the interpretive temptations a reader in my position is likely to fall into: which modern accounts of money the passage genuinely licenses, which it merely resembles, and whether Berkeley's idealism about bodies has any bearing on the monetary argument at all or is simply a separate project that *The Querist* does not need. Where the text underdetermines an answer, say so, and distinguish what the queries commit Berkeley to from what a sympathetic reconstruction might add on his behalf.
