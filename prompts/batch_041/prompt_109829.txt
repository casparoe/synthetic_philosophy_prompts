I'm setting the take-home paper for my final-year philosophy of biology option and I need one more question on free-floating rationales, plus a model answer and a marking scheme my two co-markers can actually use. The question is worth 20 marks and they get 90 minutes. About half the cohort are zoologists taking the philosophy option, so the model answer shouldn't assume they've read much beyond the set texts.

My problem with the questions I've already written is that a student can hoover up most of the marks by reciting Dennett's distinction plus Millikan on proper functions and never once have to think. So I want teh crux to be a fork they have to actually take a side on, and I've made up a case to hang it on, so nobody can pull a stock example off the shelf. Here's the case; use it, or tidy the wording, but keep the shape:

A made-up alpine beetle. Its larvae cluster in groups of about a dozen in the shade of leaves. It turns out a dozen is exactly the group size that minimises the risk from a parasitic wasp that hunts them — smaller groups lose the dilution effect, bigger ones get noticed. But the clustering is a by-product of a humidity-seeking reflex: the larvae pile up wherever the air is damp, and damp patches happen to be about the right size for a dozen of them. The wasp only reached this valley three beetle generations ago, so nothing has been selected for wasp-avoidance at all, and nothing in the beetle represents wasps, risk, or group size. Nobody, in Dennett's sense, has the reason.

The fork I want them to take: is the free-floatingness of a free-floating rationale a claim about representation, or a claim about selection history? On the first reading the larvae already have a rationale — an optimal strategy, evaluable as better or worse — and what's missing is only a vehicle that represents it; so free-floating rationales are cheap and crop up wherever there's an optimum. On the second reading, no selection, no rationale: the rationale has to be built by selection before there's anything for it to float free of, and my beetle is just a humidity reflex that happens to sit at an optimum, with no fact of the matter about whether it's doing the right thing for the right reason. They have to pick one and defend it, and say what work the word rationale is doing in their answer that a plain functional description wouldn't do. I'd also like the question to squeeze the indeterminacy point: if you take the first reading, why is the humidity-optimality description any less the rationale than the parasitism-optimality one?

Constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations — they've got no internet and I don't want them guessing at page numbers. Naming Dennett or Millikan is fine if it earns its keep. Keep the model answer to about 650 words, in the voice of a very good candidate, and make it reach a verdict — don't let it end on a shrug about both readings having merit. Marking scheme: five must-hit points, 4 marks each, 20 total, and for each point a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it. Add two or three lines on what seperates a 17-20 answer from a 13-16 one, since that's what my co-markers always squabble about.

One thing I want in the model answer itself: a short passage near the end saying which way it would go if I changed the case — say, if the wasp had been in the valley for ten thousand generations and the clustering had been shaped by it, or if the reflex itself were sensitive to wasp scent — and being honest about the step it's least confident in. If the verdict only survives because of one dodgy move, I'd rather it said so than papered over it.
