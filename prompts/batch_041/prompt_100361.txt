I've been defending a version of intellectualism: knowing how to do something is knowing a proposition, but under a practical mode of presentation. The twist is that I don't want the practical mode to be a special inner attitude or a phenomenal feel. I want it fixed functionally: a representation counts as practical when it plays the right role in guiding and correcting performance within a practice, including the agent's disposition to treat certain failures as mistakes rather than surprises. So a skilled cellist, a fluent speaker and a good chess player all know facts, but the facts are held in a practical register.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including cases where an agent reliably performs but cannot state any true proposition that captures what they know, cases where they state the right proposition but still can't perform, and cases from animal or pre-linguistic skill. What would a defender of this view have to concede counts against it? Does the functional twist make the view unfalsifiable, or does it just relocate the empirical bet?
