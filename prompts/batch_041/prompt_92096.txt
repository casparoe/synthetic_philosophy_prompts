I've ended up defending a pretty uncompromising version of prioritarianism in a seminar and I want to stress-test it before I commit it to a paper. The version I hold: what makes an outcome better or worse for a person is just their absolute level of well-being, full stop -- the fact that someone is worse off than someone else matters only because being worse off is bad for them, not because inequality is bad in itself. So making the better off worse off while helping no one is a pure loss no matter how much more equal it leaves things; I think the leveling-down objection is fatal to straight egalitarianism, and that's basically why I'm a prioritarian. But I hold the strong form: absolute priority to improving the worst off, with a priority weighting that's continuous and strictly decreasing in a person's level -- no threshold, no lexical cutoff where the worse off just win by default no matter how trivial their gain or how large the loss to others. I also want the view population-invariant, and indifferent to how well-being is spread among people who are at the same level. Two cases I keep chewing on: (a) one person at 10, another at 1000, and I can either give the first a gain of 1 or give the second a gain of a million -- which does my view say to do, and does the answer depend on whether my weighting is steep enough to be secretly lexical? (b) two people at 50 and 100, versus both at 60: I say the second is better, but I've noticed a critic would say I'm just dressing up a concave transform of the total, and that my "priority" is doing no work an egalitarian couldn't do.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this view. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results should actually move me -- not the ones critics trot out that a prioritarian just shrugs at -- and what does my view predict or forbid that its rivals don't? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be, and be honest about the ones that are merely awkward. I especially want the things I, as a committed prioritarian, would have to concede count against me rather than only what opponents say, and I want you to tell me straight whether the continuous-decreasing-weighting version has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, or whether it collapses into either utilitarianism-with-a-concave-transform or a de facto lexical view. And I'd like to know whether the leveling-down objection is really escaped or just relocated: is there a case where my view is committed to treating some worse-off person's trivial benefit as worth an enormous sacrifice by others, and if so is that the same shape of problem I use to sink the egalitarian? The thing that would move me most is a case where the view forces me to rank outcomes in a way I'd find intuitively monstrous, so if you have one, lead with it. Skip the reading list, I have enough citations -- I want the arguments themselves.
