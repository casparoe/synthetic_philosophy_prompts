My study group got into a fight about Rawls last week and I'm still not sure who came out ahead. One guy in the group, the one who's read a ton of econ, kept saying the veil of ignorance is basically just not knowing which lottery ticket you're holding. You choose a society the way you'd choose a ticket when you don't know whether yours is a winner; and since a rational person maximizes expected value rather than brooding over the worst case, the parties behind the veil would go for something like maximizing average welfare, not Rawl's difference principle. He said the difference principle is just what you get if you're irrationally risk-averse, i.e. maximin, and that maximin is a rule for cowards. He ran the whole thing as one analogy: society = the lottery, your place in it = your ticket, the worst-off position = the losing ticket, and the veil = not knowing your number.

I want to know how far that analogy actually goes. Lay the correspondence out explicitly, what's supposed to map onto what, then tell me where it breaks, which breaks matter for his conclusion and which are just incidental, and whether the conclusion (the difference principle is irrational, you'd land on average-utility instead) survives once the breaks are on the table. Two things I'm suspicious of in particular: is the veil really just ignorance of your ticket number, or does it hide a lot more than that; and does it even make sense to talk about the "probability" of being any particular person when what you're choosing is the set of rules that will define what a person's prospects are in the first place? Also, does his picture quietly assume the thing being distributed is a fixed pot, a jackpot that's just sitting there, rather than something the rules themselves help produce?

If the analogy turns out to be a bad one, is there a better analogy that captures what Rawls was actually up to, and does the difference principle come out any better justified under that one, or does it just move the problem around?
