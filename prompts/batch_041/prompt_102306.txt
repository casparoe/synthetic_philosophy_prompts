Setup: Nadia has decided she's going to put a big chunk of her savings — like a couple thousand quid — into a thing that only does any good if loads of other people do it too. Something like funding a public good that only works at scale. Rob said that's just setting money on fire: her individual contribution moves the needle by roughly nothing, so the expected payoff is basically zero, and she'd do more good keeping it.

Then it went like this.

Nadia (1): that's the wrong frame entirely, because in a big enough world there are probably lots of other people in almost exactly her position — same values, same info, reasoning the same way — and her choosing to chip in is *evidence* that they're chipping in too. So the expected payoff isn't just her contribution, it's her contribution plus all the correlated ones her choice reveals. On that accounting its obviously worth it.

Rob (2): evidence isn't control. If those other people were going to chip in anyway, her chipping in adds nothing; if they weren't, her chipping in doesn't make them. Either way she's lighting money on fire and then congratulating herself for a correlation.

Nadia (3): decision theory isn't about control, it's about acting on your evidence. If your own choice is evidence about what the others do, then a theory that says "act as if the world is best given your evidence" tells you to chip in. That's not a trick, that's just what it means to reason well.

Rob (4): fine, but then take the principle seriously and see where it goes. A guy who's dead sure the moon landing was faked can reason the same way: "my believing it is evidence that other like-minded people believe it, so we're coordinating on something." If your principle can't tell Nadia's case apart from his, it isn't doing any work. You need a restriction and I don't think you've got one.

Nadia (5): the difference is beliefs aren't actions you get to choose for evidential reasons — you can't just decide to believe the moon thing because it'd be convenient — and anyway the paranoid crowd are all tracking the same noise, not responding to a real payoff structure. For it to count, the correlated people have to be running *her* decision procedure on *her* problem, not just resemble her in some random respect.

Rob (6): right, and that's the load-bearing bit you're sneaking in for free. In a giant world, is some distant stranger really "running your decision procedure on your problem"? Different options, different payoffs, different info. Whether the correlation is actually there is an empirical question about how minds are distributed, and you're just assuming the answer that makes your conclusion come out. And if everyone symmetric reasons "my cooperating is evidence others cooperate," you can get self-reinforcing loops that arent tracking anything real.

Nadia (7): the symmetry is what fixes the loop, not what breaks it. A bunch of agents solving the same problem with the same algorithm land on the same answer — it's a fixed point. If cooperating is the stable answer for that algorithm on that problem, it's stable, full stop. And you keep saying "negligible causal impact" as if causal impact is obviously what matters, but thats exactly the thing in dispute.
