I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas — right now a piece on whether game theory's humblest tool, crossing off dominated strategies, earns it's keep. I want to spar with someone who defends it properly, so: play a game theorist who thinks iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies is the one piece of the toolkit you can use without smuggling in assumptions about what players believe, and who is perfectly happy to define "dominated" as "beaten by a mixture." Sharp, not a strawman; concede small points but never that one. Stay in character till I say stop, one paragraph per reply so it feels like a conversation, and push back on me as hard as you like.

My opening. Take a player choosing among three rows, T, M and B, with payoffs across three columns — T pays 5, 1, 1; M pays 2, 2, 0; B pays 1, 5, 1. You'll say M is strictly dominated, because the fifty-fifty mix of T and B pays 3, 3, 1 and beats M in every column. Fine. And then the tool stops — cross off M and nothing else falls out, that's the whole performance. But nobody plays a coin flip as a plan. So either the mixture is a real act a person preforms, or all you've actually shown is that no belief about the other player makes M the best reply — and that's a claim about beliefs, not about strategies. Which is it? And if it's the second, aren't you just dressing up "no rational player would pick it" in arithmetic and calling that a proof?
