A colleague in our philosophy of science reading group — I shall call her Mira — has been working through Hempel's raven paradox and has arrived at a set of verdicts that I cannot quite reduce to one principle. Her subject is the hypothesis that every raven is black, and her verdicts are these. A raven that you happen to come across on the moors and observe to be black confirms the hypothesis. A white raven in an aviary disconfirms it. A black crow in the park neither confirms nor disconfirms it. A black car in a car park neither confirms nor disconfirms it. And — the verdict I keep returning to — a white marble drawn at random from a sack which we had been told was filled by scooping up objects from a stretch of countryside containing ravens as well as marbles does confirm it, if only a little. She was quite firm about the marble. I remarked that the marble might just as well have been a shoe, and that a marble could not possibly have been a raven, and neither observation moved her.

I have rashly promised to give my prediction before she announces her verdict on a new case, and I should like it to be reasoned rather than lucky. The case is this. I am taken to a raven sanctuary. At the gate the warden explains the admission procedure: every bird inside has been DNA-verified as a raven and plumage-verified as black, and no bird is admitted otherwise. I therefore know, before I set foot inside, that every bird I am about to see is a black raven. I go in, I see a raven, and it is black. Will Mira say that this confirms the hypothesis, disconfirms it, or neither? What I chiefly want is the rule she is running. I suspect that more than one rule fits all five of her earlier verdicts, and I should like to know which of them is the best fit, what each of the others predicts about the sanctuary, and why she is better read one way than another. Finally, tell me which further case I should put to her the following week — the one that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules — and what each of them would say about it.
