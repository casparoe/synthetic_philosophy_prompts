For the year-end fiction issue of the science-and-ideas magazine I write for, I've been asked to run a short story in place of my usual reported essay, and I'd like you to write it.

The story should dramatize one specific fault line in the philosophy of causation: the split between the but-for notion of cause — C caused E if, had C not occurred, E would not have — and the interventionist notion, on which C counts as a cause of E when some hypothetical intervention that changes C, while holding the rest of the world fixed, would change E. Ordinary blame, tort law, and most everyday talk run on something close to the first; much of contemporary metaphysics and the causal-modeling literature runs on the second. Our readers are educated but not specialists, so the distinction has to emerge from what the characters do, not from exposition.

Here is the premise I have in mind, though you should feel free to change the particulars. A near-future municipal office resolves fault in accidents with a causal-forensics machine that implements the interventionist definition literally: it assembles a causal graph of the relevant events, then, for each candidate cause, simulates a surgical intervention on that event with everything else held fixed and reports it as a cause only if the simulated outcome shifts. The protagonist is the operator who decides which variables go into the graph. That choice, she comes to see, is not read off the world; it is hers to make, and different defensible choices yield different verdicts about who is at fault.

Build the story around a case that pulls the two notions apart. Two candidate scenes to draw on: an accident with two independently sufficient causes, where intervening on either one alone leaves the outcome unchanged and the machine therefore declines to call either a cause, even though everyone agrees both were; and a death the machine traces to nothing at all, because the most salient cause is an omission — someone who failed to act — and an intervention on an absence is hard even to define. Let the operator's selection of variables, and her willingness or refusal to override the machine, be the engine of the plot.

I don't want the story to end in a lecture or to announce a thesis. I want it to end in a decision that commits, through the characters, to a view about whether verdicts of this kind should be trusted, and about what is lost when a formal criterion of causation is allowed to settle questions that used to be settled by judgment. It's fine if the reader is left unsure whether the machine is a better metaphysician than the people it replaces, as long as the story shows precisely what is at stake.
