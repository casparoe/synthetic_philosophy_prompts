I'm a philosophy lecturer and I've somehow ended up co-organising a two-day workshop on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self — the usual crowd: three or four of us from the department, a couple of psychiatrists and geriatricians from the memory clinic, one or two lawyers who do Court of Protection work, and, because the funding came with a public-engagement condition, a handful of carers and former carers who are frankly the only people in the room who have actually lived this. I've got to give the opening remarks, about eight minutes, and I want them to do a job that a normal "welcome and housekeeping" can't: I want the room to feel the shape of the problem before anyone gives a paper on it. The problem, as I want to stage it, is this. A woman in her sixties, still sharp, writes and signs a directive saying that if she develops dementia she does not want to be kept alive by food and fluids; she is emphatic, she watched her own mother go that way. Ten years later she has moderate dementia, she can't follow the reasoning in the document she signed, she dosn't recognise the signature as hers, and — this is the bit that matters — she is, by every measure anyone around her can apply, content. She likes the garden, she likes the staff, she asks for pudding twice. So there are two selves in the room, the one who wrote and the one who is here, and the ordinary assumption that they're one person making one long decision is exactly what has come unstuck. I don't want to resolve it; I want the remarks to hold both of them in view at once. The framing I want to build on — and I'm happy for you to push back on it — is that a directive is less like an order issued by a past sovereign and more like a promise, or a letter left for someone you won't get to meet: the authority of the earlier self isn't a command that survives her, it's a claim the later self is asked to honour, and the real question is whether a promise can bind when the promiser, in the sense that matters, isn't there anymore. There's also the awkward fact that the earlier woman's dread was partly dread of precisely the state the later woman is now in, and the dread looks miscalibrated — she feared she'd be a torment to herself and she isn't. I want that treated as a genuine problem for the authority of the earlier self rather than an easy gotcha, because if we let it be an easy gotcha we've told the carers in the room that their relative's wishes never counted, and that's both cruel and philosophically lazy. (I was chewing this over with a colleague over coffee and we couldn't agree whether the later woman's contentment is a reason or just a fact, which is probably why I want the speech to leave it open.)

Could you write the opening remarks? Keep them speakable — short sentences, no bullet points, nothing that only works on a page — and make the philosophical structure do the work: the speech should end in the unresolved place, with both the earlier and the later self still standing, rather than tipping into "respect the directive" or "respect the person in front of you". The audience is mixed enough that anything that sounds like a lecture will lose the carers and anything that sounds like therapy will lose the lawyers, so it has to hold one register throughout.
