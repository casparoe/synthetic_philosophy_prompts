I recently sat in on a talk on personal identity and metaphysics, and one of the philosophers there summarized Kripke's doctrine of origin essentialism for the nonspecialists in the room roughly as follows: 'Origin essentialism is the claim that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones that actually produced you. This is a sharp way of putting a point most of us already accept — that you could not have had different parents — and the talk of gametes is there to make it vivid rather than to add anything. The argument is modal. Consider the possible world that matches ours right up to the moment of conception, except that a different sperm reaches the egg. The person who results, being the product of a different sperm and egg, is not you. Nothing in the case is special, so the tie between you and your actual origin holds in every possible world in which you exist, and it holds as a matter of metaphysical necessity — even though which sperm and egg produced you is something we can only discover empirically. Two consequences follow. First, the necessity is one-directional: it says your origin could not have been otherwise, not that your origin had to produce you; sufficiency of origin is a stronger thesis, which Kripke did not hold. Second, the alternative to your actual origin is not a different person but simply no one — had another sperm arrived first, you would never have been. The familiar objection is that we can vividly imagine having been conceived from a different sperm, and since conceivability is our best guide to possibility, the doctrine is refuted.'

I don't know this literature well enough to know how much of that to trust, and I suspect the packaging is doing quiet work that the modal core isn't. What I want is an accuracy audit rather than a verdict on whether origin essentialism is true: which of those sentences a defender of the doctrine, or Kripke himself, would accept as stated, which would make them wince, and which they would reject outright; whether the two announced consequences are genuine consequences or quiet distortions; and whether the objection at the end actually refutes anything or merely restates the difficulty in a louder voice. I am also curious what the doctrine, stated as carefully as you can state it, implies about two cases I have been turning over. In the first, a single sperm and egg unite, the resulting zygote divides into two embryos, and only one of the two survives to birth — is the survivor me, my twin, or neither? In the second, the same sperm and egg are used, but the resulting embryo gestates in a different womb, whether a surrogate's or an artificial one — does anything in the doctrine turn on where the gestation happened, or is your origin exhausted by the gametes themselves?

Please give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
