I'm an undergraduate in a cross-listed philosophy and computer science seminar on the safety and alignment of advanced AI systems, and I've been asked to bring a short dialogue to the next session. The seminar has spent two weeks on a disagreement I keep failing to settle on my own, and I'd like the dialogue to be a genuine attempt at it rather than a survey of positions.

The disagreement is about what kind of safety property we should demand of a system. One position holds that a system counts as safe only if it behaves acceptably under the worst conditions it might actually meet: under a shift in the distribution it was trained on, under a user actively trying to elicit harmful behavior, and under the possibility that the system itself becomes an optimizer whose goals diverge from ours. On this view, guarantees phrased as expectations over a fixed distribution are worthless precisely in the cases we care about, because deployment does not preserve the distribution. The opposing position holds that the demand for worst-case safety is either unsatisfiable or vacuous, since a property that must hold for every possible input is either trivially true or covertly relative to a distribution the worst-case camp refuses to name; the only claims we can actually check, on this view, are claims about expected behavior under a specified distribution, and worst-case talk is usually that with the distribution left implicit.

Write a dialogue between two researchers who hold these positions. I want them equally competent and genuinely opposed: each should engage the strongest version of the other's view, and each turn should answer the specific claim made in the previous turn rather than restating a position at higher volume.

To keep the exchange concrete, I'd like them to argue over a certification rule we discussed in class, which I'll state so we're both working with the same thing. The rule: a system may be deployed only if an adversarial red team, working under a fixed budget — say one week of effort and a fixed quantity of compute — cannot elicit any output in a specified set of harmful categories. I want the two of them to disagree about whether this is a real worst-case guarantee or an average-case test in disguise, and in the course of that to separate three candidate adversaries: (1) an ordinary user who wants to misuse the system; (2) the system itself, once capable enough to pursue goals that conflict with ours; (3) nothing more than the unlucky tail of the input distribution the system will meet in practice. I suspect the real disagreement is about (2), and I'd like the dialogue to reach that point rather than stall on (1).

Two further threads I don't want dropped. First, the objection that a guarantee holding for every possible input is either vacuous or secretly distribution-relative, paired with the counter that a claim about expected behavior under a named distribution is exactly what fails when the distribution changes — which is the situation at issue. Second, whether the red team's budget, being a number the developer chooses, makes the certification rule a worst-case claim at all or only a promise about how hard someone tried; and whether worst-case quantification should range over inputs or over environments, since these come apart.

No citations or reading list, please — argue from the ideas themselves. Aim for something around a thousand words. End with the dialogue reaching a definite conclusion rather than a polite draw, and close with a short paragraph stating which position you think the exchange leaves standing and why. I'd rather have a committed verdict I can push back on than a balanced summary.
