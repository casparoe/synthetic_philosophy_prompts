I have no medical training and no philosophy background, and I have never had to decide anything about anyone's death. For about two years, though, I have been reading what I can find — medical guidance, ethics commentary, the angrier corners of the internet — about how death is defined, and I have reached the point where I think two camps are talking past each other, partly because they genuinely disagree and partly because they are answering different questions while using the same word. What I want is the document they could have written together: not a dialogue, not a verdict about who is right, but a joint statement both camps could put their names to, with the remaining disagreement stated so exactly that neither side could accuse the other of caricature.

The two camps, as I understand them.

The first camp holds that a person is dead when the brain has irreversibly ceased to function — in some versions the whole brain, in others only the brainstem, since that is the part that drives breathing and consciousness and without which the rest cannot sustain a life. On this view, a body that breathes only because a machine is pushing air into it, and that shows no brain function at all, is a dead body being ventilated; the old signs of death — no heartbeat, no breath — were always just a rough proxy for the thing that really matters.

The second camp holds that death is the irreversible loss of circulation and respiration, and that the brain criterion is an inference built on top of that — one that, in their view, quietly moves the moment of death earlier, to a time that happens to suit organ procurement. They point out that bodies meeting the brain criterion are not always disintegrating: some stay warm, heal wounds, fight infection, digest, and, in cases doctors have written up, sustain a pregnancy for months. Something that can do all of that, they say, is not a corpse but a disabled person whom we have decided to stop treating.

What I would like you to produce.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims: the things both camps would sign without wincing. Include what they agree on about the biology; about what the bedside tests can and cannot show; about what the word death is being asked to do in a hospital, where it often has to be settled in minutes so that a transplant team can act; and about the difference between a person being dead and our being certain that they are.

Second, a disagreements table. For each row: the question at issue; the first camp's answer in wording that the first camp would accept as its own; the second camp's answer in wording that the second camp would accept as its own; and one line on what turns on the row — what would actually be different in a hospital or a courtroom if that side won.

Third, the cruxes: the smaller questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. I have three in mind and would like you to say whether I have them right, and to add any I have missed. (a) Is irreversibility part of what death is, or only our practical test for it? (b) Is death an event or a process, and does one camp's criterion quietly assume it is an event while the other's assumes it is a process? (c) Is a body that has lost its brain but still holds itself together a dead organism, or a living organism that has lost an organ?

Fourth, for each crux, one case or piece of evidence both camps would agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance. Where you can, use a case rather than a study, and please work at least one of my own invented cases into the document rather than reaching for the famous ones. Here is the case I keep returning to. Two patients lie in the same intensive care unit and are, to every test at the bedside, identical: no response to pain, no gag reflex, no attempt to breathe. The first has been in this state for a week, and nothing suggests it will change. The second is in the same state, but a scan that is not part of the ordinary bedside examination shows a small patch of activity deep in the brain, and a specialist tells the family there is perhaps a one-in-twenty chance that he will begin breathing on his own. At the bedside there is no difference between them; the difference is a number. The first patient's family is being asked to consider organ donation; the second patient's is not. Does the brain camp say the second patient was never dead, or that he is dead but might come back — which sounds to me like a contradiction? Does the circulatory camp say the first patient's heartbeat, kept going by drugs and a machine, is not really circulation in the sense that matters? And is it acceptable for a definition of death to turn on a probability the standard tests are not built to detect? A second, smaller case, which I would also like you to use: a woman's heart stops; bystanders press on her chest for twenty-five minutes; her heart is restarted and she walks out of the hospital a week later. Was she dead at any point during those twenty-five minutes? If not, was that because she was never dead, or only because she was lucky? Had she not been revived, would we say she died at the first minute or the twenty-fifth? And does the answer depend on a fact about her or a fact about us?

Fifth, and last, tell me whether any part of this is a quarrel about words. My suspicion is that one camp is answering a biological question — what kind of thing has stopped — while the other is answering a legal and moral question — when may we stop treating someone and take their organs — and that the word death is being stretched to cover both. If you think that is right, say which parts of the disagreement dissolve once the two questions are separated and which parts survive. If you think it is wrong, say where the mistake is. And, either way, record for each camp what it would count as evidence against its own position, and whether there is anything at all that both camps would accept as actually settling the matter.

You do not need to name laws, papers, or authors, and I know you cannot look anything up; where you would otherwise cite something, state the substance instead. Keep the whole document under about 1,500 words, and write it as a text with two signatories rather than as an argument between two voices.
