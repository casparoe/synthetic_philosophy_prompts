Money pumps are standardly introduced to show that a certain synchronic feature of preference — intransitivity, or more generally a cycle — is a defect: an agent whose preferences cycle can be walked, by a sequence of trades she welcomes one at a time, to a position she ranks below the one she started in. I want an explanation of a second and less tidy use of the same device, one aimed not at the shape of an agent's preferences at a moment but at their behaviour across time, and of exactly what that second use does and does not establish.

Make the question concrete with the following agent. At every moment her preferences are complete and transitive; she is under no confusion about what she wants. But her preferences change with the clock in a systematic way: at any given time she prefers a smaller sooner reward to a larger later one, and as time passes her ranking between adjacent rewards reverses, so that what she preferred a moment ago she now prefers the other way. Suppose an exploiter offers her a sequence of trades, each of which looks to her, at the moment she is offered it, like an improvement on her current holdings, but which taken together leave her strictly worse off than if she had never traded at all; and suppose she can foresee the whole sequence. Explain, as carefully as the material allows:

1. In what sense, if at all, this agent is irrational, given that at no single time are her preferences defective.
2. Whether the pump shows that consistency of preference across time is itself a requirement of rationality, or only that an agent who lacks it can be exploited — and what turns on the distinction. In particular, whether being pumpable and being irrational are the same accusation or two.
3. How the analysis changes when the agent is sophisticated rather than naive: if she anticipates her own future reversals and can bind herself in advance, does the pump become impossible, or does it merely move the cost elsewhere? What does the availability of a binding device reveal about where the real defect, if any, lies?
4. Whether a pump can be built against an agent who is resolute — who adopts a plan at the outset and treats the plan, rather than her momentary preferences, as authoritative. If such a pump exists, describe it; if it cannot, say precisely what feature of resoluteness blocks it.

Throughout, keep the two kinds of pump distinct — the one that attacks a cycle of preferences held at a time, and the one that attacks a drift of preferences across time — and indicate which standard objections to the money-pump argument (for instance, that it trades on the agent's being a pure price-taker who never renegotiates, or that it illicitly treats a series of separately acceptable choices as though the agent must also accept the package) bear on which.
