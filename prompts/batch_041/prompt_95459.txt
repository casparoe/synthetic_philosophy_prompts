I'm a third-year student taking a seminar on explanation and causation in history, and I'm trying to organise my thoughts before writing a term paper. We spent several weeks on overdetermination — cases in which an event would have occurred even if some particular cause had been absent — and on the apparatus that philosophers of history have built around it, from Mackie-style INUS conditions to the counterfactual and manipulationist accounts of causation that dominate the current literature. What struck me is how much of the debate proceeds through toy cases: two assassins, two fires, two bullets striking the same victim. Our seminar tried to make the problem more realistic, and I'd like you to work through the consequences.

The claim I'd like you to take seriously is this. In large-scale historical episodes — a revolution, a financial collapse, the outbreak of a war — causal redundancy is the norm rather than the exception: for almost any proposed cause C of an outcome E, there is some further cause C′ such that E would have occurred even if C had not, where C′ is itself neither necessary nor sufficient on its own. Our seminar also distinguished two forms this redundancy can take, which I suspect are often conflated. The first is horizontal redundancy, where two or more roughly independent sufficient causes converge on the same outcome. The second is vertical or path redundancy, where the outcome would have been reached by a different mechanism altogether, so that the causal path actually taken is itself replaceable. I'm inclined to think the second is far more common in history, and far more corrosive to our usual explanatory practices, than the first — though I can't yet say what follows from that. One exercise we attempted was to draw up a causal budget for a well-worn episode, assigning each candidate cause a share summing to one; the exercise collapsed as soon as we admitted path redundancy.

Please set out what you take to be the most important implications of this claim, assuming it is broadly correct. I'm especially interested in three areas, though I would rather you tell me if I have framed them badly than answer on my terms: first, what it means for the standard historiographical practice of ranking or weighting causes, and whether that practice can be reconstructed rather than simply abandoned; second, whether counterfactual analysis retains any discriminating power at all, and if so, precisely what it can and cannot establish; and third, what follows in contexts outside academic history where causal attribution carries practical weight — a friend of mine is a law student and keeps asking whether pervasive overdetermination would collapse the but-for test in tort, and I suspect the answer is more interesting than a simple yes or no. I would also welcome your view on whether the claim supports or undermines a realist attitude towards causation in history. Please write your answer in American English.
