I'm giving the keynote at the first national meeting of a small grassroots coalition — we started two years ago doing tenant organizing and mutual aid, and now we've got about 400 people coming to a hall in Sheffield to decide whether to register as a political party and run candidates. I'm the one who has to open the day. The room is split down the middle: half are ex-Labour and ex-Green organizers who think the purists are children and that if we don't take power we're just a therapy group; the other half joined precisely because they're sick of parties — the whipping, the talking points, the way you can't say a true thing if it embarasses the leadership. I want to give a speech that takes Simone Weil's line on political parties seriously without pretending we're going to abolish them. The idea I want to build it around, roughly: a party isn't just a coalition for winning votes, it's an engine that mass-produces a shared passion, and once that passion is running, members start to perceive truth and justice through the party's needs — they don't lie, exactly, they just stop being able to see certain things. Weil thought this was structural, not a matter of bad leaders, and she wrote it near the end of her life, in London, watching the Free French exiles revive the same old factional habits. I'll say upfront: I'm not a Weil scholar, I read the essay once and it stuck, so you don't need to get her exactly right, just use the idea honestly.

Write the speech, about eight minutes spoken. It has to do three things at once: make the ex-party people feel that I understand why they're impatient, make the purists feel that their fear isn't paranoia, and actually help the room think about the vote rather than pre-deciding it. Include one concrete concession I should make to the veterans — something real, not a rhetorical bone — and one concrete warning I have to give the purists, something that costs me something with my own side. Don't resolve whether we should become a party; end by handing the question to the room in a way that's honest about what's at stake either way.

No quoting Weil directly unless you're certain of the wording — paraphrase instead. And don't make it a lecture: it's a speech to be heard once, in a hall, by tired people who have been arguing in the pub since last night.
