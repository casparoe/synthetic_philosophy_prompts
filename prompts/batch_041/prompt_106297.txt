I'm a philosophy prof, and I keep getting cornered by a slogan. Last week in a faculty meeting about whether our city should override its own housing expert commission, a colleague said that the will of the people is clearly on our side, so the commission's recommendation can be ignored. I've heard the same move in op-eds about courts, referendums, and recall elections. I want to know what that slogan could actually be claiming, not just that populism is vague.

You're a political philosopher who works on democratic theory and populism. Before you answer, brainstorm the distinct possible readings of the will of the people, then lay them out for me as a menu I can bring to the next meeting. For each reading, give me: (1) the precise claim it makes -- what the people is supposed to be (an aggregate, a collective agent, a hypothetical subject, a set of institutions, a constituent power that can dissolve institutions, an audience), what a will is supposed to be (actual preference, deliberated judgment, common good, authorization, identity), and what follows from it; (2) whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; (3) whether it would actually license my colleague's conclusion that the expert commission can be ignored; and (4) what kind of argument someone would need to make to get from that reading to a policy conclusion. Include the boring procedural readings as well as the metaphysically ambitious ones, and don't stop at the usual contrast between liberal democracy and populism.

I especially want the readings seperated along these lines: is the slogan about who has authority to decide, about what the right decision is, about who counts as the people, or about what the people would want under better conditions? Is it a descriptive claim about majority opinion, a normative claim about the common good, a constitutive claim about a group agent, or just a rhetorical way of saying I don't like the commission? And which reading, if any, makes the slogan do real work rather than just restating that the speaker's side should win?

Be careful to distinguish what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this literature; don't present one camp's interpretation as the consensus. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list -- I want the conceptual map, not names and dates. If some readings are just restatements of each other, say so. If a reading is incoherent or does no argumentative work, say that too, definately. Finish by telling me which reading my colleague most likely meant, and whether that reading actually gets him his conclusion.
