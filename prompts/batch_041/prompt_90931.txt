I have spent the better part of two years developing a version of the relational account of personhood associated with the ubuntu tradition — the thought that personhood is something one attains through standing in the right relations to a community, rather than a property one possesses simply by belonging to the human species — and I have arrived at a formulation I now want to stress-test before I commit it to print. On my version the account is doubly revisionary: personhood comes in degrees, and those degrees are fixed by the recognitional practices that are actually operative in a community, not by any ideal standard those practices are supposed to approximate. What makes my version distinctive is that I take the conferring community to carry authority even when its practices are unjust and its judgments ill-informed. If a community systematically withholds recognition from women, from outsiders, or from those with profound cognitive disabilities, then on my account those people really do occupy a lower grade of personhood; the wrong consists in the community's failure to have developed better recognitional practices, not in its misrepresenting a status that was already settled independently of it. This is what allows me to say that moral progress in this domain is the revision of practices rather than the discovery of antecedent facts, and I regard that consequence as a point in the view's favour rather than as an embarrassment.

I am presenting this at a workshop, and the commentator assigned to me is an unsympathetic liberal individualist who will go straight for the relativism worry, so I would like to arrive with my answers already prepared. Tell me what would count against the view, in the strongest form each objection can take, and rank the defeaters by how much they should genuinely move me. I want them sorted by target: those that attack the gradualism, those that attack the relationality, and those that attack only my additional claim that unjust conferral is authoritative. For each, state the objection at its most defensible, say what my view must assert in order to answer it, and then assess honestly whether that answer is a real reply or merely a qualification that insulates the position from counter-evidence. Three areas interest me most: cases involving people whose standing is marginal (infants, those in advanced dementia, the profoundly cognitively disabled), the possibility of two communities returning incompatible recognitional verdicts about the same individual, and whether the view can give any non-derivative weight to a person's own claim to be recognized over against the community that denies her — that last being the objection I find hardest to dismiss, since it seems to make the person's standing hostage to precisely the body that wrongs her.

Finally, I would like an unflinching verdict on the shape of the whole position. Is this a claim that could in principle be refuted by a case, an argument, or a result, or is it the sort of view that will accommodate anything I can produce? If the latter, tell me whether I should treat that as a virtue of a relational account or as evidence that I have quietly stopped making a claim about anything at all, and say what would have to be true for me to be warranted in concluding that I have.
