I'm running a 90-minute session for an upper-level seminar, 14 students, all of them fine with expected value and shaky on anything past it, and I want a game about whether the simulation argument actually takes the wind out of fanaticism about the far future or just changes the address the fanaticism gets mailed to. If I explain it they'll nod at the three horns and walk out with the same intuitions they came in with, so I want the rules to do the work. I dont want them to hear the word fanaticism until the debrief. If you think a different structure gets there better, say so, but then build the thing out properly.

Here's my shape. Teams of three or four, each team is steward of a civilization holding 100 tokens to split across projects on a board. Everything pays out in the same unit, doesn't matter what we call it.

Project A: 3 units per token, certain.
Project B: 300 units per token, one chance in ten.
Project C: 100 million units per token, one chance in ten thousand.

The arithmetic is supposed to put the whole room on C, 10,000 per token against 30 and 3, and the first thing they should feel is that four orders of magnitude of probability is nothing next to seven of stake, and that they all just did the same obviously absurd thing.

Then a second phase, dealing evidence cards one at a time, each one forcing a minute of reallocation. Cards like: your civilization may itself be inside an ancestor simulation. The simulators may have no interest in what happens in it. Sims may be numerous enough that what you do here is a rounding error in the real ledger. Sims may be shut down early. Minds inside a sim may count exactly as much as any other mind if substrate doesn't matter.

What I want them to trip over is that round one is nearly immune to all of it. Scaling C's payoff by the chance the world is real flips nothing until that chance drops under about one in three hundred, and the simulation argument as it's usually given doesn't hand you a probability anyway, it hands you a disjunction, and one horn of that disjunction leaves the far future exactly where it was. So the naive expectation is that the sim argument dampens fanaticism and the game should make them find that it mostly relocates it. Substrate-independence should cut the other way too, and I want that noticed in the room: if sim minds count, the ledger gets bigger, not smaller.

Then the twist I actually care about. Add Project D. D pays 10^11 per token, but only for civilizations that put at least 30 tokens into it, and only in sims whose runners booted them precisely because they end up making that allocation, so the simulators made the call long before anyone here touched a token. Give them a credence of about 10^-3 conditional on the sim hypothesis being live, and zero otherwise. So the naive expected value of D is 10^8 per token and crushes everything, but the credence is not independent of the choice and the payoff isn't caused by the choice either, and now the room has to fight about whether your allocation is a reason or only evidence, and whether D is worth taking under a causal decision theory or only under an evidential one. That's where the sim argument stops diluting and starts adding stakes, and I'd rather they spend the last twenty minutes on that fight than on the token arithmetic. I'm alot more interested in the argument they have about D than in the allocation they settle on.

Mechanics, roughly: physical tokens on a board, one shared draw at the end so the whole room's fate is correlated (two 100-card decks for C, one black card in each, you need both), a worksheet with the multiplication written out so nobody gets lost in the zeros, evidence cards dealt one per round. Debrief 20 to 25 minutes, mapped to specific moments rather than to ideas in the abstract: the reallocation where someone realises a single token on A is pure waste; the round where the sim card lands and the numbers barely move; the moment someone spots that substrate-independence pushes the other way; and D, where the credence and the choice are entangled. The one honest dampener I want them to find on their own is termination, because that's a hit to how long the future lasts rather than to whether it's real, and those come apart.

Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (my bet is the trilemma collapsing into one probability, and one team trying to arbitrage the token arithmetic instead of arguing about the concept), how to handle each, and which feature of the actual idea the game can't reproduce so I know what I have to supply from the front of the room. And if the numbers are bad, if C should be more extreme, or D's threshold lower so more teams cross it and the choice-dependence actually gets exercised, say so.
