Okay, here's my situation. I'm a novelist — literary fiction, my last book was about a marriage told backwards — and for years I've been quietly obsessed with the peacock's tail problem: why an honest signal has to hurt. A friend has roped me into guest-teaching one 75-minute session of her undergrad course on evolution and human behavior, about 20 students, mixed majors, no math past high school. I want to run a game where they feel the logic in their bones instead of hearing me say it.

The thing I want them to feel: cheap talk is free, so it's worthless, and a signal only carries information if it costs something. But here's the part I actually care about and think alot of people get wrong — the cost has to be higher for the low-quality type than for the high-quality one. If everyone pays the same price, you've just invented an expensive noise that tells you nothing. I also want them to run into the difference between a signal that's costly (in principle fakeable by anyone willing to pay) and one that simply can't be faked because it's a byproduct of the underlying quality — the way actual size or actual speed can't be bluffed. And I want a twist partway through where the signal gets very expensive but equally expensive for everyone, and the whole thing collapses into nothing. I want them to notice that collapse themselves, not have me point at it.

So: design me the activity. Rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (points, tokens, candy, whatever), roles, and a timeline that fits the 75 minutes with room to breathe. Ideally they draw hidden qualities, hold some kind of budget, and have to choose partners or allies, with a real incentive to misrepresent and a real reason to want the truth. No lecture up front, and don't let the name of any of this surface until the debrief.

Then give me the debrief: map the specific moments in the game onto the specific ideas — the cheap-talk failure, the differential cost, the fakeable-versus-unfakeable distinction, the collapse — and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I'd bet good money some students just play nice and break the incentive logic, so tell me how to deal with that. And be honest about which piece of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to just say out loud at the end.
