I have a settled set of verdicts about what is and isn't a marriage, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to, what it implies for the case I keep going back and forth on, and which of my verdicts I should give up.

Two women who have been partners for twenty years and are legally married: a marriage, obviously, and I am not asking you to argue that. Two elderly sisters who have shared a house for forty years, nursed each other through illness, pooled their finances, and who now want the legal incidents — inheritance, hospital visitation, survivor benefits — are not married, and should not be. A couple who married solely so that one could get a visa, never lived as spouses, and divorced once the paperwork cleared: not a marriage, even though the state said otherwise for two years. A couple who did exactly that but became each other's real partners in year five and stayed together thirty years: a marriage, and I want to date it from somewhere in the middle, not from the wedding. An arranged marriage in which the spouses never come to love each other but are loyal, mutually dependent, raise children together, and would be lost without one another: a real marriage. A married couple who separate for good, live on different continents, speak twice a year, never divorce, and both still call themselves married: still married, and I am confident of that. A couple who love each other intensely, live together eight years, and regard any legal formality as a betrayal of what they have: not married, though by any love-and-commitment test they outrank the arranged pair. Two friends who marry for health insurance and tax reasons, like each other, share nothing else, and never become a couple: not a marriage. A celibate, childless marriage of twenty years, deliberately chosen and kept: a real marriage.

The case I waver on is a triad of fifteen years, all three exclusive to one another and raising children together; I do not think the three of them can be married, but I cannot say what distinguishes them from the arranged pair.

The nearest thing I have to a principle is that a marriage is a publicly made and kept promise to be each other's primary person. It does not survive the visa case that turned real, and I cannot see how it rules out the sisters.

Answer as a JSON object with these fields:

- "principles": a list, each entry having "statement" (stated precisely enough to decide cases), "my_cases_it_gets_right", "my_verdicts_it_contradicts", "clauses_added_to_save_them", and "cost_of_those_clauses" (what each added clause buys and what it costs in simplicity or plausibility).
- "recommended_principle": the one you would keep, with a short defense and an explicit statement of whether it is a single principle or a disjunction in disguise.
- "verdict_table": one entry per case above, each with "case", "my_verdict", "your_predicted_verdict", "match" (yes or no), and "which_clause_decides_it".
- "wavering_case": with "predicted_verdict", "confidence", and "what_would_settle_it".
- "unlisted_case": one case I did not mention, with "case", "predicted_verdict", and "why".
- "verdicts_to_abandon": the verdicts your recommended principle cannot accommodate, and why they have to go.
