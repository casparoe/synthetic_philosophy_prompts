I chair the research integrity committee at my university, and there is one item of business I must settle before we sit. A paper published from one of our laboratories last year reports the following experiment. Participants are shown a small shape at one point on a screen. The shape vanishes, and after a very brief interval a second shape appears at a different point and in a different colour. Participants overwhelmingly report that the first shape travelled across the space and changed colour partway through its journey, before the second shape had appeared. In a further condition, participants were asked to press a button the moment they saw the first shape change, and most of them pressed before the second shape had been presented. The authors conclude that the brain revises the content of experience retrospectively: the experience participants report having had at the outset is not the experience they actually had at the outset, so that what they are said to have seen when the first shape was shown is something that had not yet happened at that moment. The paper has already been reported in the national press under headlines about the brain rewriting the past.

A complaint has been lodged with us. The complainant does not dispute any of the reported data. She argues that the same reports would arise if participants experienced each shape accurately and their memory of the first were overwritten by the second before they could say anything about it; and that a button press is no better evidence than a spoken report, since it too is produced after the fact by a system that can be fooled. A colleague on the committee takes the opposite view: we have no route to experience except through reports, and a report that anticipates the stimulus cannot be explained by forgetting. The committee must decide whether the paper's central conclusion is stated more strongly than its evidence permits, and if so, what to require of the authors.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate at the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — no action, a correction of a specified kind, or referral to the journal — together with a deadline by which the authors would have to comply. It should engage the complainant's argument on its merits rather than merely restating the authors' conclusion, and it should be explicit about what this experiment can and cannot establish. Close with a single sentence stating how confident you are in the recommendation and identifying the point you are least sure of. Please write in British English, and keep the whole opinion to 600 words or fewer.
