I keep running into a pattern and cannot find a name for it. Consider a system that reasons and can also reason about its own reasoning: a person surveying her own beliefs, a program inspecting its own proofs, a machine trying to establish the reliability of the successor it is about to build. Grant it the principle that its own conclusions are true — if it has established that P, then P. The principle reads as a modest expression of self-confidence, but it is not merely unprovable: adopting it licenses the system to establish anything whatever, true or false. Withdraw it and the system is left with a strange remainder: of any single proof it has actually carried out, it can satisfy itself that the conclusion is true, yet it can never establish the one general claim that whatever it proves is true.

The shape of this is familiar from the author who is entitled to each sentence in her book but not to their conjunction, though the resemblance is one of shape only: there nothing is poisonous, whereas here the general claim, were the system able to reach it, would be corrupting.

I am not after a bare label. If the pattern has an established name, say which concept it is; if the nearest concepts are near misses, say how each differs and which fits best. In particular, explain why the self-trust principle is harmless when read as a claim about truth and ruinous when read as a claim about what the system can prove, and whether that asymmetry is a fact about provability, about belief, or about self-reference as such. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and mark its edges. Commit to a position rather than laying out candidates for me to choose from.
