Philosophers of dreaming divide over whether a dream is something the sleeper undergoes — an experience unfolding in real time during sleep — or merely the narrative that the sleeper assembles, or accepts, on waking. On the second view there is no fact of the matter to discover about sleep; the question is settled by what we are prepared to call an experience.

Consider the following six considerations, each of which has been pressed into service by one side or the other:

1. A lucid dreamer who has agreed a signal in advance can move the eyes in the agreed pattern while the sleep is being monitored.
2. A sleeper woken by a sudden noise sometimes reports a dream whose climax the noise appears to have caused.
3. Dream reports are given after waking; nobody reports a dream from the inside, except in the lucid case.
4. A sleeper questioned suggestively on waking will sometimes report having dreamt something that the monitoring gives no reason to think occurred.
5. Dreamers report pains, fears and griefs that they say they felt at the time.
6. A dream can be vivid and coherent enough that the dreamer takes it for waking life until waking.

Rate each consideration from 0 to 1 according to how much it should move someone genuinely undecided between the two views — 0 if it merely restates one side's preferred sense of the word experience, 1 if it genuinely constrains which side can be right. Rank the six accordingly, justify each rating in a sentence or two, and say which view your ranking as a whole favours.
