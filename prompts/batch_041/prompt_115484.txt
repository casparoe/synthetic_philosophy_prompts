Last week our reading group did Scanlon on the aggregation problem, and afterwards my flatmate Ruth and our friend Kwame carried on arguing about it in the kitchen for like an hour. I was there with a beer, mostly listening, and I honestly couldnt work out who was winning. Can you referee it for me? I'm not on either side here, I just want the thing sorted out — not a reading list, just the arguments.

Kwame's opening shot: contractualism can't handle numbers at all, and that's fatal. His case: you can either save one person who will otherwise die, or save a million people who will each otherwise get a broken arm, but not both. Contractualism, he says, has to look at each person's complaint one at a time — there's a restriction in it (he called it the individualist restriction) that you're not allowed to lump a million complaints together into one big complaint. And death is worse for a person than a broken arm is for a person. So the view says save the one, when every sane person says save the million.

Ruth's reply was that he's got the restriction wrong. It's not a rule that numbers can never matter, it's a rule about what can be the grounds for rejecting a principle — the reason has to be a reason for some individual, you can't just say "on net." And she said the view does have a way of counting: there's this tie-breaking move, I think it's Scanlon's own, where if the complaints are of equal size you can let the numbers decide, because the principle "in a tie, save the greater number" is one nobody could reasonably reject — if you were one of the five you'd obviously reject a coin flip. So the anti-aggregation charge is way overstated. And she said his case isn't even clearly a counterexample: maybe the hard-nosed answer is the right one, and the "save the million" intuition is exactly the aggregating intuition the theory is built to resist.

Kwame came back at that. First, the tie-break only fires when the complaints are exactly equal; nudge any magnitude and it switches off, so it's a patch, not a fix. Second, if it's justified to let the numbers decide when the complaints tie, the thing doing the justifying is that there are more of them — which is aggregation. Third, and he thought this was the big one: the only thing stopping the theory from adding things up is a stipulation, so underneath, contractualism is really a priority view (weigh each complaint by how big it is, then stop) with a no-summing rule bolted on top that does no work of its own.

Ruth then said maybe the test should be run from behind a veil — before you know which position you'd end up in. You'd ask whether you could accept the principle without knowing whether you're the one who dies or one of the million. She said that brings numbers back in a way that's not cheating, because your chance of being the dying one is tiny compared to your chance of being one of the million. She used a small example too: six flats, five of them pay £1,500 a year each for a lift, and it changes the life of the sixth resident — say she'd pay £5,000 a year for it. Ex post, contractualism says build it. From behind the veil, she thinks you'd... she wasn't sure actually, but she thought that was the right way to think about it.

Kwame said that just isn't contractualism anymore, that it's expected-value reasoning with a Kantian coat of paint on it, and that it has its own embarrassments — I think he said it wouldn't build the lift either, since you're five times more likely to be paying than to be the one who needs it, and that in the plain case where it's one person dying versus five people dying it makes you indifferent, which he says is worse than what he was complaining about in the first place.

So: rate each move, tell me who's right about what, and tell me what they're both missing. And if it turns out they're not even arguing about one thing — if "aggregation" is doing three different jobs across that exchange — say so, because I half-suspect that's what's going on.
