I am putting together a small, time-boxed research effort — about six person-years spread over two years, three researchers who are fluent in decision theory and formal epistemology but are not primarily mathematicians — and I need to decide what we should actually work on. The area is accuracy-first epistemology: the program that treats the accuracy of a credence (its closeness to the truth) as the fundamental epistemic good and tries to derive the central norms of rationality — probabilism, conditionalization, and their relatives — from accuracy-based considerations such as dominance reasoning.

The standard development of the program is first-order: it grades an agent's credences about the world. I want to push it somewhere less traveled, and I am willing to be told the push is incoherent. Specifically, I want the framework to cover what I will call self-referential accuracy — the accuracy of an agent's credences about her own credences, and about the reliability of the methods by which she forms them — and, if that survives, the accuracy of her credences about which questions are worth asking in the first place. Treat this as a live constraint on the agenda rather than a settled extension: part of what I want from you is a judgment about whether it forces revisions in the foundations of the program or merely adds an application on top.

Given all that, produce a defended ordering of the open questions in accuracy-first epistemology: which ones most deserve this effort, and in what order. I care far more about the reasoning than about the list. For each question, tell me where it sits in the dependency structure — which other questions presuppose its answer, so that settling it would unlock the most downstream work — how tractable it is with tools we already have versus what would require new mathematics or a new foundational move, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and whether its resolution would actually change what anyone does next. Flag the questions you regard as overrated, and explain why the interest in them is misplaced. Then tell me how the self-referential-accuracy constraint reorders the list: which questions it promotes, which it demotes, and whether it introduces any that would otherwise not make the cut. Finish by naming the observations over the next year or two that should move an item up or down.

I do not want a survey or a reading list; assume I already know the standard moves and the standard objections. I want an agenda with its trade-offs made explicit and its criteria defended.
