I am finishing a term paper for a philosophy of mind seminar on teleosemantics, and I have reached the stage where the disagreement is no longer about which objections are on the table but about how much weight each one carries, so what I want from you is a number and its derivation rather than a tour of the literature. The proposition I want your credence on is this: there exists a selectionist account of representational content — content fixed by the etiological function of a state, in the tradition of Millikan and Neander — that simultaneously (a) assigns a single determinate content to any state that is genuinely a representation, (b) permits that state to misrepresent, and (c) achieves both without covertly importing a norm, a purpose, or an interpreter's interests from outside the biology. Call the conjunction of (a) through (c) the thesis. Please give me your credence that the thesis is true, and then decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rests on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each: whether the indeterminacy worries — the many lawful covariations any internal state stands in, and the question of which of them a selection history privileges — can be resolved by some principled restriction on the class of relevant functions rather than by fiat; whether misrepresentation can be grounded in the failure to perform a function without the notion of failure doing normative work that the biology itself does not supply; whether the consumer side of the story, what downstream mechanisms are supposed to do with the state, can fix content where the producer side cannot, and whether that is a solution or a relocation of the problem; and whether the thesis even has a determinate success condition, that is, whether naturalizing content names a single project that could be completed or a family of projects with incompatible standards.

One case has been doing most of the work in my thinking, and I would like to know how much of your number it carries. Imagine two lineages that converged on the same internal state S, tokened in the same circumstances and issuing in the same avoidance behaviour, but with different histories. In the first, S was recruited and retained because its tokening covaried with the presence of a predator and the behaviour it drove was selected for on that basis. In the second, S arose as a by-product of an unrelated metabolic reorganisation, persisted in the population for many generations while doing nothing in particular, and was only later wired into the avoidance circuitry by a subsequent change elsewhere in the organism; at no point was its presence selected for because of its covariation with predators. On the strict etiological story the first lineage's S represents the predator while the second lineage's S either has no content at all or has a different one, even though the two organisms are, at the moment of tokening, identical in every respect I can specify. I am fairly confident that the strict story yields that verdict and considerably less confident that the verdict is acceptable, and I suspect the two attitudes cannot both survive.

For what it is worth, a friend in the seminar puts his credence in the thesis at about 0.7 and treats the indeterminacy worries as paper tigers, whereas I am nearer 0.3; I would like to know where our disagreement most plausibly lives, and whether it is really a disagreement about the thesis or about which sub-question is load-bearing. So, after the decomposition, tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, what argument or piece of evidence — an argument, not a citation — would move it and in which direction, and, if you had to bet on it, which single component of the thesis is most likely to be quietly false in a way that the other two components conceal.
