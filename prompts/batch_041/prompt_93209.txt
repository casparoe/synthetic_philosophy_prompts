Hi — retired chemist here (physical chemistry, 35 years of it, mostly spectroscopy plus enough stat mech to be dangerous). Since I retired I've been working my way through philosophy, and lately I've drifted into the decision-theory / game-theory end of things. I've written a short piece below and I'd like your comments on it. I'm not precious about it — tell me where its wrong, and especially where I'm re-inventing something that already exists and doing it worse.

The piece is about the common prior assumption — the idea that when two people disagree, its only because they've seen different evidence, and if you traced their beliefs all the way back they'd be working from the same starting credences. It's the assumption behind that nice theorem where two rational people who both know each others opinions, and know that they know, and so on, supposedly cant agree to disagree.

My claim, roughly, is that this assumption is either an empirical claim thats plainly false, or else it's a definition wearing the costume of a claim. Here's how I get there:

1. In stat mech we happily put a uniform (max-entropy) prior over microstates, and we justify it by symmetry, or typicality, or by saying its the least committal choice. But we never pretend the molecules *share* it. It's a tool we pick for tractability, and we know a different-but-equivalent prior gives the same physics. The common prior assumption feels like a different animal — it's supposed to be a constraint that *rationality* imposes, not a modelling convenience.

2. Take two scientists with literally identical data. They can still disagree, because they weight the space of possible models differently — one thinks a smooth, low-dimensional law is a priori likely, the other is happy to let the wild ones in. In the ordinary sense their information is the same. Whats different is the prior, and it isn't traceable to any data either of them has seen.

3. The standard defense would be: if they disagree about priors, that's just more information they differ on — one of them has a belief *about* priors that the other lacks. Fine. But then apply the common prior assumption one level up: now they have to share a prior over priors. And if they disagree *there*, go up another level. Either the regress bottoms out at some level where they genuinely do share a prior — in which case the assumption is being asserted rather than earned — or it never bottoms out, in which case no finite agent ever actually satisfies it.

4. So the "you can't agree to disagree" result either quietly presupposes the thing it looks like it proves, or its premise is never met by anyone real. Which is it? I genuinely cant tell if this is a deep objection or if I've just confused myself about the difference between a prior and information.

5. Counter-thought to my own piece: maybe that's actually the point, and the common prior assumption is just the definition of what counts as "having the same information," so the theorem is really saying that disagreement requires either different information or a breakdown of common knowledge. If so — is the theorem interesting at all, or is it a tautology? And does the no-speculation / no-trade result that leans on it survive, or does it inherit the same problem?

What I'd like from you:

(a) Is the regress point correct, or is there a standard move that stops it that I'm not seeing? If it has a name, tell me the name and how the usual version differs from mine.

(b) Is my dichotomy (false empirical claim vs. disguised definition) a false dilemma? Is there a third reading where the assumption is doing real work without being either?

(c) Where have I got the actual theorem wrong — I'm fairly sure I'm misremembering what exactly has to be common knowledge, as opposed to merely known by both.

(d) Is this interesting, or am I just foggy about the prior/information distinction and writing three pages of confusion? Be blunt.
