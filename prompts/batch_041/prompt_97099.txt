I am a junior at a large public high school and the student who runs our philosophy club, which meets after school on Thursdays. I have been given the session after next to design, and I want to build it around a problem in decision theory that almost nobody in the room will have met: the one usually called counterfactual mugging, where a predictor flips a coin, asks you for a small payment if it lands one way, and has already arranged a large payment to you if it lands the other way, on the condition that it predicted you would have paid. I do not want my club to hear that name, or any decision-theory labels, until the very end. I want them to walk into the problem with their hands rather than be told about it.

Please design that session for me. The constraints are these: twelve to sixteen students, ninety minutes after school in one supervised room, no real money. We will use tokens drawn from a common pool that can be traded for snacks at the end; a single round should be able to double or wipe out someone's holdings, so the stakes bite, but nobody should feel robbed. I am a student, not a teacher, and I will have one friend helping me. Anything that depends on an authority figure enforcing honesty will not work, so the rules themselves have to make cheating unattractive or detectable. I also cannot lecture for more than a few minutes at a stretch before the room turns on me.

Three features are non-negotiable, because they are the things I actually want to test.

First, the prediction has to be generated by something the participants can inspect. I do not want a magical Omega, because the instant someone says that no such predictor is possible, the exercise collapses into a science-fiction puzzle and the structure of the problem is never felt. So the prediction must come from a mechanism that is present in the room and that participants could in principle understand; if they ask how the prediction was made, there should be a straight answer.

Second, there must be at least one round in which the supposed signal is deliberately absent, so that the group can feel for themselves that in that round refusing is plainly correct, and can then ask what exactly is different in the rounds where the signal is present. There must also be a round in which the prediction mechanism is openly imperfect, with an accuracy we can state or measure, so that the group has to work out whether the case for paying survives at sixty or seventy per cent, and where the line falls. I want them to be forced to produce a number rather than an intuition.

Third, I want a variant in which the condition attached to the large payoff refers to what the participant would have done in the branch of the coin that did not occur, and in which the two branches are treated symmetrically, so that a participant who settles on one policy in advance comes out ahead whichever way the coin falls. My aim is that the person most certain that refusing is obviously right is the one who has to explain how the tokens in her hand were made conditional on a branch she never saw.

If it helps, I am happy for the branch to be settled by something that is not a chance event at all — a fact nobody in the room happens to know, such as a distant digit of some constant — because I would like to see whether the pull survives when there is no randomness to hide behind.

What I need back: the materials and room setup; the roles, including who plays the predictor and how we keep that role honest; round-by-round rules, payoffs and order of reveal, with the coin or its equivalent actually resolved in front of everyone; a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief, which must map particular moments in the session onto particular parts of the philosophical issue; the predictable ways this will go wrong the first time, with what I should do when they happen; and a frank paragraph on which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by talking.

The debrief in particular should cover at least four things. Why the case for paying does not require thinking the coin was more likely to have landed the other way. The difference between choosing an act in the branch you are actually in and choosing a policy that fixes what happens in both branches. Why a written or announced intention is not the same thing as a predictor of what you will actually do, and what that gap does to the exercise. And whether the standpoint before the coin is resolved — the standpoint from which paying looks right — is a genuine standpoint or merely a way of averaging over branches that no one ever occupies at once.

Because the tokens are small and the whole thing is a game, I expect the club to conclude afterwards that the problem is an artefact of the stakes and that at real stakes they would obviously pay, or obviously refuse. If you can build anything into the rules or the debrief that addresses that, please do.

Finally, give me a forty-minute version as well, in case fewer people show up than I expect or the period gets cut short.
