Every year our unit translates a handful of questionnaires — depression scales, quality-of-life inventories, pain measures — for use with patients who speak another language, and I have grown uneasy about the routine we follow. The workflow is standardized. Two bilingual translators independently render the instrument into the target language, and their versions are reconciled into one; two further translators, kept ignorant of the original, render that reconciled version back into the source language; a committee compares the back-translations against the original and certifies that the two versions mean the same thing, returning items for revision until the round trip comes out right; where a literal rendering would be unintelligible or absurd in the target culture, the committee is expressly authorized to substitute an item that captures the same construct in locally appropriate terms; and finally a handful of patients from the target population are interviewed and asked to say, in their own words, what each item meant to them. Running alongside all of this is a statistical requirement: before scores from the two versions may be compared at all, the instrument must show measurement invariance, and the ladder is the usual one — the same factor structure, then the same loadings, then the same intercepts across groups.

My difficulty is that the same institution appears to be doing two things that cannot both be true. In one breath it treats meaning the same as a fact that a translator can get right or wrong and that the round-trip test detects; in the next it treats it as a matter to be negotiated, since everyone here agrees that a good back-translation is sometimes a bad item and a poor back-translation is sometimes exactly the right one.

So I want to know what view of meaning this practice actually presupposes. Does it require that there be a determinate fact of the matter about whether an item in one language says what an item in another says — a fact that holds independently of how any translator, committee, or population would take it — or can everything the practice does be reconstructed out of facts about how speakers of each language are disposed to respond, so that meaning the same is at bottom a decision we make rather than a discovery we report? Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, say which rival accounts of translation it is compatible with, and tell me what would have to change if we took seriously the thought that two translators might produce manuals equally faithful to every behavioral fact and still disagree with one another, so that no test could settle which of them was right. If the answer is that we may keep the practice but must abandon a certain way of describing it, say precisely which description has to go.
