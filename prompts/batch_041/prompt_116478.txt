OK so I'm a retired chemist — 38 years in a lab, mostly heterogeneous catalysis — and since I stopped working I've been reading alot of political philosophy, which has been a strange and mostly pleasurable experience. One thing I keep bumping into is sortition, lottocracy, citizens' assemblies, the whole idea of deciding public things by lottery instead of by election. I have a pile of gut reactions to concrete cases and I cant get them to add up. I'd like you to tell me what principle (if any) actually fits them, and to be honest about which of my verdicts has to go.

Here's the pile. These are just my gut reactions, not considered positions.

- A jury of twelve randomly picked citizens deciding whether someone is guilty of a crime: fine, obviously, we already do it.
- A randomly picked panel of citizens deciding whether a new drug is safe enough to approve: not fine, I want people who know what a confidence interval is.
- Breaking a tied election by coin flip: fine.
- A citizens' assembly of about a hundred randomly selected people deliberating for a few months and recommending a law on assisted dying: fine, actually I think it's a good idea.
- Replacing the whole legislature with five hundred randomly selected citizens who actually hold the power: I'm uneasy, I lean against, and I can't fully say why when the assembly above seems fine.
- A draft lottery deciding who has to go to war when not everyone can be exempted: fine, it's the fairest of bad options.
- A lottery to decide which of two equally qualified candidates gets the job: fine.
- A lottery for the last slot at an oversubscribed school: fine.
- A lottery deciding which taxpayers get audited: wrong. It should track suspicion, not chance.
- A lottery to decide which of two patients, equally good matches, gets the one available organ: fine, seems almost required.
- A lottery deciding which judge hears a particular case: wrong, though I notice I just said the jury is fine, and both of them are just applying the law.
- A lottery deciding whether a bridge is safe to reopen: absurd.
- Selecting who drafts the actual text of a law by lottery, rather than who recommends it: uneasy.

The rough principle I keep reaching for is something like: a lottery is OK when nobody deserves the thing more than anyone else and the task doesn't need any special skill — so juries and assemblies are fine because everyone can judge, while drug approval and bridges aren't because they need expertise. But the legislature case breaks this, since judging is all anyone needs to do there too and I'm still uneasy; and the audit case breaks it from the other side, because it's not about skill at all, it's about who's done something. Maybe there are two different principles fighting each other in my head and I'm pretending there's one.

One more thing that bugs me. I spent my whole career using randomization, but for a completely different purpose — you randomize to kill bias in your inference, not because the units deserve it. Every lottocracy argument I've read is a fairness argument. Maybe that mismatch is why my gut and my reading dont line up.

What I want: a principle or two, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case; for each clause, the case of mine that forces it; what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, and please pick a couple of genuinely hard ones yourself; and which of my verdicts it can't keep. If two principles are fighting, say so and tell me which to keep. Don't give me a reading list, I'm not interested in who said what, I want the thing itself.

Also, tell me how confident you are in the whole package and single out the one point you're least sure of. And finish with a last line that is just \answer{...} around a one-sentence version of the principle you'd actually endorse.
