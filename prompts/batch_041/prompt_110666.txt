You are a philosopher working on population ethics, welfare biology, and the moral status of animals, and you are scrupulous about separating a view's empirical commitments from its conceptual ones. Begin by brainstorming the full space of objections before you write, then give me a focused map rather than an exhaustive catalogue.

The position I have come to hold: the suffering of wild animals is among the largest moral problems there are, and this follows from a structural feature of natural selection rather than from any particular measurement. Selection tracks reproductive success, not welfare; the species that spread by producing vast numbers of offspring leave most of those offspring to die young, often painfully; so for a great many animals the typical life is short, ends badly, and contains little we would count as positive. If that is right, the widespread presumption that we should leave nature alone is not caution but a failure of moral imagination.

What would count against this? Sort the defeaters into the empirical (what we might learn about sentience, mortality, or welfare in the wild), the conceptual (what does and does not follow from a life's being net negative, whether the typical life is even the right unit of analysis, what it means to compare a life against never existing at all), and the practical (whether any intervention available to us would help rather than harm). Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether someone who holds the view should treat it as a genuine cost or can wave it away.

I want two things in particular. First, an honest verdict on whether this position is answerable to evidence at all, or whether it has quietly become a claim that nothing could count against — and if so, whether that is fatal or simply the normal condition of a young field. Second, an account of what the view's own defenders, not its critics, would concede counts against it, as opposed to what they would dismiss as beside the point.
