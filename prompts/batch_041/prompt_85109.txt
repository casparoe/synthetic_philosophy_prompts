I want to pressure-test a position about the Nuremberg and Tokyo war crimes trials, and I want the pressure applied by you rather than by me. My position, stated as sharply as I can: those tribunals were genuine courts applying law, not the victors' vengeance in judicial dress, and the legitimacy of a postwar tribunal of that kind can be assessed against three conditions.

The substantive condition: the conduct charged must have been unlawful under law binding on the accused when they acted. Novelty of enforcement is not the same as retroactivity of law — a norm can bind without having previously been applied to individuals, and a bench can be new without the norm it applies being new. Here I want three distinct problems kept apart: retroactive criminalization (no norm at the time), retroactive jurisdiction (a court constituted after the fact), and retroactive penalty (a punishment no prior law provided for).

The procedural condition: the proceedings must have been adjudication rather than a scripted verdict — evidentiary contest, defence counsel with real access to documents and witnesses, deliberation that sometimes went the accused's way, and sentences that vary with individual culpability rather than with political convenience.

The symmetry condition: the tribunal's constitutive instrument must not be a mere instrument of the victors' interest. My test for this is reversal — could the victorious powers have been brought before a court built on the same charter, applying the same definitions, and would their own comparable conduct have fallen within its reach?

I hold that both tribunals satisfy all three, though not comfortably. I know the standard material and do not need it recited: the charter's crimes against peace were drafted by the prosecuting powers; the Nuremberg tribunal acquitted three defendants while Tokyo convicted every defendant who reached judgment; Hirohito was never indicted; the Japanese biological warfare programme went unprosecuted in exchange for its data; the Soviet conduct at Katyn was never charged; and the separate opinions at Tokyo, including the Indian member's long dissent, denied the court's authority outright. I also know the later career of the whole enterprise — the ad hoc tribunals of the 1990s, the eventual international jurisdiction over aggression — and I am unmoved by the claim that Nuremberg set a precedent that later constrained victors, unless you can show the constraint actually bit.

The thing that would move me most is not more ex post facto grumbling but a demonstration that the symmetry condition is either vacuous or fatal: that reversal was never in prospect, or that it is a test no tribunal could ever pass. I would also take seriously the suggestion that my criterion is the wrong test, if you can say what the right one is and why it returns a different verdict.
