I am in the big history elective at my high school, where we have spent this term reading Diamond's essay on the agricultural revolution, Harari's Sapiens, and Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything. For the current unit our teacher distributed an answer written by a student in a previous cohort, with the name removed, and asked each of us to mark it and submit written comments. I would like your help producing a careful, point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression of the answer's quality.

The question the student was answering was:

"Big history is often criticised for telling a single story in which human development runs from small egalitarian bands, to farming, to cities, to states, each step making societies larger, more complex and more unequal. (a) Using the debate over the transition to farming, explain how Diamond and Harari turn this story into a tragedy rather than a triumph. (b) Graeber and Wengrow are often read as critics of this kind of narrative: explain the main objection they would make to the story as it is told in the question. (c) Is their objection primarily a disagreement about the evidence, or primarily a disagreement about how the question is framed? Defend your answer."

The answer the student wrote was:

"(a) Diamond's argument is that farming was not adopted because it made anyone's life easier, but because it allowed far more people to live off the same land. The price was paid by individuals, whose diet became narrower and less nutritious, who had to work longer hours, and who became more vulnerable to disease and to famine. Harari tells the same story with more emphasis on meaning: he contrasts the success of the species, which multiplied and spread, with the suffering of the individuals who did the multiplying, and he adds that farming locked people in, because each generation's small comforts made the previous way of life impossible to return to, and no one could step off the treadmill once their neighbours were farming.

(b) Graeber and Wengrow's objection is that the sequence in the question is not supported by the archaeological record. Early farming communities were extremely varied: some were egalitarian, some were strongly hierarchical, and some switched back and forth between the two according to the season, while some non-farming societies built monuments and kept slaves. Since farming did not always produce hierarchy, farming cannot be what produces inequality. They also say that the whole search for the origins of inequality is a mistake, because it assumes that people were once equal and then lost that equality.

(c) I think their objection is a disagreement about the evidence. If farming did not always lead to hierarchy, then the empirical claim that it did is false, and the big historians' story collapses. Graeber and Wengrow are not disputing what the story means; they are disputing whether it is true. So this is a case where a grand narrative is refuted by better archaeology, rather than a case of two sides talking past each other."

Our teacher asked us to mark out of ten, with the marks divided roughly evenly across the three parts, and to justify the mark in writing. Please give the answer a mark out of ten, state the scale you are using, and then comment on the answer part by part. I would like you to identify anything that is inaccurate or that does not follow from what comes before it, explain why it is a problem, and say how much it should affect the mark, distinguishing slips that should cost little from errors that affect the answer's central claims. Please also state briefly what the answer would have had to say in order to be fully correct, without rewriting the answer in full, and tell me whether any part of the answer reaches a defensible conclusion for the wrong reason.
