I am putting together the handout for the opening meeting of a graduate seminar on dispositions and laws of nature, and I want it to be a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey of the literature. Please write me a decision tree that takes a student from a starting commitment to a position on what makes dispositional statements true, followed by the key that maps answer patterns onto positions.

The starting commitment, which the tree must treat as fixed and which no node may call into question, is that statements such as "this glass is fragile" and "she is honest" have truth-values, and that those truth-values are not settled by what has actually happened: a glass that has never been dropped is not thereby not fragile, and a woman who has never been offered a bribe is not thereby neither honest nor dishonest.

Every internal node must be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and must offer two or three labelled options. No node may use any of the names that appear at the leaves, and no node may be of the form "which of these positions do you accept?" The tree may not presuppose that dispositions are irreducible: a student who starts from a Humean view of properties has to be able to work through it, so the routes to non-Humean leaves must be questions rather than premises.

The tree must sort all six of the following cases. Each case must be the content of a node, phrased as a question with two or three answer options, and the four distinctions listed after them must be made somewhere in the tree, either at the case nodes or at nodes of their own.

1. A woman who has never been offered a bribe, never found a wallet, never been asked to cover for a colleague. We say without hesitation that she is honest, and we expect her honesty to show up in any of these ways rather than in one characteristic act.
2. A wine glass dropped inside a padded box; it lands intact, and nothing about the glass has changed.
3. A man who would keep a secret if he were trusted with one, except that being trusted with a secret is exactly what makes him unable to keep it: he talks the moment he is let in.
4. A steel ball sitting on a table, in a room where a prankster will destroy any object with a remote device the instant it leaves the table, so that "it would break if it fell" is true of the ball.
5. Two wine glasses, one leaded crystal and one soda-lime, of quite different microstructure, that shatter on the same drop; and a third glass whose stem has been replaced with a stem of a different material, whose fragility is unaffected.
6. A beer mug that survives a fall from a table but not from a shelf. The removal man calls it fragile; the materials scientist, asked the same question, says it is not.

The four distinctions: whether the truth-maker of a dispositional statement is the corresponding conditional or something else; whether a disposition requires a single characteristic stimulus condition or can be multi-track; whether a disposition is identical with the property that realises it; and whether a dispositional ascription is relative to a context or comes in degrees.

The tree must be able to reach a leaf for each of the following, and I would rather have the logical space built first and the positions located in it afterwards than a list of names with questions bolted on: the view that the dispositional statement is true just when the corresponding counterfactual is; the same view repaired with a clause excluding interference; a probabilistic version of either; the view that a disposition is a property of its bearer over and above any conditional; the view that a disposition is nothing over and above the property that realises it; the view that it is the higher-order property of having some lower-order property that plays the relevant causal role; the view that every property is both categorical and dispositional depending on how it is considered; the view that the fundamental properties are not dispositional and that dispositional truths are fixed by them together with the laws; the view that there is no context-independent fact about which dispositions a thing has; and the view that there are no dispositional properties at all and that such talk is shorthand. You may add leaves if the cases force you to. Give each leaf the name of the position where a standard name exists and "unnamed" where it does not, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want position names, not the names of philosophers, and no reading list.

Then give me the audit: a table with the positions as rows and the six cases as columns, entering the verdict each position is committed to in each cell, and marking the cells where a position genuinely cannot return a verdict. If two rows come out identical across all six columns, say so, and name the question the tree would have to ask to pull them apart, if there is one. Finally, list the answer patterns that no position occupies, and say of each whether it is empty because it is inconsistent, because it is unattractive, or because it has merely not been explored.

Keep the tree to ten nodes and twelve leaves at most.
