One of my grad students wants to hang an entire seminar session on a passage she found in some popular book on animal minds — I think the author is a science journalist, not a philosopher — and she's asked me to bless it before she builds her syllabus around it. My instinct says something's off, but I don't trust my memory of the details and I don't want to wave her off on vibes. Here's the passage:

Dennett's four kinds of creatures are the rungs of an evolutionary ladder. At the bottom sit the Darwinian creatures, whose behaviour is fixed at birth — they can't learn anything, they just play out the instincts natural selection handed them. Above them are the Skinnerian creatures, which is Dennett's label for anything that can learn from experience by operant conditioning, i.e. by getting rewarded and punished. Then come the Popperian creatures, which get to do their learning inside their heads: instead of acting and finding out, they run the action as a simulation and let the bad hypotheses die in their place, which is why they can afford to be exploratory. Finally there are the Gregorian creatures, named after a psychologist who studied perception, who add language and culture — they inherit other people's already-tested hypotheses, so they don't have to do all the testing themselves. Each rung replaces the one below, so a Gregorian creature like us isn't really Skinnerian or Darwinian underneath anymore. The whole scheme is Dennett's way of arguing that only the top rung has a genuine mind, and it connects up with his writing on memes, a term he coined. The scheme is tidy, but it's been overtaken by the discovery that genes and culture interact, so you can't keep the levels seperate after all.

Is this fair to what Dennett is actually up to with the four creatures?
