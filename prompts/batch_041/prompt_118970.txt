I'm a TA on an upper-level social epistemology seminar — 16 students, mostly seniors, 90 minutes, and I get the whole session to myself next week, right after we finish the Fricker unit. What I want is a game that makes them feel that testimonial injustice can be produced by the architecture of a conversation rather than by anybody holding a prejudiced belief, and that it compounds with the hermeneutical kind instead of being a seperate problem. Every year they nod through the reading and then say the fix is just to believe the marginalised speaker more, and I want them to actually try that fix inside the game and watch it not work. No lecture up front — they shouldn't hear either term until the debrief.

The shape I've got in my head, which you should feel free to throw out if you can do better: it's an adjudication. The group has to decide what happened in some incident (you pick — it should be a case where the accurate account stays genuinely ambiguous until you have the right concept, so the wrong verdict is reachable and even defensible). A few students are witnesses with private packets; everyone else deliberates and votes. Three structural things I want baked in, and I care more about these than about any particular rule set. First, asserting a claim that contradicts the group's common ground costs more of a scarce resource than asserting one that confirms it. Second, the discount rate is endogenous: when a claim gets recorded as unreliable, that speaker loses speaking time in the next round, so the two feed each other. Third, one witness has the experience but not the vocabulary — their packet only lets them describe what they saw in flat sensory terms plus a handful of near-miss words, and the group's rubric penalises vague testimony. I'd also like a second run of the same case where I equalise the credibility budgets, so they can discover for themselves that the arithmetic fix doesn't touch the vocabulary gap.

So: rules, materials, roles, a timeline that actually fits 90 minutes, the credit/payoff scheme (no real money — tokens or points are fine), and a debrief that maps each moment onto the testimonial/hermeneutical distinction without turning into a lecture. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I've definately never done anything this elaborate, so include what to do when they break the vocabulary restriction or turn the whole thing into a competition. And be straight with me about what the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
