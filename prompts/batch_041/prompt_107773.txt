I'm in a third-year philosophy of mind seminar — 14 of us, one 90-minute session a week, no tech in the room except a projector I'd rather not touch — and I've somehow ended up on the hook for running the week on whether beliefs and desires are real, i.e. the eliminativist line that believing and wanting are posits of an inherited folk theory that might end up like phlogiston. I don't want to stand up and explain it and then take questions, because the first hand goes up and it's always the same one (but I know I have beliefs, I'm having one right now) and then we're finished in ten minutes and everyone privately decides the philosophy was a bit silly. What I want instead is a session where they do something and end up standing in the eliminativist's shoes without being told to, so the question of whether these posits earn their keep feels like a live question in the room. So please design me the activity, not the explanation.

The shape I keep half-building and can't finish: a black box — literally a cardboard screen with a slot, me or a pre-briefed friend behind it with a printed rule sheet and a shuffled deck — and teams of three or four who get a few rounds of feeding it situation cards and predicting which action card comes back. First round they get only the machine's physical description and they'll be hopeless; second round they're told what it was built to do and they get good fast; third round they have to write down what it believes and wants and they get even better, and it feels obvious and natural, and that's the trap I want them sitting in before the rule sheet comes out and turns out to be an arbitrary lookup table with no goal, no memory, no representation anywhere in it. Then the round I actually care about: a person. A confederate following a silly but perfectly consistent rule (she agrees to any request containing a certain letter, say), with the teams writing belief-and-desire explanations for her and predicting her next several answers, confident and wrong. The crunch I want to land in the debrief is that the same trick that dissolved the box's desires dissolves its function too, and nobody wants to say the TIDY-UP 3000 has no function — so what exactly is doing the work? For scoring I want two currencies, not one: prediction points, plus a small pool of what I've been calling ontology chips that a team must spend every single time its manual posits a belief, a desire, a memory or a goal, so the trade-off between predicting well and positing little is something they feel in their hands rather than something I assert at them.

What I need from you: the materials list, and the actual rule sheet for the box — a concrete table, not an instruction to make it arbitrary, something that genuinely looks goal-directed for the first eight or ten trials and then doesn't; the round-by-round rules and a 90-minute timeline; the scoring scheme, including how the chips interact with the points so a team can't just hoard and win by predicting nothing; how to stage the confederate round so the explanations come out wrong in a way nobody clocks until her rule is read out, plus what to do when someone guesses it in two rounds, which they're definately going to do; and then the debrief, which has to map what happened onto the real disagreement, middle positions included — I don't want them leaving thinking beliefs are just dispositions to behave, or that a box with a lookup table has proved we're robots. Be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what this setup structurally can't reproduce; I already suspect it can't get anywhere near the first-person side of the thing, since everything here is third-person prediction, but tell me if there's more. Last thing: I want to hand out a half-page at the end with three short unnamed paragraphs — three positions on whether belief-talk is in the business of describing anything real — and have each team say which one their own manual ended up defending. Draft those three paragraphs, no names and no citations, just the positions stated plainly enough that they can fight about them.
