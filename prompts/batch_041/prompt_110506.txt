I'm a second-year PhD student working somewhere between provability logic and the theory side of AI safety, and I keep having the same circular argument with people in my department. I don't want you to settle it, I want you to diagnose it.

Here's the setup, as briefly as I can put it. Löb's theorem says that if a theory like PA proves, for some sentence A, that A is provable implies A, then it proves A outright. The corollary everyone accepts is that a consistent system extending arithmetic can't prove the soundness of its own provability predicate. So an agent whose only way of trusting a conclusion is having a proof of it can never prove to itself that whatever its successor version concludes is true — because that's precisely the soundness schema. This is what people call the Löbian obstacle: an idealised agent that wants to certify its successor's reasoning before adopting it is stuck, not for engineering reasons but because of a theorem. Nobody in the room disputes any of that.

The dispute is about what it means. There's a camp, mostly out of the alignment world, who treat it as a fundamental obstacle — a real barrier to building agents that rewrite themselves and keep trusting themselves, something you have to route around with restricted forms of self-reference or bounded proofs. Then there's another camp, more theory-side, who say it's an artefact of the idealisation: you only get the obstacle if you insist the agent is a deductively closed system that has to certify its successor in its own language, and once you let it be a bounded agent reasoning about its own actual computations, or let it hold graded beliefs that aren't closed under logic, the theorem stops biting. And I keep hearing a third line from two or three people, that both camps are asking a question with no well-formed answer, because 'trusting your successor' was never a formal notion to start with and nobody has said what would count as formalising it.

My hypothesis, and please tell me if it's wrong: this isn't a disagreement about maths — Löb is settled — and it isn't quite a disagreement about facts either. I think it's a disagreement about what counts as a solution, i.e. whether the demand is to prove in your own logic that the successor is safe, or merely to have some principled reason for not expecting to get burned. If that's right, the two camps are answering different questions under one heading and no theorem will ever separate them. If it's wrong, tell me what the real disagreement is.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Verbal, a fight about which idealisation is the right model of the target phenomenon, a hidden formal question in disguise, or two questions wearing one name? Is there a theorem or a formal result that would genuinely separate the camps, and has anyone produced one? Are they even disagreeing about the same object — is one side talking about idealised agents with full deductive closure and the other about bounded agents, so that they never intersect? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change in what's being asked — or is the honest answer that none should be expected, and why?

Two things I want from the answer. First, be explicit about the split between what's actually settled among people who work on this and what's genuinely contested — don't hand me the alignment camp's line as though it were the field's consensus, and don't hand me the dissolution line as though it were either. Second, don't just survey who says what at me; I've read enough to know the rough shape. I want the structure of the disagreement, not a reading list.
