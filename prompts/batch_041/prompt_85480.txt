I'm in a graduate seminar on judgment and decision making, and we have just finished with the dollar auction and escalation of commitment. Our readings treat escalation as a bias and pre-commitment — declining to enter, fixing a walk-away price in advance — as the standard remedy. In practice, though, organizations more often escape escalation by changing the decider than by changing the decision: a failing project is handed to a new manager or an outside consultant on the reasoning that the incumbent is too invested to judge it. I would like you to reconstruct what that practice presupposes about practical reasons. It seems to assume that a reason generated by a past expenditure belongs to the person who incurred it rather than to the role, so that a successor facing identical prospects is not merely better placed to cancel but genuinely uncommitted, while the incumbent's position is partly constituted by what they have already done. Is that a defensible account of reasons — a kind of authorship or ownership of one's own commitments — or does the practice merely exploit a psychological fact while the normative situation is the same for both parties? Consider also the ledger: the successor is normally given a project whose past spending has been written off or hidden, which changes what they know and not only how they feel. Since the magnitude of what has already been spent may itself be evidence about the project's prospects, can a cost be normatively inert yet evidentially relevant, and does the practice commit itself to an answer? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say whether it presupposes a unity of the agent over time that the pre-commitment remedy does not.
