I am a high school student working on a civics project about how unemployment benefits are actually administered where I live, and I keep running into a pattern I cannot make sense of. The scheme works roughly as follows. To receive the payment you must first sign a written agreement with the employment office setting out how many jobs you will apply for each week, which appointments you will attend, and what kinds of work you are obliged to accept. If you fail to meet any of these terms, the payment stops for a set period, sometimes for months. There is no presumption in your favour: the burden is on you to show that you had a good reason for missing an appointment or refusing an offer, and the office decides whether your reason is good enough. People judged too ill to work are routed to a different benefit, assessed by a medical examiner and carrying no job-search conditions at all. Meanwhile the biggest programmes in the same budget — old-age pensions, child payments, and a long list of tax reliefs for savings, mortgages and investments — pay out with no agreement to sign, no appointments, no conduct requirements, and often to households that are comfortably off. I have also noticed that when money is lost, benefit fraud is discussed by politicians as a moral failing and a scandal, while the far larger sums lost through tax avoidance are discussed as a technical problem with the tax code, and that the state runs advertising campaigns asking neighbours to report suspected cheats, with nothing quite comparable for the well-off. And when unemployment rises sharply in a recession, the conditions are quietly relaxed for everyone, as though joblessness had suddenly become something that happens to people rather than something they do.

My friends and I have been arguing about what the system must believe about human beings in order to be built this way, and we keep talking past each other. So I would like a careful answer to the following. What view of responsibility, desert and agency does this set of arrangements presuppose? Which rival views does it rule out, and which parts of it are strictly required by that view as opposed to merely fitting it comfortably — could the same practice be explained by something thinner, such as administrative convenience, cost control, or the need to sustain public support for the scheme? Is the practice internally consistent, or does it presuppose incompatible things at different points — for instance, treating the claimant as the author of their own unemployment when setting conditions, and as a medical case when assessing illness? And if we replaced the presupposed view with one on which most unemployment is structural rather than chosen, what would have to change in the practice itself?

Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
