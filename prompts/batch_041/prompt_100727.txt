I am an undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of emotion, and I have volunteered to draft the essay question for our end-of-term review session, together with a model answer and a marking scheme that my classmates can use to score one another's practice attempts. The topic is the paradox of fiction, and what I want is a question that cannot be dispatched by reciting the familiar three-step setup — that we appear to feel genuine emotions toward characters we know do not exist, that emotions are supposed to require beliefs about the existence of their objects, and that we hold no such beliefs about fictional characters — followed by a tour of the standard replies. The crux I would like the question to turn on is a pair of observations that, to me, sit awkwardly with every reply I have encountered. The first is that I often feel far more for a character I know to be invented than for a real person whose misfortune I read about in a news report; the more plainly real the sufferer, the less moved I seem to be. The second is that when a work I took to be fiction turns out to have been a thinly disguised account of actual events, my emotional response to it does not grow warmer — if anything it cools, and I begin to describe what I felt as having been for the character rather than for the real person behind it. I would like students to be forced to say what these two observations reveal about the leading families of solutions — the view that the emotions are genuine but require no existence belief, the view that they are make-believe or pretend emotions, and the view that we are under some illusion or confusion about what we believe — and, above all, whether any single one of these replies can accommodate both observations at once.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme totaling twenty points. The scheme should distribute its marks across the specific moves a strong answer must make, and for each of the main moves it should name the one or two errors that are common at that stage and that ought to cost marks, so that the scheme can distinguish an A answer from a B answer rather than merely rewarding the mention of the right labels. The question should be answerable in about ninety minutes and must not require any outside reading, since students will be working from the concepts and cases already discussed in the course; please do not build the question, the model answer, or the scheme around specific publications or attributions, but instead describe each position by what it claims. Write everything in American English.
