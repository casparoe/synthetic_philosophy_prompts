I am a novelist, and I want a short story — roughly 3,000 words, titled — to serve as the spine of a longer book I am building. Write it in close third person, past tense, single point of view, and keep it formally clean: no section headings, no epigraphs, no frame narrative.

The protagonist is a woman in her early sixties, a clinical researcher who chaired the working group that introduced a diagnostic category into a national manual twenty years ago, and who now sits on the committee that must decide whether to keep, rewrite, or retire that same category.

Invent the manual, the category, and every institution in the story. Do not use a real manual, a real diagnostic label, a real professional body, or a real person. I want the fiction airtight, and I do not want the reader's knowledge of any actual controversy to do work the story should be doing.

The category should be something like volitional attrition: a persistent narrowing of the capacity to initiate action, unaccompanied by sadness or anhedonia, in which the patient reports that the difficulty lies not in wanting but in the interval between wanting and doing. You may rename it if you find something better; keep it plausible and keep it yours.

Here is the conceit the story has to earn. The founders intended the criteria to be self-liquidating. They believed that a description exact enough to be recognized would function as the treatment — that a patient who read the criteria and said yes, that is exactly it, would be released by the recognition. Prevalence did not fall. It rose, year after year, and the rise tracks the wording of each successive revision almost exactly. That is the engine of the plot, and the story must show it rather than explain it.

Build in these institutional facts, all of which should be present as texture rather than argument: the manual has a sunset rule, under which a category whose measured prevalence drifts beyond a set margin across a revision cycle must be rewritten or retired; a category survives in practice only if it is reimbursable; the intake that generates the diagnosis must be completable in fifteen minutes, so only what a clinician can observe in that window can count as a criterion; and the current committee includes patient representatives who have built a durable identity, a vocabulary, and a lobbying position around the diagnosis, and who will experience retirement as an erasure.

Three requirements about form. First, the story must contain three versions of the criteria — one from each of three revisions, each block no longer than five numbered lines — and the changes in wording must be the mechanism of the story, so that a careful reader can reconstruct what happened from the three blocks alone. Second, the protagonist must half-recognize herself in each version, and there must be one scene where she answers the criteria aloud, somewhere ordinary and unbeautiful, with neither irony nor pathos. Third, there must be a clinic scene in which a patient corrects her and thanks her in the same breath, and the correction must be technically right.

Keep the prose free of the following: no character says looping effect, social construction, or interactive kind; no one delivers a lecture; no one explains the theme; no suicide; no asylum gothic; no patient-as-artist metaphor; no villain; no sentence of the form she realized that. End flat — on the vote, on an abstention, on her drafting a new criterion — not on an epiphany.

Write so that a reader who knows nothing about psychiatric nosology follows it as a story about work and self-description, and a reader who knows a great deal finds the procedure accurate, the incentives correctly drawn, and the patients competent adults rather than symptoms.

After the story, add a plain afterword of about 150 words identifying, without jargon, which mechanism each major beat dramatizes.
