Friedman's essay is the founding document of the idea that a theory should be judged only by its predictions. On his view you should never ask whether a model's assumptions are true — you ask whether it forecasts well. He goes further: the more important the theory, the less realistic its assumptions will be, because a powerful theory strips away detail and keeps only what matters. That's what licenses profit maximization and rational choice even when real people obviously aren't doing any calculating. The firm behaves as if it were maximizing profits, and that's all the theory needs. So Friedman is an instrumentalist — for him models aren't descriptions of the world, they're calculating devices, and asking whether one of them is true is a category mistake. And since positive economics is just prediction, it's also value-free: economists can settle their policy disagreements among themselves without importing moral views, which only enter at the last step, when you pick a goal. Critics complained that this makes theories unfalsifiable, since you can always wave away a bad assumption by saying it was never meant to be realistic, and Samuelson, who coined the nickname for the doctrine, demolished the argument, which is why hardly anyone defends it anymore.
