Last week I was at a dinner with two friends of mine, Devi and Marcus, both the kind of people who've read way too much, and they got into an argument about Occam's razor that I completely failed to referee, and it's still bugging me. It kicked off because someone else at the table said something like "simpler explanations are more likely to be true," and Devi said that isn't just a heuristic or a vibe, it's basically a theorem: you consider every computer program that would output the data you've seen, weight each one by 2 to the minus its length in bits, and that gives you a prior; then you Bayes on the data, and the posterior is guaranteed to converge on the truth as long as the data was generated by some computable process. So "prefer the simpler hypothesis" is just "prefer the shorter program," and the reason it works is a convergence proof, not folklore. Marcus said fine, but shorter *in what language*? The length of a program depends entirely on the machine you're writing it for, and if you swap the machine then every single prior flips around. Devi said yeah, but there's an invariance result: any two universal machines give you priors that differ by at most a constant factor, and that constant is basically the length of one interpreter, so it washes out as the data piles up and the two posteriors converge to each other — that's exactly the sense in which "objective" is supposed to apply here. Marcus said the constant is doing an enormous amount of quiet work, it's the size of an entire interpreter which can be gigantic, so for any finite amount of data — i.e. all the data we will ever actually have — the language you picked can change your answer, and there's no further fact of the matter about which language is the right one. He also said the whole theorem assumes the data comes from a computable process, which is itself a huge simplicity assumption smuggled in at the foundation, so Occam isn't justified by this setup, it's presupposed by it.

Then it got worse. Marcus offered this example: take a plain machine, and then take a machine that's identical except it has a giant lookup table baked in that just spits out the first million bits of whatever stream we're looking at. On the second machine, "this data is random noise" is a one-line program; on the first machine it's a million-bit program. Same data, opposite verdicts about what's simple, and the gap is exactly the size of the baked-in table. Devi said right, but that machine's edge only lasts until you have a million bits of data, after which the two machines agree anyway — that's all the constant means, it's a head start, not a different answer. Marcus said that's cold comfort when a million bits is more data than you or I will ever collect, and anyway he doesn't buy that "optimal on average across all possible sequences" tells you anything about the one sequence you're actually staring at, because a predictor can be lousy on every particular sequence you'll ever meet and still win the aggregate; that's just no-free-lunch, which he says proves his point — the theorem doesn't show Occam is right, it shows you had to pick a prior somewhere, and short-programs is one of infinitely many you could have picked and then dressed up as objective. Devi said that's backwards: the no-free-lunch results are exactly *why* a universal prior is the most anyone could ever hope for, and the fact that no predictor can beat it by more than a constant is a real guarantee, not an arbitrary choice.

I've read enough rationalist blogs to know the word "Solomonoff" and that Kolmogorov complexity is a thing, but nowhere near enough to actually call this. Can you go through it move by move — the invariance point, the big-constant objection, the computable-source assumption, the lookup-table example, and the worst-case-versus-in-practice thing — and say who's right at each step, and who's more right overall? And if there's something both of them are missing, I'd really like to know what it is.
