I've been trying to work out what actually bugs me about constitutional entrenchment, and I think it's the Ulysses analogy everybody reaches for.

Here's what I keep noticing. In the classic self-binding story there's one person at two times — you now, and you later when you're drunk or furious or in love — and the whole point is that the later you is still you, just in a worse state to decide. That's why tying yourself to the mast is supposed to be legitimate rather than just weird. Fine, I get it.

But a constitution doesn't obviously work like that. When a legislature can't pass something because it's entrenched, the people being stopped aren't the people who did the entrenching, not in any literal sense. They're a different parliament, a different electorate, sometimes born decades afterwards. Nobody's drunk. Nobody's in a passion. They just disagree, and they have a majority, and we still say no. Same story with central banks setting rates that an elected government can't override, and with countries writing trade rules or rights guarantees into treaties precisely so that quitting later is expensive — the expense is the point, that's the design. And then right next to all that you get sunset clauses, where we deliberately refuse to bind ourselves and make the law expire unless someone renews it.

So: what does this cluster of practices actually presuppose about who the "self" is in political self-binding? Does it need a persisting collective agent with something like a will that outlives its members — a demos that's one thing across generations — or can it get by without any such claim? And if it can get by without it, is the Ulysses framing doing real work or is it just a rhetorical trick we should drop?

Things I'd like you to seperate out: what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely fits nicely with; whether the different bits — entrenchment, independent agencies, treaty lock-in, sunset clauses, the fact that an ordinary statute can always be repealed by a bare majority — presuppose the same thing or different things; and where the whole thing is underdetermined, i.e. where several rival theories of collective agency would license the practice equally well.

Then give me a verdict and dont hedge it. My hunch is that "office-binding" is the honest description rather than "self-binding" — what a constitution constrains is whoever later holds the office, not the agent who made it — and that this dissolves the paradox but at a price, because the justification then has to come from stability and coordination rather than from autonomy or self-respect. Tell me if that's right. If it is, tell me what actually breaks when you swap the self-binding picture for the office-binding one: which arguments for entrenchment survive, which quietly die, and whether any real practice would have to change. If you think my hunch is wrong, say so plainly and give me the better account instead.

No citations or reading list, please — I don't need the literature, I want the argument. Assume I know roughly what a Ulysses contract is.
