I have an argument, assembled for a reading group, that I now suspect proves far too much, and I cannot find where it goes wrong. (1) An intermediate layer of a network represents a property just in case that property can be recovered from the layer's activations by some decoder — in practice, a probe fit to the activations and evaluated on held-out inputs, with a linear probe taken as evidence for a feature direction and a nonlinear one as evidence for a feature that is merely not linearly separable. (2) Because activations are a function of the input and, in a typical trained network, carry nearly all of it, a probe with enough capacity can be trained to recover almost any property of the input from almost any layer — including properties the network plainly does not compute, such as the publication date of a text or whether an image was in the training set. (3) So the success of a probe is not evidence that the property probed for is represented. (4) Probing is the principal evidence base for representational claims about networks; the causal work — patching, ablation, steering — earns its status by showing that a probe's target is causally efficacious, and so borrows whatever authority probing has. (5) Therefore no representational claim about a network is warranted. (6) Nothing in (1)–(4) is specific to artificial networks: expressive decoders recover any stimulus property from any sufficiently large population of cortical neurons. (7) Yet we treat representational claims about cortex as well warranted, and interpretability work is largely an attempt to earn the same standing for networks. (8) So one of (1), (2), or (4) is false. Which one, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I care most about not having to give up (7), but I want to know whether the repair that saves it also rescues the anti-realist about networks, or whether realism has to be purchased separately in the two cases.
