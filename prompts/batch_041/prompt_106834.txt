For the better part of two decades I have covered city politics for a metropolitan daily, and for eleven of those years my beat was Marta Ellery, who left the mayor's office last month after three terms and is being honored at a dinner on Saturday. I have been asked to give the speech, and I find that the standard shapes available to me — the roast, the tribute, the fond recollection — are all wrong for her, because Ellery is someone who, by my lights and by hers, did things that were both necessary and wrong and never once pretended otherwise. The clearest instance is the night in 2011 when she authorized the tactical entry into a warehouse where a gunman was holding three hostages; two came out, one did not, and every inquiry since has agreed that the alternatives on the table were worse. What matters for the speech is what she made of it. She described that decision, in the single interview she ever gave about it and in private ever since, as what she called a debt that doesn't clear — a choice that can be defended in the moment but is never discharged by the defense, so that any public honor is, in her accounting, partly a payment against something rather than a clean reward. She extended the thought in a way that cost her politically: when the council moved to pass a resolution of regret over the raid, she asked them to withdraw it, on the grounds that an institution cannot carry a debt it did not itself incur. Whatever one thinks of the doctrine, it is hers, and it is the thing the speech has to be built around.

The difficulty is the room. Two tables will be moral and political philosophers — her late husband's brother taught political theory for thirty years and is bringing half his department — and everyone else will be family, neighbors, and city staff who knew her mainly as the person who finally got the crosswalk put in. The philosophers will hear any suggestion that the raid was simply the right call as a laundering of the deed, and any suggestion that she ought to be ashamed of herself as a confusion between regret and wrongdoing; the family will hear a seminar on moral tragedy as a failure of love, and they will not be entirely wrong. So the speech has to be honest in a way both halves can live with: it must honor her without either cleaning up the hard calls or putting her on trial for them, and it must take her doctrine seriously enough that the people who know the literature will recognize their subject, without ever sounding like it was written by one of them.

Please write that speech, roughly six minutes as spoken. Work the debt that doesn't clear through the whole thing rather than mentioning it once, and let it govern what the speech can and cannot do — if it thanks the city on her behalf, for instance, it has to reckon with her refusal to let the city apologize on hers. No quotations, no citations, no footnotes, and no reading list; she would have hated all four, and I could not check any of them from here in any case, so any distinction doing real work has to be carried by plain language. Give me the text as I would deliver it, and keep the affection real.
