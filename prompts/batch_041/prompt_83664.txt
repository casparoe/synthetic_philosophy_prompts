Suppose Henry Sidgwick could read Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism: the charge that the utilitarian's justification of a man's saving his wife rather than a stranger supplies a thought the agent ought not to have to think, and that a morality of impartial justification estranges agents from the projects that give their lives point. Reconstruct the reply Sidgwick would most plausibly have made, arguing from his own resources rather than from later utilitarian writing. Would he locate the objection in the utilitarian criterion or only in the agent's deliberation? Would he answer by separating the criterion of rightness from what it is safe to teach publicly — a reading of him that critics have treated as a two-tier morality for those in the know — and would that defuse Williams's charge or concede it? What does his dualism of practical reason contribute here: does it make room for the agent's partial attachments, or only record them as a standing irrationality? And would he have been tempted by the modern strategy of redescribing apparently non-utilitarian commitments as a form of consequentialism with agent-relative values, or would he have dismissed it as a relabelling? Where his texts underdetermine the prediction, mark the extrapolation rather than smoothing it over.
