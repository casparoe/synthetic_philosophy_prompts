The 2016 United Kingdom referendum on EU membership is usually discussed as a case of populism, sovereignty, or campaign strategy. I want it treated instead as a case study in the boundary problem — the question of who rightly constitutes the demos that decides a question. The franchise for that referendum included UK citizens resident in the UK, Irish citizens, Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK, and residents of Gibraltar, but excluded most EU citizens resident in the UK (those from Ireland, Malta, and Cyprus being the exceptions), excluded UK citizens who had lived abroad for more than fifteen years, and excluded 16- and 17-year-olds. Two years earlier, the Scottish independence referendum used a different franchise: EU citizens resident in Scotland could vote, 16- and 17-year-olds could vote, and the expatriate rule did not operate in the same way. The same state, on two constitutional questions, drew the boundary of the demos differently.

Analyze this pair of episodes through the lens of the boundary problem. Set out the main candidate principles for drawing the demos — the all-affected principle, the all-subjected principle, and the idea that the demos is a bounded historical political community with a right to decide its own membership — and say, for each, what the franchise for the 2016 referendum should have been, and what the 2014 Scottish franchise should have been. Then do the reverse: say which features of the actual franchises each principle explains, which it misdescribes or cannot account for, and whether the differences between the two referendums look like principled applications of different principles or like ad hoc political settlements. Pay particular attention to the cases that pull the principles apart: EU citizens resident in the UK who were subject to UK law and affected by the result but could not vote; Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK who could vote; UK citizens abroad who were affected but excluded; 16- and 17-year-olds who could vote in Scotland but not in the UK-wide referendum; and Gibraltar, which was in the electorate though not part of the United Kingdom. Finally, say what the episode shows about the boundary problem itself. Does it confirm the pessimist view that the demos can never be constituted by a prior principle, only by a prior political decision? Does it support a particular principle by elimination? Or does it show that the problem is less a single puzzle than a family of different questions — who is subject to coercion, who is affected, who is a member — that come apart in practice? Reach a verdict, and identify the one further case or franchise variation that would most sharply test it.
