I've been arguing with two friends from a formal epistemology reading group abot the problem of logical omniscience, and I've ended up defending a position I'm not sure I can defend: that omniscience isn't just a harmless idealization we'd drop once we get serious about bounded agents, but a genuine norm -- a perfectly rational agent's beliefs really are closed under logical consequence, and an agent whose beliefs aren't is to that extent irrational rather than merely limited. What pushed me there is the coherence argument: if your credences aren't a probability function, there's a set of bets that loses you money whatever happens, and (the version I find even more persuasive) some probabilistic credence function scores better than yours on every possible world; and since logical truths have credence 1 and logical equivalents get equal credence, closure just falls out. A friend's counter-move is to relativize belief to a proof system: you believe A relative to a theory T if A is derivable in T within some resource bound, so omniscience becomes a property of the theory rather than of the agent, and the norm becomes believe whatever consequences of your beliefs you can actually reach. I think that's a bait-and-switch but I can't articulate why, so I want to know what the honest cost of my own view is.

So what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked, fatal first -- which observations, arguments, formal results, or cases should actually move me, and which are just things critics say. In particular, what does my position predict or forbid that a resource-bounded rival doesn't, and is there any possible agent -- a slow but sound theorem prover, a reasoner with no memory, a machine that can check any proof you hand it but can't find one -- whose existence or non-existence would settle anything, or is the debate entirely about what we'd say about an agent that can't exist? I'd also like you to press on whether my own view is consistent under its own norm: the preface-style cases (a long book each of whose claims is justified while the conjunction isn't) and the lottery-style cases look like cases where the norm tells you to believe something you can see you shouldn't, and I don't know whether that shows the norm is inconsistent or that the cases are misdescribed.

And be straight with me about the insulation: if every apparent counterexample -- a mathematician who doesn't notice an obvious consequence, a system that believes each axiom but not their conjunction -- gets redescribed as a performance limitation rather than a failure of the norm, is anything left that could falsify the view, and is that a problem for a norm of this kind? What would someone who defends my position concede actually counts against them, versus dismiss as a confusion between the norm and our ability to meet it -- and if it turns out no finite agent can satisfy the norm, is it vacuous, or does ought-implies-can simply not apply to epistemic norms? If it helps you rank things, the kind of result I'd personally find most persuasive is a formal one showing the coherence argument needs omniscience as a premise -- that the Dutch-book or accuracy case only has force against agents who already see all the logical consequences of their own credences, so it can't be used to convict the non-omniscient of irrationality.
