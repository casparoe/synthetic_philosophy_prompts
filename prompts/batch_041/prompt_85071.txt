My brother's a butcher and I've been helping him on Saturdays, and his wall charts have got me stuck on something I can't stop chewing on. He says the chart is a map and not the animal — the cow doesn't come with dotted lines on it — and it turns out butchers in other countries draw those lines in different places, so the word brisket means one part of the animal here and a different part over in Britain. That seems to be the same fight philosophers have about whether science finds the real seams in nature or just draws handy lines on it, with chemistry and biology being fancier versions of my brother's chart.

Could you write me a sestina about it. Six end words, use these: cut, joint, seam, bone, chart, kind — and I want every one of them to pull double duty somewhere, a butchering sense and a classifying sense (a cut of meat and a cut in a classification, a joint you carve around and the joints of nature), without the poem ever stopping to tell me thats what it's doing. Let the form carry the argument: the same six words keep coming back in a fixed order no matter what the stanza is talking about, which is exactly what a chart does to an animal, and the little three-line bit at the end, where all six get jammed together, is where I want the question pressed hardest — is the order in the animal or in us — and not tidied up. Real poem first, argument second, and accurate enough that someone who teaches philosophy of science wouldn't wince.

No headings or bullet points or bold or tables — just the poem, and if you want a sentence or two about a choice you made, put it in plain prose after, like you'd write in an email.
