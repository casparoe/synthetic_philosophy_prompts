I'm writing the final for my upper-level epistemology seminar — mixed third-years and MA students, 90 minutes, closed book, no internet, and this essay question is worth 25 marks. I want it on the dogmatism paradox, and I keep failing to write a version that isn't just "state the paradox, state two replies, done." The students who skimmed a summary do fine and the ones who actually understand it get nothing to bite on. So I need you to build me one that can't be answered by recital.

Act as a seasoned epistemology professor who has marked a few hundred of these and can smell bluffing from the first line. Before you draft anything, spend a bit of space thinking on paper: brainstorm what the actual crux is, sketch two or three rival framings of the question, and then pick the sharpest and write it up. Please don't just produce your first idea.

Here's the raw material I want the question built on. Assume the students know the paradox in outline because we covered it, but assume nothing about any particular paper, and don't let the question depend on knowing who said what or when — no names, no dates, no "X argues that…". If a student can only place the thing historically, they should top out at a middling grade.

The paradox, roughly as I'll restate it for them: if you know that P, then you know that any evidence against P is misleading, since P is true and so such evidence can't be pointing at the truth. So once you know P, you're entitled to ignore later counter-evidence. Knowledge licenses dogmatism.

The case I want it hung on:

Priya, an accountant, has been through the ledger line by line and knows the £40k discrepancy was a rounding artifact, not fraud. It's textbook; she's done this a thousand times. Two things then land on her desk. First, a colleague whose judgement she rates tells her flatly that the discrepancy is fraud and the books were cooked. Second, an internal memo reveals that the software version Priya used had a bug that, in rare cases, turns a small deliberate fraud into something that looks exactly like a rounding artifact — so the very fact she saw a rounding artifact is, in a certain light, evidence about her grounds rather than about the ledger.

What I want the question to force: does her knowledge let her dismiss the colleague? Does it let her dismiss the memo? Is the paradox's derivation really the same move in both cases, or does it only work for one of them and quietly equivocate when it's applied to the other? And then the payoff — does what's left of the paradox show that knowledge can't be defeated by evidence about its own reliability (i.e. that it's really an argument against fallible knowledge), or does it instead just show that the principle generating the paradox is false, or that it needs a closure-style assumption that's independently dubious? I want them to have to take a stand that costs them something, not hedge on all three.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. The question as it would appear on the exam sheet — addressed to the student, with the case in it, telling them what to do and roughly how to spend the 90 minutes. Keep it under about 250 words.
2. A model answer of roughly 700 words that a strong student could plausibly write in the time, written in prose, not bullet points. It should be genuinely good, not just safe — it should commit to a line.
3. A marking scheme: exactly five must-hit points, each with a mark value, summing to 25. For each point give a one-line note on how candidates typically blow it. Then add a short paragraph on what separates a first-class answer from a solid upper-second — name the specific extra move the top answer has to make, not just "more depth."

One more thing: the wrong answers here are predictable and I'd like the scheme to punish them specifically — the student who says "knowledge is defeasible, so the paradox fails" without saying which premise it fails at, the student who treats the two pieces of evidence as the same kind of thing, and the student who just reports that some philosophers accept the conclusion and calls it a draw.

I've asked for no reading list or citations on purpose, so don't put any in the model answer either — just argue.
