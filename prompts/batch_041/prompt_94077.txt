I'm building a small anthology of short stories where each one dramatizes a single idea from philosophy of science, and I need you to write the entry for verisimilitude — the thought that a theory can be flat-out false and still be closer to the truth than some rival, and that this "closer" is something real we're groping toward even though nobody can put a number on it.

Skip the obvious costume dramas (no Galileo, no phlogiston, no "the Earth isnt the center of the universe, you fools"). Use this setup instead and stay inside it:

The Freeport Cartographers' Guild charts an archipelago that's mostly fog and reef. Ships can only reach an island if someone has already landed on it, and a landing needs a chart, so the guild's charts are the bottleneck for basically everything. Their rule for rating a map is the part I care about: a chart is scored by the fraction of its drawn features that have been confirmed by an actual landing. So a map that draws three islands and gets all three right beats a map that draws sixty, gets fifty-five right, and puts five islands where there's only open water. Blank space costs you nothing. (You can work out the funny consequence yourself — under this rule the technically perfect chart is one island on an otherwise empty page, and the guild's best-ranked surveyors draw almost nothing.)

Your protagonist is a young surveyor who has been out past the last confirmed island and come back with rough triangulations — a whole guessed coastline she's maybe seventy percent sure of. She's figured out that under the guild's rule she can only lose, no matter how much of the real archipelago her guess captures, and that the surest way up the rankings is to draw less. The old guildmaster isnt a fool or a villain; he has reasons, and one of them is that a wrong island has killed people. I want their disagreement to be the spine of the story.

Couple of things I want in there: a concrete scene where you can feel what the scoring rule does to a person's incentives (a promotion, a rivalry, a chart spread out on a table), and at least one moment where someone tries to say what "closer to the truth" would even mean here and mostly fails — dont let them land a tidy definition, because there isnt one, and dont let the story resolve into "the bold girl was right all along." The interesting thing is that both rules have a real point and neither is simply wrong. 1,500 words or so. No epigraphs, no philosophy lecture, keep it a story.
