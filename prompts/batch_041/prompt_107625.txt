I am a third-year student in a course on ethics and political philosophy, and I have spent the term defending a view about moral compromise that I want to stress-test before I commit it to a paper. The view is this. When two parties whose value systems conflict must arrive at a single joint decision, their situation is best modeled as one of potential exchange: each surrenders something it ranks lower in order to secure something it ranks higher; a good compromise is one that lies on the Pareto frontier of the parties' rankings, so that no party can be made better off without another being made worse off; and the moral case for compromising is exactly the moral case for trade, namely that it can move both parties past the status quo. I am drawn to it because it explains why compromise need not be a concession to weakness or a dilution of principle, and because it treats each party as the authority on what it values rather than importing a ranking from outside.

I want to know what would count against it, sorted by how decisive it would be. In particular I want to know what the view predicts that rival accounts of compromise do not, so that I can see which observations would be evidence for or against it, and whether it has quietly been made safe from refutation by qualifications I have not yet noticed myself making.

Four worries I cannot resolve on my own, and which I would like assessed rather than merely listed. First, whether the exchange model survives the fact that a party's ranking of an outcome often depends on how the outcome was reached: if agreeing to a trade is itself something one can value or disvalue — integrity, complicity, the sense of having been bought — then the goods change value in the exchange, and the frontier may be empty or undefined. In markets this is a frictionless assumption; I suspect that in morality it is the normal case. Second, whether the model can handle the non-separability of issues: a bundle of individually Pareto-improving trades can leave every party worse off than before, so a local test may endorse a globally inferior settlement. Third, the baseline problem: the frontier is defined against a status quo that may itself be unjust, so a Pareto improvement can entrench an injustice, and trade arguably presupposes just initial holdings in a way that compromise does not. Fourth, that the parties to the bargain are not the only ones affected — future people, dissenters within each camp, those who never consented — and the model seems to have no place for them. I would also like to know whether a view built on a unanimity-style test can serve as a complete criterion at all, given the familiar tension between such a test and even minimal individual prerogatives over one's own affairs.

If you can point to actual settlements — constitutional bargains that ratified an injustice, amnesty arrangements, environmental deals — that the view either endorses and should not, or cannot describe at all, that would move me more than any abstract objection. What I would find most persuasive is a case in which the frontier test approves of a compromise that any morally serious person would call a sell-out, or condemns one that was plainly right. Tell me which of these defeaters the view's defenders would themselves concede, which they would dismiss as changing the subject, and what it would cost them to absorb the rest.
