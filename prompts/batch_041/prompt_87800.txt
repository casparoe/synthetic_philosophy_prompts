I have been asked to run a single seventy-five-minute session on the Knobe effect for a mixed group of graduate students in cognitive science, a few of whom have done some philosophy of action and none of whom have met experimental philosophy before. I do not want you to explain the Knobe effect to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it. Give me the following, laid out in the order in which the components must be introduced and with an explicit note on what depends on what — in particular, tell me what has to be on the table before the central finding can land as a genuine puzzle rather than as a curiosity the audience files away under the heading of framing effects.

On the carrying example: the standard chairman vignette — a business decision that will foreseeably harm the environment, taken by someone who declares that he does not care about the environment — is the one everyone uses, and I am inclined to keep it out of the opening and bring it in only late, if at all. I want to build the session instead around a case of my own: a hospital administrator who adopts a cost-cutting measure that she knows will, as a side effect, cause a small number of preventable infections, and who is indifferent to that consequence. Tell me whether that case can carry the session, what it must contain and what it must leave out if the audience is to generate the asymmetry themselves rather than have it pointed out to them, and what the chairman case adds at the end that my case cannot. If you think my case is the wrong vehicle, say so and offer a better one on the same accounting.

I have a suspicion that I want the session to test rather than assert: that what drives the asymmetry is not the badness of the side effect but the agent's indifference to it — that an agent who does not care is read as having brought the outcome about intentionally, while an agent who minds but proceeds anyway is read differently, and that in every standard vignette the good/bad contrast is confounded with the caring/indifferent contrast. Design the sequence so that these two candidate explanations are visibly pulled apart by the time the session ends, and tell me which of the cases, in which order, would separate them.

Then the misconceptions, in the order the audience will actually form them, each with the point in the sequence at which I should head it off. I expect them to reach immediately for the thought that this is merely a bias, and for the thought that it shows the concept of intentional action to be incoherent; I would rather have your list than mine.

Finally: one question I can put to the room at the close that would tell me whether they have understood the finding as a claim about the concept rather than merely memorized the result; and an explicit list of what I should leave out at this level, together with the form of words I should use to say that I am leaving it out.

Do not give me readings and do not cite anything; I will supply the references myself.
