I'm in a reading group that's been chewing through the old induction literature, and we've gotten stuck on grue in a way that's turned into a running joke. The setup we all accept: define a predicate — call it grue — that applies to an emerald examined before some future time T just in case it's green, and to an emerald not examined until after T just in case it's blue (take your favorite version of this; the details don't matter for what follows). Every emerald we've ever seen is green and therefore also grue, so the evidence looks like it confirms 'all emeralds are grue' exactly as well as it confirms 'all emeralds are green', and yet we all project green without blinking and treat grue as a cheat. Fine, we get the setup. The problem is that after a whole semester nobody can agree on what it's supposed to show, and we've split into four camps that talk straight past each other. The first says it's a deep result about confirmation itself: that 'confirms' isn't really a two-place relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis at all, and the riddle proves you can't say what confirms what until you've first fixed a language or a set of predicates. The second says it's just Hume's problem in a new outfit — any inductive inference has to bring something extra to the table (priors, an assumption of uniformity, entrenchment, whatever), and grue merely makes you notice that the something extra was always there and was never free. The third says the whole thing is an artifact of a gerrymandered predicate: 'grue' is stitched together out of a disjunction, 'green' isn't, and once you see that there's no question left about the world — only about which words we choose, or about how our classificatory schemes happen to be built. And the fourth says the other three are just saying the same thing in different vocabularies and there's nothing left to disagree about. We meet Thursdays and it's become clear the disagreement isn't going to resolve by itself.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on Goodman — I don't need you to settle whether grue is a real problem. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it substantive? Verbal, in the sense that the camps mean different things by 'confirms' or 'predicate' or 'projectible'? Or several genuinely different questions wearing one word, so that the first is really asking what makes an inference rational while the third is asking what makes a predicate natural and they were never in competition? Here's my hypothesis, and I'd like you to test it rather than just agree: I suspect the stall comes from the word 'confirmation' doing double duty, with the camps answering 'what makes an inductive inference good?' and 'what makes a predicate the right kind of thing to project?' as if they were the same question. To smoke this out I came up with a variant I want you to run through each camp. Define a predicate whose switch is a fixed physical event that has nothing to do with us or our examining anything — say, an emerald is sgrue just in case it's green and lies on the near side of some future radioactive decay, or blue and lies on the far side. The observed emeralds are all green and all sgrue, so the projection puzzle looks identical, but the usual jab that 'grue' secretly smuggles in a reference to our knowledge doesn't apply in the same way. My question is whether each camp's diagnosis predicts the same verdict on sgrue as on grue; and if all four camps end up saying the same thing about sgrue, tell me whether that shows the disagreement was verbal all along or just that they were answering the same question and never noticed.
