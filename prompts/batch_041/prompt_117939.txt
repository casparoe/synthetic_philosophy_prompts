As I understand it the view is this: outcomes get ranked from worst to best by how much you care about them; a gamble is characterized by the chance of getting an outcome at least that good; and instead of multiplying each value-increment by its probability you multiply it by a risk function applied to that probability, which is where your attitude to risk lives, and I think it's supposed to be the same risk function across all your gambles, money or health or whatever. Make the risk function the identity and you get plain expected utility back; being risk-averse means giving a given chance of your better outcomes less weight than its probability. Independence goes out the window and that's the point, since the Allais-style choices are supposed to come out rational.

My opening move: I'm not going to fight you on the Allais numbers, I'll grant that those preferences are coherent-sounding. I want to know what the common consequence is doing in your theory. Take the two pairs. In the first, a sure million against an 89% chance of a million, 10% chance of five million, 1% chance of nothing. In the second, an 11% chance of a million against a 10% chance of five million. Now look: within each pair the two options agree on a branch, the 0.89 branch, which pays a million in the first pair and pays nothing in the second. And the parts where they disagree are exactly the same in both pairs, namely a certain million against a 10-in-11 shot at five million. So if the branch the two options share can't make a difference, then the two pairs are the same choice and my preference can't flip between them; you need the shared branch to matter. But what can it be doing? If it comes up, the two options in that pair are the same world, same payoff, same probability, so it can't be a difference in what happens to me. And don't tell me it changes the shape of the chances, because that's just a restatement of the flip: what happens to me is one of three amounts of money either way, and the shared branch doesn't change which amounts those are or how likely they are. So give me an account of how a branch that pays the same in both options can be the thing that decides between them, and tell me why that isn't just "independence is an axiom I reject", which would make risk aversion a preference about probabilities rather than about anything in the world.
