A freind of mine who's read Gödel, Escher, Bach too many times has an argument he's convinced shows machines can't understand anything, and I can't decide if its clever or just a tangle. Here's my cleaned-up version of it:

(1) For a mathematical sentence, to understand it is to grasp why it's true; for a formal system, that just is being able to derive it from the axioms.
(2) By Gödel, any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic has a sentence G that is true but not derivable in it.
(3) So the system doesn't understand G.
(4) We do understand G, though — we can see that it's true, as long as the system is consistent.
(5) Any machine is a formal system.
(6) So no machine understands arithmetic, and a fortiori no machine understands anything.
(7) But if we're made of matter and our reasoning runs on physics, then we're formal systems too, so by the very same reasoning we don't understand G either.
(8) And an inconsistent system derives everything, so it understands nothing in particular.
(9) So nothing understands anything.

He treats (9) as a reductio of (5), and says that's exactly the strange-loop point: mind has to be more than a formal system. I treat it as a reductio of the whole chain. I'd like to keep (2) and (4) — I'm not giving up Gödel, and I'm not giving up the thought that we can see the truth of a Gödel sentence. So which step do I drop, and what does dropping it cost me? I suspect the trouble is somewhere in how (1) leans on the word understand, or in (5), but I can't say which or why. And whatever fix you propose, tell me what it does to the GEB analogy, because if the escape is that the mind is never a finished system but keeps growing new axioms, I want to know whether there's still a self there for the self-reference to be about, or whether we've just swapped a loop for a ladder and called it a mind. If the only way out is to say we're inconsistent, fine, but then say what that buys and what it wrecks.
