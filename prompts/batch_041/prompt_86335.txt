Ok so I'm a program officer at a small grantmaking outfit that just decided to put a real chunk of money into wild animal welfare -- call it two researchers for three years, which for us is a lot -- and now I have to decide which open questions we're actually paying people to work on. I don't want a survey or a reading list (and please, no bibliography, I can't check it anyway), I want an agenda and a defense of the ordering.

What keeps tripping me up is that there seem to be a handful of load-bearing conceptual questions sitting under everything else, and I cant tell which ones are upstream of which. Candidates I've been chewing on:

- The "is nature net negative?" question. Once you notice that a lot of wild animals are r-selected -- a fish or a frog or an insect dumping thousands or millions of eggs, of which a tiny handful ever reach adulthood, the rest dying young and often badly, of starvation or parasites or being eaten -- it stops being obvious whether a typical wild life contains more pleasure than pain, or whether an ecosystem nets out positive at all. And that seems to silently decide whether habitat destruction is a harm, a benefit, or a wash, which a ton of downstream work just assumes.
- Whether the goal is making wild animals' lives go better or reducing the number of animals born into bad ones. Those point in pretty different directions and I honestly don't know if anyone has settled whether they're even in tension.
- How you aggregate across taxa at all. Does a million short insect lives weigh against one long-lived mammal life, on what basis, and does our uncertainty about invertebrate pain mean discounting them heavily or just flagging it and pressing on?
- Death itself: is dying early a welfare loss for an animal that may have no future-directed wants, and does the answer change the whole shape of the project?
- The awkward fact that a lot of standard conservation work would increase the number of animals, which under the net-negative view is backwards from what a welfare-focused funder should want.

So: which of these, or which ones I'm missing entirely, are the actual bottlenecks? Give me a ranking with the reasoning shown -- what's upstream of what, which are tractable for two philosophically-minded people with no lab and no fieldwork, which are neglected, and which look big but wouldn't change much downstream even if someone settled them tomorrow. And tell me what kind of result or argument over the next couple years should move an item up or down the list.
