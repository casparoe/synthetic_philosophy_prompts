I hold a strong conventionalist position about historical periodization: the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are not real periods that historians discover; they are retrospective classificatory artifacts, useful at most as pedagogical shorthand, whose apparent boundaries are imposed by later observers who have already decided which continuities and ruptures matter. Treat this as a package of three claims: first, that these labels do not pick out natural or even robust historical kinds; second, that their boundaries are not discoverable from the inside; and third, that their continued use is explained by institutional and narrative convenience rather than by their tracking anything in the past. What would count against this position? I do not want a survey of historians who disagree, and I do not want a bibliography. I want the defeaters themselves, sorted by how decisive they would be, including observations, arguments, intuitions, and possible findings that should move a committed conventionalist, together with an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability.

To make the question sharp, use a distinction I will stipulate rather than assume: call a period label discovered if it tracks a boundary that agents on both sides of it could in principle have registered as a rupture in their own practices, and stipulated if it becomes visible only from a later vantage point that has already fixed the relevant continuity. You may also treat period names as chrononyms, singular terms that purport to refer to bounded stretches of time, and ask whether any of these labels refer at all or only coordinate inquiry. I do not claim either distinction is standard; treat them as tools. Then tell me what would have to be true for the strong conventionalist position to be false under them. Which defeaters would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which would defenders dismiss as begging the question? I am especially interested in defeaters that arise from the conventionalist's own commitments: if all periodization is retrospective, what follows for the conventionalist's own act of dividing the historiography into before and after the invention of these labels? And what does the strong conventionalist predict about the historical record that a realist about periods would not? Personally, I would be most moved by a boundary that insiders registered in their own vocabulary, or by a case in which a similar periodization emerged independently in a different historiographical tradition, but do not restrict yourself to those.
