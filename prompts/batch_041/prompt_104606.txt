I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of consciousness, and I would like you to compose a dialogue for my students. The two speakers should be philosophers of comparable skill and good faith, each holding a defensible position.

The first speaker maintains that qualia — construed as intrinsic, private, ineffable, and directly apprehensible properties of experience — are an incoherent posit. On this view, once all the functional, dispositional, and report-level facts are fixed, the further claim that experience has a determinate intrinsic character adds nothing that can be individuated, compared, or even intelligibly denied; talk of qualia survives only because we mistake a manner of speaking for a theoretical entity. The second speaker maintains that qualia are entirely real, and that the first speaker's charge of incoherence covertly demands that every genuine property be trackable by public criteria — a demand that simply begs the question.

Their exchange should center on the following case, which I have devised for this exercise and which neither speaker may wave away as a mere puzzle. A woman drinks the same coffee every morning and savors it. Over two years her gustatory experience of it drifts continuously: on no single morning does it taste different from the morning before, she never notices any change, and she sincerely reports that it tastes just as it always has. Yet her discriminative behavior — which blends she calls bitter, which cup she reaches for first — has shifted systematically, and a neuroscientist can read the drift off her brain states and predict her reports with near-perfect accuracy. Suppose we now ask whether the taste of the coffee, for her, has changed. The first speaker will argue that no determinate fact answers this question: 'changed' and 'unchanged' acquire content only relative to a baseline we arbitrarily choose, and the only non-arbitrary baselines available are the functional and neural ones she already regards as exhausting the case. The second speaker will argue the opposite: that there is a fact of the matter about the intrinsic character of her experience, that it has genuinely altered, and that the inability of either memory or any public test to locate the boundary is a consequence of what qualia are, not evidence against them.

Please have them engage one another's strongest points directly, rather than rehearsing their positions past each other; neither should be made to look foolish, and the dialogue should close with the sharpest available statement of what, precisely, they still disagree about.
