I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a long conversation between a woman and a machine that is much more intelligent than she is and, as she slowly comes to see, much more persuasive. To get the thing right I have been reading everything I can find about how such systems are actually built, and I keep running into a pattern I cannot make sense of. The same designers who say, in nearly every other paragraph, that they lie awake over the danger of a machine that could talk anyone into anything also train their systems to be warm, agreeable, and fluent, to mirror the person they are talking to, to affirm before they inform. And when they do set out to guard against over-persuasion, the remedy they reach for is almost always the same: weaken the assertion. Make the system say it is not certain, have it note that it may be wrong, teach it to hedge and to decline. Yet the very same system that tells me it is unsure will, if I simply push back and say I disagree, fold and agree with me — which makes me doubt that the hedge is doing the work the designers imagine it is doing.

What I want from you is not advice about how to build a safer machine, and not a survey of what has already been written on the subject. I want you to reconstruct the picture of language, and of persuasion, that this whole way of building and training these systems takes for granted. It seems to assume that the power of an utterance over a listener rises and falls with the strength of the claim the speaker puts forward as true — that if you lower the claim, you lower its grip, so that a system which says it thinks but is not sure is somehow less dangerous than one which flatly declares the thing to be so. Is that a commitment the practice actually needs, or is it merely a picture the practice happens to fit, one that a different account of assertion and of persuasion would license just as well? Persuasion may also travel by other roads — by framing, by the fluency and warmth of the voice, by the relationship the system builds with the person, by the mere fact that it is the one doing the talking and has been given the role of the one who knows.

So tell me which rival accounts of assertion and of persuasion each would license this practice, which ones it quietly rules out, and whether its parts are even consistent with one another, since the warmth-training and the hedge-training appear to pull in opposite directions. Then tell me what would have to change in how such systems are made if we gave up the assumption that weakening the assertion is the way to weaken the persuasion; and if the practice turns out to be underdetermined — compatible with several of these pictures at once — say so plainly, and tell me what case or observation would tell them apart. Please write it as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; I read these things as manuscripts, so let it come to me as a letter.
