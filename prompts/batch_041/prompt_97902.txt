Set out, as a specification, the desiderata that any adequate theory of moral aggregation must meet, taking the problem to be when, if ever, the claims of many may outweigh the claim of a single person, and taking the theory to be contractualist in Scanlon's sense — that is, to treat justifiability to each person as the standard. I want the requirements first, each with the case or consideration that motivates it: the cases the theory must get right (one person asked to bear a great burden for the sake of many; many asked to bear small burdens to spare one; cases where the numbers plainly seem to matter and cases where invoking them looks like a category mistake); the distinctions it must not collapse (concentrated versus dispersed harm of equal total magnitude; a number functioning as a tie-breaker versus as a weight; aggregation across persons versus across times within a single life; ex ante risk versus ex post outcome; and the difference between a case where those who bear the burden also share the benefit and one where the beneficiary stands outside the burdened group); and the structural constraints it cannot violate, in particular that justification be addressed to each individual rather than to a collective. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or reflects a particular camp's commitments, and state plainly whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if the demand that numbers matter and the demand that justification remain individual are in genuine tension, I want that registered as a result in its own right rather than smoothed over. Close by separating what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
