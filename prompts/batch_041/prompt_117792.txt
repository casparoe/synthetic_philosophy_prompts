I'm in a reading group on AI safety and one of the guys in it has a favourite move. Every time inverse reinforcement learning comes up he says the same thing: human values are the source code, behaviour is the compiled binary, and IRL is just decompilation — you never get to see the source, but you feed the binary to a good enough decompiler and the logic falls out. His conclusion is that aligning a model with human values is essentially an observation and data problem rather than a conceptual one: the only reason we can't read off the reward function is that we haven't watched enough humans, and better and more plentiful demonstrations will close the gap. I'm suspicious, but I can't quite say why, partly because I actually find the analogy illuminating and partly because when I push back he just says "obviously it's not *literally* a compiler" and I don't know how much that concession costs him. Also he's a bit smug about it, which I realise is not an argument.

Can you audit it for me? I want the correspondences laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the source code, the compiler, the binary, the decompiler, the test suite, the build flags, and whatever else is doing quiet work — and for each one whether the mapping holds, half-holds, or fails, with a note on why. Then the disanalogies, but only the ones that matter for *his* conclusion specifically, i.e. the "it's just a data problem" bit; I don't need a general list of ways computers differ from people. Two things I'd especially like you to settle: whether the ambiguity in going from behaviour back to objective is a practical nusiance that more demonstrations fix, or something that extra data can't touch; and whether the fact that humans can't be re-run, don't ship with a fixed pre-compiled objective, and change what they want in response to being observed (or to being optimised against) makes any real difference, as opposed to just making the dataset messier. Finally, does his conclusion survive once the breaks are on the table — and if it doesn't, what analogy would do the work he wants without smuggling his conclusion in with it?

Format: a JSON object, and please lead with the verdict rather than burying it, so start the object with "conclusion_verdict" and "conclusion_reasoning". Fields: "conclusion_verdict" (one of "survives", "survives_only_with_changes", "collapses"), "conclusion_reasoning" (a few sentences), "correspondences" (array of objects with keys "source", "target", "holds" — one of "holds", "partial", "fails" — and "why"), "disanalogies" (array of objects with keys "break", "why_it_breaks", "matters_for_his_conclusion" as a boolean, and "note"), "ambiguity_verdict" (a short string on whether the non-identifiability of the objective is fixable with data), "better_analogy" (an object with keys "analogy", "what_it_fixes", "what_it_still_misses"), and "smugness_note" (one line, in case I need it).
