I have come around to a version of what is often called the afterlife conjecture, and I want an honest audit of how exposed it is. The view, as I would state it, is this. The distinctive badness of human extinction — the part that is not simply the sum of the deaths of whoever happens to be alive when it happens — consists in its foreclosing the possibility that there will be future people who remember us, care about us, and carry forward something they regard as theirs and ours. On this reading the harm does not fall primarily on the never-born. It falls on us, the living, because the value we now find in our projects and attachments is sustained by an implicit expectation that we will be survived in that way; remove the expectation and the valuing is undercut. Call this the survival-condition reading: extinction is bad because it strips the present of a background condition on which present valuation depends.

What I want is a map of what would count against the survival-condition reading. Separate the kinds of defeater: empirical ones (claims about what people actually value, how the reactive attitudes behave, and whether the posited dependence shows up in anyone's psychology, including in cases of anticipated extinction or in communities that already expect no collective afterlife); conceptual ones (whether a harm that is conditional on our caring is a genuine harm or merely a frustrated preference, whether it collapses into a preference-satisfaction account and thereby loses its distinctiveness, whether the dead or the never-born can figure in it at all, and whether the view covertly requires an immortal subject of concern); and structural ones (whether the view can explain why extinction is bad rather than merely why it is feared, and whether it makes the badness of extinction hostage to a contingent psychological fact in a way its defenders should find embarrassing).

Sort these by how decisive they are, not by how often they are rehearsed, and say which ones a committed defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it rather than dismissing as confusion or as changing the subject. Tell me what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and which of those predictions have already been tested by events or by ordinary experience. Then give me an unflinching verdict on insulation: is the survival-condition reading falsifiable, or has it been qualified until nothing could count against it, and if the latter, is that a fatal defect or an acceptable feature of an account of this kind?

I would also like the view run against a set of cases, since I suspect it lives or dies on them. Consider the Unremembering Future, in which humanity survives for a very long time but retains no knowledge of or concern for us, having passed through an amnesia so complete that we are not even a myth; the Replacement Future, in which humans go extinct but are succeeded by flourishing non-human minds — posthumans, uplifted animals, or machines — who care about their own past but not about us; the Contemptuous Survivors, who remember us and hold us in contempt; the Last Generation, where extinction arrives only after everyone now alive has died, so that no living person is deprived of anything; and the Inheritors Who Care, in which our successors are non-human but do remember and value us. For each, say whether the survival-condition reading classifies it as bad, as not bad, or as an unresolved case, and whether those verdicts are the ones the view ought to want. Where the verdicts come apart from what the rival accounts — lost future welfare, the impersonal value of the future, the value of an ongoing civilizational project, the thwarted interests of future people themselves — would say, make the disagreement explicit, since that is where the view earns or loses its keep.
