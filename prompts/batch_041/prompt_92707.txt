I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar in the philosophy of biology, and I also work about ten hours a week as an editorial assistant for a publisher that produces a digital high-school biology textbook. My job is line-editing and flagging claims for review, and the same problem keeps returning: sentences that say the function of some trait is to do something. There are hundreds of them across the book, I have to clear roughly forty a week, and I have about three minutes per sentence. I have no research budget, no access to the primary literature on most of these organisms, and no background in the taxa in question; the writers I work with are generalists too. Our standards reviewer has begun returning pages with the note "unwarranted teleology," which nobody in the office can define.

The seminar has given me two ways of answering the question what the function of a trait is. On the first, a trait's function is what it was selected for: the effect that explains why the trait is present in this lineage, given its history. On this view a trait that exists for no such reason has no function at all, however useful it may be, and a trait that was selected for one effect and is now used for another has the function it was selected for rather than the one it now serves. On the second, a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some capacity of the larger system it belongs to. On this view history is irrelevant: a trait can have a function in this sense even if it arose by accident, and the same trait can have as many functions as there are capacities you might ask about.

I need a procedure I can actually run, sentence by sentence, that uses these two accounts to decide what to do with a sentence. Please give me:

1. The steps in the order I should ask them, with a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and where the procedure stops with a verdict.
2. Three possible verdicts and when each applies: accept the sentence; rewrite it so that it asserts only what the evidence supports; send it to a consultant. For the rewrite option, include a formula a writer with no philosophy training can execute in two minutes.
3. The points where the procedure hands the decision to a person rather than settling it, and an honest account of which of those points are real judgment calls and which are merely places where I am ignorant.
4. What the procedure cannot do at all, and the kind of sentence it will get wrong.

Then run it on three sentences:

(a) The familiar case of feathers, where the standard account is that they arose in a lineage in a thermoregulatory or display context and were later co-opted for flight. What do I do with "the function of feathers is flight"?
(b) A gene inserted into a crop for pest resistance, in our biotechnology chapter, where there is no selection history at all. What does each account license me to say?
(c) A confident sentence about the function of a trait in a little-studied organism, where we have a clear description of what the trait does and no evidence whatever about its history.

I have also been told that a reviewer disputes the standard story about the giraffe's neck. I do not need you to settle that, but tell me how the procedure behaves when the experts themselves disagree about the history, since I cannot adjudicate that from my desk.

The one idea I have had so far is a test: before letting a sentence through, imagine the same organism with the same trait but with no history of selection for it at all — a mutant, or an engineered version — and ask whether the sentence would still be true. If it would, then the sentence is a claim about what the trait does rather than about what it is for, whatever it says. Tell me whether that test is sound, what it gets right, and where it breaks; if it is broken, replace it with something better.

Finally: at each step, say which of the two accounts is doing the work, and flag the steps where the two accounts would give different verdicts on the same sentence, because I suspect those are where the procedure is most fragile. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to kill a sentence I would rather keep. The procedure itself should fit on a page; the applications can run longer.
