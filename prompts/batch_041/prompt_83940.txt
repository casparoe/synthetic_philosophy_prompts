I'm a philosophy PhD student in the middle of a chapter on symmetry and surplus structure, in which the hole argument in general relativity is the main event, and I've found myself leaning on Black's two-spheres case as a warm-up toy model: two qualitatively identical iron spheres alone in space, and the question whether we have one possibility or two. My difficulty is that a comment from my supervisor, very much in the spirit of a referee report, has made me realize that my chapter's conclusion rides on a modal-metaphysics question I had been treating as obvious — namely, whether such a world is possible at all — and I would rather have a number with its weak points marked than another survey of positions. So: what is your credence that a world containing exactly two numerically distinct but qualitatively indiscernible spheres, with no other material objects and nothing else in the world to break the symmetry, is metaphysically possible? If you think the question is ill-posed rather than answerable, then give me your credence that it is ill-posed, and decompose that instead.

I want the decomposition, not just the figure. At minimum I would like a rough probability for each of the following, one line of justification apiece, and an account of how they combine into the final number: (i) whether the notion of a purely qualitative property can be drawn cleanly enough for the question to have a determinate answer at all, or whether the dispute is at bottom verbal; (ii) whether identity-involving properties — one sphere's being identical to A, or being the one my supervisor is currently thinking about — count as admissible grounds of discernibility, which seems to settle the case almost by stipulation; (iii) how much work the inference from conceivability to possibility is doing here, and how much credit our modal imagination deserves in a case this thin; (iv) whether the scenario is genuinely a case of two indiscernible objects or instead a case in which the description simply fails to pick out two things; (v) whether the impossibility of the case requires anti-haecceitism, and whether anti-haecceitism is independently plausible; and (vi) whether it matters whether space is substantival or relational, since on the substantivalist picture the spheres occupy distinct spacetime points and so might be discernible by their positions, which would make the case a miniature of the symmetry-related-model arguments I am actually writing about. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move it most; I care more about that than about the figure itself.

One further thing: please keep what is settled apart from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested reading of the case as though it were the consensus. Where a step in your decomposition is one that specialists actively dispute, flag it as such and give me some sense of how live the disagreement is.
