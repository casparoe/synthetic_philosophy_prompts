I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether the distinction between a state's being access-conscious and its being phenomenally conscious marks a real joint in nature or is an artifact of how we describe ourselves, and I would like an actual number from you rather than a tour of the positions. The claim I want priced is what he calls asymmetric illusionism: the view that illusionism is correct about phenomenal character but incorrect about phenomenal existence — that is, the specific felt quality of any given state is a misrepresentation generated by the brain's self-model rather than a property the state possesses, while there nevertheless really is something it is like to be the subject at all, and no amount of debunking the self-model removes that fact. He thinks this is obviously the stable resting place, since a global-workspace-style account of broadcast, control, and report seems to explain everything except the bare fact of experience, and that bare fact looks like the one thing you cannot explain away without explaining away the explanandum. I think the position is unstable, because once you have stripped a state of its character I cannot see what "there is something it is like" is supposed to add, and I suspect the residual intuition is just the self-model's own report doing its work under a different description. To make the disagreement concrete he offered a case I cannot decide about: two subjects with identical workspace dynamics — the same contents broadcast, the same downstream control, the same dispositions to report — whose self-models differ only in whether they register the subject as undergoing anything, and he says the first has phenomenology and the second does not. I say that if every functional and report-level fact is held fixed, the alleged difference is either unobservable or verbal, and I do not know which of those two it is.

Please give me your credence, as a number between 0 and 1, that asymmetric illusionism is the correct resolution, and then decompose it rather than defending it in prose. I would like a rough probability, with a one-line justification, for at least the following terms: (a) that illusionism about phenomenal character is true — that apparent felt quality is a self-model artifact rather than a real feature of the state; (b) that phenomenal existence is a genuine further fact over and above states being broadcast, available to control, and reportable; (c) that these two commitments are independent, so that (a) can hold while (b) fails without incoherence — this is the hinge, and I expect it to carry most of the weight; (d) that the question is empirically resolvable at all, rather than a dispute about how to use a word; and (e) that a mature theory will retain some revised form of the access/phenomenal distinction even if illusionism about character wins. Show me how the terms combine into your final number, tell me which single term you are least confident in and what evidence or argument would move it most, and say whether my friend's swapped-self-model case is even coherent as stated.

Finally, since the whole argument has become a dispute about where the dispute lives: my credence in asymmetric illusionism is about 0.3 and his is about 0.8. Tell me which of the terms above most likely accounts for that gap, and whether you think the remaining disagreement is one that further theorizing about global workspace architecture could settle or one that only a decision about what we are going to mean by "experience" could settle. If your answer is that the number is meaningless, say so, but say what you would put in its place and why that is not just a refusal to commit.
