I write about science and ideas for a living, and next month I am to give the after-dinner speech at a small workshop dinner marking the retirement of a philosopher of probability whom I have interviewed, on and off, for fifteen years. Her career has been one long argument that a rational person often should not put a single number on her uncertainty — that in many cases the honest state of mind is a range, and that the demand for a point value is a demand for a confidence nobody actually has; she was always insistent that this is not the same thing as being unsure about your own probabilities. The room will hold some thirty specialists who know her work by heart, her two grown-up children and her brother, and four or five decision theorists who spent their careers telling her she was wrong. I want six minutes that are warm and that her family can follow without being lectured at, but I do not want to pay her the compliment of pretending to a precision about her that I do not have: the speech should be candid wherever my evidence is thin, without collapsing into a welter of hedges, and it should concede out loud the one standard criticism of her position that I think is genuinely fair. Her doctrine should shape what the speech can and cannot say rather than decorate it. Please write it.
