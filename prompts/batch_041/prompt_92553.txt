I'm a third-year student in a seminar on moral change — the course is about how big moral shifts actually happen rather than about what the right answers are — and I've got a pattern stuck in my head that I can't find a word for, so I'm hoping you can help me carve it up. Take the role of someone who works on norms, honor and moral change, go slowly, and start by brainstorming the candidate concepts before you commit to one; also, please dont' just hand me a label and stop there. A bit of context for why I care: I have a response paper due Friday and this has eaten my week.

Here's the pattern. A practice that everyone treats as a matter of honor — dueling in Britain, foot binding in China, owning people across the British empire — sits there for decades while the argument against it is completely available. Everyone's read it, it gets reprinted, it gets preached from pulpits, and nothing moves. Then inside roughly a generation the thing is not merely rare but embarrassing, and the people who quit mostly don't announce that they've been persuaded of anything; they just stop, and later they write memoirs that don't mention it, and their sons can't imagine why anyone would ever have done it. Two details are what bug me. First, the arguments that get credited afterwards — the ones you find quoted in the retrospectives — are ususally not written by the people who actually stopped first, and plenty of them were written after the change was already under way. Second, the abandonment runs from the top down and the outside in: the elite and the cities quit while the practice is still going strong further down and further out, which is the opposite of what I'd expect if the argument had finally landed, since the argument is addressed to everyone equally. So it looks less like a case being won and more like the audience whose good opinion made the practice worth keeping quietly dispersing.

What I want to know is whether there's an established name for this specific mechanism — not for moral change in general, and not for the bare fact that things sometimes change fast. My flatmate says it's just a tipping point; a guy in my seminar says it's just that everyone secretly disapproved all along and was waiting for cover; my professor says it's really about who profits from the practice and who doesn't. I can see each of those catching part of it and I can also see where each misses — the "they secretly already disapproved" story seems plainly false to me, since people really did seem to think dueling was honorable rather than just saying so, and the elite-first pattern doesn't obviously fall out of the interest story either. I've been calling it "honor leakage" in my notes, on the theory that the practice runs on the approval of a small audience and dies when that audience stops paying attention, but I can't tell if that's a real mechanism or just a redescription of the change. So: if there's a name, or a family of near names, tell me which fits and, more importantly, exactly where it stops fitting — what the described thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. If nothing established covers it, coin something and give me a definition I could actually use, with at least one case that superficially looks the same but that your definition excludes, and tell me what evidence would show whether the argument is doing persuasive work or only coordinating a change that was already happening for other reasons. A bare name won't help me here.
