I have been reading a trade book on moral psychology — the kind written by a science journalist who surveys a research program and then draws large philosophical morals from it — and I would like to know how much of its central chapter to believe. The chapter concerns the finding, now generally called the Knobe effect, that people's willingness to say an agent brought an outcome about intentionally depends on how bad they take that outcome to be. The book's summary of the research runs roughly as follows.

'In the experiment, subjects read about a corporate executive who proposes a new program that will increase profits. As a side effect, the program will either harm or help the environment. In both versions the executive says that he does not care at all about the environment and cares only about profit. Subjects were then asked whether he brought about the environmental outcome intentionally. In the harm version, most said yes; in the help version, only a minority did. Knobe concluded that the concept of intentional action is a moral concept through and through: whether we call something intentional depends on whether we disapprove of it. Later work has found the same asymmetry in judgments of causation and of knowledge, and has found it for violations of norms that are not moral at all, which shows that the effect is really about evaluation in general and not about intentionality in particular. Since the two scenarios differ only in the moral valence of the outcome, and since the executive's intentions, beliefs, and indifference are held fixed, the experiment shows that the ordinary notion of acting intentionally is internally inconsistent: there is no fact of the matter about what he did on purpose. The philosophical upshot is that the armchair method of testing theories of action against intuitions about cases is bankrupt, and that moral judgment is not a faculty that operates on independent facts but a lens that constitutes the facts it judges.'

I am not competent to grade this, and I suspect it overstates matters, but I want to know precisely where. Please go through the summary claim by claim and sort what it says into three piles: what a researcher in this area would accept as accurate; what is a defensible simplification, or a live interpretive option rather than a mistake; and what is a genuine distortion — bearing in mind that the book is summarizing a real body of work, so I need to know which of its errors are errors of emphasis and which are the kind of thing a specialist would call simply wrong. Where the summary attributes a conclusion to Knobe, say whether the conclusion is his or the book's. The summary also seems to me to pull in two directions at once: it says the concept of intentional action is moral through and through, and it also says the effect is not about intentionality in particular. Tell me whether that is a real tension in the literature or merely careless prose.

Finally, a friend of mine who works on this tells me that the experiment cannot by itself distinguish between two readings: that the effect reveals something about the content of the concept of intentional action, and that it reveals a bias in how people apply a concept whose content is perfectly ordinary. The book appears not to notice the distinction. Explain what turns on it, and say whether the book's closing claims — that the ordinary notion is internally inconsistent, that armchair philosophy of action is bankrupt, and that moral judgment constitutes the facts it judges — survive once the summary has been corrected. I am not asking for a reading list; I want the audit.
