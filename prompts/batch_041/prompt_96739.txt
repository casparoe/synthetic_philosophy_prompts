I am organizing an adversarial collaboration on moral trade. By that I mean the arrangement in which two parties, each moved by their own moral priorities, agree to act in ways that advance the other's priorities, in order to secure action that advances their own. The idea is familiar enough in recent work on effective altruism and in the older literature on logrolling and vote trading, but I want the concept treated from first principles rather than reported.

The two parties are these.

The market camp holds that moral trade is a legitimate and often desirable form of exchange; that the ordinary case for voluntary trade — that it lets each party get more of what it values than it could get alone — carries over to the moral case; that the party who cares about animal suffering and the party who cares about, say, existential risk can each do better in their own terms by trading than by acting unilaterally; and that there is nothing specially corrupt about an act performed because it was part of a trade rather than from direct conviction.

The integrity camp holds that moral trade is either incoherent or corrupting. On the incoherence horn: there is no interpersonal metric in which the gains can be measured, so the appearance of mutual benefit is an artifact of describing each side's private ranking as though it were a common currency. On the corruption horn: to treat one's commitments as bargaining chips is to stand in the wrong relation to them, and an act done to secure a counterpart's cooperation is not the same act, morally, as the same act done from conviction.

Write the joint statement these two camps could put their names to. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — definitional and structural points both camps accept. Include what does and does not count as a moral trade; the difference between trading with someone who shares your values but differs in priorities and trading with someone whose values you reject; what each camp is willing to mean by "mutual benefit"; and what both accept about ordinary market exchange as an analogy or a disanalogy.

The formulations must be neither vacuous nor slanted; if you cannot phrase a view so its holder would sign it, say so and explain what the obstacle is.

Consider at least the following candidate cruxes, adding any others you find and dropping any you judge idle. First, commensurability: does "mutual benefit" require a shared metric, or is improvement in each party's own terms sufficient, and does anything follow from the fact that an exchange rate between two moral currencies can only be defined privately, by each party for itself? Second, motive and the moral status of the traded act: take the same donation to a cause, made first from conviction, then as one leg of a trade, then in response to a threat; do these differ in moral value, and if so in what respect? Third, non-tradable goods: are there acts that may not be made the object of a trade even when the end is good, and what marks them off? Fourth, the logrolling analogy: two legislators trade votes on unrelated bills, each thereby getting something he values less than what he gives up in the other's eyes but more in his own; does the analogy to moral trade illuminate or mislead? Fifth, publicity: would a world in which moral trades are openly made and priced corrode the dispositions that make moral conduct reliable, and is that a reason against the practice or merely against advertising it?
