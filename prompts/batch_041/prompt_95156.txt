I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on formal epistemology, and I have to write a final essay on a question our seminar circled for two weeks without settling: whether logical uncertainty is a genuine kind of uncertainty at all, or whether the phrase names something else — the computational limits of a bounded reasoner, or a non-probabilistic attitude dressed up in probabilistic vocabulary. I would like the essay to take a side and defend it, not to catalogue positions.

The case against the coherence of the notion goes roughly as follows. A credence function is a probability measure over a set of possibilities, and a possibility that fails to determine the truth value of some sentence is not really a possibility. Any logically valid sentence is therefore true at every possibility and must receive credence 1, and whenever one sentence entails another the conditional credence must be 1. On this picture there is no room in the space of possibilities for logic itself to be uncertain, and all that remains when I report being sixty per cent confident in an unproved conjecture is a fact about how much computation I have performed — an epistemic failing rather than a genuine partial belief about a necessary matter. If that is right, the only escapes are to relax the underlying picture (possibilities that leave truth values open, or worlds at which logical truths fail) or to abandon the requirement that credences be probabilities; I would like the essay to say which escape, if either, is worth taking.

The case on the other side is what drew me to the topic. In the work on logical induction we read in week seven, the object is a computable procedure that assigns probabilities to every sentence of a formal language and refines them over time: it learns to predict patterns of truth and falsehood long before it can evaluate the sentences themselves, it assigns something like ten per cent to the claim that the nth digit of pi is a 7, it assigns probabilities to claims about its own probabilities, and it learns to trust its future beliefs more than its present ones. Its beliefs are coherent only in the limit; at every finite stage they violate the probability axioms, and that incoherence is not an incidental blemish but the price of computability.

I want the essay to adjudicate this, and to do four things in particular. First, separate three things that get run together: graded attitudes towards logically valid sentences, towards necessary but not formally valid mathematical claims, and towards the outputs of one's own reasoning — a partial belief, say, that one's proof search will never output a contradiction. Say which of these, if any, deserves the name logical uncertainty, and whether the term picks out one phenomenon or three. Second, press the objection that if coherence arrives only in the limit, and a bounded agent stops or is destroyed long before the limit, then the agent's actual credences are never probabilities; ask whether the framework is explaining logical uncertainty or dissolving it by idealisation. Third, confront the self-referential pressure: no consistent, computable theory of arithmetic proves its own consistency, and none proves that whatever it proves is true, since that would yield a proof of its own consistency — so trust in one's own reasoning must be partial or non-probabilistic. Does that show the framework's demands on self-trust cannot be met, or only that self-trust comes in degrees? Fourth, reach a verdict about whether logical uncertainty is coherent and, if so, of what. A qualified verdict is welcome — for instance, that it is coherent for mathematical necessity but not for logical validity — provided the qualification is earned rather than used to dodge commitment. Please also say what would have to be true for the opposite verdict to be correct.
