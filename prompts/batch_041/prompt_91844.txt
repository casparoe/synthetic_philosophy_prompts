Next month I am giving the closing address at a two-day symposium on evidence and belief that has been deliberately built as a collision between two tribes: analytic epistemologists who work on the ethics of belief, and senior investigative reporters and standards editors who run verification desks. I am a science and ideas journalist of twenty years' standing, invited because an essay of mine on how reporters decide what to believe has managed to irritate both camps. I have fifty minutes and then an hour of questions, and I would rather spend the flight rehearsing than improvising.

The thesis I intend to defend is this: the evidential threshold at which belief becomes permissible is not a single, person-invariant line. It is partly a function of the believer's role, and more precisely of the error-correcting structure of the practice she inhabits. Two people holding the same evidence may differ in what they are permitted to believe, because one is a reader alone with a claim and the other is a reporter inside a practice that will corroborate, publish, and, if necessary, retract. What licenses the reporter's earlier and thinner belief is not the strength of her evidence but the recoverability of her error: if she is wrong, the practice is designed to catch it before it does lasting harm. I call this the recoverability condition, and I offer it as the newsroom-usable descendant of the conditions James laid down for believing without sufficient evidence — a live, forced, momentous option that the evidence alone cannot settle.

The flashpoint will be my anchor case. A source will speak only to a reporter who already believes her. The belief is a precondition of obtaining the evidence that would justify it; withhold it and the evidence never arrives. I argue that a belief can be permissible precisely because it helps bring about the evidence that later vindicates it, and that the licence attaches to the inquiry rather than to publication.

Here is the room. Roughly half are epistemologists — temperamentally evidentialist, several of whom work on permissivism, the view that a body of evidence may permit more than one doxastic response, and on pragmatic encroachment, the view that practical stakes can affect whether a belief counts as knowledge. One or two of them think the whole ethics of belief is a category mistake. The other half are working journalists and standards editors who have spent their careers insisting that you do not believe a source, you verify a source, and who regard belief as a word with no place in a newsroom. Two media scholars will also be present; they hold that the value-free ideal for science was always a fiction and will argue that my problem is that I have not gone far enough.

The room knows the James and Clifford essays cold, so I can take the standard moves for granted; I do not need citations or a reading list. What I need is a Q&A preparation sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually receive, ordered by how much damage they would do to the thesis, each phrased in the idiom of the person who will raise it, each with a two-minute reply and a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which concessions I should make in my opening rather than be forced into. Tell me which single reply must land for the thesis to survive the hour, and which of the two tribes poses the greater danger to me. And tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one I will have no good answer to.
