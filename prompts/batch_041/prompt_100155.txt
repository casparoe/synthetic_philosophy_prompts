I am helping design a small research program -- roughly eight scholars, three years, philosophy and formal theory rather than empirical statistics -- on the conceptual foundations of aggregation and disaggregation in causal and statistical reasoning, the family of puzzles of which Simpson's paradox is the best-known instance. The remit is deliberately narrow. The program will not fund data collection or applied epidemiology; it will fund work on what it means for a group-level and a subgroup-level answer to diverge, which of them a reasoner or a policymaker ought to act on, and what would have to be true of the world or of the question for the divergence to be resolvable at all.

I want a defended agenda rather than a survey. Which open questions in this area most deserve the program's attention, and in what order? For each, explain why it is upstream of the others -- what later work would be blocked or wasted if the question were left unsettled -- whether it is tractable with the tools philosophers and formal theorists actually have rather than requiring new mathematics, and what developments over the next two or three years would move it up or down the list.

Several constraints should shape the ranking. First, I am not interested in questions whose answer is already settled by the standard causal-inference apparatus; if the only remaining work is applying an existing formalism, say so and rank it low. Second, the program cares both about the epistemic question (what a rational agent should believe when the levels disagree) and about the practical question (what an agent who must act on a single population should do), and I want to know whether these come apart. Third, I want the agenda to engage a case I find under-theorized: an intervention that is beneficial within every identifiable subgroup yet harmful in the aggregate, where the aggregate harm is produced not by confounding but by the fact that the intervention's own uptake reshapes the composition of the groups, so that the subgroups one conditions on are partly constituted by the treatment itself. Treat that case as a stress test. Does the usual distinction between the "right" and the "wrong" level survive it, and which of the open questions you list does it most sharply raise?

Finally, include at least one question you believe is currently unfashionable or wrongly neglected, and say what would have to happen for the field to take it seriously.
