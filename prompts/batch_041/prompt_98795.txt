I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in economics or philosophy, and I've been handed an unglamorous design task: specifying the rule that divides a shared, divisible resource between two internal teams — concretely, a fixed monthly pool of GPU-hours — in the case where the teams fail to agree on a division themselves and a documented fallback allocation therefore applies. A colleague with some game theory background has proposed basing the rule on the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution rather than on an even split or on a Nash-product rule, and I need to judge whether that is a good idea. Please set out the most important implications of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution for a rule of this kind: what adopting it actually commits the rule to, how its behavior in the cases we are likely to encounter differs from that of the obvious alternatives, and which of its properties should be expected to become liabilities. Two features of our setting seem relevant and I would like them addressed explicitly: the set of feasible divisions keeps expanding as the teams find new ways to cooperate, and each team can shift the fallback point by threatening to do less.
