A friend in my philosophy of mind reading group has a favorite way of explaining the color phi phenomenon, and I want to know whether the analogy can bear the weight he puts on it. The phenomenon, as he describes it: two dots are flashed in quick succession at different points on a screen, the second a different color from the first, and observers report a single dot traveling from the first position to the second and changing color somewhere along the way, even though nothing moved and no dot of the intermediate color was ever displayed. My friend says this shows that the brain is essentially a film editor. On his picture the raw footage is what the eyes take in, the editor is whatever assembles the final cut, and what we consciously see is the broadcast. He then says the editor could be doing one of two things: either it inserts the missing frames before anything is screened, so that the experience of motion and of the color change is manufactured on the fly and shown to us as if it had been there all along, or it leaves the show alone and instead re-cuts the archive afterward, so that what we remember seeing is a version that never aired. He calls these the show-trial option and the memory-hole option, borrowing the names of the two totalitarian styles of rewriting history, and he concludes from the whole business that the timing of experience is an illusion — that consciousness is a construction assembled after the fact rather than a record of what happened.

How far does the analogy actually hold? I would like the correspondence set out explicitly — what plays the role of the footage, the editor, the cutting room, the broadcast, the airtime, and the archive — and then the places where the mapping breaks. In particular I want to know which features of film editing the analogy quietly imports that the brain may not have: the assumption that there is an unedited original existing before the editing, the assumption of an editor distinct from what is edited, the assumption of a screen and an audience for the broadcast. If those are the load-bearing parts, say what becomes of the conclusion. Please separate the disanalogies that matter for the use he is making of the analogy from the ones that are merely decorative.

Two further things. First, the analogy seems to presuppose a definite timeline — the footage exists, then it is edited, then it is shown — and that timeline is precisely what is in dispute; can the analogy be stated at all without already deciding the question it is meant to illuminate? Second, my friend treats the show-trial and memory-hole options as two live hypotheses that further evidence could choose between. Is that right, or does the analogy make the choice look more substantial than it is, given that the two options seem to yield the same thing at the only place we can check, namely the report? If the distinction cannot be cashed out in anything we could in principle observe, tell me what that does to the analogy and to the conclusion he draws from it.

Finally, if film editing is the wrong source domain, is there a better one? I am not after a slogan; I want an analogy whose structure does not smuggle in the very things the phenomenon calls into question, along with a clear statement of what survives of the claim that the timing of experience is an illusion once the disanalogies are on the table.
