Next month I'm presenting at a political theory workshop and I need to be ready for the Q&A. My thesis: dirty hands in politics is real, but we've been describing it wrong. On the standard picture, the officeholder who authorizes something heinous -- a torture, a dirty war, a broken promise that saves a country -- has done genuine wrong and therefore owes us his guilt; the anguish is supposed to be the proof that he's still decent and that the wrong wasn't merely "worth it." I want to argue thats backwards. The wrong is produced by the structure of the office, not the soul of the man, and the demand for his remorse does two bad things at once: it privatizes a problem that belongs to the institution, and it buys us a catharsis that quietly substitutes for actually fixing the office. I'm also denying that there's some "remainder" of guilt he's obliged to carry afterward. So the remedy isn't a suffering politician, it's a redesigned one.

The room is mostly people who work on public ethics. Two of them are old-school on this and think the residue and the guilt are the whole point -- a politics without them is just managerial ethics with a body count. A couple of institutionalists who'll like where I end up but think I've cheated my way there. And one Kantian who thinks the whole phenomenon is a confusion and that anyone who says "he did wrong but rightly" is talking nonsense.
