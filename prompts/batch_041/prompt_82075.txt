I have spent three years writing a novel whose two central characters — a Kantian physician and a utilitarian economist, married to each other and to their arguments — keep quarreling across the same dinner table, and it has lately occurred to me that since I am the one holding the pen, I could at least make them quarrel well. What I want from you is the document they should have written together: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the camp that holds the paradox of deontology to be fatal (once you demand a rationale for constraints, the rationale either collapses into the very aggregation the constraints forbid or dwindles into a bare taboo) and the camp that holds the paradox to be an artifact of a mis-stated rationale (so that constraints are vindicated rather than embarrassed by their own justification). In my notes I have been calling these the Ledger camp and the Separateness camp, purely as my own shorthand, and I would like those labels kept, but the substance must be the real thing: a deontologist and a critic who have read each other closely and are prepared to sign the same page.

Draft it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims — what a constraint is being claimed to be (agent-relative, a prohibition on intending harm as a means rather than a prohibition on causing it, and so on), which parts of the terrain both sides agree are not in dispute (I take it neither of them thinks the debate simply reduces to the doing/allowing distinction, and both should say so explicitly if so), what form a rationale would have to take to count as a rationale at all, and whether the alleged paradox is best read as a formal inconsistency or as a pragmatic tension that only bites when you try to state the rationale. Second, the residual disagreements, each stated in a formulation its own side would accept as faithful — not a strawman and not a blur — followed by a table. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each crux the case or argument both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I expect at least these three: whether the justification of a constraint belongs at the level of the individual act or at the level of the practice of having such constraints; whether the separateness of persons is a genuine premise or a restatement of the conclusion; and whether using someone merely as a means can be cashed out without smuggling consequences back in. For the second of these, and any others where it helps, give them a shared test case — I have been using two variants of a ferry disaster in the novel, one in which the single passenger is deliberately put in the path of the hazard to slow it and one in which he is merely foreseeably drowned by a manoeuvre aimed elsewhere, with the same numbers saved in both — and have each side write down what it predicts about the two variants before either is allowed to explain the prediction away. Finally, if you judge that part of the quarrel was only ever verbal — a disagreement about how to describe a case rather than about what to do in it — say which part, and why the two of them could have stopped arguing years ago had they noticed.
