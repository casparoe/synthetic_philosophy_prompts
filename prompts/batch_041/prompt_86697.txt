My father has been in hospice, and I recently lied to him to spare him a painful truth. I still cannot decide whether that was a deontological exception or a consequentialist calculation, and the paradox of deontology has stopped being an academic puzzle for me. I am also a teaching assistant for an upper-level ethics course, and I am grading the midterm. The question and one student's answer are below. Please give the answer a grade out of 20 and then point-by-point comments that justify it. I would like specific comments tied to the answer, not a general impression: what it gets right, what is subtly off, and what does not follow.

Question: The paradox of deontology is often put this way: deontological constraints forbid us from doing certain things even when doing them would prevent greater harm. If morality is supposed to make the world go better, a constraint that makes the world go worse seems irrational. Consider the following response, which I will call the separate-authors reply. The constraint is not a rule for producing the best outcome; it is a rule that defines the terms of cooperation among agents who are each the author of their own life. On this view, each agent is responsible for what she does, not for what others do, and the point of the constraint is to protect each person from being conscripted into another's project, even a project of minimizing harm. Does this reply resolve the paradox, or does it merely restate the deontological commitment in different words? In your answer, explain what the paradox is, assess the separate-authors reply, and say whether it can account for a case in which refusing to violate a constraint foreseeably leads to many more deaths. A strong answer will distinguish the question of whether constraints are rational from the question of whether they are absolute.

Student answer: The paradox of deontology is that deontological constraints forbid us from doing certain things even when doing them would prevent greater harm. If morality is supposed to make the world better, then a constraint that makes the world worse seems irrational. The separate-authors reply says that morality is not one agent's maximization project. It is a set of rules for many agents, each of whom is the author of her own life. So if I refuse to kill one person to prevent five killings, I am not responsible for the five killings; I am responsible only for my own act. The reply therefore explains why the constraint is not irrational: it protects me from being used as a tool for others' ends. It also shows that constraints are not just rules of thumb, because a rule of thumb can be outweighed by consequences, while a constraint cannot. The reply is convincing because it shows that if I violate the constraint, I make myself the author of a wrong, and the fact that this would prevent others from doing wrong does not transfer their agency to me. However, the reply does not fully resolve the paradox. In a case where refusing to violate the constraint foreseeably leads to many more deaths, the separate-authors reply can only say that those deaths are not my doing. But the paradox was about whether the constraint is rational, not about who is responsible. If the reply is just a restatement that each agent must follow the rules, it has not given a reason to accept the rules. A better reply would say that the constraint has a threshold: it is not absolute, and when the harm prevented is sufficiently large, the constraint can be overridden. That would make deontology more like a prerogative than a restriction, because it would allow an agent to choose the lesser evil. But then the constraint is no longer doing the work it was supposed to do.
