A popular history of economic ideas that I have been reading devotes a chapter to Adam Smith on empire, and its summary of his position has been nagging at me. I know Smith wrote at length about the American colonies in The Wealth of Nations, and I know he was no friend of the mercantile system, but the chapter's account feels tidier than the original can possibly be, and I would like an audit before I repeat any of it in a seminar.

The chapter's account runs roughly as follows. Smith, as the founding theorist of free trade, regarded the whole colonial enterprise as a mistake — a system of monopoly and privilege that enriched a handful of merchants at the expense of everyone else. Since the colonies existed chiefly to serve that monopoly, and since Britain bore the cost of defending them while the profits went to the merchant interest, Smith concluded that the colonies were economically worthless to the mother country and that Britain should cut them loose. Consistent with this, he condemned the Navigation Acts as a straightforward piece of mercantilist folly. He also predicted that the American colonies would eventually break away, and he thought this was the desirable outcome. The chapter closes by calling Smith the first anti-imperialist economist — a man who saw that empire is always a bad bargain.

I suspect some of this is right and some is the kind of smoothing that happens when a complex eighteenth-century argument gets compressed into a chapter. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a historian of economic thought would accept, what they would call a fair simplification, and what is actually wrong. Where the chapter's conclusions depend on a misreading, say so explicitly, and tell me what Smith's own recommendation with respect to the American colonies actually was.

Please structure the response as follows:

1. The summary, sentence by sentence — take each claim in turn and give it a verdict, with brief reasons. (Roughly half the response.)

2. The two or three places where the summary is most misleading — explain what Smith held instead, and why the difference matters. (Roughly a third of the response.)

3. What survives — which of the chapter's claims I can safely repeat, and which I should drop. (A short closing section.)
