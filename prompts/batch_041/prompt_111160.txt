I have come round to epiphenomenalism about phenomenal properties: experiences are caused by physical goings-on but cause nothing in turn, and every behavioural fact — reports, discriminations, avowals, aesthetic preferences — is fully accounted for by the physical states on which the experiences supervene. I want an honest map of the view's exposure rather than reassurance, so tell me what would count against it, what if anything it predicts that its rivals do not, and where its defenders would themselves concede that it is vulnerable.

Separate the considerations that would genuinely tell against it from those that merely feel uncomfortable. Among the former I would expect the self-undermining worry — often called the paradox of phenomenal judgement — that if experiences are causally inert then no judgement to the effect that things seem thus-and-so can be an effect of how things seem, together with the various defusing moves open to the epiphenomenalist: a purely functional account of how such judgements are formed; the claim that their content is fixed by the physical state that carries the report rather than by the experience reported; and the suggestion, which I am tempted by, that what we call introspection is really perception directed at our own physical states and never at our experiences. Say whether that last move is coherent, whether it buys the epiphenomenalist what it needs, and what it costs. I would also expect the evolutionary objection, and the question whether it shows more than that experiences are nomologically tied to what is selected; and the causal-exclusion family of arguments, with a verdict on whether they are selective or would sink any non-reductive physicalism alike. For each, say whether a committed epiphenomenalist would concede it, dismiss it as question-begging, or be genuinely exposed by it, and order the whole lot by how much they should move me, from the ones that would settle the matter to the ones that are merely awkward.

Then the harder question: does the position as I have stated it risk anything at all? If a counterfactual or difference-making account of causation makes the experiences count as causes because their physical bases do the work, is epiphenomenalism being rescued too cheaply, and is that a defect or simply the view's proper resting place? And if the covariation between phenomenal character and physical state is brute — if two creatures could differ phenomenally while matching physically, with no trace in behaviour — is that a commitment the view can live with, or a reason to prefer an identity theory, or something panpsychist, that explains the covariation rather than positing it? What would move me most is a case in which the physical story is complete and yet phenomenal character still seems to do explanatory work that the physical story cannot absorb; if you think no such case is possible, say why the demand is misplaced rather than manufacturing one.
