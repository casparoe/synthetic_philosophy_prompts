I have been trying, without much success, to explain to a skeptical friend why breaking a promise is wrong in a way that is about the person you promised and not just about the social costs of unreliable behavior. Every account I try gets some cases right and stumbles on others, and before I commit to one I want the specification rather than another candidate. So write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of promissory obligation must deliver — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must respect, the phenomena it must explain, and the results it cannot contradict — with the consideration behind each requirement.

To fix the target: I am after an account of the moral bindingness of a promise, not of promising as a social institution, and not of when a promise is legally enforceable. The account should explain why a breach wrongs the particular person it was made to, and it should be usable in ordinary conversation, so it cannot rest on machinery that only a specialist would accept.

Here are the requirements I can already see. Add to them, prune them, reorganize them, and for each say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

First, the creation problem. A voluntary act — uttering certain words with a certain intention — appears to generate a reason for action where none existed, which is why critics call it alchemy. Any theory must either explain how this happens or deny that it does, without invoking magic.

Second, directedness. The obligation is owed to the promisee: ordinarily only that person can release it, that person can waive it, and a bystander cannot. The theory must respect the difference between how strong a reason is and whose reason it is.

Third, near-arbitrary content. I can promise the trivially optional — a specific brand of tea — and thereby come to owe it; and I can promise to refrain from something otherwise permissible, such as staying out of a dispute, thereby making my help wrong rather than merely unnecessary. A theory that only covers promises to do things you already had reason to do will miss this.

Fourth, immoral content. A promise to do something seriously wrong does not bind, yet bindingness cannot be made hostage to whatever the promisor decides later counts as wrong.

Fifth, independence from reliance. A promise binds even when the promisee never relies on it and would suffer nothing from breach; reliance may add reasons but cannot be the source.

Sixth, coercion and threat. A "promise" extracted by threat seems not to bind, or binds only weakly, while some related obligation may still arise. The theory must separate promise from threat and still explain why they feel so similar from the inside.

Seventh, forgetting and death. If I promise something minor and then forget it, does the claim survive? If I promise a dying person to do something small, does it bind after they are gone? If I promise something in a game, where both parties know the words are not meant, does anything bind at all?

Eighth, self-promises. Whether or not they bind, the theory should have a principled story about whether they are the same phenomenon.

Ninth, release. A promise can be canceled, ordinarily only by the promisee, and a promise to release can itself bind — so the theory needs an account of how obligations are extinguished, not only how they arise.

I suspect at least one pair here cannot be jointly satisfied: the demand that bindingness be independent of reliance and social utility seems to pull against the demand that its source be something non-mysterious, and the directedness requirement seems to pull against any purely practice-based account. Tell me whether that is right, and which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out. Finish with a brief verdict on how the main families fare against the list — the view that bindingness comes from a social convention or practice, the reliance view, the view that it comes from a second-personal claim or the promisee's authority, and the view that promising is one instance of a broader power to create obligations. Keep the weight on the criteria; I will do the scoring myself if it comes to that.
