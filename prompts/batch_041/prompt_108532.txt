ok so this has abruptly become my problem and I need to think it through properly. I'm a paralegal at a small human rights outfit and I've ended up on a working group pushing for a special tribunal to prosecute aggression over the invasion of Ukraine — the ICC can't reach it for jurisdictional reasons, so there's a campaign for a separate court, and the whole thing keeps running into the objection that you can't try people for something that wasn't a crime when they did it. Every time that comes up, someone in the room says "Nuremberg, it's settled" and we move on. I've started to suspect that phrase is carrying a lot of weight nobody has actually checked.

So take the actual practice at Nuremberg and Tokyo — not the speeches about them, the practice. They charged and convicted people for waging aggressive war when that wasn't a crime in any treaty or statute you could have pointed to at the time; the novelty of the charge was the whole point. They more or less refused to let a defendant say he was only following orders. They gave themselves a charter, defence counsel, rules of evidence, the full apparatus of a court. And the crimes-against-humanity count only reached atrocities tied to the war itself, so the same mass murder committed in peacetime would have been nobody's business — which is a strange line to draw if the point is that certain acts are wrong no matter what. Meanwhile the people who set the whole thing up had, on their own side, done plenty that looked a lot like what was in the indictment, and nobody was ever going to try an Allied air marshal for area bombing.

What I want from you is the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Not a history. Which views about law does it only make sense under — does it need there to be some law above the enacted law that reason can find on its own, i.e. does it quietly require that legal positivism is false in at least some cases, or can a positivist who thinks validity just is whatever the rule of recognition says absorb the whole thing without embarassment? Does refusing the superior-orders defence commit it to a particular view about individual moral responsibility inside institutions, and if so which one? What does that peacetime/wartime line presuppose about sovereignty — that what a state does to its own is only the world's concern once the state has broken the peace? And the selective enforcement: does the practice presuppose that law's authority depends on being applied impartially to everyone, in which case it refutes itself, or is impartiality a separate commitment it doesn't actually need?

Then tell me where the practice is underdetermined — which parts several different theories would equally license, and which parts it genuinely forces — and what actually changes downstream if you swap the presupposed view for a rival. Does our special-tribunal campaign lose something, or gain?

Be committed. Don't lay out five positions and tell me to pick; tell me which reconstruction you'd actually defend and why, and where you'd bet against yourself.
