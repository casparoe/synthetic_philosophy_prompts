I am preparing a short session for a reading group on social ontology, and I have been trying to get clear about a picture of joint action that keeps coming up in our discussions. On this picture, two or more people do something together when four things hold at once: first, each of them intends the activity itself and not merely their own contribution to it; second, their plans interlock, in the sense that what each intends to do is precisely what the other's plan calls on them to do; third, each is disposed to adjust what they are doing in the light of what the other is doing or is about to do; and fourth, each knows that the first three things hold of the other, and knows that the other knows, as far as is needed for the plan to be carried out. What I cannot settle is which of these four conditions is doing the real work, and in particular whether a case that satisfies all four while involving no commitment of any kind between the participants -- no promise, no agreement, no obligation to the other, no entitlement on either side to complain if the other simply stops and walks away -- would still count as a case of doing something together. I suspect it would, and I want to see whether the suspicion survives a case built carefully enough to be convincing.

So please construct such a case for me. It must be an ordinary, small-scale activity of the sort that could happen on a Tuesday afternoon, with no exotic technology, no contracts or promises, no explicit prior agreement, and no reliance on the participants feeling wronged, betrayed, or answerable to one another if one of them quits midway; the obligation-free character of the case has to be genuine rather than smuggled in through a background convention. It should also be a case in which an onlooker would describe what happened in the singular -- they did it -- and in which both participants, asked afterwards, would say without hesitation that they had done it together. Alongside it, give me a near-miss twin: a case that keeps the behaviour, the interlocking plans, and the participants' own sense of acting together as close to identical as possible, and differs in exactly one respect, which you choose and must defend as genuinely a single respect. The twin should fail to be a case of doing something together.

Then do the checks. For the main case, show explicitly how it satisfies each of the four conditions, and show that the commitment conditions I listed are absent. Say which single feature of the case is carrying the weight in making it joint action rather than mere coordination, and what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed. Finally, put the strongest objection you can in the mouth of someone who thinks commitment is essential -- I expect the objection to be that a case like this cannot be built at all, because intending an activity as something we are doing just is accepting a standing commitment to the other person -- and tell me how the case survives it, or concede that it does not. A case that stipulates the conclusion, by describing the participants as cooperating or as acting as a unit, does not help me: the jointness needs to fall out of the structure of the case rather than be assumed in the telling.
