I am a junior in high school, and this term I have been sitting in on a philosophy club that is working through a unit on moral reasoning. Our sponsor introduced us to the is–ought gap, the claim that no conclusion containing an 'ought' can follow validly from premises that contain only 'is'-statements, and separately to the naturalistic fallacy, which she described as the mistake of identifying the good with some natural property such as pleasure or evolutionary fitness. I decided to take both ideas as seriously as I could and see how far they stretch, and I ended up writing out the following argument, which I now cannot decide whether to keep or throw away:

(1) Any valid argument whose conclusion is normative must contain at least one normative premise, on pain of the is–ought gap.
(2) For a normative premise to be rationally supported, it must itself be the conclusion of a valid argument.
(3) Any such supporting argument either contains a normative premise, in which case we simply repeat the demand, or contains none, in which case by the is–ought gap it cannot yield a normative conclusion.
(4) Therefore the support for any normative premise either runs on forever or terminates in a normative premise that is merely asserted.
(5) A normative premise that is merely asserted has no more rational authority than its denial.
(6) Therefore, whenever two people agree on all the descriptive facts but disagree about what ought to be done, there is no rational procedure that could settle the matter.

The conclusion strikes me as plainly false — it would imply that my friend and I, arguing over whether our club should keep its remaining funds for a spring trip or give them to the local shelter, where she says keeping them would be selfish and I say it would merely be prudent, could not even in principle be reasoning well with one another — and yet every step looks to me like something I already believe. I would most like to keep (1), and I am reluctant to surrender (5), but I am willing to give up any single one of the others if I must. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me — that is, what else that I seem to believe has to go along with it? I would also be grateful if you would explain how the main families of answers — intuitionist foundationalism, coherentism or reflective equilibrium, and non-cognitivism or expressivism — each respond to this argument, and whether any of them can retain (1) and (5) while denying (6), or whether each must instead pay in some other currency.
