Next term I am recording a long-form interview with a philosopher who has spent the better part of a decade defending utilitarianism against the objection from the separateness of persons, and I would like your help drafting the questions. Her position, which she will state briefly at the top of the episode and which I want the questions to take for granted rather than re-litigate from scratch, is this. She grants the plain metaphysical claim: there are distinct loci of experience, and no collective subject who feels the aggregate of a population's welfare. Her contention is that nothing normative follows from that grant. The objection, she says, commits the fence fallacy -- it reads a fact about where welfare is located as a rule about how welfare may be summed -- and its defenders personify the ledger, supposing that a sacrifice must be answerable to some owner of the total, when the total has no owner and needs none. Her positive account is that one principle governs trade-offs within a life and across lives alike: welfare counts, whoever has it, and the fact that the loss and the gain fall on different subjects is no more a barrier to weighing them than the fact that they fall at different times. She also runs a spillover argument: any constraint strong enough to forbid interpersonal aggregation would forbid taxation, conscription, and the ex ante distribution of risk across a population, which she takes to be a reductio of the objector rather than of herself.

I need eight to ten questions that go after the substance of that position rather than inviting her to restate it, and I would like the sequence to make her favourite deflection -- that the objection presupposes a personal standpoint she has already abandoned -- progressively harder to use. At least one question must press the intra- versus interpersonal asymmetry and ask what becomes of it on a reductionist view of personal identity, where the line between successive stages of one life and the line between two lives differs only in degree; at least one must ask whether her rejection of any compensation requirement leaves her unable to say that the person sacrificed is wronged at all, as opposed to merely being the place where the loss lands; and at least one must test whether the spillover argument refutes only an absolutist objector, since a threshold deontologist who permits taxation while forbidding the forced transplant is untouched by it. Two cases should appear in the questions. The first is the split-brain patient: if the objection draws its force from counting subjects, and the number of subjects is unsettled in such cases, does the objector's constraint come and go with our neuroscience, and what does her view say about a creature whose subjecthood is genuinely indeterminate? The second is a village lottery in which each of a hundred people gives up a modest amount of welfare so that one of them is cured of a serious illness, the losers receive nothing in return, and the aggregate gain exceeds the aggregate loss. I want to know whether the objector's verdict in that case is driven by a thought about using people rather than by separateness, and if so, what work separateness is doing. Close with a question about what would have to be true, or what argument or evidence she would have to encounter, for her to give up the view. Our listeners are mostly graduate students and colleagues from outside the field, so the questions must be intelligible without a literature review, but they should not be soft; please include no questions about her career, her influences, or how she came to the topic, and none that invite her to survey the literature or recommend reading.

Give me the result as a JSON object with two fields. sequence_rationale is a string of no more than 120 words explaining why the questions are ordered as they are. questions is an array of objects, each with: n (integer, in order); kind (one of clarificatory, pressure, reductio, falsification); question (the question as I would ask it aloud, one to three sentences); presses (a string naming precisely the commitment or standard reply it targets); likely_reply (one or two sentences, the answer she is most likely to give); follow_up (the question that comes next if she gives that reply); and dodge_to_block (a string naming the evasion the follow-up is meant to close off).
