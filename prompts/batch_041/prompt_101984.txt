I serve on the board of a small groundwater district that manages the shared aquifer under our valley. Next month we vote on a plan to cut pumping by a third over five years, and I have to circulate a written recommendation to the other board members before then. I'd like you to draft that memo.

Two proposals are on the table. The first is a hard cap: every irrigated acre gets the same allotment, meters go on every well, and the district fines anyone who overpumps. The second is tradable shares: each landowner gets a share of the valley's total pumping budget, and shares can be sold or leased to neighbors, but only inside the district and only one year at a time, so no one can permanently sell out and leave.

A neighbor of mine, who has farmed here longer than I have, objects to both on principle. Water, he says, is a gift and a basic need; putting a price on it turns a shared inheritance into a commodity and lets the biggest operations buy their way to the front of the line. He favors a voluntary pledge that each of us commits to cut back, with a committee of neighbors keeping track and naming those who break their word.

A hydrologist we hired threw a wrench into the discussion. She said the real question is not whether to price water but whether this aquifer is even the kind of thing neighbors can govern by agreement. She pointed out that one farmer's pumping genuinely takes water away from the next farmer's well, whereas a stretch of river that everyone can use does not get used up in the same way. She also warned that the pledge approach has a built-in problem: the farmer who cuts back pays a real cost while the benefit is spread across everyone, so the sensible individual move is to promise and then quietly keep pumping.

What I keep running into is that people around this table say "tragedy of the commons" as if it settled the matter — as if any shared resource is doomed and only private ownership or a hard government cap can save it. I am not sure that is right, and I am not sure our aquifer is even the same kind of thing as the examples they wave around.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the board. It should recommend one concrete course of action, name the enforcement mechanism and the timeline we would commit to, and give the reasoning behind it. It should take my neighbor's objection seriously rather than dismiss it as sentimentality, and it should be honest about the strongest case for whichever proposal I end up rejecting. It should also say plainly whether our aquifer is a public good, a common-pool resource, or something else, since people use those terms loosely here and I think the difference matters. Please write it in American English.
