I'm TA-ing an upper-level seminar on the socialist calculation debate and I have to write one of the exam questions, and I keep hitting the same wall: every version I draft can be anwered by reciting the standard beats — Mises said no prices so no rational allocation, Lange said trial-and-error prices can do the job, Hayek said local knowledge can't be centralized — and I want something that forces them to actually run the argument instead of summarizing it.

Here's the angle I want to use. Hand the central planner something nobody in the 1930s had: a computer that can actually solve the full general-equilibrium optimization, given whatever data it's fed. Call it the computational planner. The question is whether that dissolves the calculation problem or just relocates it. I want them to pin down what Hayek's point about the knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place is really claiming — is it an argument about computational tractability, an argument about incentives, or something else entirely — and then say whether a computationally unlimited planner still faces that thing, and if so in what form. Push them on the tempting move that "the information problem is just a bandwidth problem," and make them commit on where that move goes right and where it goes wrong.

So: write the exam question, a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and a B answer won't — things like correctly separating the knowledge problem from the incentive problem, and actually dealing with the case where preferences are themselves only revealed through the market process rather than given to the planner as data. It's for a 90-minute exam, so keep the question answerable in that time. And write the whole thing as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just the way you'd write it out in an email.
