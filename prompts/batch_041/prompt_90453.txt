I am serving as the grader of record for a final examination in an upper-level undergraduate course on metaethics, and I would like an independent assessment of one script before I return it to the candidate. The examination question was as follows:

"Some philosophers contend that evolutionary considerations debunk morality. Critically assess the claim that evolutionary debunking arguments establish that moral realism is false. Your answer should (i) distinguish the epistemic ambitions of such arguments from any metaphysical ambitions they might have; (ii) reconstruct in detail one prominent version of the argument; and (iii) evaluate the most serious objection to it."

The candidate's answer, transcribed in full, reads:

Evolutionary debunking arguments take as their starting point the fact that our moral beliefs and evaluative attitudes have been substantially shaped by natural selection. Their conclusion is that moral realism — the view that there are mind-independent moral truths — cannot be sustained.

The best-known version of the argument is Sharon Street's Darwinian dilemma, developed at length in her book The Evolution of Morality. Street argues that the realist faces an unavoidable choice. Either the selective pressures that shaped our evaluative tendencies tracked the independent moral truths, or they did not. On the second horn, our moral beliefs are largely off-track, and realism gives us no reason to trust them. On the first horn, the realist must hold that natural selection favoured tendencies that happened to correspond to the independent moral facts; but since the adaptive value of a tendency — its contribution to reproductive success — is quite independent of its truth, such a correspondence would be an extraordinary coincidence. Either way, realism is undermined.

Street's central weakness is that she commits the genetic fallacy. She attacks moral beliefs by pointing to their causal origins rather than by offering evidence against their content, and the fact that a belief has a biological function tells us nothing about whether it is true. A moral realist can therefore dismiss the argument out of hand.

The most serious objection to the debunking strategy is Richard Joyce's. In The Evolution of Morality, Joyce argues that natural selection would have favoured moral beliefs because they strengthen cooperative motivation rather than because they are true, and he concludes that we should abandon morality altogether, a position he calls moral abolitionism. This is a more radical conclusion than Street's, and it shows that the real issue is whether moral facts exist, not merely whether our beliefs about them are justified.

A more promising line is to observe that the argument threatens realism but leaves constructivism untouched. Since constructivists hold that moral truths are constituted by our evaluative attitudes, they need not worry whether those attitudes track anything independent of them, and evolutionary debunking therefore leaves constructivism standing as the most defensible metaethical position.
