I sit on the drafting committee of a deliberative-reform working group in a mid-sized city, and our mandate is deliberately vague: reduce the weight of career politicians in how the council actually works. Our meetings keep collapsing into a three-way argument. The sortition faction wants a randomly selected chamber with a single fixed term and no re-election. The term-limit faction wants to keep elections but cap service at two or three terms. The skeptics say both reforms simply relocate power to whoever sets agendas, writes the briefing documents, and staffs the committees, and that we should stop pretending otherwise. I have been asked to convert this into something more useful than a shouting match: a research agenda, not a literature survey. Assume the resources are one full-time researcher for two years plus a few part-time collaborators — enough for three lines of work done properly, not ten done badly.

What I want is a ranked and defended agenda of the open questions, at most four or five of them, with the reasoning spelled out. For each one: why it is upstream of the others, in the sense that other questions become moot, easier, or differently framed once it is settled; whether it is the sort of thing that conceptual argument can settle at all, or whether it is hostage to data we cannot plausibly collect before our report is due; and how much it actually bears on the charter text we will end up writing, since a question that cannot change a clause is a question I cannot spend a researcher on. I also want to know what would move each question up or down the list over the next year. Candidates we have been arguing about, in no particular order: whether sortition's legitimacy case and its epistemic case are separable, and whether it matters which one we think we are buying; the self-application problem, that any reform must be enacted by the incumbents it demotes, and whether that changes which questions are worth settling first; the migration of careerism into the permanent administration, the lottery machinery, and the facilitation apparatus that any assembly needs — one of our members calls this second-order careerism, and I genuinely cannot tell whether it names a real mechanism or is a rhetorical trick for defending the status quo; whether term limits reduce careerism or merely transfer influence to staffers, party organisations, and lobbyists; and the selection problem, meaning who declines to serve, who gets exempted, and whether the people willing to be drafted are unrepresentative in ways that no quota can repair. Add whatever we are missing, but only if it earns its place against those.

Please be willing to tell me that the framing is wrong. If the honest answer is that most of these are empirical questions our scale cannot resolve, or that the choice among them is settled by contested values rather than by argument, or that what we actually need is a decision procedure for the committee rather than a research agenda, say so plainly instead of manufacturing an agenda to fit the request. Where you are genuinely unsure, mark the uncertainty rather than smoothing it over; I would rather have your confidence levels, and an explicit note on which of my candidates you think are overrated or do not follow, than a confident-sounding list.
