I'm a retired chemist (35 years of spectroscopy, mostly Raman) and since I packed it in I've been reading philosphy on my own and mostly enjoying it, but I've hit something I can't get past. A guy in my little reading group gave me a principle he treats as sacred, and I've built an argument from it that seems to show it proves way too much — except I can't find the bad step, and if there isn't one I can't tell what I'd be giving up by ditching it. I'd like your help.

His principle, roughly: a rational person's present degree of belief in some claim should equal her best estimate of what her degree of belief in that claim will be later, after she's thought about it longer and learned more. He calls it just the formal version of 'trust your future self, she'll know more than you do.'

Here's my argument.

(1) My present credence in a claim A should equal my expected future credence in A — the average of the credences I might hold later, weighted by how likely I think each is.
(2) I'm about to begin a long, careful inquiry into some historical question H — months of reading primary sources — and I know my own habits well enough to be certain that by the end my credence in H will sit at about 0.9.
(3) So by (1) I should already be at 0.9 in H right now, before doing a single day of the reading.
(4) And applying (1) again: once I've finished the inquiry, my credence then should equal my expected credence even later; run this out and my credence at any moment should equal the credence I'd eventually settle on after unlimited further reflection — my 'final' credence.
(5) But I've no idea what that final credence is, and if I did, I wouldn't need the inquiry at all.
(6) A principle that tells me to hold now whatever I'd only settle on at the end of endless reflection, and that makes investigation pointless, proves far too much.
(7) So (1) is false.

My friend says the repair is to add a clause: the principle only bites when no information is lost between now and the later time. But that seems to make the whole thing either circular (you'd need to already know which future credences are the good ones) or empty. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) and lose (6), but I want to know the price of that.

Please lead with your bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question.
