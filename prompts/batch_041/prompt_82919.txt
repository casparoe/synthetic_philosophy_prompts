I have been trying to work out what I actually think about decisions that are 'good enough', and I keep getting tangled. A colleague tells me the whole business is about satisficing, and that some people hold that satisficing is not a genuine standard of rationality at all, merely optimising with the costs of deliberation counted in, while others hold that it genuinely permits the lesser option even when the better one is free and lying in front of you. I do not know which of those I believe, and I suspect my verdicts below commit me to one of them. What I should like from you is the principle, or small family of principles, that my verdicts amount to, and an honest account of where they force me to give one up.

My verdicts, on cases that have mostly happened to me.

1. Flats. Before I began looking I set myself a bar: within budget, under forty minutes' commute, a room I could use as a study. I saw three flats; the second cleared the bar; I took it and cancelled the fourth viewing. I later learned that the fourth was cheaper, closer and larger, and would also have cleared the bar. I am confident I did nothing wrong.

2. Laptops. No bar, no list. I handled one in a shop, it felt fine, I bought it and walked out, past the shop next door where I would very likely have found a better machine for the same money. I am not willing to call this irrational. But it is not the same kind of thing as (1), and I cannot say what the difference is. Had you asked me why I stopped, I should have had nothing to say beyond its seeming fine.

3. The same laptop purchase, except that I had told myself in advance that I would buy the first one that felt fine, and had written it down so as not to spend the afternoon dithering. That, I think, is all right.

4. Flats again. Late in the search, tired, I quietly dropped the study requirement and took the next flat that met the reduced bar — a flat I should have rejected on the first day. Not all right. That was fatigue, not a decision.

5. Now suppose I had written on the first day: 'if I have not found anything by the twentieth, I shall drop the study requirement.' On the twentieth I drop it and take the next adequate flat. I want to say that is all right. Suppose instead I had written: 'if I become bored by the twentieth, I shall drop the study requirement.' I want to say that is not all right, and I cannot see how it differs from the previous case in any way that matters, since in both the later me is simply following the earlier me's instruction.

6. Vouchers. At a counter I am offered a £10 voucher or a £12 voucher, with no cost, no uncertainty and no further searching to be done. I take the £10 one, on the ground that ten is plenty. I do not think I have been irrational, exactly, but I can find nothing to say in defence of the choice, and if I made a habit of it I should think something was the matter with me.

7. The same counter, except that I take the £10 voucher because I hold a standing policy of not always taking the largest share when the difference does not matter to me, and I can give an account of why I hold that policy. That is all right.

8. Hiring. I sit on a panel for a junior analyst. We agree a standard, the first candidate we see clears it, and we appoint them without seeing the rest of the shortlist. I am comfortable with this, even though the cost of our stopping is borne by candidates we never met and the benefit accrues to us.

9. A routing application returns a route a few per cent longer than the shortest, because computing the true shortest would take longer than the journey saves. I do not think it is satisficing at all. It is optimising, with computation counted among the costs.

One further wrinkle. In (1) I said the fourth flat was cheaper, closer and larger, and I stand by my verdict. Had it been £250 a month cheaper, however, I suspect I should have felt differently about having stopped, and I cannot tell whether that is a genuine difference or merely regret at the sight of a better thing.

What I want: a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible; a precise statement of each, with the case that forces each clause; your verdicts on cases I have not listed — in particular, a doctor who prescribes the first treatment meeting a clinical standard when a marginally better one would cost no more to prescribe, and a person who takes the £10 voucher because they are simply incurious as to whether a £12 voucher exists; and a plain statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which of them I ought to abandon. Where my verdicts conflict, say so rather than papering over it. Tell me how confident you are in the account you settle on, and single out the point in it of which you are least sure.
