I have come to hold a position about the mind and causation, and I want to know what would count against it. Lead with your bottom line in your first sentence — your overall verdict on how exposed the position is — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. I am in high school and writing this up for an independent-study paper, so keep the machinery explicit rather than assuming I already know the literature.

The position: mental states such as my decision to speak or my embarrassment genuinely cause things, and they are not identical with anything physical, even though they are entirely fixed by the physical. I do not think mental properties are additional forces pushing the physical world around. I think of them as the features that pick out which of the many physical events count as the causes — the difference that makes the difference — so that when I say my reasons caused my action, I mean they selected which physical happenings were doing the relevant work, not that they added a push of their own.

The standard argument against this, as I understand it, is a three-way squeeze: mental properties are distinct from physical ones; the physical world is causally complete, so every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause; and there is no systematic overdetermination. Together these seem to leave mental properties with nothing to do. I want to keep all three and escape by reinterpreting the first. I need to know which of the three my view really ends up denying, and what would have to be true of the world for each escape to be stable rather than a verbal dodge.

So: what would count against the position? Rank what you give me from fatal to merely awkward, and include (a) results about causation that undercut the distinction between selecting a cause and being a cause; (b) the worry that the squeeze can be re-run on the selecting relation itself, since whatever does the selecting is presumably itself a physical state, so either the selecting is idle or the view collapses into ordinary physicalism about causes; and (c) the test I have been told makes this precise — that a property is causally irrelevant if, once you know all the physical facts, learning whether that property is present changes nothing about the probability of the behavior. I would like to know whether that test is right, whether it begs the question against my view, and what would count against the test.

Tell me which of these the position's own defenders would concede, not only what critics say. Tell me whether it is well insulated: is the claim that mental properties select which causes count a claim about the world or only about how we explain things, and if only the latter, does that sink it? Tell me what my view predicts that a rival does not, and what empirical finding, if any, could count against it. I suspect I would be most moved by an argument that the selecting relation is itself a physical state, and I would like to be told if that is the wrong thing to be moved by. If the honest verdict is that the position is unfalsifiable, say so and say whether that is a problem, rather than manufacturing defeaters to satisfy me.
