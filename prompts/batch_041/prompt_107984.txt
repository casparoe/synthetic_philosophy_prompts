OK, so I teach a unit on admiration and envy, and I've realized I dont' actually have a principle — I have a pile of gut reactions, and when students push I wave my hands and say "it's contextual." I grew up with the American Dream version of all this: you're supposed to rise, and when you rise people are supposed to cheer. Then I spent a year in Norway and got a dose of Jante — you know, the ten rules from that 1930s satirical novel about a small Danish town where nobody is allowed to think they're anyone special. I came back with alot of my intuitions scrambled. Here's where I actually land, case by case:

1. An old friend's book wins a big prize. I'm genuinely glad, no ambivalence, and I'd think less of myself if I felt a twist of envy.
2. The parade for the local kid who medaled at the Olympics. Charming. Nothing wrong with it.
3. My department puts up a monthly leaderboard of everyone's citation counts. I hate it, and not because it's a management fad — it seems to do something wrong to the people at the bottom of it.
4. A colleague who is genuinely brilliant and will tell you so, unprompted, at lunch. I can't stand it, and I think it's a real character flaw, even though everything he says is true.
5. The same colleague, asked point blank whether he's the best in the field, says "oh, not even close," which is false. I think that's fine, even admirable.
6. Me declining to be put up for an award I'd probably win, so my colleagues don't have to think about me as a contender. Cowardice. I know it.
7. A student who's the sharpest in the room and never speaks up because she doesn't want to look like a show-off. That's a loss — I wish she'd talk.
8. A Norwegian colleague who heard about my grant and said, well, don't go thinking you're anything special. It stung and felt hostile, and yet I also think she's protecting something I'd want protected.
9. A friend who posts her salary, her marathon time, and her kid's college acceptance on social media. Tacky — but I can't say why, when the same friend telling me those exact things over dinner is completely fine.
10. An obituary that notes the man was never rich or famous and that this is how he wanted it. Something's off, like it's cutting the successful down sideways.
11. A colleague I'm convinced is a fraud gets a big award and I'm furious, and that feeling isn't envy. I think it's appropriate.

So: is there one principle here? My tentative one is that celebrating achievement is fine, and what's objectionable is converting achievements into a ranking of persons. But that makes my contempt for the braggy colleague come out wrong — he isn't ranking anyone, he's just stating facts about himself — and it makes the Jante business come out fine, which I don't want. A friend mentioned there's a Buddhist term, mudita, for the pleasure you take in someone else's success, and suggested that's the virtue I'm circling, but I can't tell whether that's a distinct thing or just the absence of envy. Give me the principle, or the small set of candidates. For each clause, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Then tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed: a boss who singles out one employee for praise in front of the others; a school that stops naming a valedictorian so nobody feels bad; a Nobel laureate's memoir; me telling my daughter she's the smartest kid in her class. And be honest about which of my verdicts I'd have to give up. If it's my self-effacement in case 6 or my contempt in case 4, fine, I can live with that — but tell me which, and why. If the real answer is that I'm running two separate norms, one about envy and one about status, just say so.
