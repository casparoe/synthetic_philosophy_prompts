I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and I run a fortnightly session for a cross-disciplinary reading group that mixes philosophy, computer science, and economics students. I have sixteen participants, one room, ninety minutes, and no computers. I want to run a session on the intelligence-explosion and takeoff-speed debate that does not consist of everyone reciting the positions they already arrived with.

What I want participants to feel, before anyone says it out loud, is this: the disagreement between the fast and slow camps is not a disagreement about how intelligent the system in question is. It is a disagreement about the shape of the returns to cognitive reinvestment and about which bottleneck binds. The same premise — a system whose research capacity grows with its capability, and which can spend that capacity on improving itself — yields a doubling trajectory, a polynomial one, or a straight line, depending on two assumptions that are almost never stated when the intuition is presented: whether the next increment of capability costs the same as the last one, and whether anything outside cognition (an experiment that has to be run, a machine that has to be built) can be skipped.

Design the activity so that this comes out of the play. My rough picture, which you should feel free to replace with something better: two teams, each running a lab, each handed a sealed card stating the lab's conditions, and told only that the two labs are not alike. Teams start at capability 1, receive insight tokens each round in proportion to their current capability, and spend tokens to buy increments of capability, with a fixed number of rounds and a target capability as the win condition. The cards differ in one respect each: constant cost per increment; rising cost per increment; a rule that only one increment can be validated per round no matter how many tokens are held. Run it long enough that the three trajectories visibly diverge — the fast team will pull away and then hit a wall, the others will look sluggish and then keep grinding — and let the fast team attribute its lead to its own cleverness for a while. Then have the teams swap cards and play three more rounds, so each team occupies the other's world from the inside. Only then reveal the trajectories side by side and name what the difference was.

The deliverable: materials list; the sealed cards and the payoff tables, with arithmetic simple enough that a team can settle a round in under a minute (hand-check the eight- or ten-round trajectories yourself and print them for the debrief); a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; a short set of roles, including one observer per team who records the numbers rather than playing, so I have a clean record; the reveal; and a debrief that maps specific moments onto parts of the debate — the round where a team could not spend the tokens it held, the round where spending more bought less, the round where growth became arithmetic despite everything going right — and closes by having each team write one sentence stating the empirical claim about the world that would have to be true for its card to be the right description of reality, which I will then use to open the discussion. Do not lecture at the start, and do not use the term 'intelligence explosion' in front of them until the debrief.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time. I expect that the fast team will finish early and start commenting from the sidelines; that someone will try to pool tokens across teams to break the scoring; that at least one participant will refuse to play in character because the payoff tables are stipulated rather than derived; that arithmetic will go wrong under time pressure; and that the slow team will read its own trajectory as failure and disengage. Give me the handling for each, including what to do with the participant who objects that the tables are arbitrary — I take that objection to be the debrief's first line rather than an interruption, and I want a way to record it that makes it useful.

Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce, and say so explicitly in the design notes rather than burying it. I can see three things already: the payoff tables are fixed from outside, so the activity cannot stage the strongest version of the fast argument, in which the system alters its own return structure; it supplies no evidence whatever about the real elasticities of research, so the vote I take at the end is a vote about priors; and it scores capability rather than influence, so it leaves out deployment, adoption, and complementarity, which is where a slow takeoff actually lives. Add anything I have missed, and tell me what I will have to supply myself in the debrief that no arrangement of the game can deliver.
