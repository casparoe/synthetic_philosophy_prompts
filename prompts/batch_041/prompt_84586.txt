I'm a second-year PhD student putting together a reading list for a seminar on normativity, and I've been working through a survey chapter on fitting-attitude analyses of value. There is one passage I've now read four times and I still can't tell how much of it to trust; a friend in the seminar thinks it is obviously correct, and I'm inclined to think something has gone wrong somewhere but I can't locate it. Here is the chapter's summary, more or less verbatim: "On the fitting-attitude analysis, to call something good is simply to say that it is fitting to value it — that there are reasons to admire it, to desire it, or to approve of it. The trouble is that there are reasons to have these attitudes that have nothing to do with the value of the thing. The standard example is the demon, who will torture you unless you admire him: this gives you an excellent reason to admire him, but he is not admirable. Since the reasons-to-value account counts the demon as good, the analysis fails, and this is the wrong kind of reasons problem. The obvious repair is to say that only the right kind of reasons — moral reasons, as opposed to self-interested ones — make something valuable. But that is a circle, since what makes a reason moral is precisely that it bears on the value of the object. So the fitting-attitude analysis is either false or circular, and the upshot is that value cannot be analyzed in terms of our attitudes at all."
