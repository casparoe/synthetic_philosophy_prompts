I'm a fourth-year PhD student in bioethics, and because my program sticks a grad student on the hospital ethics committee, I'm the one who ends up drafting the written opinion whenever we've got a live case, and Thursday's case is a mess I can't pawn off on anybody. Patient is a 43-year-old man, four months out from an anoxic brain injury after a cardiac arrest, no behavioral signs of awareness on repeated bedside exams, no command following, nothing. His sister is his surrogate and she wants us to withdraw the ventilator. The wrinkle is that he's enrolled in a study our neuro-ICU is running using the TMS-EEG complexity index -- the measure the IIT people like, where you zap the cortex and see how rich and irreducible the echo is -- and his last two runs came back inside the range the protocol labels conscious. So now two of the intensivists want to keep him ventilated indefinitely and start a standing opioid infusion on the grounds that if there's a subject in there we owe him pain control; the palliative attending thinks we're being captured by a number; the chaplain keeps asking what suffering is even supposed to mean here. We have to produce something by Friday because the sister is definately going to the press otherwise.

My own instinct is a two-week trial of scheduled analgesia with continued ventilation, then a formal re-review, but I keep flipping back and forth, and what I actually want help with is the conceptual part: whether a high complexity reading is a proxy for the very capacity that grounds moral status, or just a correlate of cortical dynamics that could be present with nobody home. As I understand it the index was originally calibrated on patients who could at least sometimes follow commands, which is exactly the thing he can't do, so in his case we're extrapolating from the one thing we can't observe. Someone on the committee also pointed out that you can redescribe almost any feedforward system as a recurrent one doing the same input-output work, and I think that means recurrence can't be doing the evidential work all by itself, but I honestly can't tell whether that's a real objection or just me being clever at 1am. Also worth knowing: the study team wants us to say the measure is clinically informative so they can keep the protocol open, and I don't want us quietly doing science policy in a bedside memo.

So please draft the short opinion I'll circulate Wednesday night. One page or so: a concrete recommendation with a time frame and who does what, the reasoning behind it, and a genuinely fair statement of the strongest case for the side I'm leaning against -- both the possibility that we're overreading the science and the possibility that we're underreading it. Skip the literature, no citations, half the committee will tune out the moment they see a reference list, and don't just restate the legal definition of death or tell me to consult the family more; I need the actual argument about what the measurement can and can't license.
