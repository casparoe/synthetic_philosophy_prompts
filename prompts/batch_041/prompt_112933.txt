I have been trying for some time to reconcile my verdicts about the surprise exam, and I cannot get them to sit together. I should like you to find the principle behind them, tell me what it says about cases I have not put to it, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. If the way I have set the cases out makes the question ill-posed -- if, for instance, "know" is quietly doing different work in different variants -- I would far rather be told that than be handed a principle that fits the list only by equivocation.

The basic case. On Monday morning a teacher tells her class, "There will be an exam this week, and on the morning of the exam you will not know that it is that day." Five days are available. The class reasons backwards: the exam cannot fall on the fifth day, since if we reach the fourth evening with no exam we shall know it must be the fifth; the same step rules out the fourth, and so down to the first; therefore there will be no exam. The teacher then sets the exam for the third day, and the class is caught out. My verdict: she keeps her word, and the backwards induction is a fallacy rather than a proof. I take it she could have kept her word on any of the five days, the last included.

Now the variants, with my verdicts.

(a) One day, truthful teacher. "There will be an exam tomorrow, and you do not know today that there will be an exam tomorrow," spoken by a teacher the class knows to be strictly truthful. My verdict: she cannot keep her word. If the announcement is true and the class believes it, they know there will be an exam tomorrow; the second clause is then false; so the announcement is false; there is no way for it to be both true and believed.

(b) Two days, truthful teacher. The same teacher, the same form of announcement, but the window is two days. My verdict: she can keep her word, by setting the exam on the first of the two days. The class, having convinced itself that there will be no exam, is genuinely surprised.

(c) One day, known liar. The announcement of (a), made by a teacher the class knows to be a prankster who usually lies. My verdict: she can keep her word, and the announcement comes out true -- there is an exam tomorrow, and the class, disbelieving her, does not know it.

(d) One day, infallible teacher. As (a), except that the class knows the teacher cannot err, and knows that she knows they know this. My verdict: impossible, and more plainly so than in (a).

(e) Surprise measured by credence. Five days, truthful teacher, and the announcement is, "There will be an exam this week, and on the evening before the exam your credence that the exam is the next day will be below one half." My verdict: she can keep her word, but only by avoiding the fifth day. On the fourth evening with no exam yet, a class that believes her must be certain that the exam is the next day, and the announcement would then be false.

(f) The class never reasons about it. Five days, truthful teacher, and a class that has never heard of the puzzle and will not give the matter a moment's thought. My verdict: trivially possible, and uninformative -- which suggests to me that the difficulty lies wholly in what the class is entitled to infer, not in what the teacher does.

(g) Surprise in kind rather than in timing. Five days, truthful teacher: "There will be a test this week, and on the morning of the test you will not know whether it will cover this week's material or last week's." My verdict: possible, and possible on any day including the last; there is no ordering here for the class to work backwards along, so the induction has nothing to bite on.

Please give me the principle, or a small set of rival principles, in a form precise enough to check against these cases, saying for each clause which case forces it. Then apply it to at least two cases I have not raised -- in particular, a three-day window with a truthful teacher, and the five-day window with a teacher known to be infallible -- and give me its verdicts there. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether you think the verdict to be given up is mine or the principle's. Where you are unsure, say so; I would rather have a candid map of the difficulties than a confident synthesis.
