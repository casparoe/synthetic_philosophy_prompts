Me and a few friends have a translation workshop going, and we've been stuck for about a year on the same arguement, so I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The source text is short: fourteen lines, strict syllable count, ABAB rhymes, a refrain that comes back twice, and — the thing we can't stop fighting about — an acrostic, where the first letters of the lines spell the name of the person the poem addresses. It's a farewell to someone who's dying. We're working into English. The acrostic is a real gesture in the original, not an accident, but the name it spells has no English form that rhymes or scans, and if you force it you get filler.

Two camps have formed. The artifact camp says a version that drops the acrostic isn't a translation of this poem at all, it's a paraphrase with the spine pulled out — the hiding of the name is part of what the poem does, maybe the whole point of it, and a poem that says the same things in a different shape is a different poem. The sense camp says the original poet was working under their own constraints in their own language and our job is to make a poem that works under ours; the acrostic was available to them and isn't to us, and sacrificing sense to keep a pattern the reader can't hear anyway is fetishizing the container.

Then we made it worse by adopting a rule we called compensation: whenever a formal feature can't be carried over, the translator has to add a feature of comparable strictness that wasn't in the original — if the acrostic dies, hide something else, or make the whole version a lipogram, or make the line lengths spell a word. The artifact camp calls this forgery: you're handing people an object the poet never made and putting their name on it. The sense camp calls it the only honest move there is — if you can't reproduce the music you write new music that fits the words, and the reader gets the experience instead of the blueprint.

One of us keeps citing that Hofstadter book where he collects dozens of translations of one tiny poem and never settles which is best, which I take as evidence the question is ill-posed, though I'm not sure that isn't just cowardice.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Are we disagreeing about facts (what the source really does, whether the acrostic is load-bearing), about norms (what a translation is for), about concepts (whether there's any such thing as the same poem across two languages), or about bedrock taste in what a poem is supposed to give you? Are the two camps even answering the same question under one word — one asking what this object is, the other what it says? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction that splits our cases, a demonstration that for some texts the form and the sense constraints are jointly unsatisfiable, or just a decision to stop using one word for two jobs? Don't give me a tour of translation studies, use our case. And tell me if there's a third position that makes both camps look like they're missing something.
