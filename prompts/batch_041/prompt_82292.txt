Since retiring from a career in experimental physics — thirty-six years, most of them spent on the magnetic behaviour of thin films — I have been trying to repair an education that stopped at Mill, and for the past year I have been reading Ayn Rand's ethical essays with a small group of friends, two of them professional philosophers and two of them not. What has surprised me is how much of her position I find genuinely compelling, and how much of it sets my old scientific instincts on edge. Her central move, as I understand it, is to define "sacrifice" as the surrender of a greater value for the sake of a lesser one or of a nonvalue, so that the term applies only where the agent actually loses by his own lights; which means the moral status of an act can be settled only once one knows the agent's value hierarchy, and that much of what ordinary people call generosity turns out on inspection not to be a sacrifice at all. She is equally careful to separate altruism from kindness, to insist that helping those one loves is no self-sacrifice, and to treat emergencies — the shipwreck, the fire — as a temporary condition with its own narrow rule rather than a standing claim of the needy on the lives of the competent. Please write a satire that takes all of this seriously rather than the easy version in which she is simply against kindness; she anticipated that objection, and a piece that ignores her distinctions would only flatter readers who have never opened her.

The form I have in mind is a field guide — the sort of pocket taxonomy a naturalist carries, with numbered species entries, each giving habitat and distinguishing marks and notes on confusion with similar organisms — written by an earnest, slightly testy in-universe adherent of her ethics who is trying to teach beginners to recognise sacrifices in the wild. The deadpan premise is that sacrifice is a genus to be identified rather than a moral accusation to be thrown about, and the comedy should come entirely from the taxonomy being exactly as she defined it: full sacrifice against the partial virtue of giving at the cost of one's own discomfort; sacrifice to a stranger against sacrifice to a beloved; the trader as a different order altogether; and the misidentification problem, whereby the laity report as sacrifice many acts that are in fact trades or expressions of value. I want a reader to feel the standard objection without ever hearing it stated — that a taxonomy which can always reclassify a reported sacrifice as a trade has no way of failing — and I want the guide's author to remain scrupulous and unwinking throughout, so that someone who knows Rand recognises the real positions being worked out and someone who has never read her can still follow the joke. Do not put claims in her mouth that she would disown, and do not invent quotations attributed to her; if you want an epigraph, write it in the guide's own institutional voice. Around 800 words, and I do not need citations or a reading list.

Please label the sections and keep to this order. First, "How to Use This Guide" (roughly 120 words), the author instructing beginners on when a sacrifice may properly be reported. Second, "The Principal Species" (roughly 400 words), four to six numbered entries, each using the same labelled fields — Habitat, Distinguishing Marks, Confusion With. Third, "Confusable Non-Sacrifices" (roughly 150 words), covering the acts most often misreported. Fourth, "Family Note: Emergencies" (roughly 120 words). Then a closing section titled "Where This Guide Takes Liberties" (roughly 200 words) in your own voice rather than the guide's, saying how confident you are that this is a fair reading of her position, which of the guide's jokes rest on a contested reading of it, and which single point you are least sure of.
