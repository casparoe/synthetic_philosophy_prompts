Next Friday I am giving the farewell address at the twentieth-anniversary dinner of the small Dutch-Japanese engineering firm I have run for eleven years, and I would like you to write it for me.

The room is genuinely split, and the two halves will hear different things. My Dutch colleagues — the engineers, two of my old classmates from Delft, my brother — take it for granted that a farewell speech states what actually happened, including what went wrong; they will be listening for whether I name the Osaka contract and the two years we burned on it, and if I glide past it they will hear the whole evening as evasion. Our Japanese side — the founding family, the Kyoto office, the widow of the man who hired me — will hear the same speech as a ceremony: an occasion for gratitude that leaves everyone's standing intact, in which an explicit accounting of failure would publicly diminish people who are still here. Neither half is being unreasonable, and I will not write a speech that is secretly aimed at one of them.

Two further constraints. The speech is being interpreted live into Japanese by a friend who has asked me, sensibly, to avoid sentences that cannot be carried into Japanese without either hardening into an accusation or evaporating into nothing; she will have no time to repair me. And I promised my deputy, who resigned over Osaka and is coming anyway, that I would say nothing at this dinner that I believe to be false. I mean to keep that promise literally, not approximately.

Here is where I have ended up, and I want the speech built on this rather than decorated with it.

First: not everything said at a dinner like this is a claim about the world. When I thank the room for its patience, or say that we were fortunate in our people, I am not asserting a proposition that could be checked against the facts; I am making a move in a form, the way one makes a move in a game. Reading such sentences as falsehoods is a category mistake — and so is reading my colleagues' demand for plain speech as mere rudeness, or the Kyoto office's preference for form as dishonesty. The speech should contain ritual moves that are honest as moves, and it should never smuggle a claim in under the cover of one.

Second: where I am making claims, the line I care about is not the line between true sentences and false ones. It runs between declining to burden someone and preventing them from acting. A softening crosses into a lie when the listener, learning the facts later, would see that the sentence had been shaped to stop them from doing something they would otherwise have done. On that test silence is not automatically innocent: a silence lies when the surrounding context will supply the missing content by itself.

Third, about the interpreter, who is the only experiment I get to run once. Where a sentence can be carried into Japanese only by adding a hedge or an apology, the harshness was in the sentence; where it can be carried only by dropping something, that something was never in the sentence — it was in the room. The speech should be written so that the sentences I most need the Dutch half to hear survive the crossing without an apology added, and the sentences I most need the Kyoto half to feel survive it without anything subtracted.

Write the speech: five to seven minutes, roughly eight hundred words, ending in the toast. No quotations from any text, no citations, no names of philosophers or books — the audience would not know them and I do not want this to sound like an essay. The position above should be visible only in what the sentences do and in what the speech declines to do, never in commentary. Include at least one concrete, checkable particular about my deputy, the kind of detail only I would have, because to the Dutch half generality reads as evasion; and include at least one thing I know and do not say, arranged so that the speech makes clear, without ever saying so, that the omission is not a concealment.

After the speech, add a short note to me — three or four sentences, clearly marked as not part of the speech — telling me which sentences you wrote as ritual moves rather than as claims, which single sentence you judged closest to the line in the second point and why you kept it on the near side, and what you would have written differently if the Dutch half had not been in the room.
