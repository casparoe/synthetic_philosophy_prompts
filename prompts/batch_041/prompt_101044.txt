I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy in a more or less systematic way, and my reading has collided with an unanticipated duty: I chair the allocation committee of a modest private foundation, which commits its funds in stages and revisits earlier commitments as results come in. Several of my fellow trustees have taken up the money-pump argument as a reason to fix our preferences once and never reopen a decision, and while I find the argument powerful in the abstract, I cannot tell from it which of our actual inconsistencies expose us to a pump and which are merely embarrassing on a page. I would like you to convert the argument into something we can use.

Write a decision procedure that the committee can run, in the order it should be run, to determine whether a proposed policy — our own, or one a grantee has submitted — is vulnerable to a money pump. It must be executable by people with no formal training in decision theory: specify what to elicit, what to check or compute, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure returns a definite no, and where it must stop and hand the question to a person because the issue is one of values or of unquantifiable judgment. Build the procedure on the argument itself rather than on the slogan, and be candid about the places where the argument is weaker than its reputation. In particular, I want the procedure to distinguish exploitability as a fact about what a sufficiently clever counterparty could do from exploitability as a genuine failure of rationality, and to separate the chooser who can escape by foreseeing the whole sequence, or by adopting a commitment device, from the chooser who cannot. Because our decisions recur over time and each is defensible at the moment it is made, the diachronic version of the problem concerns me far more than the one-shot version; the procedure should make clear when a cycle does its damage on its own and when it does damage only through the trades we would have to accept. Mark the steps that are judgment dressed up as procedure, and — since I remain a scientist at heart — state, for each verdict, what observation or argument would overturn it.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a counterparty who, at each of our quarterly meetings, offers to convert our position in one funding vehicle into another for a small administrative fee, where our committee's revealed preferences among the three vehicles run in a cycle. Second, a choice over an uncertain prospect that we resolve in two sittings rather than one, where our preferences exhibit the pattern behind the Allais example, which violates the independence condition, so that the two-stage presentation and the one-stage presentation can pull us apart. Third, our own standing policy of never reopening a commitment once made. At least one of the verdicts should be one I will not like — if the procedure says that a policy we consider prudent is in fact pumpable, or that the argument does not license the rigidity we have adopted, say so plainly. Finish by stating what the procedure cannot settle, who on the committee must sign off on each step, and how long the whole exercise should take, given that we meet quarterly, decide amounts that are modest but not trivial, and cannot consult a specialist.
