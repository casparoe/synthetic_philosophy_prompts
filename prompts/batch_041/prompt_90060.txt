I'm a grad student TAing a second-year undergrad course on personal identity, and next week it's my turn to run the 90-minute discussion section on Parfit's teletransporter. There are about 16 students, it's a Tuesday afternoon slot right after lunch so half of them will be half asleep, and I've got the whole 90 minutes to myself with no lecturing allowed -- they've already done the reading. What I really want is for them to feel, not just nod along to, the thing Parfit is getting at: that identity as such might not be what matters in survival, that the thing they actually care about when they care about surviving (psychological connectedness, continuity, memory chains, whatever we want to call it) comes in degrees, and that once they've really internalized that, they'll start making choices that treat their own continued existence as a more-or-less rather than an all-or-nothing matter. I want them to walk into that conclusion themselves through the activity, and I don't want to say the words reductionism or Relation R until the debrief, if at all.

So: design me a runnable activity for the session -- a game with rules and stakes, a staged thought experiment built on real choices they actually make, a role-play, a simulation, whatever you think lands hardest, plus the debrief. I'd like the structure to push them gradually rather than hit them with one big case, so ideally there's a dial or slider somewhere: a transporter they have to set, or a series of cases where some parameter moves by small steps from obviously fine to obviously horrifying, so that at some point they realize they've been drawing a line with no principled place to put it, or that they've already conceded that a perfect copy is good enough and it's now awkward to explain why the original walking in the door would add anything. It'd be great if it eventually involves something like the case where a person is duplicated or split into two and both continue, since that's the point where the identity answer and the what-matters answer visibly come apart, and maybe some element where parts of them get swapped out gradually. Keep the stakes real but tiny -- points, snacks, a token, public commitment, whatever, no actual money. They should come away having made concrete choices under the activity's rules and then finding those choices awkward to square with each other.

Then the debrief: map the specific moments in the activity onto the pieces of the idea (which move corresponds to caring about continuity rather than identity, where the degree-language sneaks in, where the fission case bites), tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it (I fully expect at least one student to dig in on the "but the copy isn't me" line and derail everything, so tell me how to use that rather than fight it), and be honest about which part of Parfit's view the activity simply can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in words afterward. Also give me a rough minute-by-minute timeline and a materials list I can actually prep the night before.
