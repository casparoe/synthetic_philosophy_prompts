I am in the last stretch of a graduate seminar on nationalism, where we have spent most of the term on the claim that nations are modern artifacts: Hobsbawm's and Ranger's volume on invented tradition, and Anderson's account of nations as imagined communities. What I keep running into is that both frameworks were assembled out of nineteenth- and twentieth-century material — state schooling, conscription, mass print, colonial administration, the vernacular novel — and that both authors died before the phenomena I would most like to test them against appeared. Hobsbawm has been dead since 2012 and Anderson since 2015, so neither ever saw what generative models can do to cultural memory or what a territorially scattered, platform-resident community can now claim about itself. I would like you to predict what Hobsbawm would have said about the two cases below, and then to say where Anderson would have parted company with him.

The first case is a small country whose culture and tourism ministry, wanting a distinctive national brand, commissions a generative model to produce a body of folk songs, a founding legend, and a calendar of seasonal festivals in a regional dialect. The ministry makes no secret of the provenance: the material is published as commissioned and contemporary, with the model credited. Within a decade the songs are sung at weddings, the festivals have been written into school calendars, and a party in parliament invokes the founding legend to argue for tighter borders and a stricter citizenship test. The second case is a diaspora of a few million people spread across three continents, with no territory, no shared vernacular, and no print culture of its own, whose members live their public life inside one global platform, conduct their disputes in a lingua franca, and have begun to describe themselves as a nation, adopting a flag, an anthem and a calendar of commemorations, and petitioning international bodies for recognition as a people. For Hobsbawm's part, I want to know how far the apparatus still bites when the invention is advertised rather than concealed and there has been no lapse of time in which forgetting could do its work: he draws a line between inventing a tradition and merely starting one that makes no claim to age, and I want to know which side of that line he would put the ministry's songs, given that they end up believed anyway. Which of the mechanisms he identified — ritual repetition, the manufacture of continuity with a suitably remote past, the use of tradition to legitimize authority or to weld a new collectivity together — are present in these cases and which are simply absent, and what does he predict the outcome will be: that the synthetic culture takes, that it fails, or that it takes while remaining hollow for those who live inside it? In the diaspora case, where I expect the two thinkers to diverge, ask whether a community whose medium is a single platform and whose language is a lingua franca satisfies Anderson's criteria at all — he took nations to be imagined as bounded and sovereign, and he traced their possibility to the convergence of print capitalism with vernacular languages — or whether what I am describing is a different kind of object that his framework would classify as something else, and whether he would expect such communities to multiply.

Please give me the answer as a set of labelled predictions rather than a continuous essay: one entry per thinker per case, each stating the premise from that thinker's framework on which the prediction rests, the prediction itself, and how much confidence the framework can support, since I want to argue in seminar that these theories are less portable to the present than we casually assume — or that they are more portable than they look. Where the two cases contain something neither framework can accommodate, say so explicitly rather than stretching the vocabulary to cover it, and where a prediction would depend on a judgment call about the thinker's temperament rather than his theory, mark that too.
