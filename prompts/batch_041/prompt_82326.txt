I build distributed systems for a living, and there is a pattern in them I cannot make philosophical sense of.

Two parties that need each other to be predictable — two services, two firms, two teams inside one company — rarely just announce what they will do. They lock it in. They burn the admin key, hard-code the fee, put the rule in a contract that cannot be changed without a fork, publish a public "we will never…" commitment. Then, when the other side answers with a commitment of its own, they escalate, because a commitment that can be matched or quietly dropped is not credible, and credibility is the only thing it was ever for. What people around me call a commitment race is exactly this: each side's precommitment raises the price of the other side's, and there is no natural place for the escalation to stop.

What puzzles me is that the commitments that seem to bind hardest are often the ones no one can verify — a public promise, a stated company culture, a handshake — while the ones that are genuinely unbreakable, the timelock and the burned key, often matter least, because everyone can see them coming and simply routes around them. So the practice treats a cheap, unverifiable promise as binding and an expensive, enforced one as a mere fact about the world.

I would like to know what this practice presupposes about commitment itself. Is a commitment a constraint I place on my own future action, or a signal I send into other people's reasoning? The two come apart precisely in the race: if it is a self-constraint, escalation is self-harm; if it is a signal, escalation is a bidding war in a currency no one can audit. Does the practice assume my future self is the same agent I am now — so that binding it is self-coercion and breaking it is betrayal — or a different one, so that a commitment is really a threat or a gift handed forward? Does it assume that credibility needs an enforceable sanction, or that sincerity can be enough? And does the racing itself assume something stranger: that commitments are comparative, that what matters is committing more than the other side rather than making my own conduct predictable — which would make a commitment closer to a status move than to a promise?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory the practice needs. Keep it under 900 words. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and mark the places where it is underdetermined, where two or three incompatible views would all license what we do. Then say what would change in the practice under a rival view — for instance, one on which a commitment is nothing but a public prediction, or one on which my future self is a distinct person whose consent matters. Lead with your bottom line.
