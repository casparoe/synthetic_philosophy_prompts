I should like to ask about a pattern of argument that I keep meeting, and about whether it — and the reply I want to make to it — has a name. I am not after an explanation of the physics; I am reasonably clear about that. What I want is to have the thing properly located, and to be told what it is not.

The pattern is this. A fact about the world is established by appeal to what differently situated observers would say. Two people walking past each other on the street assign different times to an explosion in a galaxy a million light-years away — differing by a day or so, on any honest calculation — and neither is mistaken, since there is no fact of the matter about what is happening at that distance at this moment. From this it is concluded that the explosion is occurring now for someone; that "now" is where reality is located; and hence that the distant explosion is as real as anything here. Chaining observers, the same reasoning makes every event that will ever occur already real.

What troubles me is not the conclusion but the step. The premise licenses every observer's assignment equally. The conclusion is then drawn from the assignments of one observer at a time. But if an assignment is equally licensed for all, it is also arbitrary for each — and running the reasoning with another, equally licensed way of assigning times at a distance selects a different set of events as present. It therefore looks as though the conclusion is carried by the manner of assignment rather than by anything discovered about time. The argument also seems indifferent between the past and the future; it does not single out the future, which is what its users generally claim it shows. I suspect that "now" is being asked to do two jobs — first as the slice of events simultaneous with a given observer, then as the locus of what exists — and that the argument goes through only because the two are allowed to blur.

So: does the move have a name, and does the reply have one? More to the point, how is my reply to be distinguished from the things nearest it — from the bare observation that simultaneity is relative; from the stronger claim that our way of assigning times at a distance is a matter of convention, which I gather is itself disputed; from the general charge that an argument proves too much; and from the point that a conclusion holding equally under every choice tells us nothing about any one of them? And is my reply distinct from the familiar argument that there is no privileged present, or is it that argument run backwards? If it is the same, I should like to know it; if it is not, I should like a name for it, together with an account of where its edges lie — which cases fall under it, and which merely resemble it.
