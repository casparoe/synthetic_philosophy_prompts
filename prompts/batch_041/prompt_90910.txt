I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've been circling a piece on J.L. Austin ever since a friend told me she's convinced The Importance of Being Earnest is secretly a play about speech act theory. I can't stop thinking about it. The whole plot runs on utterances that are supposed to *do* something rather than describe anything -- engagements, christenings, Bunburying, Lady Bracknell granting or withholding her consent -- and the farce seems to turn on Austins felicity conditions: the proposals only work if the right people with the right authority do them properly, the christening Jack keeps arranging with Dr Chasuble is a textbook performative complete with a minister and a liturgy, and the great reveal at the end, when the Army List shows his father was Ernest John Moncrieff, means Jack was already christened Ernest as a baby and the ceremony everyone was racing toward turns out to be pointless. So I want a reading of the play through Austin -- and Searle too, if he pulls his weight -- that takes both the philosophy and the comedy seriously: which of Austin's distinctions the plot actually illustrates (misfires versus abuses, the constative/performative line he eventually gave up, the locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary trio), where the play dramatizes something the theory under-describes, and where it quietly cheats.

The bit I most want you to press on is the name pun. Gwendolen and Cecily both insist they could only love a man named Ernest, which is like treating a name as if it guaranteed a character trait -- as if the act of naming could confer the very property of being earnest. Is that just a mistake Wilde is satirizing, or is he onto something Austin missed, namely how much of social reality is constituted by names and naming? And does the ending vindicate the performative picture or undercut it? Everyone gets what they want because a genealogical fact -- who your father was -- happens to match the name, so the resolution looks less like a speech act making the world and more like the world conforming to a description. I also want to know whether the epigram-heavy dialogue itself counts: so many of the famous lines look like statements but don't describe anything true, and I suspect they're doing something else entirely, maybe closer to Austin's abuses than to plain constatives.

Where does the play resist the reading, and what, if anything, does it add to the Austin-Searle debate that the standard seminar cases (the ship, the promise, the betting) don't? No need to quote lines at me, I've seen it staged more than once; just tell me which scenes carry the weight and why. A bit of structure would help -- I'm drafting a feature, not a seminar paper, so I care about what's genuinely at stake and what I should leave out.
