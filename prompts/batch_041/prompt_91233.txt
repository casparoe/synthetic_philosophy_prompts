So I want a short story out of you, and I want it to be about the perdurantism/endurantism thing — the worm view, where a person isn't one thing that's wholly present at each moment but a four-dimensional object made of stages, each of which is as real as the others, versus the view where you're one enduring thing that's all there at every moment. Not as a lecture, obviously. Here's the setup I keep carrying around and can't get to work: a portrait sculptor in a small town, maybe on the coast, has always seen people as long. She looks at someone and sees the toddler and the teenager and the arthritic old man all seperately and equally solid, none of them more really him than the others, the whole length of the person standing in the room like a log. She's had it since she was a kid so it isn't spooky to her, its just how a face works. Then the town commissions a memorial bust of a boy who drowned at seventeen, and the parents want him at seventeen, that year, and she can't do it — carving only that year feels to her like carving somebody's hand and calling it their portrait. That's only the setup though. What I actually want the story to chew on, and I've started calling it the inheritance problem: if every moment of you is its own thing, then no moment of you ever earns anything for the next one, and no moment owes anything for what an earlier one did, so gratitude, resentment, promises, apologies all come out looking like category errors — and they're also the entire substance of a life. So: somebody close to her did something at a stage they aren't anymore, and she has to decide whether to hold the long one responsible or only the slice, and the story shouldn't rescue her by revealing it was really about memory, or about people changing. If you can, put in a scene where two people disagree about whether an apology can be owed for something the apologiser doesn't remember doing, and let neither of them win it. She can also see her own long one, which she mostly avoids looking at. No teleporters, no uploading, no amnesia — normal town, clay and bronze and a mortgage. Don't have a wise old man explain the metaphysics to her in dialogue, and don't end on a revelation about her condition; I'd rather the last beat be small and practical, she either delivers the bust or doesn't, and we see what that costs her. 1200 words or so.
