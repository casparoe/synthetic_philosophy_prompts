I am a philosophy professor putting the finishing touches on a position piece about disagreement, and before I commit the following theses to print I want an audit of whether they can be held jointly.

(1) Aumann's agreement theorem is correct: two agents who begin from a common prior, whose posterior credences each arise by conditionalizing that prior on their private information, and whose posteriors and rationality are common knowledge, cannot have different posterior credences concerning the same proposition.

(2) The theorem's hypotheses are idealizations, but I take such idealizations at face value rather than dismissing them: because human beings share a great deal of language, training, and cognitive endowment, two interlocutors who are arguing in good faith typically do have roughly common priors and roughly common knowledge of one another's opinions and rationality.

(3) Two people who have weighed the same evidence and regard one another as epistemic peers may rationally continue to disagree; there is no general obligation to split the difference or to drift toward the other's view.

(4) There is no rationally correct prior: prior credences are not the sort of attitude that can be assessed as right or wrong, so the selection of a prior is not a locus of rational appraisal.

(5) Learning that a peer disagrees with me about a proposition is itself evidence bearing on that proposition, and should move my credence at least somewhat toward theirs.

Please tell me which of these are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be; order the genuine tensions by severity; and for each say what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair costs me. Then name the single revision that would resolve the most while conceding the least, and say what it leaves unresolved. I do not want a survey of the disagreement literature — I want the audit. Lead with a one-sentence verdict on whether the set is consistent as it stands, and keep the whole response under 600 words.
