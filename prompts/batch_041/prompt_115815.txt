A friend and I argued late into the night about whether Rousseau's case against the theatre in his letter to d'Alembert about Geneva still has teeth now that nearly all of us live through screens, and we got nowhere; I'd like you to settle it. Please take the role of a historian of early modern philosophy who specializes in Rousseau's moral and political thought. Think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side rather than rehearsing what Rousseau actually said.

Here is what I want you to decide. Rousseau's objection to the theatre ran roughly like this: it renders the spectator passive; it substitutes the display of virtue for its practice; it inflames the self-love that lives on the opinions of others; and it dissolves the transparent bonds of a small community in favour of private, artificial feeling. Now imagine he could spend a fortnight inside contemporary screen culture — prestige television, reality television, livestreams, comment sections, algorithmic feeds — and above all inside a world in which the audience is also the performer, everyone curating a self for watchers while the watchers watch one another instead of a stage. Would he read this as his diagnosis coming true, or as something his diagnosis cannot reach? Press him on two points in particular. First, does the collapse of the actor/spectator distinction make his worry worse, since we are now all implicated at once, or does it quietly refute him, since his complaint presupposed a merely passive spectator who no longer exists? Second, what would he make of an entertainment that is watched alone, on demand, and tailored to the single viewer rather than staged before a civic audience — and of the confessional mode of reality television, given that Rousseau himself laid his inmost self before a reading public? Answer first as Rousseau would, then step out of the role and tell me where the extrapolation is doing honest philosophical work and where it is anachronistic. Give me a focused essay, not a survey.
