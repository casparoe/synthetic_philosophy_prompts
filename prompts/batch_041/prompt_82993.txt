My doctoral student is writing her dissertation on pain asymbolia — you know the ones, the patients who'll tell you quite cheerfully "yes, that hurts" while you're pressing on the wound, and who sometimes laugh at it. She's built an arguement out of them that I've now read about nine times and I can't find the flaw in it. I'm pretty sure it proves too much. Have a look.

(1) When a person is in pain, two things are going on: the sensation — the stabbing in the hand — and the suffering, the way it bothers her.
(2) Asymbolia pulls these apart. The patient still has the sensation; she can point to it, describe it, say how bad it is. But on her own account of the cases it doesn't bother her at all, and she isn't moved to do anything about it.
(3) So the sensation on its own isn't what makes pain bad. The badness lives in the being-bothered.
(4) But being bothered is itself just another state of mind — it's an aversion, a wanting-it-to-stop, and there's nothing bad about an aversion except that it's a want going unsatisfied.
(5) So a pain is bad for you just when, and just because, you want it to stop.
(6) Wants, though, are contingent. The asymbolic patient has none. A masochist has the opposite. And there are creatures who, at a given moment, simply aren't wanting anything to stop: the newborn mid-procedure, the dog too absorbed in the smell to notice, the person so far gone she's stopped minding what happens to her body.
(7) So in none of those cases is the pain bad for the one who has it. Nothing is lost when it comes, nothing gained when it goes.
(8) Which is absurd. We're sure it's wrong to keep hurting someone who's stopped caring, and sure that a newborn's pain is a bad thing even though she can't want it away. Stick a cheap pill on the shelf that makes you asymbolic for a day and her view seems to say there's no reason left to avoid the root canal.
(9) So one of 1–7 is false and I want to know which.

She's already heard the obvious objection to (6) and she has an answer: fine, drop the "at that moment," wants can be dispositional, standing, unconscious. But then she says the asymbolic patient has no standing want either, and a newborn has no standing want in the sense that matters, because there's nothing it's like for her to be wanting anything. So the conclusion survives.

I'd rather keep (2) and (8) than anything else on the list. Tell me which step to cut, why it looked fine until I stared at it, and what it costs me to cut it. And if you think the thing is actually valid and I simply have to swallow (7), say so and tell me what I'm swallowing.
