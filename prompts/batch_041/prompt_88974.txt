I'm a novelist, and I've been invited to give a craft lecture on what fiction might take from the frame problem. Before I commit to it, I've written down what I actually believe, and I'd like you to test whether the set holds together.

1. The frame problem in its original guise — representing everything an action leaves unchanged, without an axiom for every such fact — is settled, I take it, by the standard move of assuming persistence unless something signals otherwise.

2. But there is a deeper version, which I'll call the relevance frame problem: a reasoner must also know which of the indefinitely many things that do or might change bear on the question in hand. This I do not think is a problem of representation at all, and I do not expect any formalism to solve it.

3. What fiction does when it succeeds is hand that problem to the reader: the text states a handful of facts, and the reader supplies the rest of the situation silently and without deliberation. Call that supplied remainder the unstated room.

4. I believe the reader's filling-in is a skill in roughly the sense that riding a bicycle is: not the application of any finite set of rules the reader could state.

5. In my own practice I plan a scene by asking, of each character, what that character would notice first, and I find the question reliably settles what I must state and what I may leave to the unstated room — reliably enough that I plan whole novels this way.

6. And I hold that a fictional world is determinate only where the text and the reader's ordinary norms make it so: for most unwritten detail there is no fact of the matter, and it is a mistake to ask whether the unstated room contains this or that.

I suspect at least one of these must give, and I would rather learn it from you than from a reviewer. Work through the set: tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension and which pairs only appear to be; for each real tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be; then tell me which single change would clear up the most. I want to know what my own views commit me to, not a survey of the literature.

End with one line beginning "Verdict:" that names by number the commitment whose revision resolves the most, followed by a dash and a phrase of at most eight words describing the revision.
