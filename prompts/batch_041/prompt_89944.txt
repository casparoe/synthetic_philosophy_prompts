Film club is doing The Truman Show next month and I've somehow been volunteered to lead the discussion, and I want to do it through the liberty literature rather than the usual "media manipulation bad" stuff, because I think the film is more interesting than that. My angle: Berlin's two concepts on one side, and on the other the republican idea — Pettit's non-domination, Skinner's neo-Roman point that a slave stays unfree even under a master who never actually interferes with him. Truman is the test case. Nobody in Seahaven is really coercing him, he's healthy, employed, married, and by the standards of pure negative liberty he's about as unobstructed as a person can be. But Christof has arbitrary power over every detail of his life and Truman sort of knows it, which is why he can't relax with anyone, can't say what he actually means to Marlon or his wife — he fails that eyeball test republicans like to use, the one about whether you can look the person across from you in the eye without having to watch yourself. So my first claim is that the film is a better dramatization of non-domination than most of the philosophy is, and I want you to tell me if that holds up.

Then two things I can't get straight and want you to take a real position on. First, Berlin. Christof's whole self-defense at the end is the classic positive-liberty move: he claims to know what Truman really needs, he's given him a life, the world outside is worse, so keeping him in is doing him a favour. That's exactly the move Berlin warns about, where the "real self" gets to coerce the empirical one. But is Christof actually a positive-libertarian, or is he just a paternalist with a nice speech, and does the film need Berlin at all once you've got Pettit? Second, the ending. Truman walks out the door and it plays as a triumph, but the picture of freedom on offer there looks like pure non-interference: there was a wall, now there isn't, so he's free. The republican tradition is supposed to say that's exactly the mistake — the freed slave stepping out of the house isn't free while the master's power is still standing there. And the film never shows us the outside at all. So is the ending a republican ending or a liberal one dressed up as a republican one? While you're at it, the audience: they cheer him and then immediately start flicking around for something else. Are they dominated too? If domination has to have a dominator — an agent who could interfere — then who is it for them, the network, the corporation, the show itself? If your answer is that nobody dominates them, that they're just bored, tell me why that doesn't sound like a cop-out.

What I want from the reading: it should be faithful both directions. Don't reduce the film to a diagram of Pettit, and dont bend Pettit to make the film come out looking prescient. Point me at the scenes that actually earn their keep — I'm assuming the marina and the boat, the dead father coming back, the mirror scene, the exit — and tell me which scenes I should leave out of the discussion because they cut against the reading rather than supporting it. I'd rather have three scenes I can defend than a list. And finish by saying what the film adds to the debate that the arguments alone don't; if the honest answer is that it's doing something cruder than the philosophy and I'm overreading it, say that instead of flattering me.

Last thing: tell me how confident you are overall, and single out the one point in your reading you're least sure of — if I'm misremembering a scence or quietly collapsing two of these distinctions into each other, that's the bit I want flagged rather than smoothed over.
