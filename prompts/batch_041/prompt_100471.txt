I sit on the editorial standards board of a public broadcaster, and we vote Thursday on whether to transmit a six-part drama about a real, recently deceased civil-rights organizer. The producers invented a scene in which she informs on a colleague under interrogation; her descendants and surviving comrades call this a posthumous betrayal, while the writers say composite characters and invented pressure are the price of dramatic truth. Our charter requires respect for the dignity of persons portrayed but says nothing about the dead, and my own family's story was fictionalized last year, so I have some sense of what is at stake. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. Recommend a concrete disposition—approve as submitted, approve only with an in-work notice identifying which central acts are invented, or require removal of the scene—and give reasons that both the producers and the descendants could engage. In doing so, address the strongest case against your recommendation, including the view that the dead cannot be wronged because they no longer have interests, and assess the test one member has proposed: a counterfactual-consent standard, under which an invented act may stand only if the real person, knowing the full script and its likely audience, would not have had decisive reason to refuse, with the burden on the producers whenever the invented act would attract moral blame.
