I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind currently working on the ethics of panpsychism, and I would like help working through a problem that keeps slipping away from me. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers, each a fully competent defender of their own position.

The first is a panexperientialist. She holds that phenomenal properties are fundamental and ubiquitous, and she endorses a principle I will stipulate for the purposes of this dialogue (I am not attributing it to any published author): the Continuity of Valence. Roughly: if a physical process P is, in creatures like us, the process that realizes the unpleasantness of pain, and if P comes in degrees and occurs throughout the physical world, then every occurrence of P carries some minimal degree of unpleasantness. She draws the uncomfortable conclusion that the overwhelming majority of morally relevant suffering in the universe is not located in animals at all, but is distributed across the microphysical realm.

The second is a physicalist. He holds that suffering requires a unified subject, that subjects are not fundamental, and that whatever the microphysical correlates of pain are doing in isolation, there is no one there to suffer. He regards the Continuity of Valence as a category error.

I want them to reach a genuine crux rather than a tidy resolution. Let the panexperientialist press the hardest question for the physicalist: whether the subject requirement is a threshold (and if so, why that threshold is not arbitrary) or a matter of degree (and if so, why there are no micro-subjects). Let the physicalist press whether the Continuity of Valence can individuate its bearers at all, or whether it multiplies subjects without limit — and let him give a non-question-begging account of why the step from human pain to microphysical P is anything more than a correlation. Neither character should win merely by being handed the better lines.

Then, after the dialogue, take a definite stand: state which position you find more defensible and why, and identify the single strongest objection to your own verdict.

Keep the entire response under 800 words.
