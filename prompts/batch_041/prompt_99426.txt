A scientist has examined 10,000 emeralds before 2030, all green (hence all gred and not bled). She predicts the next emerald examined after 2030 will be gred. Using Goodman's own argument, explain whether the critic has correctly identified the flaw in the gred prediction. The student wrote:

"Goodman's grue puzzle shows that inductive logic cannot be purely syntactic. The critic is basically right that gred is a disjunctive predicate: an emerald is gred if it is green before 2030 or blue after 2030, so it picks out a heterogeneous class. Green, by contrast, is a qualitative predicate that figures in laws of nature. That is why we are entitled to project green but not gred. Goodman's key point, though, is that this cannot be decided by logic alone; we need a theory of entrenchment. Entrenchment means that predicates that have been used successfully in past inductions get to be projected. Green is entrenched; gred is not. So the critic is right about the disjunctiveness but wrong to think that natural-kind membership is the criterion Goodman himself endorses. Goodman is a conventionalist: the choice between green and gred is ultimately a pragmatic choice, not a metaphysical one. Also, the green/blue vocabulary and the gred/bled vocabulary are not symmetrically definable: you can define gred and bled using green, blue, and a time index, but you cannot define green and blue using gred and bled without already assuming green or blue, because gred only tells you what happens before and after 2030. Thus the gred prediction is not a genuine inductive prediction at all, whereas the green prediction is."
