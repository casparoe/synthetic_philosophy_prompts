I have been reading about heuristic arguments — the kind that raise a conjecture's credibility without proving it, by exhibiting a model or an estimate rather than a derivation — and about attempts to formalize the presumption of independence that such arguments seem to rest on. A colleague states the underlying analogy as follows: a heuristic argument is a random model. One specifies a probability distribution over the objects in question, treats their parts as independent unless something forces a correlation, computes the probability that the phenomenon of interest occurs, and, if that probability is not vanishingly small, concludes both that the phenomenon is real and that a proof of it should be findable. He takes this to be the shared structure of the claim that the primes behave like a random set of integers with density 1/log n, and of the claim that a cryptographic hash behaves like a random function with independent, uniformly distributed outputs. Independence, he says, is simply the default when no mechanism creates a correlation, so presuming it is licensed by the absence of a reason not to. He concedes that the naive independence model misestimates twin primes by a constant factor, since divisibility by small primes is not independent, but he regards the corrected constant as a refinement of the same presumption rather than a counterexample to it.

What I want is an audit of the analogy, not a general defence of heuristics.

1. Give the correspondence: in an argument of this shape, what plays the role of the sample space, the random experiment, the independence assumption, the probability estimate, and the conclusion?

2. Say what the randomness in the model is supposed to correspond to in an object that is fixed and deterministic, and whether that correspondence can be made literal or whether it is doing covert work.

3. Separate the failures. Distinguish cases in which the probabilistic model is simply false of the object, so the estimate comes out wrong, from cases in which the model is accurate and the inference from it to existence, structure, or provability still fails. Give a concrete example of each.

4. Identify what the analogy imports without arguing for it — about the relation between independence and structure, between a short description and unpredictability, or between an event's having positive probability in a model and its occurring in fact.

5. Then give the verdict. Does the colleague's conclusion survive in any weakened form, and what would a genuine formalization of the presumption of independence have to establish in order to be a principle rather than a habit?
