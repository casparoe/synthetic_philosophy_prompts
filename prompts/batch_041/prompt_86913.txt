Ok so I think I've talked myself into the insect welfare thing, and specifically into the scale-argument version of it: there are on the order of a billion billion insects alive at any given moment, we obviously don't know whether any of them are sentient, but the credence we should put on it sure isn't zero, and once you multiply a smallish probability by numbers that big the expected amount of suffering in play swamps basically every animal-welfare issue we currently spend money on, farmed chickens included. Which means either insects are the top moral priority, or at minimum the argument can't be waved off with "they're probably just tiny robots."

I want the strongest case against. Not "bugs are gross" -- the actual defeaters, ranked by how much they should move me. Stuff I keep circling:

- Is the arguemnt even falsifiable, or is it built so nothing could count against it? What would *hurt* it? If a lab finds nociceptors in a fly brain that project somewhere plausibly integrative, that helps the case; but what result would make me lower my credence? And if nothing would, is that a bug or is it just what a moral-risk argument is supposed to look like?

- The Pascal's-mugging shape of it. The same structure runs for nematodes, for individual neurons, for whatever comes after insects -- any tiny probability times a huge enough number wins. Does that show the form is broken, or does the insect case survive because the probability isn't arbitrary, it's a real empirical credence you can't just push down to 10^-30 to make the problem go away?

- The comparability assumption. Multiplying headcount by a probability assumes there's a common unit of welfare across species, so that a marginal insect-sufferer and a marginal chicken-sufferer both get counted on the same scale. If welfare isn't cardinally comparable across taxa -- if insect suffering and chicken suffering aren't even in the same currency -- does the multiplication mean anything at all?

- The grain question. Do I scale by individuals, by lives, by seconds of experience? An insect life is short; a chicken's isn't. The ratio swings enormously depending on which unit I pick and I can't see a non-arbitrary way to choose.

- Social insects specifically. If an ant colony is the relevant individual -- one organism spread over many bodies -- then headcount isn't the right multiplier for a big chunk of the insect biomass, and it's not obvious which way that cuts.

- The population-ethics mess. If the practical upshot is "fewer insects" (less agriculture, less of whatever), a totalist view might say we just prevented a colossal number of possibly-happy insect lives. Does that undercut the argument or just complicate the remedy?

Tell me what someone who *holds* the scale argument would themselves concede counts against it, not just what critics say from outside. Which of the above are fatal, which are merely awkward, and which are things the view can absorb by tweaking a parameter? And if it turns out the whole thing is well-insulated -- you can always nudge the credence or the unit until it survives -- just say so plainly, and then tell me whether that's actually a problem for it or whether I should accept it as a moral-risk argument and stop worrying. I'd personally be most moved by something empirical that either collapses the credence or makes it irrelevant, so weight it that way.
