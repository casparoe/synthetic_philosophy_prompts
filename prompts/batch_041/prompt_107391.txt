After a career in chemistry I have retired, and I have been spending the time since then reading moral philosophy with more enthusiasm than method. I have just finished a well-regarded popular book on ethics, and its chapter on utilitarianism and its critics has left me uneasy. I would like you to audit that chapter's treatment of Bernard Williams, claim by claim, and tell me how much of it I should trust.

The book's account runs roughly as follows. Utilitarianism instructs the agent to maximize overall welfare, and to do that she must treat her own projects, attachments, and relationships as just one more set of interests to be weighed impartially against everyone else's. Williams objects that this demand is impossible to meet without a kind of self-betrayal: to be a good utilitarian you must stand ready to discard any project or commitment the moment discarding it would produce more total welfare, and a person who was genuinely prepared to do that would already have lost the commitments that gave her a life of her own. The book calls this the requirement of integrity. Its decisive illustration, it says, is the case of a man who can rescue only one person from drowning, with his wife among the possible candidates: Williams holds that the man must not weigh his wife's life against a stranger's on utilitarian grounds, because if he needs a moral justification before he may save her — if he must first satisfy himself that saving her maximizes welfare — then he is having one thought too many. From this the book draws two conclusions: first, that Williams has refuted utilitarianism, since no one could really live as it demands; and second, that Williams's own position is that we should simply follow our deepest commitments and refuse to let morality interfere with them.

I suspect the chapter is being unfair to Williams — it reads more like a prosecutor's brief than an exposition — but I do not know the literature well enough to say where. Please separate the claims a careful reader of Williams would accept, the ones that would make such a reader wince as a simplification or a conflation, and the ones that are simply wrong. Tell me in particular whether the integrity objection and the one-thought-too-many point are one argument or two, and whether the drowning example is being put to the use Williams put it to. Then, suppose everything accurate in the book's summary is granted: do its two conclusions follow from that accurate core, or do they depend on the parts that are not accurate? I am less interested in a verdict on utilitarianism than in knowing what I can safely carry away.
