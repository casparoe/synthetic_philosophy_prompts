I have no academic training — I run a hardware store — but I have read philosophy on my own for years, and for six years I have been a lay member of the board that governs a small pilgrimage chapel in my county. There are nine of us: three clergy, three lay volunteers, and three members of the family on whose land the chapel stands. Our written rules permit the board, by a two-thirds vote, to enter an event in the chapel's record as a miracle, and that entry is what authorizes the annual commemoration, the plaque, and the line in the newsletter that brings us most of our visitors. In November we take up the first such request in the chapel's history, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before the vote.

The request concerns a woman of about sixty who was prayed over at the chapel by a visiting group two years ago and who is now, by every measure her oncologist has, free of a cancer that had reached her liver and her bones. Her own account is of a warmth and a voice, which no one can check. The record is stranger than I expected. The scans after are unambiguous; the scans before were read at a clinic that has since closed, the slides are gone, and the only account of how ill she was comes from two nurses and a physician who have all moved away. Her oncologist says he cannot explain the remission, then adds that remissions of this kind, though rare, are documented, and that he would have said the same of any of them. A shrine of another denomination twenty miles away certified a very similar cure for the same cancer in the same year, and I notice that the reasons we give for doubting theirs are the reasons an outsider would give for doubting ours. I also notice that certification brings pilgrims, and that the family on our board has had a hard few years financially. Two of my fellow members say that refusing would be a failure of faith; one of them buried a daughter who was prayed for every day for four years.

I read Hume's essay on miracles long ago and it settled the question for me, which is exactly why I do not trust my own judgment now. Please draft the opinion I will circulate. I need something I can defend in a page: a clear bottom line, the date by which the board should act, and any conditions or wording the recommendation requires. It has to be usable by members who have never read Hume and will not, so the arguments must stand on their own rather than on his authority. Argue the case for certifying as well as it can be argued before you answer it — including the part where a refusal is itself a judgment about what God did and did not do, made by people with no standing to make it. Say plainly what the board would be asserting if it voted yes, and whether a board of this kind can assert it on the evidence it has, given that whatever we enter in the record will be quoted by others as testimony long after everyone in this room is gone. And tell me whether our rule asks the right question at all. It seems to me that if God could act through ordinary causes, then a test of inexplicability is beside the point, and if that is right, then my voting against this request for the wrong reason would be worse than useless.
