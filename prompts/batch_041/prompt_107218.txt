Okay so I'm in a third-year metaphysics course and one of our units is Parfit and personal identity, and for the final creative piece we have to write a short story that actually puts one of these ideas to work. I was hoping you could help me draft it. You're basically a philosopher who also writes literary fiction, so lean on both sides of that — I want the philosophy doing real work inside the story, not just name-dropped in the dialogue.

Before you start writing, spend a few sentences brainstorming out loud: what's the actual philosophical crux you want the story to dramatize, what single scene will carry it, and where are you going to resist the obvious/tempting version? Then write it.

Here's the setup I want, and please don't sand off the weird bits. Set it maybe sixty years after teletransportation became totally mundane — it uploads you, kills the body, rebuilds you somewhere else, everyone's fine with it, it's as boring as flying. The part I care about: the company that runs it has, in the last decade or so, started selling what they call a "continuity audit." When a transport goes wrong (power flicker mid-scan, partial packet loss, that kind of thing), you can pay to have an auditor reconstruct what actually survived. My protagonist is one of these auditors. Her job is to look at a botched scan and decide whether the person who came out the other side is the same person as the one who went in — and if not, how much of them is left. The company pays "survival insurance" to your family on a sliding scale depending on her verdict, so this is not academic, it's money and grief and lawsuits.

One thing I want you to build in, because it's the bit from class I keep chewing on: there seem to be at least two different things we might mean by continuity, and they can come apart. There's memory/psychological continuity in the standard sense — the new person remembers being the old one, the traits line up. And then there's what I've been calling concern-continuity — whether the new person actually cares about the old person's ongoing projects, promises, and relationships, whether they'd feel the old one's commitments as their own. A rebuild can score high on the first and low on the second. I want the audit to turn on that gap. So the concrete case at the spine of the story should be genuinely borderline: the rebuilt person has all the memories but the weighting is subtly off, the cares don't quite line up, so the auditor can't just say "same" or "different" and move on.

I want the reader to feel the pull of two things at once: the reductionist view that identity was never what mattered and that enough connectedness is enough (so the whole audit is arguably asking the wrong question), and the gut feeling that when you're the one in the scanner, the audit is the only question there is. Don't resolve it cleanly — I'd rather it end on a genuine dilemma than a lesson.

Couple of smaller asks. Please avoid the worn-out examples everyone reaches for (the sleeping pill, the amoeba splitting, the brain transplant into a senator) — if you need a comparison, invent one. And make the ending turn on something specific to this world: maybe she has to audit someone close to her, or she finds an earlier audit that contradicts her own method, something that forces her to take a side she didn't want to. Aim for roughly 1200–1800 words. Think carefully about the structure before you write, and keep the dialogue natural — nobody lectures in real life.
