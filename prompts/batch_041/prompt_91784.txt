Two camps in the theory of collective judgment have argued for decades about the discursive dilemma without ever producing a document both could sign. Camp P holds that a body which must act as one — a licensing board, an appellate court, a hiring committee — is obliged to aggregate its members' judgments on the reasons and let the decision follow by its own stated rule; a body that licenses a treatment while denying one of the conditions it has adopted for licensing is not merely awkward but irrational, and collective irrationality is a defect to be designed away. Camp C holds that the body's business is the question actually before it, decided by majority; the members' judgments on efficacy and safety are steps in the members' own reasoning rather than items the body is obliged to endorse, and it is no defect of a body that its findings fail to entail its decision. I want the joint statement these two camps could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

Anchor the document in a single concrete case rather than arguing in the abstract. A five-member licensing board must judge three propositions: P, that the evidence meets the threshold for efficacy; Q, that the evidence meets the threshold for safety; and R, that the treatment should be licensed, where the board's rules stipulate that R holds if and only if P and Q both hold. The members' judgments are as follows, in the order P, Q, R: yes, yes, yes; yes, yes, yes; yes, no, no; no, yes, no; no, no, no. Every member's set is internally consistent; the majorities are not.

The joint statement should contain the following.

1. A numbered list of claims both camps accept, covering what the dilemma is and is not; what this profile does and does not show; what each procedure would license this board to do; and what follows for a body that must reach a single decision rather than merely record findings. Every claim should do work — reject formulations that are true but inert.

2. Each residual disagreement stated twice, once in wording Camp P would accept as its own and once in wording Camp C would accept as its own, with neither version loaded in favour of the other side. Include, if you find it there, the disagreement about whether the board has judgments at all or only decisions.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each, give a case, argument, or consideration that both camps agree bears on it, record each camp's prediction about how it comes out, and classify it as empirical, conceptual, or normative.

4. An assessment of the standard escape routes. The dilemma is often dissolved by imposing a priority order on the propositions — settle P first, then Q, and let R follow — or by having the body vote on the agenda before voting on the propositions, or by requiring the members to deliberate to consensus before any vote. Say whether each move dissolves the problem or relocates it, and in particular whether, if the priority order must itself be chosen by the board, the dilemma reappears one level up. If the camps divide here, record the division.

5. A treatment of one variation on the case: suppose the board's rule is itself contested, with some members holding that R requires both P and Q and others holding that efficacy alone suffices. Both camps are to say whether this changes the analysis, and whether the dilemma as originally posed presupposed an agenda the members had never agreed on.

6. An attempt to identify any part of the dispute that is merely verbal. Consider in particular whether the two camps use claims about what the board judges in different senses — a descriptive sense, about the output of a procedure, and an ascriptive sense, about what the body is answerable for — and, if they do, give a disambiguated formulation under which the residual disagreement survives.

7. A closing paragraph, signed by both, stating what each camp would count as evidence or argument against its own position and what, if anything, both agree would actually move the debate.

Sections 1 and 2 should be presented as a numbered agreed-claims list followed by a disagreements table. Argue the case on its merits; no citations or reading lists are wanted.
