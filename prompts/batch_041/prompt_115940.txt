I have chaired the standing policy-review committee of a mid-sized research institute for two years now — eight members, mostly working scientists and administrators, none of us trained in philosophy — and each quarter we take up proposals to repeal or substantially rewrite an existing rule: a credentialing requirement, a data-retention rule, a safety protocol, a sign-off everyone agrees is slow. Our de facto method has been to ask who wrote the rule and whether anyone can remember why. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I have come to think that method is exactly backwards: the Burkean suspicion of reform, and the image of the fence whose purpose the reformer has not bothered to learn, are the right place to start — but only if they are turned into something my colleagues can actually run, rather than a license for inertia.

Please give me a decision procedure the committee can follow. Specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, which steps one member can complete in advance as homework, where the procedure terminates in a recommendation to keep or repeal, and where it must hand the question back to the committee as a judgment about values or about who bears the risk. It has to survive the obvious abuse — 'we cannot reconstruct the purpose, therefore keep it' — so it should state explicitly when the burden of proof shifts to the rule's defenders, and it should treat differently the rule whose original purpose is documented but obsolete, the rule that is undocumented yet evidently load-bearing, and the change that can be reversed cheaply. Assume one hour of committee time per proposal, no access to the original authors, and a final decision that goes to the board.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a legacy rule I personally regard as turf protection that the procedure nonetheless tells me to keep; a rule with a noble-sounding rationale that the procedure tells me to repeal; and one case where the procedure stalls.

Structure the response as: 'The procedure' (about 800 words, numbered steps); 'Worked cases' (about 600 words, one per case); 'Where judgment cannot be removed' (about 250 words, naming the steps that are value judgments dressed up as method, and what the procedure cannot do at all); and 'The one-page version' (about 150 words, a card members can keep in front of them).
