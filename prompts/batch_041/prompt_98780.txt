I want to spar with someone who genuinely believes in path dependence — not the watered-down "history matters" version, the strong one: lock-in is common, increasing returns do the heavy lifting, and "it would've happened anyway" is almost always hindsight talking. So play that person, and stay in character till I say stop. You can use the real machinery — increasing returns, network effects, sunk coordination costs, adaptive expectations — but you have to actually argue with it, not just intone the words. Dont strawman yourself either: if I land a clean hit, concede it and keep going, don't pretend it bounced off. A paragraph or two per turn, like two people actually arguing.

Heres my opening. Take a case I've been chewing on: a string of fishing towns up one coast, none of them in contact with each other, that all ended up with the same oddly shaped lateen rig within about a generation, even though a rival rig was measurably faster in the winds they actually get and was sitting right there the whole time. A historian I like calls it textbook lock-in — once enough boats had the first rig, the sailmakers, the spare-spar trade, the apprentices, all of it bent around that design, and switching stopped paying even after the fast rig got proven. Fine. But you can tell that story about any outcome whatsoever after the fact. When two regions with the same coast and the same menu of options diverge, you call it lock-in; when they converge, you call it functional selection. Heads you win, tails I lose. So: what would count, in advance, as evidence against lock-in — and can you save the sail case without the story turning into an unfalsifiable one? Be honest too: isn't there a decent chance the slower rig just wasn't as good as the historian claims, and there's no lock-in here at all?
