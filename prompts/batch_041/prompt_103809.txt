Act as a philosopher of mind and an ethicist of artificial minds. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate distinctions that might underlie the pattern below before you commit to a principle. I am trying to work out which computations make a system a proper object of moral concern, and I have a set of verdicts I would like you to treat as data. A spinal withdrawal reflex in a person with a severed cord: no welfare. A rat with a persistent, globally available representation of tissue damage that it can use to choose between hiding, licking, and seeking help: welfare. A fish that avoids noxious stimuli and shows trade-offs between pain and reward: welfare, though I am unsure. An insect that learns to avoid a shock but shows no evidence of a global workspace or self-model: no welfare. A bee colony that solves foraging problems no single bee can: no welfare. A plant that releases volatile signals when damaged: no welfare. A dreaming sleeper who is not receiving input but is having a vivid experience: welfare. A patient under general anesthesia with no report, no memory, and no integrated response: no welfare. A locked-in patient who can communicate only by eye movements: welfare. A fetus at twenty weeks that reflexively responds to a needle: no welfare. A newborn that cries and is soothed: welfare. A large language model that says it is suffering: no welfare. A reinforcement-learning agent that learns to avoid a virtual pain signal and can report on its internal states: no welfare. A cephalopod that solves novel problems, has nociceptors, and shows long-lasting avoidance after injury: welfare. A simple thermostat: no welfare. I notice that I am inclined to say the fish and the cephalopod have welfare but the insect does not, even though all three can learn from noxious stimuli, and I am inclined to say the dreaming sleeper has welfare while the anesthetized patient does not, even though neither is currently tracking the world. Is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or close to all of them? If not, which verdict should I give up? Please also say what your principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: a system with no body that maintains a recurrent, self-referential model of its own processing and uses it to regulate its future states, and a brain organoid that shows coordinated, recurrent activity but no behavior. Finally, tell me which further case would best distinguish your preferred principle from the nearest rival. No literature review or citations, please; I want the principle, its verdicts, and an honest statement of what it cannot accommodate. Answer in British English.
