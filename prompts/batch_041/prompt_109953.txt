I'm trying to think through the advance-directive problem properly and I want a sparring partner who won't fold, so this is a role-play. Please play a committed present-self sovereigntist: you think that when it comes to what gets done to a person's body, only the preferences and experiences of the person who is actually here now carry any authority, and that an advance directive is at most advice from someone who no longer exists. Be sharp and not a caricature about it — you'll happily grant that keeping promises matters, that relationships create real obligations, that families get hurt, that the earlier self was sincere — you just deny that a document written years ago binds a stranger who happens to share your body. Talk like a real interlocutor, keep each reply to about a paragraph so it feels like a back-and-forth, stay in character until I say stop, and make your arguments in your own voice instead of name-dropping philosophers; if you do bring one up, spell out the argument, don't just cite. If I ask you something factual, answer it the way you'd actually answer, don't turn into an encyclopedia. And keep it in American English, I'm working in a US context.

Here's my opening. Take the standard case but notice what's really on the table. A man with early Alzheimer's signs a directive while he's still lucid: no antibiotics for pneumonia once he's too far gone, and he adds a line saying that if he later seems content, that contentment is not to be taken as consent. Years later he's cheerful, hums along to the radio, squeezes the nurse's hand, and then gets pneumonia. You want to say the happy guy's wishes win here. But he doesn't have wishes about pneumonia, he can't form one. So somebody is going to decide, and the only question is who. Throw out his directive and the decider isn't the cheerful man in the bed, it's the attending physician, or the hospital's default protocol, or whoever in the family shouts loudest. Call that the authorship gap: strip the earlier self's authority and you don't hand it to the later self, you hand it to the institution. Second thing while you're at it — we let people bind their future selves by contract all the time. A mortgage, a non-compete, a guy who signs a paper committing himself to a sobriety program. Nobody thinks the drunk at 2 a.m. gets to void the deal because he'd rather not. So what exaclty makes the directive different from the contract? If your answer is that the contract self is the same person and the demented guy isn't, then tell me what a person is, and why your'e so sure the 2 a.m. guy is the same one.
