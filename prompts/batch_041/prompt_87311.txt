Two members of a student events committee, Priya and Marcus, are deciding what the committee should recommend to the university about two proposed speaker events. The first event (call it Event A) is a talk by a controversial outside speaker; the second (Event B) is a talk by a student group that wants to critique that speaker. The recommendation will name exactly one of four options: both events go ahead (AB), only the first goes ahead (A), only the second goes ahead (B), or neither goes ahead (N). Priya and Marcus each have the following considered rankings:

Priya: AB > A > B > N
Marcus: B > AB > N > A

The committee's decision procedure is meant to honour three commitments:

1. The liberal principle of personal domain: where a choice falls squarely within a person's own sphere of concern, the collective decision should follow that person's ranking of the alternatives, whatever anyone else thinks. In this case, Priya is treated as the decisive voice on whether her own side's event proceeds given the other side's, and Marcus is treated as the decisive voice on whether his side's event proceeds given the other side's. Concretely, this principle requires that the committee rank AB above A (following Priya) and rank B above N (following Marcus).

2. The weak Pareto principle: if both Priya and Marcus strictly prefer one option to another, the committee must prefer it too. Both rank AB above N, so the committee must rank AB above N.

3. Rationality of the collective outcome: the committee's overall ranking must be a genuine ordering -- complete and transitive, with no cycles.

Please explain what goes wrong here. Work through the requirements one at a time and show how, taken together, they generate a contradiction, and why the contradiction is not an artifact of any one member's preferences but is generated by the liberal principle itself once the two domains interact. Then say what this case is meant to teach: what exactly the liberal paradox shows about the conflict between a commitment to individual domains of choice and a commitment to the standard aggregative principle of collective choice, and why a defender of individual liberty cannot escape the result simply by insisting that people should be left to decide their own business. I am less interested in a formal proof than in a clear, vivid account of the mechanism: which preference reversals do the work, why each person's ranking is individually defensible, and why no obvious repair -- dropping Pareto, or weakening the transitivity requirement, or letting the committee simply pick a winner -- actually rescues all three commitments at once. Where you reach a point that looks like it could be dissolved by a merely verbal manoeuvre, say so and explain why the manoeuvre fails.
