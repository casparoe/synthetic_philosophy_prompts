Problem. In class we introduced the Invariant Parameter Principle (IPP) as a proposed refinement of the principle of indifference for problems with continuous symmetries:

(IPP) Suppose an experimental setup is described only by a group G of transformations that map the set of possible outcomes to itself. If there is a probability measure on the outcome space that is invariant under every transformation in G, then that measure—and only that measure—is the rationally required prior. Indifference is to be applied to the orbits of G, not to arbitrary parameterizations.

Consider a unit circle. A chord is generated by one of the following three procedures:

(A) Endpoint method: choose two points independently and uniformly at random on the circumference, then draw the chord joining them.

(B) Midpoint method: choose a point uniformly at random in the disk, then draw the unique chord having that point as its midpoint.

(C) Radial method: choose a direction uniformly at random, then choose a distance d uniformly at random from [0,1] along the corresponding radius, then draw the chord perpendicular to that radius at distance d from the center.

Let L be the length of the chord. Let E be the event that L is greater than the side length of the inscribed equilateral triangle.

(i) Compute P(E) for each of the three methods. Show your derivations.

(ii) Apply the IPP to each method. Which of the three methods, if any, does the IPP select as rationally required? Explain what symmetries each method is invariant under.

(iii) Does the IPP resolve Bertrand's paradox? In your answer, address whether the fact that a method is invariant under the Euclidean symmetries of the circle is sufficient to single it out, and whether the principle needs to be supplemented by a choice of parameterization or a larger symmetry group.

Student's response:

(i) For the endpoint method, let θ be the smaller central angle between the two random points. The chord length is L = 2 sin(θ/2). Since the two points are independent and uniform, θ is uniform on [0, π]. The side of the inscribed equilateral triangle is √3. So L > √3 iff 2 sin(θ/2) > √3 iff θ > 2π/3. Therefore P(E) = (π − 2π/3)/π = 1/3.

For the midpoint method, let d be the distance from the center to the midpoint. For a chord with midpoint at distance d, the chord length is L = 2√(1 − d²). So L > √3 iff 2√(1 − d²) > √3 iff d < 1/2. Under the midpoint method, the midpoint is uniform in the disk, so the probability that d < 1/2 is the ratio of the area of the disk of radius 1/2 to the area of the unit disk: π(1/2)²/π = 1/4.

For the radial method, the chord length is again L = 2√(1 − d²), and d is uniform on [0,1]. So L > √3 iff d < 1/2. Hence P(E) = 1/2.

(ii) The IPP says to use the unique invariant measure under the symmetry group. For the unit circle, the natural symmetry group is O(2), the group of rotations and reflections that preserve the circle. The endpoint method is not invariant under O(2) because the two endpoints are ordered: the procedure produces an ordered pair (p, q), while a chord is an unordered pair {p, q}. A rotation or reflection acts on the ordered pair, and the uniform measure on ordered pairs is not the same as the uniform measure on unordered pairs. Therefore the endpoint method fails the IPP. The midpoint method, by contrast, is defined by choosing a point uniformly in the disk. The uniform area measure on the disk is invariant under all rotations and reflections, and the midpoint is a complete parameterization of the chord. So the midpoint method satisfies the IPP. The radial method also chooses a direction uniformly, but it then chooses d uniformly on [0,1]. This is not invariant under the same group because the radial coordinate d is not a Euclidean coordinate; a reflection or rotation does not change d, so the measure on d is not forced. In fact, the radial method treats the distance from the center as a primitive parameter, whereas the midpoint method treats the midpoint as primitive. Since the midpoint method's parameter space is the disk itself, and the disk is the natural outcome space for a chord's midpoint, the IPP selects the midpoint method uniquely. Thus the rationally required probability is 1/4.

(iii) Yes, the IPP resolves Bertrand's paradox. The paradox arises because we were applying indifference to different parameterizations—endpoints, midpoints, or radial distances—without noticing that these parameterizations are not all invariant under the same symmetry group. Once we require invariance under O(2), the endpoint method is excluded (because of the ordering of endpoints), and the radial method is excluded (because it introduces a non-Euclidean radial parameter). The only method that remains is the midpoint method, which gives a unique answer, 1/4. The fact that the other methods are also invariant under rotations is not enough, because they are not invariant under the full Euclidean group of the disk; the midpoint method is the only one that respects the disk's geometry as a whole. Therefore Bertrand's paradox is not a genuine paradox about the principle of indifference, but a sign that we had failed to apply the principle to the correct parameter space.
