I'm a second-year epi masters student and I've gotten weirdly obssessed with a made-up dataset I built for a class exercise, and I want to see a proper dialogue about it. All of it is invented, there's no actual drug involved: call them Drug A (new) and Drug B (old), for a fictional infection, 1000 patients on each. Among the patients who ended up in the ICU in the first 48 hours, 20% of the Drug A patients died (120 of 600) versus 25% of the Drug B patients (50 of 200). Among the patients who never went to the ICU, 10% of the Drug A patients died (40 of 400) versus 12.5% of the Drug B patients (100 of 800). So inside each group Drug A looks better. But overall 16% of the Drug A patients died (160 of 1000) versus 15% of the Drug B patients (150 of 1000), so overall Drug A looks worse. The reason is obvious enough: 60% of the Drug A patients are in the ICU group versus 20% of the Drug B patients. (The overall gap is tiny and it's a toy example, sampling error isn't the issue here.) This is the part that keeps me up: the drug gets started in the ER and the ICU transfer happens after that, so you can tell a story where going to the ICU is just a marker of how sick they looked on arrival, meaning the doctors gave the new drug to the sicker patients, meaning the overall comparison is contaminated and the within-group comparison is the honest one -- or a story where the drug itself is what puts people in the ICU (it tanks their blood pressure early), meaning ICU transfer is downstream of the treatment and conditioning on it is exactly the mistake the biostatisticians are always yelling about, meaning the ugly overall number is the real one. Same table, opposite conclusions, and nothing in the table picks a story.

So: write the dialogue between my two friends. Priya is a biostatistician; she thinks the within-group comparison is the one to trust here, and that people who shrug and say it just depends what question you're asking are dodging. Marcus is a philosopher of science who works on causal inference; he thinks the fact that you can flip which comparison counts as correct just by telling a different story about one variable means neither number is a fact about the world until the causal story is settled, and that the data can't settle it. Let them go at it for real -- they should respond to each other's actual moves rather than talk past each other, press on each other's weakest step, throw each other's numbers back in each other's faces. If either of them wants to zoom out (you could slice the table even finer and get a third answer; the overall number is the one that answers the policy question of what happens if everybody gets Drug A), great, as long as it lands back on this table. If one of them gives ground it should be because the other actually earned it, not because they split the difference. Marcus can't be allowed to end on a shrug -- if he thinks the choice is underdetermined by the data, Priya should force him to say what he'd tell the attending physician who asks him about a patient that afternoon. And neither of them should be a strawman; I honsetly don't know who I think is right and I want to read something that makes me less sure, not more. A page or so is fine.
