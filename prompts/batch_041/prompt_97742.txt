I am designing a 90-minute session for a 20-person undergraduate seminar in decision theory and philosophy of probability, and I want a runnable activity that makes students feel the St. Petersburg paradox rather than hear it summarized. The activity should unfold in three stages: a calculation stage in which small groups derive the expected value of two lotteries that both have divergent expected value but different tail behavior; a market stage in which each student bids tokens for a single ticket to play one of the lotteries, with the winner then able to resell the ticket in a second sealed-bid auction; and a play stage in which the ticket is resolved publicly and payoffs are made in tokens that have no cash value. Do not name the paradox or introduce expected utility until the debrief.

The first lottery is the standard doubling lottery: a fair coin is tossed until the first tail, and if the first tail occurs on toss n, the payoff is 2^n tokens. The second lottery has a heavier tail: if the first tail occurs on toss n, the payoff is 2^{2^n} tokens. Both have infinite expected value. Students should calculate both, then be asked to choose which ticket they would rather hold; the point is that expected value maximization gives them no basis for the choice, while their preferences are not silent. The market stage should force them to put a finite price on each ticket, and the resale stage should expose whether their buying price and selling price for the same ticket are consistent. By the end, they should be able to explain why two lotteries can both have infinite expected value yet be rationally ranked, and why that is a problem for a simple expected-value maximizer.

Please provide: a minute-by-minute timeline; the materials and a physical randomizing device, preferably a spinner or card mechanism that makes the doubling-and-continuing structure visible; the exact rules, roles, and payoff scheme; a debrief organized around the moments in the activity where the mathematical fact, the decision-theoretic response, and the psychological response come apart; a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each; and a candid statement of which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I, as facilitator, will have to supply. The debrief should connect what happened to the distinction between expected value and expected utility, to the idea of bounded utility, and to the question whether rational choice requires maximizing expected value. The activity should be self-contained, with no assigned reading and no reliance on web access.
