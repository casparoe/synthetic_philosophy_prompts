ok so I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and I've been going back and forth with a friend of mine who's a physics postdoc about why time has a direction. His line is that it's just the second law. Picture a deck of cards, he says: it starts in order, and any shuffle you do only makes it less ordered, and the reason we call one direction the future is just that it's the direction the deck keeps getting more shuffled in. The ordered start is a brute fact he calls the past hypothesis, and there's no deeper mystery to chase. The conclusion he wants out of this is that time's arrow isn't really in the physics at all, it's a bookkeeping fact about a special starting point, so nothing would be incoherent about a universe whose arrow pointed the other way, and we should stop looking for a deep explanation of it.

Two things nag me about it though. First, as a programmer, in order isn't a property of the deck, it's a property of whoever wrote the sorting rule. Any particular arrangement of the 52 cards is one exact permutation, no more or less special than any other, so calling one of them shuffled seems to be about my description of it, not about the cards. Second, a shuffle is just a sequence of swaps, and every sequence of swaps can be run backwards, so if the underlying dynamics are deterministic and reversible, where does the probability even get in? He says unshuffled states are just incredibly rare, but the exact sorted arrangement is exactly as rare as any other exact arrangement, so that can't be the whole story.

So: how far does the deck analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondence first, what plays the deck, the shuffle, the sorting rule, the sorted start, and whether anything plays the sorter, then tell me where it breaks. Seperate the breaks that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just pedantry, and tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. And if the deck is the wrong picture, what should he be using instead?
