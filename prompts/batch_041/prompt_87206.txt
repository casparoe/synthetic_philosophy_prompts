I am a novelist, and next month I am to give the dedication address for a memorial plaque in the small mill town where I grew up. Forty years ago a chemical release there killed eleven men; for the decade that followed, the company that owned the plant proved — with its own engineers, its own chronology, its own vocabulary — that nothing of the kind had occurred. The plaque is going up on the fence. I have been rereading Arendt on lying in politics, and what I cannot get past is the difference between the old political lie, which conceals an act and in doing so still concedes that there is a fact to conceal, and the modern one, which manufactures a whole world, so that the men who repeated it for thirty years can no longer say what they themselves believed. Several of those men will be in the front rows, with their grandchildren. The address must not accuse them and must not absolve them; it cannot claim that we always knew, because we did not; and it must not offer the plaque as proof, since a fact stated is not a fact believed. I have spent my working life making things up, and the only defence I have ever had for my trade is that a novel asks to be taken as made while a lie asks to be taken as fact, the difference lying not in the sentences but in what they demand of the reader. I would like the address to turn on that. Nobody in that yard has read a word of her and I do not want her named from the podium. Write it: four minutes, spoken outdoors, and let it end by proposing the wording for the plaque itself, a dozen words at most, which the speech has earned.
