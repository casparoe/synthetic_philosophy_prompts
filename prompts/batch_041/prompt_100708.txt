OK so our theory reading group has been going in circles on backward induction for like three months now and I want you to help me figure out whats actually going on rather than just tell me whos right.

Heres the setup as concretely as I can put it. Two players, a pot of money that grows every round that both players pass, and either player can stop at any point and take the bigger share of whatever is in the pot right then, and everybody knows there's a fixed final round. Backward induction says: in the last round the player to move takes, so the second-to-last player knows the last move is take and therefore takes, and it unravels all the way back to round one — you take immediately. Same story in a finitely repeated prisoners dilemma: since the last round is a one-shot defection, the round before unravels too, and you defect from round one. And then people just don't do that. In the lab they pass in centipede games, they cooperate in repeated PDs, and even people who know the BI argument cold sometimes don't play the BI move. That's been known for ages and the theorists haven't given up backward induction — they say the subjects aren't rational, or don't beleive the others are, or have odd payoffs, or are making mistakes.

The part that bugs me is that some people insist the puzzle isn't empirical at all. They say the real problem is that common knowledge of rationality plus the structure of the game is inconsistent — that you can't coherently be rational and know that everyone else is rational in a game whose solution depends on everyone being rational — or that the notion of rationality being used is the thing in question, since your best move here depends on what other rational agents will do. Others say thats just a confusion and the theory is fine. And meanwhile the experimentalists act like the data has settled it, which the theorists think is beside the point. Oh, and everyone agrees that if the horizon were unknown or infinite cooperation is an equilibrium, but the whole puzzle is that when the end is known it supposedly falls apart and yet it doesn't.

So: why has this not been resolved? Is it a substantive disagreement about the theory of rationality, a verbal one about what the word rational is picking out, an empirical disagreement about what people actually do, or are the two sides not even answering the same question — one asking what an idealized agent must do under perfect common knowledge, the other asking what a real agent should do? Give me a diagnosis with reasons that actually engages what each side claims, not a survey of the literature. And tell me honestly whether anything could end it — a theorem, a better equilibrium concept, a change of question — or whether it just keeps going because the question is ill-posed.

My own hunch, which I'd like you to attack: it's really a fight about whether rationality is a normative ideal you reason from or a descriptive model of what agents do, and that once you seperate those the whole thing dissolves. I'm not confident in that though.
