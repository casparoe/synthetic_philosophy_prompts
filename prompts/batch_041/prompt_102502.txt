I got into a long argument with a friend last night — she's an econ PhD student, I did a philosophy of science minor — and I can't stop chewing on it. Her line was basically that it doesn't matter whether a model's assumptions are realistic, only whether the predictions come out right; she said some famous old essay by Milton Friedman settles the whole thing, and that anyone who complains about "unrealistic assumptions" in economics just doesn't understand how modeling actually works. I pushed back that some assumptions clearly aren't innocent — like when an epidemic model assumes everyone mixes with everyone else equally, and then it completely misses superspreading, she said that's "just an idealization" and the model is still useful for the average behavior. But that felt like cheating to me, because the whole reason superspreading matters is that the average behavior is exactly what's misleading you. So I want you to explain the actual conceptual issue here. What is the difference, if there's one, between an idealization that's genuinely harmless because it just simplifies something you could in principle relax later (frictionless planes, point masses, no air resistance) and one that's doing real theoretical work and so can't be waved away by pointing at predictive success? Is there a principled way to tell which is which, or is it really just "it depends on what the model is for" and nothing more?

And seperately, is Friedman's view really as crude as "assumptions don't matter, only predictions do," or is there a more defensible reading of it that survives the obvious objections? I'd like a gut-level, concrete explanation — not just a slogan about the purposes of models, but an account of what actually does the distinguishing work. A couple of concrete examples of idealizations on each side (harmless vs. load-bearing) would help alot.
