I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and something has happened in my family that I can't stop turning over, in part because it bears directly on a paper I'm trying to write about why pain is bad. Six weeks ago my father had a stroke, and since then his left hand has been giving him trouble in a way none of us anticipated. He says the back of it burns and aches — he uses the word "pain" without any hesitation, and when a nurse asked him to rate it out of ten he said eight — but almost nothing about his behaviour matches that number. He doesn't pull the hand away from anything, he doesn't wince or grimace, he sleeps through the night, and when the nurses offer him something for it he waves them off. When I asked him whether it bothered him he looked puzzled and said, "It's there. It just doesn't bother me." He is not a stoic about pain; he complained for months about a bad back. What unsettled me most was that his neurologist seemed unsurprised by all of this, said the stroke is as recovered as it is going to get, and told us there is nothing further to be done about the hand.

What I can't get past is that I have always assumed that if someone is in pain, they are suffering, and that relieving the suffering is the entire point of treating the pain. A friend who works in palliative care told me that what torments people is often not the sensation itself but the fear, the sleeplessness, the sense that it will never end — and I could not tell whether that was wisdom or evasion. So I would like you to treat this as a philosophical problem rather than a clinical one; his neurologist is handling the medicine, and I am not asking you to second-guess her. Please take the role of a philosopher of mind who works on the affective and motivational dimensions of experience and who also knows the clinical material well enough not to say anything foolish. Before you commit to any reading of the case, lay out the competing ways of interpreting what is going on with him, and only then tell me which you find most defensible and why.

Here are the questions I actually want answered. Is my father suffering? Does that question even have a determinate answer, or does it depend on which account of pain's badness one accepts — that it is bad because of how the sensation feels, because of the desire it generates to make it stop, because of what it does to a life, or something else again? If the hurting and the minding can come apart the way they appear to have come apart in him, what does that show about what pain is and about where its badness lives? And if he genuinely is not suffering, is there any reason left to treat the pain at all? I would like you to be scrupulous about which parts of your answer are settled among people who study pain and which are genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested view as the consensus, and if the way I have framed these questions already presupposes an answer, say so.
