I am a third-year philosophy student, and this term I am taking a seminar on metaethics and its applications, which is how I have ended up with a part-time internship at the standards and ethics desk of a provincial health ministry. Three days a week I screen the draft briefing notes that analysts send up to the deputy minister: six or eight a week, twenty to forty pages each, and I have roughly ninety minutes per note before the director signs off and it leaves the building. Our current rule of thumb, written by a lawyer and beloved of nobody, is to flag anything that looks like a value judgment. It flags every sentence containing 'should' and waves through precisely the notes that worry me — the ones where a table of numbers slides into a recommendation and nobody can say afterwards where the seam was. I have been told to build something better, and I have the run of the metaethics material to do it.

What I need from you is a procedure I can actually run on a draft, not an essay about the is-ought gap. Concretely: an ordered list of questions to put to a note, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and returns that the note does not support its recommendation, and the point at which it hands the matter to a person — the deputy minister, or whoever owns the value premise — because what looked like an inference has turned out to be a question about what we care about. I do not need a reading list; I have the reading, and I need the instrument. The difficulty is that the standard alleged counterexamples to the gap are exactly the notes that land on my desk. One says that a manager promised to report the quarterly numbers and concludes that he is obliged to report them, and I cannot simply tell the analyst that this is an illegitimate move from an institutional fact, because the office will not accept that a promise generates no obligation whatever. Another says a home-care program is 'failing' because it missed targets set two years ago and forty per cent of referrals drop out — the figure does not by itself yield the word 'failing', yet 'failing' is not obviously a concealed 'should', and the same seems true of an attributive 'good' in 'a good intake form'. A third reaches its recommendation through a thick word such as 'coercive' or 'neglectful' that appears to carry its evaluation inside it. Your procedure has to say what to do with each of those without collapsing into either everything is a value judgment or only the word should counts.

Then run it on three notes from last month. The first argues from a table of median surgical wait times to the conclusion that the ministry should contract out to private clinics. The second is the home-care note above, in which the targets, the dropout figure and the recommendation are all on the page and only the middle term is missing. The third is a pure cost-benefit table with no evaluative vocabulary anywhere in it, which the desk has been quietly asked to block on other grounds; I expect the procedure to clear it, and I want to be told so plainly even though it will make my week worse. For each, say where the procedure lands, name the bridge premise it exposes, and say whether that premise is one the desk is entitled to supply on the note's behalf or one that must go upstairs. Finally, tell me the two or three places where the procedure will return a wrong verdict — where a clean note gets flagged as unsupported, or where a note that is really just an enthymeme with an unstated and controversial premise gets passed as rigorous — and how someone using it should recognise that it has misfired.
