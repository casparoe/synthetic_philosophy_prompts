I am a retired experimental physiologist — forty years spent on the physiology of sensory systems — and since retiring I have been teaching a second-year seminar in the philosophy department on pleasure and pain. I am now setting the final examination and would like your help with one essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The topic is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the objection that the things we call pleasures — the taste of a cool drink on a hot day, the relief when a headache lifts, the click of a solved crossword, a phrase of music, learning that a friend has recovered — appear to share no common phenomenal or functional feature, so that a hedonism which treats pleasure as a single quantity to be maximized is either vacuous (pleasure is whatever we happen to like) or parochial (pleasure is the particular sensory thrills of the theorist). I have taught the standard positions in the unit — the sensation account, the attitude account, the desire account, the hedonic-tone account, the apparent-good account — and I do not want an examination that can be passed by matching each position to its stock objection. I want the students to have to reason about a case I have built for the occasion, and to say what it does and does not establish. Please use the case exactly as I set it out below, and instruct the students that they may not appeal to any empirical finding beyond what is stipulated.

The case (I have been calling it the Dimmer Dial). Suppose a subject is fitted with a device that scales the felt pleasantness of everything she experiences. The device leaves sensory content untouched — the drink tastes the same, the headache has the same throbbing character, the crossword has the same structure — leaves her desires and her functional dispositions untouched, and leaves the sequence of her experiences untouched. It changes only how pleasant those experiences feel, along a single scale running from a floor at which she reports total indifference to a ceiling at which every experience is intensely pleasant. Two further stipulations: the dial is continuous, and it is modality-blind, so that one setting governs the taste, the throbbing and the crossword alike. You may assume the device is physically possible; you may not assume that the subject's reports about it are theoretically innocent.

The question should have three parts, and I would like the parts to build.

(a) Separate what the heterogeneity objection is a claim about. In particular, distinguish the conceptual claim that we have no single concept under which all pleasures fall from the metaphysical claim that there is no single property they share, and say why hedonism's prospects look different depending on which of these is at stake. I do not want the question to presuppose that hedonism requires a single scalar quantity; the student should have to decide whether it does.

(b) Then turn to the case. What, if anything, does the coherence of the Dimmer Dial show about the heterogeneity objection? I want the answer to reckon with the possibility that the case is question-begging — that a device scaling pleasantness along one dimension simply presupposes the common currency the objection denies — and with the possibility that it is not, and then to reach a verdict about which it is.

(c) Finally, ask the student to take the two accounts from the unit that she thinks are strongest and to say, for each, whether it can accommodate the case and what it must give up in order to do so.

Please give me the question as it would be printed on the paper, with a note on how long it should take and what materials are allowed; a model answer of about 650 words that would earn full marks; and a marking scheme worth 25 marks, itemized as the specific moves a top answer must make, with the marks for each and, for each, the mistake students typically make instead.
