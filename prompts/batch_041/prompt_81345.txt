I write simulation and numerical software — Monte Carlo estimators, randomized algorithms, seeded pseudorandom number generators, cryptographic randomness. My working notion of probability is entirely internal to that practice, and I have long suspected it is not the philosophers' notion. I have read enough philosophy of probability to recognize the three labels — frequentism, the subjective or credence-based interpretation, and the propensity interpretation — but not much more than that.

Please translate each of the three, one at a time, into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, without quietly importing the target framework's assumptions and calling the result a translation. I want each one restated in terms of the things I actually work with: a generator and its seed; the distribution object a sampler draws from; the ensemble of runs and the stream of outputs; the difference between a seed I do not know and a seed that is genuinely undetermined; calibration and long-run frequency as things a test suite checks; and what it means for an adversary with bounded resources to be unable to distinguish my stream from a truly random one.

Then give me the residue report: which claims in each interpretation have no counterpart in my vocabulary, which of my ordinary locutions have no counterpart in theirs, and where you had to distort something to make it fit. Four points interest me in particular.

First, whether my vocabulary can express any notion of a single-case objective chance, or whether, in my terms, a statement about the probability of this particular run collapses into either a statement about the seed, which is deterministic, or a statement about my ignorance, which is subjective. If it collapses, say whether that is a defect of my framework or evidence that the philosophers' single-case chance is doing less work than it appears to.

Second, whether the propensity interpretation survives the trip at all, or whether the generator's bias parameter is a translation of it or merely a replacement for it.

Third, whether pseudorandomness — a stream that is deterministic in fact yet indistinguishable from random to any observer with bounded resources — is a fourth interpretation of probability that the standard taxonomy simply lacks, or a restatement of one of the three. If it is genuinely a fourth, state it and say whether it is coherent; if it is not, say which one it is and why my instinct that it is different is mistaken.

Fourth, the reference-class problem. In my world the ensemble is always the set of seeds I might have drawn from, and that set is fixed by how I wrote the code. Tell me whether the philosophers' worries about reference classes under frequentism are the same problem or a different one wearing similar clothes.

Close with a verdict: if I keep only my own vocabulary, what do I lose that actually matters, and what do I keep that the philosophers' debate obscures?
