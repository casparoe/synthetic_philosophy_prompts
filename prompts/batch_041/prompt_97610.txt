I'm in the second year of a philosophy PhD and, for reasons that started out practical and have turned embarrassingly existential, I deleted all my social media accounts last spring after several years of near-constant participation, and I have been trying to understand what I was actually doing there. The odd thing is that the seminar I'm taking on nineteenth-century Danish thought keeps ambushing me: the more time I spend with Kierkegaard's writings on the press and on the curious entity he calls the public, the more I suspect he had already described, with unnerving precision, the thing I was caught in — long before anyone could have imagined a feed. I have to give a short presentation in that seminar, and I want to spend it on a deliberately counterfactual question rather than on exegesis.

I would like you to take the role of a historian of philosophy with a specialist's knowledge of Kierkegaard's authorship and of the social and journalistic world he was writing against, and to reason as carefully and concretely as you can about something for which there is no settled answer: what would Kierkegaard have thought of the phenomena that only appeared long after his death — the algorithmic feed, engagement metrics such as likes and shares, anonymous and pseudonymous accounts, viral pile-ons, the fact that a controversy can now be conducted entirely by strangers who never meet, never act, and never take responsibility for anything? I am especially interested in three things. First, whether his critique of the press and of the public transfers cleanly to platforms, or whether the platform changes the diagnosis in kind — for instance, whether the sheer speed and the quantification of opinion make the problem better or worse than what he was complaining about. Second, whether his own habit of publishing under pseudonyms gives any support to online anonymity, or whether his pseudonymity was doing something so different that the comparison collapses. Third, what he would say about the individual who participates in these things — is the person who posts, likes, and shares simply the crowd, or has the platform produced a new kind of participant he never had a word for?

Before you answer, brainstorm: list the commitments in his authorship that seem most relevant, and be explicit about which ones you are treating as load-bearing. Then give me your best reconstruction of his likely verdicts, and — this matters most to me — separate what actually follows from his principles from what is merely plausible extrapolation, flagging the places where the counterfactual is underdetermined and where our own categories may be smuggling in assumptions he would have rejected. Where you think he would have been ambivalent, or would have refused the question as posed, say so rather than manufacturing a verdict. You do not need to provide citations, a reading list, or references to particular works; I care about the reasoning, not the bibliography, so write it as a continuous argument and make the structure of your answer clear.
