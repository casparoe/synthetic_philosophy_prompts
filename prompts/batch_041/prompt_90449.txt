I have drafted a short essay on Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, and I would like detailed comments on it. The draft is below; my specific requests follow.

For several decades the debate over Frankfurt-style cases has been largely a debate about a residue. Everyone grants that in the standard case the agent cannot avoid doing what he does: an intervener stands ready to force the action should any sign of resistance appear, and because no such sign appears, the action proceeds on the agent's own. The live question is whether something smaller survives the intervener's presence — a flicker of freedom, an alternative that is real but thin, such as the option of beginning to form the intention to refrain, or of showing the sign that would have triggered the intervention. The incompatibilist says the flicker is enough to preserve the principle of alternative possibilities, on which responsibility requires that one could have done otherwise. The compatibilist says a flicker is not a genuine alternative, and that a principle read so thinly does no work.

I want to defend a claim that cuts across this standoff. The flicker strategist is right that an alternative survives, and right that its survival is not an accident, but wrong to think that this rescues the principle in any form an incompatibilist can use. What the flicker shows is that some alternative event is compossible with the actual sequence, not that the agent had an alternative course of action. The difference matters because responsibility attaches to what an agent does, and doing is not merely being the site of an event.

To see the gap, consider what I will call the Manufactured Flicker. The intervener does not merely stand ready to force the agent's hand; he also stands ready to produce the very sign that would trigger his own intervention. If the agent is about to waver, the intervener will first cause in him the twitch of hesitation that counts as the sign, and then, having received the sign, force the action. In the actual sequence nothing of the kind occurs: the agent deliberates and acts on his own. Now ask whether he could have done otherwise. The flicker strategist must say yes, since the twitch was possible. But the twitch is now twice over the intervener's artifact: it is what the intervener would have made, and it is what the intervener would have responded to. If that counts as an alternative for the agent, the notion of an alternative has come loose from the notion of an agent's own doing.

The natural reply is that the Manufactured Flicker is a gimmick: the original case already contained the point, and the addition changes nothing. I think that is half right, and the half that is wrong is instructive. What the gimmick isolates is that the flicker strategist never specified whose alternative the flicker is. The flicker is an alternative in the causal order, not in the agent's practical reasoning. Once we ask what the agent could have done for a reason — what was available to him as an agent — the thin alternatives evaporate.

This suggests a diagnosis. The principle of alternative possibilities is not refuted by Frankfurt-style cases but quietly reinterpreted by them. What survives is a principle about robust alternatives, ones the agent could have taken up as his own, and the flicker strategist has spent his efforts defending a thinner premise that is either false or unnecessary. If that is right, the interesting question is no longer whether a flicker survives, but what makes an alternative robust enough to bear the weight responsibility places on it.

I would like comments on four things. First, accuracy: are the two camps as I have described them, and is the flicker reply characterized fairly, or have I set up a version of it that no one holds? Second, whether the Manufactured Flicker does any independent work or is merely the original case with extra machinery, and if it does work, whether it works for the reason I claim. Third, whether my conclusion is a defense of incompatibilism or a quiet abandonment of it; I suspect it is the latter but have not convinced myself. Fourth, whether the essay is interesting as a contribution rather than a restatement, and where it would most need to be strengthened.

In your comments, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus. Write in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a candid letter to a colleague.
