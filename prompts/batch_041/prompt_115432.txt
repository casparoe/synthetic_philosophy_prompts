I teach moral and political philosophy, and through a series of poor decisions I've ended up as the academic member of the panel that advises our Ministry of Defence on honours and awards — the body that looks at cases where a decoration might be forfeited, restored or upgraded. I'm the only person on it who isn't a retired officer or a career civil servant, which means I write most of the opinions and they mostly ignore them, but here we are. The case in front of us is this: a gallantry award given to a sergeant, now deceased, for an action during a campaign that our own government later conceded had been launched on a false pretext, and that most historians now treat as an unjust war of aggression. He was on the side we've since decided was the aggressor. At the engagement in question he held a position under heavy fire so that a group of civilians could get clear, and was badly wounded doing it; he died of the wounds a few days later. His family have asked us to keep the award. A veterans' group has asked us to strip it, arguing that you can't honourably win an honour in a dishonourable war and that decorating him launders the campaign. Two of my colleagues say the courage is the courage and the justice of the cause is none of the panel's business — which is roughly what our own terms of reference say, since they contain a clause providing that awards are assessed 'irrespective of the merits or lawfulness of the conflict in which the act occurred'. That clause now has its defenders and its enemies on the panel, and the chair wants the whole thing resolved in one go.

So what I need from you is the written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: a clear recommendation on the sergeant's award — keep it, strip it, or keep it with some qualification, and say plainly which — plus a recommendation on what to do about that clause, keep, amend or strike. It has to be a document the panel can actually vote on, so give me the bottom line up front and the reasoning after it. And it has to take the losing side seriously rather than patting it on the head; I've taught this debate for fifteen years and it never once looked like a panel decision, and half the people round that table hold the view I'll probably be arguing against, so I need to be able to hand them an opinion that shows I've understood why they think as they do. Something in British English, please, and don't hedge the recommendation into mush — I'd rather be voted down than be told it depends. Oh, and no bibliography; we're not marking essays, and half of them have never read a word of the stuff I'd be citing anyway.
