I recently took over the upper-level seminar in my department, and the first unit I inherited is a formal-systems module built around a puzzle my predecessor devised. The students are meant to spend two weeks inside it, applying rules mechanically, before anyone is allowed to step back and talk about the system as a whole. Last week a student asked me, in front of the class, whether "you can't get there" is something the rules themselves ever tell you, or whether it's always something you smuggle in from outside. I gave an answer I'm not happy with, and it has been nagging at me since — partly because I think it bears on how we teach reasoning generally, and partly because I suspect I've been quietly conflating two very different kinds of "stepping out."

The system is called XY. Its alphabet is the three symbols x, y, and z. There is one axiom: the single-symbol string x. There are four rules of transformation, each of which may be applied to any substring of a string that matches its left-hand side:

- Rule 1: replace any occurrence of x with the two-symbol string yz.
- Rule 2: replace any occurrence of the adjacent pair yz with the pair zy.
- Rule 3: replace any occurrence of the adjacent pair zz with the single symbol x.
- Rule 4: delete any occurrence of the three-symbol run yyy, leaving the surrounding symbols to close up.

A string counts as derived just in case some finite sequence of these rule applications leads from the axiom x to it. The seminar's guiding question is whether the string yyy can be derived.

I'd like a short story that dramatizes what it is to be stuck inside such a system, and then to get out of it — not by finding a longer derivation, but by a move of a different kind. I want the reader to feel the difference between the two. Please set it somewhere that has nothing to do with logic classrooms or mathematics: the characters should have their own reasons for caring about the puzzle, and those reasons should be under pressure from the story's events. The formal material should be load-bearing, not decorative — the reader should be able to see why the escape move works and why no amount of working within the rules could have produced it. You may let characters be wrong, and you may leave some things unresolved, but the central intellectual pivot should be sound and honestly earned.
