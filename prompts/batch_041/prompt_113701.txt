I have been trying to reverse-engineer the rule behind a colleague's attributions of dispositions, and I would like you to predict her verdict on a case she has not yet seen. To keep extraneous chemistry out of the way she works with a fictional material: bars of an alloy she calls Tarn, which she defines as bendy exactly when a bar of it bends upon being placed in a magnetic field. Her recorded verdicts on five bars are as follows.

(1) A standard Tarn bar, straight off the shelf. Bendy.

(2) A Tarn bar fitted with a charm that, at the instant the bar enters the field, re-tempers its internal lattice so that it stays straight. The charm acts on the bar itself, and only when the field is applied. Not bendy.

(3) A Tarn bar that has stood for years inside a rigid external clamp, which would hold it straight when the bar is placed in the field. The clamp touches the bar but does not alter it in any way. Bendy.

(4) A steel bar, of an alloy that does not bend in fields, fitted with a charm that bends it the instant it enters the field. The bending is produced by the charm and not by the bar's own structure. Not bendy.

(5) A Tarn bar that was annealed years before she acquired it, so that it no longer bends in a field; no charm, no spell, and nothing about the annealing has anything to do with the field. Not bendy.

The new bar: a Tarn bar fitted with a charm that, at the instant the bar enters the field, projects an external brace that holds the bar straight. The brace is not part of the bar and neither touches nor alters its lattice; the bar's own structure is exactly what it was before the test.

Please do four things. First, predict her verdict on the new bar. Second, state the rule she appears to be running. Third, set out at least one alternative rule that fits all five earlier verdicts equally well, and give the verdict each rule yields on the new bar. Fourth, give your best guess at her verdict together with your reasons for preferring one rule over the other, and name the further case — one she has not yet considered — whose verdict would separate whatever rules remain tied once the new bar has been decided.

Some constraints on the answer. Her five verdicts are the entire dataset: I do not need citations, references to the literature, or the names of published positions, and I would rather you argue from the cases than from what anyone has written about such cases. (I recognize that these are the sorts of case that get called finks, masks, and mimics; the labels are not what I am after.) Note also that whatever she is running, it cannot be the plain conditional analysis, since a bar that would not bend because of the clamp is bendy by her lights, while a bar that would bend because of a charm is not. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the verdict and in the rule you favour, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
