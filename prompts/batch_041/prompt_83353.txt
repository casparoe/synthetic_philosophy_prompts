You are a philosopher of mind and social ontology with a good ear for when a dispute is really about words. I want a diagnosis of an impasse, not a survey of the literature, so think carefully before committing.

Here is the dispute. On one side are individualists, who hold that anything we say about "we intend" or "we did it together" must be constructed out of the intentions, beliefs, and plans of individual agents — a shared intention being just a structure of interlocking individual intentions plus common knowledge, with nothing further needed to explain how two people walk together, cook a meal, or play a duet. On the other side are those who say this is a category mistake: when I intend that we do something, either the subject of the intention is not me, or the intention is irreducibly a we-intention, and joint action rests on a joint commitment that generates obligations neither party holds on their own. Both sides have exchanged arguments for decades; the examples are shared, the intuitions about the examples are largely shared, and nobody moves.

My hypothesis — to be tested, not assumed — is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one is giving an explanatory-psychological account of how coordination is produced, the other a normative or conceptual account of what makes an action ours. If that is right, both could be correct and the appearance of conflict is manufactured by the word "intention." But take seriously two rival diagnoses as well: that the dispute is verbal in a shallower sense, each side stipulating its own sense of "shared," and that it is substantive but rests on a disagreement about what a philosophical explanation of a social phenomenon owes — reduction to individual psychology versus fidelity to the first-person plural.

To make this concrete, use a case I have not seen treated in exactly this form. Two strangers in a crowd each independently begin pushing a stalled car; neither knows the other's intention in advance; each adjusts continuously to the other's force; afterwards both would say, sincerely, that "we pushed it out." Say what each camp must say about this case, and whether any camp's answer reveals that it was addressing the other camp's question all along.
