I'm a novelist — third book just came out, so I've been doing a lot of standing at podiums lately, and this Saturday I have to give the toast at my little brother's wedding. He's marrying a philosopher, and not just any philosopher: she works on decision theory, and a solid third of the guest list is her department. The rest is our family, who are lovely and who will tune out completely the second I say the word probability. So the thing has to work on both floors of the building at once, and I'd rather it be a real toast than a TED talk with cake.

Here's what I want it built on, and I don't want this to be decoration — it should actually decide what I can and can't say in the speech. There's a game decision theorists kick around (I'm fairly sure it's called the Pasadena game; I read about it years ago and it's never left me). You flip a fair coin until it comes up heads. If the first heads lands on flip number n, you win 2 to the n over n dollars — except the payoff is negative when n is odd and positive when n is even. So flip one pays you minus 2, flip two pays plus 2, flip three costs you eight thirds, flip four pays 4, and so on, growing in both directions. Every single outcome is a perfectly ordinary finite number, and the game ends with probability one, because the coin has to come up heads eventually. But when you go to compute the expected value — multiply each payoff by its probability and add them all up — every term collapses to plus or minus 1/n, and that alternating series only settles on a number if you add the terms in one specific order. Shuffle the order and you can make the total come out to anything you like. So the game has no well-defined expectation at all, even though it always ends and every outcome is finite. There's a cousin of it where all the payoffs are positive and the terms are just 1/n, which marches off to infinity instead — that game has an infinite expected value, which is a completely different disease from having none. That distinction, undefined versus infinite, is the thing I want the toast to get right, and I want the philosophers in the room to hear that I got it right, without me standing there lecturing their relatives about series convergence.

So: write me the toast, about four or five minutes spoken. It needs to actually do something with the idea that you can't assign a single expected value to a thing when the value depends on the order you add the pieces up in — and that this doesn't make committing to it irrational or blind. I don't want the lazy move where undefined just gets quietly swapped for infinite and we all say love is infinite; the infinite version is the boring one and it's also false. I also dont want a math lesson on a napkin, and no citing papers or naming researchers, my brother would never forgive me. Land it as affection. If it helps, you can invent one small concrete anecdote about the two of them — they met at a conference, she was presenting, he asked a question that was mostly an excuse — but keep it light and don't let it eat the speech.
