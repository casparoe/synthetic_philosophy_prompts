I've spent the last few weeks arguing with friends after a seminar on Kantian ethics, and I've talked myself into a position I haven't seen stated quite the way I want to state it. The familiar murderer-at-the-door case is almost always posed as a binary: may you lie to the would-be killer about your friend's whereabouts, or must you tell the truth? The rigorist says you may not lie; nearly everyone else says that of course you may; and the entire debate proceeds as though those were the only two moves available. I want you to argue, in the role of a philosopher who works on deontology and on the ethics of assertion, that the case is a false dilemma — that there is a third option, refusing to answer, declining to serve as an information source at all, and that once that option is taken seriously, both the rigorist and the humanitarian have mis-described the moral terrain. Begin by laying out, in a few sentences each, the strongest versions of the two standard answers, so that I can see you are not knocking down straw men, and then make the positive case: why non-assertion is a genuinely available move, what it is about assertion in particular that the other two answers have wrongly treated as unavoidable, and why the difference between refusing to speak and speaking falsely is a moral difference rather than a merely verbal one.

The objection that keeps me up at night — and that I want you to meet head-on rather than talk around — is that refusal is just deception by other means. If the murderer will reliably infer from your silence that you are hiding your friend, then declining to assert produces exactly the false belief that lying would have produced, and anyone who condemns the lie while embracing the silence is drawing a distinction without a difference. If your argument cannot survive that objection, say so plainly, rather than rescuing the position with a stipulation about what this particular murderer happens to infer. Think carefully and brainstorm before you commit to a line; I would much rather have one argument that walks straight into the hard case than three that dodge it.

Be candid throughout about what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus. I especially want to know which parts of your argument rest on a controversial view of what assertion is, or of what the person at the door is owed, and where a reasonable interlocutor would simply dig in.
