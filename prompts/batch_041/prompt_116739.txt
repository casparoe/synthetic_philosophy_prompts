Reading group politics question, hope this is the right place to ask. Our group has a rule that if we can't reach consensus in twenty minutes we settle it by a show of hands, majority wins. Last week someone defended the rule by saying it's basically the Condorcet jury theorem — wisdom of crowds, two heads are better than one, the more people you ask the more likely the majority is to be right, so the group's verdict is more reliable than any one of us. Felt like alot of weight to hang on one sentence.

Here's our actual situation. We're eight people. Four of us came out of the same seminar and have basically the same priors about everything; one guy is contrarian enough that when the rest of us like a paper he'll usually argue the other way just to be difficult; and a couple of us genuinely know the literature better than the others do. Also, half the things we vote on aren't yes/no — they're "which of these three interpretations is the author going for."

Can you break "the more people you ask, the more likely the majority is right" into the different precise claims it could be making, from the ones that just restate what a majority is up to the ones that are real results with conditions attached, and tell me for each whether it's true, what it needs, and how fragile it is? Specifically I want to know what happens to each version when (a) the voters aren't independant, (b) some of the voters are worse than random, (c) there are more than two options on the ballot, and (d) whether any given voter is better or worse than random is itself a coin flip. Then tell me which of those readings my friend actually needs for our rule to be justified, because I suspect the version doing the real work is way weaker than the version that sounds impressive. Lay it out as a numbered list so I can paste it into the group chat.
