I chair the founding convention of the Meridian Federation, a new union of seven provinces, and I must circulate a written opinion before Thursday's vote on how we are to read the charter we are about to adopt. The preamble and the bill of rights promise that no member shall deny any person the equal protection of the laws; the charter speaks throughout only of persons and never names the class it is protecting. But a separate article, placed in an appendix and inserted to secure the adhesion of one province, grandfathers that province's hereditary labor-conscription system for twenty-five years and bars the other members from interfering with it. One bloc of delegates argues that if we take the charter's own language seriously, the equality clause already condemns the practice, and that we should say as much in an interpretive resolution; the other bloc, which supplied the votes that carried the convention, insists that the grandfathered article expresses the founders' true intention and that overriding it would tear up the very bargain that created us. A delegate keeps quoting a nineteenth-century American orator who held that a founding text must be judged by what it plainly says rather than by what its authors privately wanted. Please draft the opinion I will read aloud: recommend a concrete course for Thursday, give my reasons, and set out and answer the strongest case for the side I decide against.
