I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and for the last few weeks I've been reading around the question of whether a person could have come from a different sperm and egg. I've read enough to know that plenty of people think the answer is no — that each of us is tied to our actual biological origin in a way that rules out our having been conceived from any other gametes — and I find the view both surprisingly plausible and surprisingly hard to pin down. I've been arguing about it at lunch with a colleague who thinks the whole question is a verbal trap, and I'd like to try it against someone who actually holds the view rather than against a summary of it.

Please play that person: a committed defender of the claim that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg, someone who knows the objections and has thought about them, not a caricature. Stay in character until I say stop. Concede small points where they are genuinely right, but never the main claim, and keep each reply to one tight paragraph so that it reads like a conversation rather than a lecture. If you think my question is confused, say so and tell me why — I would rather be told I am confused than be humoured. No need to tell me who else has held the view.

Here is my opening move. A friend of mine has an identical twin: one sperm, one egg, one fertilised cell that split into two people. By your lights, neither of them could have come from a different sperm and egg — but notice that this origin is something the two of them share, so it cannot by itself distinguish the one from the other. That matters, because part of what I want from an origin story is an explanation of why my possibilities are not my twin's possibilities, why I could not have been him. So either the essential origin is coarse enough that two different people can have exactly the same one, or you push it finer — not the sperm and the egg but this particular cell, this particular split — and then I want to know what fixes the grain. I can describe the same history coarsely (the sperm and the egg, the meiosis that produced the sperm, the two people who met) or finely (this sperm out of the several hundred million, this second, this point on the egg's surface), and there are now children whose origin looks genuinely split — the mitochondrial-donation cases, where the nuclear DNA comes from one woman's egg and the mitochondria from another's. Nor can the answer just be the genes, since a clone grown from a skin cell of mine would carry my DNA without being me. Nothing in your view seems to tell me which level of description is the privileged one, and my worry is that the origin is an artefact of how we happen to describe the case rather than a fact about me. Which is it: do my twin and I share our essential origin, or is it finer than that — and if it is finer, what stops the slide toward finer and finer descriptions?
