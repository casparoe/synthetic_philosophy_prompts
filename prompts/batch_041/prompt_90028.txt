A colleague of mine and a visiting philosopher spent most of a dinner arguing over Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness and whether it genuinely escapes the Cartesian theater or merely relocates it. I stayed out of it and took no side. Here is the exchange as it actually went.

(1) The visitor began: the multiple drafts model is built to deny that there is any place or moment at which contents come together and thereby become conscious. But the model still has to say which of the simultaneous, partially revised drafts the subject is conscious of, and its answer is whichever one wins the competition and goes on to influence the most downstream processing. That is a finish line. Calling it a threshold of causal influence rather than a place does not change the logic; a theory that grades contents by their downstream reach has moved the stage out of the theater and left the audience sitting in it.

(2) My colleague replied that the audience is as fictional as the stage, and that this is the model's central claim rather than a hole in it. Nobody is watching the drafts. The subject is a narrative center of gravity inferred from what the system says and does, and the theory is deliberately revisionary about the data it explains: it accounts for the reports, not for an inner spectator who reads them.

(3) The visitor said this trades one problem for a worse one. If the subject is a construction, the model cannot say of any content that it was conscious at a given time before the system was probed or asked to report. She offered a case: a system probed twice about the same brief interval gives incompatible reports, first that it saw a face, then that it saw only noise. On the model, she said, both reports are in order and neither is the error, since the probing is part of what fixes the content. That, she claimed, amounts to abandoning the very question a theory of consciousness is supposed to answer, namely whether the content was conscious at the time independently of whether anyone ever asked.

(4) My colleague answered that the intuition she was appealing to — that there must be a fact of the matter at the time, waiting to be discovered — is precisely the Cartesian intuition the model exists to dislodge, so demanding it back begs the question.

(5) The visitor pressed on: even granting that, the model owes an account of why some contents become reportable and others never do. If the answer is simply that the winners win, it is either circular or it is doing the ordinary functionalist work that a global-workspace-style theory does, in which case the anti-Cartesian rhetoric is doing no work and the two theories differ only in vocabulary. My colleague replied that real work is being done, because the model denies that there is a determinate temporal order in which contents become conscious, and that denial is a substantive claim with consequences.

Neither would budge, and I genuinely could not referee it. Please adjudicate the five moves one by one, saying for each what is right and what is wrong in it, then identify where the two are talking past each other, what both are missing, and who has the better of the argument overall.
