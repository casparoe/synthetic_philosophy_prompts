I'm a TA for a graduate decision theory course. The midterm asked students to state and explain Good's theorem on the value of information, say what it shows about the principle of total evidence, and identify the theorem's key assumptions. One student's answer is below. Grade it (A–F, with plus/minus) and give me the specific comments that justify the grade — point by point, not a general impression. I want a committed assessment: if you think the answer deserves a B+, say so and defend it, rather than listing what's right and wrong and leaving the verdict to me.

Student's answer:

"Good's theorem states that the expected value of information is always non-negative. Formally, for any decision problem, if an agent is a Bayesian expected utility maximizer, then the expected utility of the optimal act after receiving free information is at least as great as the expected utility of the optimal act before receiving it. The proof is straightforward: the agent can always ignore the information and choose the act that was optimal under the prior, so the availability of information cannot make the agent worse off in expectation. This result underwrites the principle of total evidence, which says that an agent should use all available evidence in deciding what to do. Good's insight is that the principle of total evidence is not an independent epistemic norm but follows from expected utility maximization. The theorem assumes that the information is free, that the agent can recognize and ignore it if desired, and that the agent's utility function and the set of available acts remain fixed. It does not imply that information is always beneficial ex post, only that it is non-negative in expectation. However, Good's theorem also shows that an agent should never pay for information, since the expected value of information is non-negative but the cost of acquiring it might exceed the benefit."
