I teach a seminar on truthfulness and deception, and every year I present Augustine as the great absolutist about lying: no lie is ever permitted, not to spare another person injury, not to save a life, not even to serve a cause you believe to be just. Every year a student objects that this is inhuman, and I find myself explaining that the interesting question is not whether the conclusion is harsh but whether the position is coherent on its own terms. I would like you to write an essay that answers that question rather than sidestepping it.

Augustine does not define a lie as a false statement. He says that a person lies when he holds one thing in his mind and signifies another, and he locates the fault in the will to deceive: the man who asserts what is false while believing it true does not lie, whereas the man who asserts what is true while believing it false, and utters it in order to mislead, does. Two elements are therefore supposed to be doing the work — a formal element, the signification of something the speaker takes to be false, and a mental element, the intention to deceive. He also offers a rationale that seems to place the wrong in the deception rather than in the falsity of the signification: speech, he argues, was given to us so that we might make our thoughts known to one another, not so that we might deceive one another, and to use it for the purpose of deception is to pervert its purpose.

I want you to decide whether the absolute prohibition can be made coherent, and I want the pressure to come from cases in which the two elements come apart.

Take first signification without deception. Augustine sets jokes aside as not lies at all, on the ground that the joker means no deceit, and he treats a person who asserts what is false while believing it true as merely mistaken rather than lying. Extend the pattern: fiction, acting, role-play, hyperbole that everyone recognises as such, the false statement made to someone who already knows it to be false. If none of these is a lie, then signifying what one believes false is not sufficient for lying, and the weight falls on the intention to deceive.

Take next deception without false signification. Consider the witness who answers every question literally and truthfully but selects his true answers so that the jury will draw a false conclusion; the man who hides a fugitive by telling the pursuer a truth he knows will be misread; the speaker who relies on a calculated silence, or on a true statement whose false implication he means the hearer to supply. None of these signifies anything the speaker believes false, yet each is an attempt to make someone believe what is not so.

The two sets of cases pull the prohibition in opposite directions, and my worry is that Augustine cannot have it both ways. Read the wrong as the misuse of signification and the prohibition turns out to be narrow: it leaves the accomplished dissembler who never asserts anything false untouched, which is an odd place for an absolutist to end up. Read the wrong as the will to deceive, as the rationale seems to invite, and the prohibition turns out to be wide: it condemns the misleading truth and the strategic silence along with the outright falsehood, and so reaches further than the word lying would suggest. Either way the verdict appears to rest on a ground either narrower or broader than the argument offered for it can support.

Reach a conclusion. Either show that one of the two grounds can be defended and that the awkward cases can be handled without strain — say which ground it is, and why the other is a misreading — or argue that the prohibition cannot be sustained in its absolute form, and say what should replace it: a prohibition on deception rather than on false signification, a rule that locates the wrong in the betrayal of trust or in the injury to the deceived, a return to case-by-case weighing, or some distinction I have not drawn. Defend your conclusion against the strongest objection you can raise to it, and do not end by surveying the options.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: identify the consideration that, if it turned out to be sound, would move you to the opposite verdict, and say where in your own reasoning you think the weak point is most likely to lie.
