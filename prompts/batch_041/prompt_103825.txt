I am a high-school student, and for an elective I have spent the term reading Maimonides' Guide of the Perplexed. I have arrived at an argument about his account of the sacrifices that I cannot decide is a genuine difficulty in his position or a trick of my own construction, and I would like help diagnosing it.

In the Guide's treatment of the reasons for the commandments, Maimonides explains the laws of sacrifice by appeal to the historical situation of the Israelites. The prevailing form of worship in which they had been raised, he says, consisted in offering sacrifices in temples to images; and because it is contrary to human nature to abandon all at once everything to which one is accustomed, God did not command the people to discontinue that mode of service but instead transferred it to His own worship, so that the same acts — building a sanctuary, erecting an altar, sacrificing, bowing, burning incense — were now directed to God alone and forbidden toward any other being. He adds that many other precepts follow from the same line of reasoning, and he himself raises the objection that a commandment explained in this way appears to have been commanded not for its own sake but for the sake of something else.

I have tried to press that objection into an argument:

(1) On Maimonides' account, the sacrificial commandments were commanded only because of a contingent historical fact — that the Israelites were already habituated to sacrificial worship.
(2) Had that fact been otherwise, those commandments would not have been commanded.
(3) A commandment whose content depends on a contingent fact in this way has no reason of its own; it is justified only by its effect on a particular audience at a particular time.
(4) A law that has no reason of its own is arbitrary: its content could have been otherwise, and nothing would have been lost by the change.
(5) Maimonides applies this same form of explanation to many other commandments.
(6) Therefore, on Maimonides' own principles, a large portion of the ritual law is arbitrary.
(7) But Maimonides also holds that the commandments are the product of divine wisdom and are in no way arbitrary, and he presents the accommodation of the sacrificial law as itself an instance of that wisdom.

So either (6) is true and (7) is false, or something in (1) through (5) is mistaken. I would far rather keep (7) than accept (6), since I do not believe the commandments are arbitrary; yet I am reluctant to give up (1), because Maimonides seems to assert it so plainly, and I cannot see how to reject (5) without simply picking and choosing among his explanations.

Could you tell me which step I should reject, why it looked plausible enough for me to write it down in the first place, and what I would have to give up in order to reject it? If more than one step is vulnerable, I would like to know which rejection costs the least and which costs the most.
