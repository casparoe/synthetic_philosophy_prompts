I am a city planner who has recently been asked to advise on a long-term development plan that will substantially affect the size and composition of our city's future population. In preparing my recommendation, I have been studying population ethics, and I have become stuck on the repugnant conclusion. Please explain, in intuitive terms, why the repugnant conclusion is thought to follow from a few seemingly innocuous principles about the value of adding people to the world. In particular, I would like an explanation of the role that the transitivity of the betterness relation plays in the argument, and why some philosophers think the only way to avoid the conclusion is to deny that betterness is transitive in cases involving different populations. Also, please consider the following principle, which I will call the No Mere Replacement principle: it is never better to replace an existing person with a different person whose life is only slightly less good, when the alternative is to keep the existing person and slightly improve their life. Does this principle help avoid the repugnant conclusion, or does it generate its own paradox? Please answer in American English.
