I have no philosophical training, so I may be reinventing something, but here is a puzzle I keep turning over on my own. A wealthy stranger offers you a very large sum of money — enough to matter — on a single condition: that by midnight tonight you sincerely intend to swallow a spoonful of a foul but harmless substance at noon tomorrow. If you form that intention, the money is wired to you at midnight, irrevocably, and it stays yours whether or not you actually swallow the stuff. Come noon tomorrow, with the money already sitting in your account, you will have no reason at all to swallow it; it will be pointless and disgusting, and you will know it. The stranger does not care about your willpower in the ordinary sense — he cares only about the intention, which he has some way of verifying.

I want your credence that a normal, rational adult who fully understands this offer can actually pull it off — that is, can form the required intention by midnight and collect the money, without self-deception, without becoming irrational, and without any trick that quietly changes the terms of the offer. Please do not give me a bare number. Break it into the sub-questions the number depends on, give each a rough probability, explain how they combine, and tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to. In particular, I would like you to price at least these:

- the chance that intentions are the kind of thing a person can adopt at will, the way one decides to raise a hand, as opposed to states that simply arise once one believes one will act;

- the chance that intending to do something actually requires believing that one will do it — because if that is true the puzzle looks impossible on its face, and I want to know how much of your final number rests on rejecting that requirement;

- the chance that the offer is even coherent — whether a payment can genuinely be conditioned on a private mental state without the state collapsing under its own self-awareness (if I already know the money is guaranteed by the intention alone, what is left for me to intend?);

- the chance that a person could manage it through some legitimate form of self-manipulation — making a solemn vow, arranging in advance to be the sort of person who follows through, distracting themselves from tomorrow's pointlessness — and whether that should count as winning or as cheating;

- the chance that the puzzle exposes a real gap between being rational at each moment and being rational over the whole episode — whether someone can be fully rational tonight and fully rational tomorrow and still be unable to win, and if so whether that is a paradox or merely a fact about plans.

Then give me a second number, for a variant I invented. Suppose the stranger cannot read intentions and instead pays you if a well-calibrated machine predicts tonight that you will swallow the stuff at noon — and pays on the prediction alone, so the money is yours before you decide anything at noon. Is your credence that a rational person collects the money in this version higher, lower, or the same as in the first, and what does the difference, if any, reveal about whether the original puzzle is really about the privacy of intention or about something deeper in how intentions and reasons for acting line up across time?

Finally, tell me which single consideration, if you became convinced of it, would move your main number the most, and in which direction.
