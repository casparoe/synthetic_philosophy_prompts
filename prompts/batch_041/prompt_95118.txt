ok so I teach a second-year decision theory seminar (18 students, one 75-min session) and I just lost a two-hour argument with my friend Priya about Ellsberg urns and I'm still not over it. I want my students to actually feel this one in the gut instead of nodding along at a slide. So: design me a runnable in-class activity.

The thing I care about most is the moment where they catch themselves. The standard version (urn with 30 red, 60 black-or-yellow in unknown proportion, bet red vs bet black, then bet red-or-yellow vs bet black-or-yellow) is fine as a warm-up but everyone's seen it and half of them will just pick the consistent-looking options on purpose once they smell a trick. So I want the structure to be doing more of the work than the reveal.

Here's roughly what I have in mind, but make it your own and fix it where its broken. Set up enough urns/rounds that nobody can keep track of which one is the safe one. Have them place private bets with tokens (no real money, but make the tokens buy entries into one small real prize at the end, a decent chocolate bar or whatever, so there's a tiny real stake and they cant totally shrug it off). Then tally on the board and show them the pattern. Then the part I actually want: run a round where the composition of the unknown part of the urn was set by an actual adversary, someone in the room or a "house" I tell them wants them to lose, and a matched round where the same kind of urn was filled by a coin toss or some random process. My hunch is the ambiguity aversion mostly survives the adversary round and mostly evaporates in the random round, and if that happens it lets me pull apart two things that usually get smushed together: dislike of not knowing, and suspicion that the unknown was chosen against you. I also want a round where one group gets to peek at the composition (or gets a "consultant" who's seen it) and another doesnt, to see whether it's the knowing-you-dont-know that's doing the work.

So I need: rules, a materials list, the payoff scheme and why it doesnt need real money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 min, and a debrief that maps each moment of what just happened onto the actual ideas, the sure-thing principle, why those two bets can't both be rational under any single probability for black, and the fight about whether ambiguity aversion is a mistake or a rational response to something real. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce and that I'll just have to tell them.

Two things I'm worried about, handle both in the design. First, as soon as a sharp student sees the pattern they'll either defect to the "rational" choices to look good, or they'll say the unknown composition is just a random variable, slap a uniform prior on it and declare it all ordinary risk. I need the activity to survive that move, not get derailed by it. Second, I don't want the debrief to smuggle in a verdict. Some of my students will walk out thinking the ambiguity-averse chooser is an idiot and some will think they're the only sane one, and honestly I'd rather the thing leave that open while making sure they all know exactly what the disagreement is about.

no lecture up front. they should be placing bets before I've said the word Ellsberg or ambiguity.
