I've been teaching the dogmatism paradox in my grad seminar on epistemic defeat for years now, and somewhere along the way I stopped treating it as a party trick and started actually believing the conclusion. So let me state the position flatly, because I want it on the table rather than hedged: if you know that P, then finding out that someone has produced evidence against P does not give you a reason to lower your confidence in P. You know P; anything that tells against P must therefore be misleading evidence; and the rational thing to do with misleading evidence is set it aside. Stronger still — a knower who re-opens the question on the strength of counterevidence is being irrational, or at least less rational than the knower who doesn't bother. I'd like you to try to break this. What would count against it? I dont just mean the moves I can already anticipate from my students — I want the defeaters sorted by how much they should genuinely move me, including ones from places I might not have looked: the higher-order evidence and disagreement literature, the bootstrapping and easy-knowledge stuff, formal work on how evidence about your own reliability bears on your first-order beliefs, and anything about whether the position can even be stated without quietly equivocating on "knows." A colleague told me I only like this view because it's the philosopher's version of refusing to back down in an argument, which stung because it's probably a little true, so I'm braced for unflattering diagnoses.

Tell me too which of these a committed defender would concede as a real cost, not just which ones critics enjoy waving around — I want to know what I'd actually be giving up, not what I'd be accused of. And be honest with me about whether the view has been quietly insulated into unfalsifiability: there's a version of it where "knowing P" just means "being in a state that survives counterevidence," which makes the whole thing trivially true and empty, and I want to know whether the interesting version can be stated in a way that risks anything at all. If it can't, say so, and say whether that's fatal or just the price of a thesis about the structure of justification. What does the view predict that its rivals don't, and are any of those predictions things I should be embarrassed by — say, about what a lone knower should do when a room full of equally good epistemolgists tells her she's wrong, or about whether her conviction has to be accompanied by a certain smugness? Is there a version where the counterevidence leaves the belief untouched but does touch my entitlement to it, or is that just wanting to have my cake and eat it? Be committed: no menu of positions, tell me which version you'd defend and where you think it actually breaks.
