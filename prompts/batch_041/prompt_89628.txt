I chair the deployment-review board at a mid-sized AI company -- six of us, we meet twice a month, the calls are about whether to ship a model or a feature, and a bad one could hit millions of users. Our charter says we're not supposed to decide by what we currently think is right, but by what a deliberation would endorse: participants fully informed, fully rational, unbiased, and given unlimited time to sort out their disagreements. Fine in theory. In practice we vote and then dress it up. Write me a procedure we can actually run on each agenda item -- the steps in order, what counts as enough of an answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand the call to a person because the question is one of values, not facts. I want it to keep two things apart that we keep mashing together: (1) making the deliberation better (more information, more time, less bias, more people at the table) versus making the participants' values themselves better or more coherent, and (2) the fact that if you idealize hard enough the verdict may land somewhere we don't recognize, so I need a rule for what to do when the "ideal" answer conflicts with what we'd actually endorse. Have it say who counts as affected, since we can never agree on that either. Run it on two cases: shipping a model that's plainly net-useful but that some customers will predictably use in ways we think are bad, and holding back something safe that our biggest customers say they need for a use we find distasteful. At least one of those should come out in a way I won't like. And be straight with me at the end about what this thing can't do, and which steps are just judgment in a lab coat.
