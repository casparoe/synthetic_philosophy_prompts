I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of mind and I've somehow gotten pulled into a side thing with the civil engineering folks — I sit in on a road-safety working group, and my partner's brother does accident reconstruction for a living, so I hear about this constantly. Here's what's been nagging me. In road design and in crash litigation everyone works with a perception-reaction time: the sight-distance formula assumes the driver needed roughly a second and a half to perceive the hazard and another second to get on the brake, and in reconstruction they take the moment the pedestrian stepped out from behind the parked car, add the PRT, and that's when a competent driver should have begun braking. Later than that and the driver was inattentive; shorter sightline than the required distance and the road is deficient. It gets handled like a constant, same status as the deceleration rate. But the calculation seems to need a fact about when the driver *perceived* the hazard — not when the foot moved, but when the perception itself happened, timestamped somewhere inside that window. And I genuinely don't know what that fact could be.

Because of the color phi thing. You flash a blue dot in one spot, then a red dot somewhere else about a tenth of a second later, and people report a single dot travelling across the gap that changes color — and they report the color change as occurring before the second dot was ever on the screen. So what someone reports about the earlier stretch of the experience depends on a stimulus that hadn't arrived yet when that stretch was supposedly happening. I've seen the options laid out as Orwellian vs Stalinesque: either the experience occurs and the memory of it gets rewritten afterward, or the whole thing is reconciled before anything reaches consciousness, so there was never a moment at which the first dot was experienced as blue. And then the extra twist, which I find vertiginous, is that the choice between those two might be empty, since both assume there's a finish line in the brain that content has to cross, and maybe there isn't one.

So: what does the engineering practice actually presuppose, and how much of it survives if you take the phi stuff seriously? Does it need the realist picture — determinate content of experience at each moment, with a real timestamp — or could it get by with something much weaker, like perception time as an instrumental fudge factor that just makes the stopping distances come out right, or a deflationary reading on which the only real facts are about when the foot moved and the perception talk is a placeholder for whatever went on in between? And is there a tension internal to the practice: perception is treated as a point event with a definite onset while reaction is treated as a distribution with a mean and a spread, even though they're presumably the same kind of process? I'd like to know which commitments are load-bearing and which are inherited folk psychology, and what would actually have to change if you swapped the presupposition out — would the numbers move, or only the story told about them?
