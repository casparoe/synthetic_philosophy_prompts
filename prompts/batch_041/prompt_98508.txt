I retired a few years ago after 35 years as a physical chemist — lots of statistical mechanics and reaction kinetics — and I've spent my retirement doing what I always wanted to: reading philosophy. I've gotten in deep enough that I've actually written a paper, and now I've been invited to present it to the philosophy of science reading group at the university where I'm basically an unpaid hanger-on. It's in three weeks and I'm nervous.

The paper defends the propensity interpretation of probability — the idea that probabilities are real, objective, dispositional properties of physical setups, not degrees of belief and not just limiting frequencies. But I've got my own spin on it and you need to know it, because the whole prep sheet has to target my actual position and not the textbook version:

My view, in a nutshell: a propensity is a relational disposition that belongs to what I call a chance setup, and a chance setup is specified by (a) the token physical arrangement, (b) the set of degrees of freedom you're treating as free and relevant, and (c) the test procedure. So my propensities are single-case — I'm with the people who want an objective chance for the individual decaying atom — but they are not intrinsic to the object; they belong to the whole arrangement, apparatus included. Two claims I already know will get me in trouble: (1) I think the reference-class problem is not a bug but a feature — there's no such thing as the propensity of an object, only the propensity relative to a level of description, and I want to say that's exactly right, whereas the frequentists and the Bayesians both flinch from it; (2) I deny that conditional propensities are propensities at all — I treat conditional probability as a derived, higher-order notion, so I never have to answer what the propensity of A given B is a property of. I also claim the whole thing earns its keep by doing real work in statistical mechanics (the disposition of a macrostate, not just a frequency in an ensemble).

The audience, as precisely as I can describe it: two committed subjective Bayesians — one a hard probability-is-only-coherent-credence type, the other more moderate, fine with objective chance as a primitive but demanding a theory of it; a statistician who is a died-in-the-wool frequentist and thinks propensity talk is a metaphysical ghost that does no predictive work; a historian of science who mostly asks what scientists in the 19th and early 20th centuries actually meant by these words and is allergic to retrofitting our vocabulary onto them; and six or seven grad students who will ask whatever the Bayesians prime them to ask.

What I want from you: a Q&A prep sheet for the hour after my talk. Give me the eight or nine objections I'll actually get from this specific room — not the generic ones out of a probability textbook — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does to my position, each one phrased the way that particular person would put it (the frequentist's version of an objection and the Bayesian's version are two seperate animals). For each: the two-minute reply, and an honest note on where that reply is soft or hand-wavy. Tell me which one or two things I should just concede up front to buy credibility with this crowd. And put at the end the one question I'm dreading — the one that goes straight to the softest joint in my view and that I'm hoping nobody in the room notices.

Don't hand me reading lists or citations, I can't reliably look anything up and neither can whoever answers this — just the arguments themselves. And don't pull your punches: if my reference-classes-are-a-feature move is actually incoherent, say so in the sheet and tell me how the frequentist is going to gut it.
