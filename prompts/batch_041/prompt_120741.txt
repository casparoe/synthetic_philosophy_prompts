I am writing the final examination for a second-semester course on the epistemic foundations of game theory, aimed at advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students who have already seen extensive-form games, subgame perfection, Kripke models, and the muddy-children puzzle. The exam is seventy-five minutes long, and I want one long-answer question worth twenty-five marks on the relationship between common knowledge of rationality and backward induction. I do not want a question that can be answered by reciting the statement of Aumann's theorem or by paraphrasing the standard gloss that common knowledge of rationality makes backward induction valid; the students have that gloss memorized, and it hides the interesting structure. What I want them to confront is the distinction between the theorem as a consistency result about an epistemic model and the theorem as a claim about practical deliberation, and the way the result depends on the genericity of the payoffs.

Here is the material I want the question built around. Use the following three-node centipede game. Player 1 moves first and chooses Take or Pass; Take yields (1, 0) and ends the game. If Player 1 passes, Player 2 chooses Take or Pass; Take yields (0, 2), and Pass leads to a final node where Player 1 again chooses Take or Pass, with Take yielding (3, 1) and Pass yielding (2, 4). Then ask the students to do three things. First, run the backward induction and state precisely what Aumann's theorem asserts about this game. Second, perturb the final payoff so that the last mover is indifferent at the final node — for instance, make the final Pass payoff (3, 4), so that Player 1's payoffs from Take and Pass at the final node are both 3 — and have the students construct two distinct epistemic models for the perturbed game, specifying each player's information cells or knowledge partition over the set of strategy profiles together with a common prior, in which each player's strategy is sequentially rational given his information and rationality is common knowledge, but the backward-induction path is played in one model and not in the other. Third, ask them to say what the contrast between the two models shows about the role of the no-relevant-ties or genericity assumption in the theorem, and why the theorem should not be read as a description of the deliberative process by which a player arrives at the backward-induction move.

Please write the examination question itself, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words, and a marking scheme worth twenty-five marks. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must contain, assign marks to each, and for each must-hit point give a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs students marks — for example, treating common knowledge of rationality as equivalent to common knowledge of the strategy profile, or treating the theorem as refuted by the empirical fact that subjects cooperate in centipede experiments. Keep the question self-contained: the students will not have web access and should not need to recall any citation, and do not put any quoted text in the question or the model answer.
