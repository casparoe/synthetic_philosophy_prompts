I have to give the after-dinner address at the annual general meeting of a workers' cooperative — a printing house, one member one vote, founded in 1898 — and the room votes the next morning on selling a controlling stake to a regional commercial chain. The offer raises every member's pay, keeps every job, clears our debts, and turns members into employees under an ordinary shareholders' agreement. Half the audience are lifelong cooperators who will hear any caution as a betrayal; the other half are guests from the chamber of commerce and a free-market think tank, invited precisely because they think the model is sentimental. Our pay currently sits below what the chain is offering, so nothing in the speech may use the nobility of association to argue that members should settle for less money.

Write the speech, built on Mill's late views on socialism rather than on generic solidarity: his sense that the existing distribution of property stands in need of justification rather than reverence, his expectation that the relation between employer and employed might gradually give way to association, the weight he gave to self-governing enterprise as a school of character, and, just as much, his fear of a society that presses its members into one mould and his doubts about how far any large centralised administration can be trusted with the conditions of individual independence. The speech must hold both sides of that tension honestly — it cannot conscript Mill as a patron saint of cooperation or as a cautious liberal who never really meant it — and must leave the room free to vote either way without feeling the speech lied to them. Roughly seven minutes; plain language; do not quote Mill or put any words of his in quotation marks.
