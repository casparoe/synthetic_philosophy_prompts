I am a novelist at work on a multigenerational novel, and to decide how to shape its structure I have been reading the cyclical historians—Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, Toynbee—alongside the various linear or progress narratives, from providential history to Enlightenment optimism to later emancipatory accounts. My reading group has been arguing about this for months, and the argument has stalled in a way I find genuinely puzzling. The cyclical side says that societies pass through recurring phases—rise, flourishing, corruption, collapse—and that because human nature and the basic dynamics of power, scarcity, and legitimacy are constant, the same kinds of events recur; the linear side says that history has a direction, that later stages are genuinely novel, and that the recurrence the cyclical side sees is either superficial or an artifact of looking at history from too far away. Both sides agree on a great many historical facts, and neither seems to be missing a decisive piece of evidence, yet neither moves. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on which side is right.

My working hypothesis—offered as a hypothesis—is that this is not one debate but several, and that it stalls because the words cyclical and linear are doing too much work. Cyclical seems to mean at least three different things: recurrence of event-types (wars, famines, dynastic collapses), recurrence of the same events (a literal eternal return), and a spiral or dialectical pattern that is neither simple repetition nor simple progress. Linear seems to cover at least irreversible time, cumulative improvement, and purposeful direction. If that is right, then some of the disagreement is verbal, some is empirical, and some is about what a philosophy of history is even supposed to explain. But I may be wrong, and I would like you to tell me where the hypothesis fails.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Which parts are substantive, which are verbal, and which are hidden empirical or formal questions? Are the sides answering different questions under one name—one about the descriptive shape of historical processes, one about the meaning or value of history, one about the possibility of prediction or moral learning? Do they differ about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither can argue for? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a taxonomy of senses, a testable claim about recurrence, a distinction between recurrence and repetition, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected? Please support the diagnosis by engaging with what each side actually says, and be honest about whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely that it is hard.
