I am a journalist writing a long-form piece on the philosophy of logic, and for the past few months I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group devoted to the tortoise-and-Achilles puzzle about modus ponens. The room has split into camps that seem to talk past one another. One camp treats the puzzle as a genuine regress: no matter how many further premises you add—if p then q, p, therefore q, and then a premise saying that if the first two are true the conclusion must be true—the tortoise can always ask for the rule that licenses the step, so deduction can never be justified to someone who does not already infer. A second camp says this is a confusion: the tortoise illicitly treats a rule of inference as if it were another premise, and once you distinguish believing a conditional from being disposed to infer according to a rule, the regress evaporates. A third camp insists the real issue is the normativity of logic: what is at stake is not whether we can add a premise but whether logical rules can be binding on thought at all, and whether that bindingness is a matter of truth, entitlement, or something practical. A fourth camp suspects the whole debate is a pseudo-problem generated by a bad picture of justification, in which every reason must be a further proposition. One philosopher I interviewed argued that the tortoise shows inference cannot be reduced to a relation among propositions, because any such reduction leaves the act of inferring unaccounted for, and I cannot tell whether that is a deep diagnosis or a restatement of the problem.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. You are an expert in philosophical logic, epistemology, and the theory of reasoning. My own hunch is that the impasse is not really about deduction but about whether justification must be propositional, and that the camps are answering different questions under one name. Before you answer, brainstorm the candidate fault lines: is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of justification, a verbal dispute about the word justification, two camps answering different questions under one heading—one about the metaphysics of inference, one about the psychology of rule-following—or a hidden formal point about rule-circularity versus premise-circularity? Consider the distinction between a warrant that consists in a further premise and a warrant that consists in an entitlement or a rule of inference, and the distinction between theoretical justification and practical entitlement. Then give me your best diagnosis, with reasons drawn from what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. I am especially interested in whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed or just that it is hard, and whether any of the camps has quietly assumed a controversial theory of what it is to follow a rule. Think carefully and be explicit about which side, if any, your diagnosis vindicates.
