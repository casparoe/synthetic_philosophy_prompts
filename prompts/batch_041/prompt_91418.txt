I've somehow ended up agreeing to run a session at a small lettering and type meetup in my town — about 14 people, mixed bag, a few design students, a couple of hobbyists who do calligraphy, one retired signwriter, and me, who has no formal background in any of this and has just got stuck on a puzzle I can't let go of. The session is 90 minutes, I've got a big pile of grid paper and black markers and basically nothing else, no projector, no slides. What I want out of it is this: I want them to feel the difference between two questions I keep hopelessly conflating when I'm thinking about it on my own — whether a shape is still the letter a at all, and whether it's in the same style as the shapes sat next to it. I've half-remembered for years some story about a research project where the aim was to get a computer to design a whole alphabet in one consistent style and then judge whether a new letter fitted in, and the interesting part apparently wasn't the drawing, it was that the machine had to keep two kinds of judgement seperate. I reckon people will get the point faster with pens than by hearing about it. The thing I keep bumping into myself is that the two judgements pull against each other: push a letter far enough into a style and it stops being obviously that letter, and keep it safely that letter and the style thins out to nothing. And then there's the fact that the a with a bowl and a stem and the a that's just a circle with a tail are obviously the same letter and share almost no features, which I want them to run straight into rather than nod at.

So: design me the actual session. Rules, who draws what and in what order, how we swap things round, and above all how the judging works — I want the judging to be the engine of the whole thing, not a nice bit at the end. Give me the small stakes so it doesn't collapse into just a drawing exercise, plus a timeline that actually fits 90 minutes with 14 people, and materials beyond grid paper and markers only if I really need them. I don't want to stand up and explain anything at the start; the rules should make them walk into the tension before either of us names it. I do want a debrief, and I want it specific: for each moment in the activity, which part of the puzzle it's standing in for, and where the mapping is loose, say so instead of pretending it lines up neatly.

Also tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time — I'd rather know now — and which parts of the design I should be ready to change on the fly mid-session. And give me a straight note on what the activity can't do: I assume there's some part of this that only a talk or a proper reading could give them and the game can only gesture at, so tell me which part that is. Since I'm going to be repeating this afterwards to people as if I know what I'm on about, I'd also like you to flag where your own framing is shakiest — where the activity quietly assumes something contestable (that letters have an essence at all, or that a style belongs to a set of letters rather than to each one on its own, or whatever it is), and what you'd expect to see in a first run that would make you change your mind about the debrief. Write it all in British English please, thats what I'll be reading out from.
