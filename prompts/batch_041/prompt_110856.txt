I recently left a philosophy PhD for a forecasting and decision-support team, and I have inherited an argument that resurfaces in every methodology meeting. One senior colleague treats the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization — the money-pump and Dutch-book constructions, the representation theorems — as settling that an agent who fails to maximize expected utility is irrational, and he invokes this to dismiss our clients' precautionary and ambiguity-averse recommendations as incoherent. A second colleague, trained in axiomatic choice theory, insists the arguments establish nothing of the sort: at most they show that certain preference structures admit a representation, or that agents with certain structural features can be exploited, and neither of those amounts to a substantive requirement to maximize expected utility. Since I am the one drafting the appendix on our decision standards, I need a document both of them can sign.

Please draft the joint statement of the adversarial collaboration they should have had. I want the claims both can accept: what the money-pump and Dutch-book arguments actually establish; what work each axiom (transitivity, completeness, continuity, independence) is doing, and whether any of them is a genuine requirement of rationality rather than a convenience of the formalism; what coherence is being demanded of — preferences at a single time, choices across time, or merely the existence of a representation; whether expected utility maximization is being advanced as a criterion of rightness or as a decision procedure; and which objections each side regards as beside the point (transaction costs, the one-shot character of the pump, the fact that nobody is in practice pumped). Then the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept. Then the cruxes. I suspect three: whether the argument targets the structure or the content of preferences; whether an agent who foresees a pump can escape it without violating sequential choice, and what that implies for an agent who binds herself in advance to a plan she would otherwise revise; and whether the assumptions about betting dispositions and zero transaction costs are load-bearing. For each crux, give a case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal, identify it and say why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, with no citations — this is an internal appendix, not a survey.
