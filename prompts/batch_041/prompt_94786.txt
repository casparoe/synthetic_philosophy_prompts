I just lost about two hours of my evening to an argument with my roommate — she's finishing a PhD in econ, I'm doing a policy masters and working part time at a state environmental agency — about whether being risk averse can be rational in its own right, or whether it's always either an illusion or a mistake. I said of course it's rational, that's why people buy insurance on things the math says are bad bets. She said no, that's just diminishing marginal utility of money, and once you spell the outcomes out in terms of the thing you ultimately care about, you have to be risk neutral, so if you'd rather have a sure loss of 50 than a coin flip between 0 and 120 that's either you misperceiving the odds or you being irrational, end of story. Then she pulled out this thing she's been reading about where you line up the outcomes of a gamble from worst to best and each outcome's weight isn't its probability but some function of the chance of that outcome happening *or anything worse than it* happening — so the same one percent chance of a catastrophe gets treated completely differently depending on what else is on the table, and your attitude to risk is baked into the rule itself instead of into the utility function. Which sounds insane to me, but then I went to work the next morning and started looking around at how the stuff on my desk is actually set up, and now I can't tell who's right.

Some of what we do is pure expected value arithmetic. We put a dollar figure on a statistical life, a few million give or take, multiply by the expected number of deaths a rule would prevent, subtract the compliance cost, and if the number comes out positive we do the rule. Risk there is just probability times harm — a one in ten thousand chance of killing someone is worth exactly a thousandth of killing them outright, no more. Fine. But two floors down, contaminated sites run on a totally different logic: the cleanup target is a one in a million added cancer risk, which everybody in the building treats as basically zero, i.e. as a line below which the risk stops counting at all rather than as a small number that gets multiplied by anything. And there are statutes — the food additive one from the late fifties is the famous example, if I'm remembering it right — where a substance that causes cancer in lab animals can't be approved at any dose, no matter how tiny, because that risk simply isn't tradeable against the benefit. The safety people write rules saying you have to keep reducing a hazard until the next increment of protection costs *wildly* more than the benefit it buys, not merely equal to it, and other rules that set an absolute ceiling on individual risk that no economic benefit can justify crossing even if the benefit is enormous. And over in the courts, someone exposed to a chemical can sometimes recover damages for the increased risk of a disease they haven't got and may never get.

So what view of practical rationality does this whole bundle presuppose? Is there a single theory under which the linear cost-benefit stuff and the no-safe-dose stuff are both correct rather than one of them just being a political bone thrown to frightened people — and does the rank-dependent thing my roommate was going on about actually do that job, i.e. does the practice presuppose a non-linear weighting of chances, and if so what shape does that weighting have to have to generate ceilings, floors, and gross disproportion tests all at once? Which rival views does the bundle rule out, and where is it merely compatible with several, so that the practice turns out to be underdetermined — could a straight expected utility view with the right utility function license all of it, including the absolute ceilings, or do the ceilings need something else entirely, like a deontic constraint or a threshold below which probabilities just don't register? I want the strict presuppositions separated from the stuff that's just there for litigation risk and public panic. And if the answer is that we're implicitly running something like her theory, tell me what actually changes if we swap in straight expected value: which of these rules has to go, and what does the replacement rulebook look like?
