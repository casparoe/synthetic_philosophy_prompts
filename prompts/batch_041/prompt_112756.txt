I'm a third-year PhD student in the philosophy of the social sciences, and I've been sitting in on a seminar on explanation where the same argument keeps reappearing in new costumes. My sparring partner, an economic sociologist, holds the strict individualist line: any claim that a state, a regime, a class, or an economy did something is, until it is cashed out in terms of the beliefs, opportunities, and choices of individual people, either an abbreviation for a long conjunction of individual facts or a piece of mysticism — there are no macro-level causes, only macro-level summaries. My own view is weaker and, I find, harder to state cleanly: that some macro-level facts enter an individual's practical reasoning as premises rather than as aggregates. A border guard can be responding to the fact that the regime's authority has evaporated, and that fact seems not to be a summary of what other individuals are doing, precisely because he may be the first to act on it. We have both noticed that the disagreement becomes tractable when it is pinned to a particular episode rather than argued in the abstract, and we keep returning to one we know well enough to describe without hedging.

The night the Berlin Wall opened, 9 November 1989. The East German spokesman Günter Schabowski held a press conference that evening and, reading from a note he had not been briefed on, gave the impression that the new travel regulations took effect at once; the broadcast went out at 8:00 p.m. Within the hour thousands of East Berliners were at the Bornholmer Straße crossing demanding to be let through. The guards there telephoned their superiors repeatedly and found no one willing to take responsibility for an order, whether to shoot or to hold; at 9:20 p.m. they let a handful of people through with their passports invalidated, and shortly before 11:30 p.m. the officer in charge, Lieutenant-Colonel Harald Jäger, raised the barrier without orders, and some 20,000 people crossed. Please take the historical facts as I have given them and do not add detail you are unsure of.

I want the episode used as a test case running in both directions, not as an illustration of a conclusion either of us already holds. So: give me the description of what happened that both sides could accept, saying exactly which entities are the individual actors and which are the macro-level facts, and flag any point where the description already begs the question; then the individualist reading, naming what was causally decisive and applying the obvious counterfactuals (would the crossing have happened that night without the misstatement, without Jäger's discretion, or would a hundred smaller decisions have produced the same outcome by morning?); then the holist reading, saying whether the macro facts here function as summaries of individual behaviour or as inputs into it, and whether something like downward causation or constitutive constraint earns its keep or is decoration; then an honest account of what each side cannot explain — the individualist's difficulty with why these particular officers had the discretion they had, why no superior would take responsibility, and why the order's authority had evaporated, and the holist's difficulty with the timing, with why this night rather than an earlier one, and with the causal weight of an off-the-cuff remark; and finally what the episode shows about the debate itself — whether it favours a side, shows the dispute to be partly verbal or badly posed, or points to a refinement (for instance, that methodological individualism is a thesis about the ontology of causes while its critics are making a point about the structure of explanations, and that these can come apart). Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
