Write a dialogue between two people who hold opposing views about what makes an original work of art valuable, placed in a situation that forces the disagreement to become concrete.

The situation: a conservator has built a device that scans a painting and produces a duplicate that cannot be distinguished from the original by any inspection or measurement available to us — not under magnification, not under raking light, not by chemical or structural analysis — because it reads the craquelure, the old repairs, and the layer of grime off the original and reproduces those too. The duplicate is not a print or a photograph: it is an object of the same kind as the original, differing from it only in its causal history, in that it was made now, by the device, out of the original, rather than by the artist centuries ago.

The first speaker holds that the value of a work is carried by its perceptible surface: two objects that are indistinguishable to any suitably informed viewer present the same work, and the large premium paid for an "original" is explained by scarcity, status, and the incidental pleasure of being near the real thing rather than by any aesthetic difference. The second speaker holds that a work is constituted by its history: the original is an achievement, the duplicate is a record of that achievement, and a record of a performance is not the performance.

Let the dialogue work through at least these pressure points. First, if the original is destroyed and only the duplicate survives, has the work been destroyed or has it survived? Second, what changes if the duplicate is itself scanned and duplicated again — a copy of a copy — so that there is a chain of objects each differing from the last only in its causal distance from the artist? Third, what changes if the surviving original has been restored so heavily that it is, by any measure, a less faithful presentation of what the artist made than the duplicate is — which object, then, is the work? Fourth, can the second speaker's position avoid making the work's value rest on facts that no viewer could ever detect, and can the first speaker's position avoid the consequence that the work is not tied to any particular object at all?

Both speakers should be equally competent; neither should be a strawman, and neither should be handed the easier position. Each reply should answer the specific point just made rather than talking past it, and each should press where the other is weakest. Do not resolve the disagreement, and do not let one speaker simply concede.

After the dialogue, add a brief note in your own voice stating how confident you are in the central claims you placed in each speaker's mouth, and identify the single claim in the piece about which you are least confident.
