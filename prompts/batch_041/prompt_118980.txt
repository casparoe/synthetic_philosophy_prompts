I teach a graduate seminar on competence without comprehension, organized around two inversions that reorganize how we think about design and mind. The first is Darwin's: the production of something that looks designed — an organ, an instinct, a mechanism of uncanny fit — does not require a designer who understood what he was making, because the appearance of foresight can be the residue of a process with no foresight in it. The second is Turing's: the production of something that looks thoughtful does not require a thinker who understands what it is doing, because a system can keep getting the answers right without any insight into why they are right. Both inversions are well rehearsed, and I do not need another story that simply illustrates either one.

What I find genuinely under-explored is a third, reflexive inversion that the first two seem to license without ever quite stating: our own recognition of comprehension — the confidence with which we say that this one understands and that one merely behaves — may itself be a competence we exercise without comprehending. We are, on this view, extremely good at sorting the comprehenders from the non-comprehenders while having no articulated account of how we do it.

Write a short story, roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words, that stages this third inversion. Some constraints, which I care about more than the premise you choose:

The concept must survive as drama, not as exposition. No character may deliver a seminar-style explanation of the inversions, and the story should remain intelligible to a reader who has never encountered either one.

Do not set the story in the obvious places — no research lab, no Turing test, no robot acquiring sentience, no artificial intelligence pleading for recognition. The inversion is a structure, not a subject matter. Put it somewhere readers will not be braced for it: a trade, a family, an institution, a craft, a form of maintenance, an act of reading.

Give the story one concrete thing — a procedure, an artifact, a ledger, a repair protocol, a dialect, a piece of music — whose evident purpose the protagonist must infer, and let the inference be the plot.

The ending should be unresolved in a particular way: the reader must not be able to determine whether the protagonist's final judgment about who or what understands is a genuine insight or a competence running blind. Do not resolve this by having anyone announce that it is ambiguous.

Please avoid the stock vocabulary of the theme in the prose itself; if the story needs the words, it has probably become an essay.
