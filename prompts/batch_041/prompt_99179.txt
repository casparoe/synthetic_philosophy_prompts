Ok so I keep getting into arguments about wether a nation can be guilty, and I've got a pile of gut calls that won't line up, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here's what I think. My great-grandparents were ordinary Germans, not in the party, but they knew what was happening and did nothing, and I feel like that's some kind of guilt that lands on me, even though I'd have a hard time defending it. A friend of mine is German too, born in 1995, but her family is Turkish and came over in the 70s — she says she feels nothing about any of it and I think she's basically right, it'd be weird for her to carry it. An American whose family got here in 1900 and has no connection to slavery, I don't think is guilty of anything. But a guy I know whose family money came off a plantation — I feel like he owes something, even though he personally never owned anybody. A university that built its endowment on slave labor — I think the university itself is guilty and owes, even though nobody running it now had anything to do with it. A country that did a massacre in the 70s, where most adults alive today were around and voting — I think the country is guilty, but I don't think every citizen is, and yet I also think they have to pay.

My friend keeps saying this is all just Jaspers and his different kinds of guilt, which I guess is a hint, but it doesn't tell me which kind I'm actually tracking in each case.

I keep wanting one rule: it's guilty if it's the same agent over time, and a nation or an institution is the same agent across generations, but a person isn't — so the university and the country are guilty and individuals aren't. But then the plantation heir owes, and the ordinary German's descendant feels guilty, and neither of those is an institution, so that rule can't be the whole story. Is the difference that the heir benefits and the German descendant identifies? Those feel like totally different things to me, and I can't tell if one of them is doing all the work or if I need two separate principles. And I definately can't settle the one I keep going back and forth on: a German whose family were Jews who fled, and who's now a citizen and gets asked to share in the national guilt — I want to say no, her family was the victim, but she's also a member of the same continuing nation, so by my own rule...

So is there a principle, or a couple of them, that gets all of these, and which of my calls does it force me to give up? And what does it say about a case I haven't thought about — say a company that's still around and still profitable, whose predecessor did something awful fifty years ago, with none of the same employees?
