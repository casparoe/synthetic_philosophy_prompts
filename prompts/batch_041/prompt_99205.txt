I am a novelist at work on a book about a theater critic whose career is destroyed by a satirical newspaper — a plot I chose because I have long been drawn to Kierkegaard's writings on the press and the public, and to the line of his that gets repeated at literary festivals whenever anyone suggests that the reception of a book might be evidence about the book: that the crowd is untruth. I heard it again last month, on a panel about criticism, when a fellow novelist used it to brush aside my question of whether a novel's being loved by a great many readers could count as any kind of evidence for its merit; the implication was that once a great many people are involved, the question of truth does not even arise. I am not a philosopher and I do not want a lecture; I want to see the machinery. Please lay out, as a menu I can keep beside my manuscript, the distinct precise claims that this slogan could be making — separating the ones that are near-truisms (large groups are often wrong, anonymity makes people cowardly, aggregate ratings measure nothing in particular) from the ones that are substantive and defensible (that the public is not a subject that can hold a belief at all; that the press manufactures an abstract entity with no real situation and no real simultaneity; that truth in the relevant sense requires an individual's inward appropriation and so cannot be had by any collectivity, even a unanimous one; that participating in a crowd dissolves the responsibility that alone makes a judgment mine) from the ones that are, on inspection, false or incoherent (that a majority's being right is impossible; that no generalization about groups can be true). For each reading, say what it commits you to, what it would rule out, and whether it would actually license the move my colleague made — using the slogan to end inquiry into whether mass reception bears on merit. I would also like a note on which readings are likely to be in play when the slogan is spoken by a novelist rather than by a philosopher, and which of them a character in my novel could sincerely hold without becoming a caricature. Where a reading depends on the specifically nineteenth-century setting — the satirical paper, the established church, the small city where everyone reads the same sheet — say so, and say what, if anything, survives translation into a world of algorithmic recommendation and one-star reviews.

Close with a single line beginning "Verdict:" naming the one reading that best supports the dismissal my colleague performed.
