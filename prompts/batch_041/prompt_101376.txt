I teach a seminar on the Vienna Circle, and through a slightly absurd chain of events I've ended up as the resident philosopher for the exhibition design team at a science museum — they put out roughly one big public chart a week (vaccination coverage, antibiotic use, water quality, that kind of thing), and those charts get picked up by schools and by the city public-health office, so a misleading one travels a long way. My problem is that the designers keep saying a chart "speaks for itself," and I keep having to explain that in the tradition I actually know something about, that phrase is doing almost no work. So here's what I want from you, and I want you to think carefully and maybe start by brainstorming before you commit to anything: treat yourself as someone who is genuinely at home in two places at once, mid-century logical empiricism and contemporary information design, and write me a decision procedure the design team can actually run.

Let me put the Neurath material on the table so you don't have to guess at it. The Vienna Method, later called ISOTYPE, was built by Neurath and Gerd Arntz at the Social and Economic Museum in Vienna, and its first rule was that you never show a larger quantity by enlarging the symbol — you repeat an identical symbol, because a bigger blob leaves you unable to say whether you're comparing height, area, or something else, whereas repeated units stay countable. Perspective was avoided for the same reason. But the part my designers always miss is that Neurath never thought this replaced words: it was a helping language, always accompanied by verbal elements, and he was clear it could never be a fully developed language at all — he treated it as a language-like technique. Which means there is no picture that is self-interpreting; there is always a key, a unit, a reading convention, something learned. And the technique is very good at some things (how many, how much, where, in what order) and structurally bad at others (negation, conditionals, generality, causal claims — anything with an if or a not in it).

So: give me the procedure. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and — this is important — where it stops and hands the decision to a human because the question is about values and not about pictures (how much simplification the museum is willing to buy, whether the exhibit is there to inform or to nudge, whether to show the uncertainty at all). Constraints: the designers have no philosophy background, the chief curator has to sign off on every chart so the procedure needs to end in a clean verdict (publish as is / publish with a key or caption added / send back), and it has to be runnable in about fifteen minutes by someone who is tired. Then run it on three cases: (1) a chart showing vaccination rates across city districts using symbols scaled up and down by district, (2) a diagram the team is proud of showing how an outbreak spreads, with arrows between boxes and one box meaning "no transmission," and (3) a six-panel handwashing sequence. I expect your procedure to tell the team something they won't enjoy hearing on at least one of those, and I'd rather you not soften it. Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do — where it will misfire or give a false sense of rigor — since I'd rather they know its edges than trust it blindly.
