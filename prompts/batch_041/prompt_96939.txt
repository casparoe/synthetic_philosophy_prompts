For a course on nineteenth-century moral philosophy, I am trying to understand what the early Society for Psychical Research's investigative program—and Sidgwick's central role in it—presupposes philosophically. The practice I have in mind is this: the Society, founded in 1882 with Sidgwick as its first president, treated telepathy, apparitions, mediumistic communications, and related phenomena as objects of exact and unimpassioned inquiry, on a par with the natural sciences, while also treating the results as potentially bearing on the truth of religion and the authority of morality. Sidgwick, who had argued in The Methods of Ethics that practical reason ends in a dualism between egoism and utilitarianism, devoted considerable energy to this research and hoped it might provide evidence for personal survival and a moral government of the universe. What philosophical commitments does that practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that survival after death is an empirical hypothesis whose evidence can be weighed without antecedent metaphysical commitment, or does it presuppose a particular view of the self as a substance capable of persisting beyond bodily death? Does it presuppose that moral philosophy needs external metaphysical support—that the dualism of practical reason cannot be resolved by practical reason alone and requires empirical evidence of a benevolent cosmic order? Or is it compatible with a more modest view on which psychical research is simply one more domain in which the ethics of belief applies, and its results need not bear on ethics at all? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it. Then tell me what would have to change in Sidgwick's ethical project, or in the Society's investigative norms, if we replaced the presupposed view with a rival one—for example, if survival were treated as a purely religious hope rather than an empirical question, or if the dualism of practical reason were taken to be permanent and unremediable by any conceivable evidence. Please respond in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
