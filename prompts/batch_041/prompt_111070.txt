You are a philosopher specializing in supererogation and the limits of moral requirement. I am preparing a seminar exercise and want your considered ratings, not a literature survey. Take the following as the working definition and do not re-litigate it: an act is supererogatory just in case it is (1) not morally required, (2) morally better than the best alternative that is required, and (3) performed at some net cost to the agent. Start with a brief brainstorm, a few lines at most, on which of the three clauses you expect to be doing the real work, and then proceed to the ratings.

Rate each of the six acts below on a 0–10 scale for how clearly it counts as a case of supererogation, where 0 is not at all, 5 is genuinely borderline, and 10 is a paradigm instance. Give a one-line justification for each. Then state, for each act, which of clauses (1)–(3) it satisfies and which it fails. If you think the definition makes the category unavailable for some of these, say so outright rather than forcing a number.

(a) A nurse on a long-haul flight answers a call for medical assistance and stabilizes a passenger. Her employer indemnifies her, she finds emergencies exhilarating, and she was within her rights to stay seated and watch the film.
(b) A commuter gives up his seat to a passenger who is tired but not unwell, and stands for twenty minutes.
(c) A scientist turns down a lucrative industry post to keep working on a neglected tropical disease, forgoing most of a career's earnings.
(d) A man forgives the person who defrauded him, drops the matter entirely, and tells no one; the fraudster is spared public exposure.
(e) A retired teacher spends her savings on an attempt to become the oldest person to summit a difficult mountain. She succeeds, at real risk and real cost, and no one else benefits.
(f) An executive donates a large sum toward a new hospital wing, well beyond anything required of her, mainly because the naming plaque will help her political ambitions.

To finish: identify the pair of acts whose ratings you would have to change if clause (2) were dropped, and say which single clause you would rather revise than keep, and why. Finally, say whether case (f) gives you reason to add a fourth clause about the agent's motive, and if so, what it would have to say to exclude (f) without also excluding (a). Think carefully before scoring, and keep your reasoning visible at each step.
