I teach an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology and decision theory — about sixteen students, a single ninety-minute session — and I want an in-class game that makes the common prior assumption register as a live constraint rather than as a clause buried in a theorem statement. What I want the room to collide with before anyone names it: two agents can be flawless Bayesians, can each know the other's posterior, and can still be unable to keep disagreeing — and the only thing preventing them is an assumption neither of them noticed they were making.

The design I have in mind, which you should feel free to overhaul if you can make it land harder, runs the same betting dialogue twice with one concealed change between the runs.

Round one. The state of the world is fixed by a device whose construction the whole room is told, so that everyone begins from the same prior over the possible states. Each participant then receives a private signal drawn from a commonly known signal structure and writes a posterior for the target event on a card. The cards are read aloud in a fixed order; each participant recomputes in light of the full announcement history; the cards are read again; and so on until no one's number moves. What the room should watch, without being told to expect it, is disagreement dying.

Round two. Identical, except that before play you privately tell different subsets of the room different things about the device's construction — so their priors differ — while leading each participant to believe that everyone received the instruction they did. Now the dialogue refuses to converge, and the disagreement survives every public announcement.

Throughout both rounds, a designated broker is tasked with proposing a bet that every participant, given their own information and beliefs, judges favorable and accepts. In round one the broker comes back empty-handed; in round two the broker can always produce one, and can pocket a riskless margin on it.

Then give me, as a runnable packet: the materials and setup; the roles (participants, broker, facilitator); a payoff scheme that needs no real money and that makes the round-two betting vivid without real stakes; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; and the debrief. The debrief should map particular moments in the play onto the underlying results — the agreement theorem, in particular the parts played by the common prior, by Bayesian updating, and by common knowledge as against mere mutual knowledge of the posteriors — and should make plain that persistent disagreement after a full public dialogue leaves exactly three exits: different priors, non-Bayesian updating, or a failure of common knowledge of the structure. It should name the doctrine that belief differences are attributable entirely to differences in information, and should spend its last ten minutes on the fact that the theorem is all-or-nothing and admits relaxations (common p-belief yielding a bound on how far posteriors can drift; priors that are merely close yielding posteriors that are merely close), and on the result that a common prior is equivalent to the impossibility of a bet that every agent regards as favorable.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

Finally, in the write-up, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material — which claims are theorems under their stated assumptions, and which are interpretive or disputed — and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
