I'm in a seminar on personal identity and we've been kicking around the idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity — the view that there's no little homunculus inside, just a story that keeps being told and that creates the illusion of a stable center. I'm supposed to bring in a poem for the last session, and I definately want the form to do something the discussion couldn't: I want the poem to make the point by how it moves, not just by what it says. Here's the twist I've been trying to work out, and I don't think it has a name yet — call it the listener's gravity. The usual picture is that the narrative gravity is generated by the person's own self-telling. But in alot of my conversations lately, the story bends toward whoever is listening: I notice I narrate my past differently depending on who's across the table, and after enough retellings the version shaped for that listener starts to feel like the real me. So the center of gravity isn't in me, it's in the expectation of the audience, and the 'I' is just the shape the story takes as it falls toward them. I want a poem that takes that seriously without just saying it.

Could you write me a pantoum — four quatrains, the standard interlocking pattern where the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third of the next, and the last stanza loops back to the first — in the voice of someone who has just finished telling their life story to a stranger and realizes the story has more solidity than the memories it's made of? The repetition should do the work: each time a line returns it should mean something slightly different because it's now being said to a different imagined listener, so the poem enacts the way the self gets re-centered by the act of telling. Maybe let the pronoun 'I' migrate through the poem, or allow one of the returning lines to shift so that 'I' becomes 'you' or 'she' at the turn, so that by the end the center of gravity has visibly shifted. It doesn't need to resolve whether there's a real self underneath; I actually want it to leave that hanging. But someone who knows the narrative-gravity idea should recognize it, and someone who doesn't should still get a good poem out of it. Maybe 16 lines total, no need for a strict meter, but keep the pantoum repetitions exact enough that the form is audible.
