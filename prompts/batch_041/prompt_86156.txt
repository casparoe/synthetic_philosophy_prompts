I've been attending a small philosophy reading group that meets in the back room of a bookstore, and I joined mostly because I've been trying to make sense of a long-running disagreement in my own family — my mother and my aunt have been feuding for years over how to read my late grandfather's decision to sell the family land, and both of them seem to me to be right in ways that can't both be right. A friend suggested I look into the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, the idea that reality is many-sided and that every judgment is true only from a particular standpoint. I'm not a philosopher by training — I studied accounting — so some of the vocabulary went past me. Last week the group read a chapter on Jain epistemology, and two of the more philosophically inclined members, Asha and Vikram, got into an argument that I couldn't follow to the end.

Asha began by saying that the seven-fold predication scheme (syādvāda) — which qualifies each judgment with "syāt," roughly "in some respect," and then runs through: somehow it is, somehow it is not, somehow it both is and is not, somehow it is inexpressible, and combinations of these — is either trivial or contradictory. If "syāt" means "from some standpoint," then "somehow it is and somehow it is not" is no contradiction, but the scheme tells us nothing we didn't already know: different standpoints yield different predications. If "syāt" is doing any real work, then the third predication is a straightforward contradiction. Vikram replied that "syāt" is not a hedge but a modal operator indexed to a standpoint, and that the seven predications are not about the same standpoint; the standpoints are fixed by the kind of question you are asking — substance, mode, time, relation — so the scheme is a taxonomy of the ways a thing can be considered, not a license for arbitrary predication. Asha came back: that just relocates the problem. Either the taxonomy is complete, in which case you need a standpoint from which its completeness is visible, and by the theory's own lights that standpoint is also one-sided; or it is incomplete, in which case the scheme is just one more partial view with no special claim on us. Vikram said the completeness claim is not made from a standpoint — it is a claim about the structure of predication itself, not a predication about a thing, and so it is not the kind of judgment the theory relativizes. Asha replied that the theory says all judgments are standpoint-relative; if the completeness claim is a judgment, it is standpoint-relative too, and if it is not a judgment, then what is it? Vikram said it is a rule rather than a judgment, a second-order rule about how to deploy first-order predications. Asha closed by saying that rules can be wrong, and if this rule is wrong the scheme fails, while if it is right then it is a standpoint-independent truth, which contradicts anekāntavāda.

Could you go through their exchange and rate each move — Asha's opening dilemma, Vikram's modal-operator reply, Asha's completeness regress, Vikram's rule-versus-judgment distinction, and Asha's final dilemma — from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each? I'd also like to know whether there is a formulation of anekāntavāda that escapes Asha's dilemma without collapsing either into the triviality she describes or into the contradiction she describes, and if so, what it costs. Please don't just tell me who won; tell me which specific claims each of them got right, which they got wrong, and what both of them are assuming without argument.
