Ok, novelist here, and I'm about 200 pages into a book whose whole plot turns on whether a character's loyalty is real when she never gets a chance to show it. A philosopher friend handed me the argument below after a fight we had about it; I've tidied it up but the bones are hers. Take the role of someone who works on dispositions for a living, and brainstorm before you commit — I want the real thing, not a textbook survey.

(1) "x is disposed to M when C" is true only if "if x were in C, x would M" is true. (2) You can always bolt an interfering factor onto a case: my protagonist's smoke alarm is disposed to shriek when there's smoke, but the landlord has wired it to cut its own power the moment it smells any; my neighbours key is disposed to open her door, but she's had a lock fitted that seizes up whenever the correct key goes in; and a wine glass is fragile even wrapped in a towel, though wrapped in a towel it won't shatter when I knock it over. (3) A counterfactual like "if x were in C, x would M" is true only if x Ms however it happens to be in C, interfering factors and all. (4) So for any disposition ascription there is some way of being in C in which the thing doesn't M. (5) So no such counterfactual is ever true. (6) So by (1) no disposition ascription is ever true — the alarm isn't disposed to shriek, she isn't loyal, and the same move shows nothing causes anything. (7) But "this glass is fragile" is about as true as a sentence gets.

So which step do I cut, and why did it look fine until I cut it? I'd sell my car before I gave up (1) or (7) — if dispositions aren't grounded in how things would go, "she's loyal" is just a compliment — but if the honest answer is that I have to lose one of them, tell me what that costs, and what each of the other candidates would charge me instead.
