My mate Dan and I had a proper two-hour arguement last night about the coin-tossing bit at the start of Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead — Rosencrantz calling heads and getting it ninety-two times on the trot, Guildenstern getting steadily more rattled and starting to wonder whether they've come under some unnatural or supernatural influence, Ros just scooping up the coins. Dan's reading is that it's all determinism: they're minor characters in somebody else's play, the script settles everything, so of course the coin does what it's told, and the scene is really about fate and free will. I think he's half right, and the half he's missing is the interesting half — the scene is a question about what probabilty is even supposed to mean — and I want you to write me the reading that makes that case. I don't need the free-will essay, Dan can have that one.

So: read the play through the interpretations of probability. For each of the three usual suspects — the frequency view, propensities, the subjectivist/credence view — say what it makes of those ninety-two heads and where it has to wriggle. Which one makes Guildenstern's horror sensible, and which one makes it a straight confusion? A subjectivist presumably just says he should have updated hard towards the coin being crooked or the game being fixed. A frequentist can't even state the probability of a single run like that, or else says one run can't touch the law either way. A propensity theorist has to say whether the propensity changed or was always tiny and they got unlucky. Be specific rather than gesturing at the slogans.

Two things I keep coming back to. First, there's no long run anywhere in this play — they're dead before any collective exists, and the whole business is the single case, which is the one thing frequency views can't handle. Is that a point about the play or just about frequentism? Second, if the tosses are fixed by the author, there's no objective chance in the play at all and the only thing left is credence — so is the law of probability they keep invoking really just a norm about what you ought to believe, rather than a fact about coins? Dan also reckons the bit where they try to work out the first thing they can remember is the reference class problem. Real connection, or is he reaching?

Some tests I want you to run the reading through. Suppose the coin actually is a trick coin that neither of them ever examines — I don't think they ever look at it, and that seems to me the most philosophically loaded thing in the scene. Does the scene still work, and is it making a point about chance or only about ignorance? Suppose instead the coin is fair and they've just been astronomically unlucky — does the scene mean something different? And what about the Player and the tragedians, dying over and over as a professional turn: is there a thread there about a propensity as a disposition that might never manifest, or am I overreading?

Don't reduce the play to an illustration of one of the three views, and don't bend the views to fit the play — tell me where each of them sits awkwardly with it. Finish with what the play adds that the arguments on their own don't: what it's like to be the single case, and to be unable to tell an improbable run from a rigged one from the inside. Don't bother quoting the play at me, I know it, and no need for sources. British English, please — the whole thing.
