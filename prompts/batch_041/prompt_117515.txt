I am a novelist at work on a book set in the German lands in the opening decades of the seventeenth century, and I have agreed to run a single session at a writers' residency: fourteen participants, ninety minutes, no prior reading, mixed backgrounds, several of them privately convinced that the history of science is costume drama. I do not want a lecture on Kepler. I want the participants to feel in their hands why his reform of astrology was an intellectually attractive position rather than a quaint error, and then to feel what it cost him. They should arrive at the problem themselves, so that when I name it at the end it lands as recognition rather than information.

Here is the position I need the activity to embody, and I would rather you work from this description than from anything you may half-remember, because the details are what make it interesting. Kepler spent his working life casting horoscopes and writing almanacs, and he defended astrology against those who wanted to discard it wholesale. But he wanted to purge it. The traditional practice, in which each planet carries a fixed nature and each placement a fixed signification, so that a reading is assembled from a table of correspondences, struck him as crude and largely false. What he took to be genuinely effective was not the planet considered alone but the aspect — the angular relation between two planets, the sextile, the square, the trine, the opposition. And the reason the aspect matters is geometrical. The soul, on his account, is itself structured by geometry and carries an innate capacity to be stirred by proportion, an attunement to ratio, much as the ear is attuned to musical intervals. When the light of two planets reaches the earth at a given angle, what acts on the soul is the proportion, not the planet. Because each soul has its own attunement, the same configuration stirs different people differently, and the effect is a stirring rather than a push: it inclines the dispositions, it does not compel the event. This is what let him fold astrology into his larger Pythagorean program, in which the same ratios govern musical consonance, the architecture of the cosmos, and the responses of the soul, so that a reformed astrology becomes continuous with astronomy and music theory rather than a superstition parked beside them. It is also what let him treat the astrologer's art as a matter of conjecture about inclinations rather than certainty about outcomes — a distinction he took seriously and did not regard as a defect.

I want an activity in which the participants run into three things, in order. First, they should try to read a set of cases using the scheme of fixed planetary natures and find that it fails, and fails in a way that is not mere bad luck: the same planet has to account for opposite outcomes across the cases, and ingenuity only multiplies the ad hoc rules. Second, they should be handed the Keplerian toolkit — the aspect as the effective thing, souls with different attunements, influence as stirring rather than pushing — and find that it works, and works beautifully: it explains the variation across persons, it connects to the mathematics they already sense in music and in astronomy, and it has the feel of a principled theory rather than a patch. Third, they should discover that this same flexibility lets the theory absorb any outcome after the fact, and that to incline without compelling is precisely what makes it both humane and unfalsifiable.

Give me the design: the setting as staged, the roles or players, the materials, the rules and any payoffs or small stakes, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the debrief. The debrief should map particular moments in the activity onto the parts of Kepler's position described above, say what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the position the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in my own voice. These are writers, not philosophers: the mechanics have to be graspable within five minutes, with no handout longer than a page, no real money, and nothing they need to have read beforehand.
