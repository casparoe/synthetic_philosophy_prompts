Okay, so — context. I'm a novelist, and for about nine years now I've taught a fiction workshop inside a maximum-security prison, through one of those college-in-prison programs where incarcerated students take real university courses and get a real degree. Next month the program is having its graduation and they've asked me to give the commencement address, and I'm completely stuck, so I want you to write the thing for me.

First, act as someone who is genuinely fluent in two things at once: the philosophy of punishment (the whole retributivist-versus-consequentialist fight about what punishing people is even for) and the craft of ceremonial prose that has to be spoken out loud to a room. Think carefully, and please brainstorm before you draft — I'd rather you spend some time working out what this speech can and can't say before you write a single line of it.

Here's the room, and this is the hard part. About thirty graduates in caps and gowns. Some of them are getting out in a year or two. Some of them are serving life without parole and will die in that building — they are not going anywhere, and everyone in the room knows it. Then there are their families, including young kids. Then a row of donors and university administrators who keep using the word "transformation" like it's a product they bought. And then the corrections staff, who are not villains but who are also not exactly relaxed about any of this.

What I can't stop circling is this: a normal commencement is built on desert — you earned this, you deserve this — and in this room that word is radioactive. The retributive frame says they deserved what they got and maybe still do; the consequentialist frame says the education is worthwhile because it reduces recidivism and makes them better risks, which is just a polite way of saying they're worth educating only insofar as they pay off later. The second one collapses entirely for the lifers, who will never "pay off" in that sense — so the speech has to be able to say that learning is good in itself, for a person, without any future attached to it.

There's also a distinction I keep chewing on and I want the speech to live inside it: being released is not the same as being forgiven. The state released them; nobody forgave them, and a graduation that quietly pretends otherwise is lying to a room full of people who can smell a lie. So don't tell them they're forgiven and don't ask them to perform remorse for the donors.

Constraints. Do not use the words redemption, second chance, paying your dues, debt to society, or turning your life around. Don't promise anyone a future. Say something directly to the lifers, without pity, and mean it. It should run seven to eight minutes aloud and land for people who have never read a page of philosophy in their lives.

Give me the speech, then a short bulleted note on the two or three moments where you had to pick a side between the retributive and the consequentialist framing, and why you picked the one you did.
