I've gotten weirdly into futarchy lately — the Robin Hanson thing, "vote on values, bet on beliefs," where the polity settles on a welfare metric and then conditional prediction markets, rather than a legislature, pick which policies to adopt: you run a market on "if we adopt policy P, will the metric be higher in five years than if we don't?", and P wins if its conditional price clears the bar. I was arguing about it with a friend who works in municipal finance and neither of us could definately say who'd be to blame when a market resolves the wrong way, and now I'm trying to write a short story about it. Two things about the mechanism are eating at me. First, the scheme needs "if we adopt P" to be crisp and the metric to be contractible and verifiable after the fact, but real policies get half-implemented, amended, and reinterpreted, so somebody has to write the resolution language in advance — and whoever writes that fine print, not the market, ends up deciding. Second, and worse: once the conditional price is published and everyone knows it determines which policy wins, the traders aren't estimating "what happens if P" anymore, they're betting on "will this number get P adopted," which is a different question, and the market starts eating its own tail. So it isn't a truth-finder sitting outside the decision; it's part of the thing it's supposed to be measuring. (I keep thinking about Tyler Cowen's old objection that you can't cleanly seperate values from beliefs — the metric committee looks like the exact place where that seperation gives out in public.)

Here's what I want from you. Take the role of a science fiction writer who also actually knows the mechanism-design and social-choice literature, and who's read enough political fiction to avoid the obvious moves — no evil billionaire cornering the market, no sentient AI running the exchange, no closing speech about how democracy was better all along. Before you write anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming: give me two or three possible angles on the snags above, say which one lets a single scene carry the whole idea, and then write that one. Setting seed: a mid-sized coastal city, maybe twenty years out, that went futarchy after a pension blow-up gutted the old council; almost nobody has heard of the Bureau of Conditional Resolution, whose clerks write the resolution language for every market the city runs. Tell it from inside that office, close third person, 1500–2500 words. I want at least one market's actual wording visible on the page (invent it — it doesn't have to resemble any real proposal), a protagonist who's genuinely good at the job and is only now figuring out what the job actually is, and an ending where they have to make a call the mechanism definitively can't make for them — not because the market is wrong, but because it's silent exactly where it looked loudest.

Then tack on a short afterword, three or four paragraphs, written as a note to me rather than to a reader: what in the story is a real mechanism-design or philosophical worry and what you invented for the plot, and what the story manages to say that a straight argument for or against futarchy couldn't. Think carefully, and don't let it turn into an explainer — the ideas should be doing work inside the scene, not lectured at it.
