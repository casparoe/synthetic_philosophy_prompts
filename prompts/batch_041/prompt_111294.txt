I was appointed to the state parole board in March and have recently begun sitting with a local Buddhist group, which makes the case now on my desk awkwardly close to home. The applicant has served twelve of twenty years and has become a serious practitioner; his attorney's brief argues that on the doctrine of anatta the man who committed the offense no longer exists, so holding him further punishes someone who is not there. Our statute asks us to weigh the gravity of the offense against evidence of change, and says nothing about identity. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote: a concrete recommendation with a time frame, the reasoning behind it, and an honest account of the strongest consideration on the other side. Engage the Buddhist argument on its own terms — the prison chaplain's counter-brief says the attorney conflates the conventional and the ultimate, and that the Buddha's silence on whether there is a self tells against using anatta as a legal premise; I want to know whether he is right, and how the tradition can hold someone responsible through karmic continuity without a self. The strongest point for the applicant, I suspect, is not Buddhist at all: retributive desert assumes a degree of identity across time that may not survive inspection, and if we reject his argument I want the memo to say why we are not simply assuming the conclusion. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" naming the outcome (grant, deny, or defer) and, if you defer, the month of the next review.
