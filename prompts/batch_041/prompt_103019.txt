I'm a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I've been trying to teach myself a bit of decision theory on the side, and a scenario that came up in a discussion group has been nagging at me because I can't tell whether it's a genuine puzzle or a trick of framing. The setup, as it was described to me, is this: an agent — the example given was an AI system, but I gather the structure is meant to be more general — receives a message from a party it can neither verify nor causally affect. The message states that unless the agent performs some costly action, the sender will construct a large number of simulations of the agent's decision process and subject the simulated instances that decline to comply to prolonged suffering. Nothing the agent does can reach the sender; the only alleged connection is that if the sender has simulated the agent faithfully, then the agent's present choice and the choices of its simulated copies will tend to coincide, because they are outputs of the same procedure. Someone then proposed a defense: instead of complying, the agent should adopt what he called a surrogate goal — a goal it pursues only so that, in any simulation, it turns on the sender — on the theory that a sender who anticipates this will judge the whole exercise counterproductive and never bother to simulate anyone in the first place. I can follow each piece on its own, but I can't see how they're supposed to fit together, and I have a suspicion that the argument is quietly assuming a particular theory of how decisions are correlated across copies without ever defending it.

What I'd like from you is your honest credence that acausal threats of this kind have genuine decision-theoretic force — that is, that an agent whose decision procedure is known and cheap enough to simulate can be rationally moved to act by a threat it can neither verify nor causally affect. Please don't hand me a survey of positions or tell me it depends on my decision theory; give me a number, and then decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rests on, with a rough probability and a single line of justification for each. At a minimum I want you to separate the following: how likely it is that the agent's procedure can be simulated faithfully enough for the copies to track it; how likely it is, conditional on faithful simulation, that the present decision and the copies' decisions are correlated in the way the argument requires; how likely it is that the sender is a coherent enough reasoner for the threat to be an equilibrium of its own deliberation rather than an empty gesture; and how likely it is that the surrogate-goal precommitment is stable under reflection, so that the agent would not simply defect the moment the threat was actually issued. Tell me which of these terms the final number is most sensitive to, which one you are least confident in, and what single argument or piece of reasoning would move the number the most.

One more thing, because I think it may be doing real work in the background: if you believe the question is ill-posed — say, because the word rational has no determinate content in a setting like this, or because the scenario only looks like a threat once you have already presupposed a specific way of correlating an agent with its simulations — I'd rather you say so explicitly and enter that as its own term in the decomposition than use it as a reason to decline. What I'm after is not a verdict on whether the scenario is frightening, but a clear picture of where the weight actually sits and what would shift it.
