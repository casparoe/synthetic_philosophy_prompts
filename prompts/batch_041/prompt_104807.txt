Last week a friend and I got into an argument that ran well past midnight, and I still cannot say which of us was defending the more defensible position. She had rewatched Groundhog Day and came away convinced that it is, at bottom, a karma film: Phil Connors is trapped in February 2nd until he has earned his release, the loop is a ledger on which every good and bad act is registered and eventually balanced, and the ending — the perfect day in which he saves the boy from the tree, helps the choking man in the restaurant, changes the elderly women's tire, and at last wins Rita — is the payout of accumulated merit. My objection was that this cannot be quite what the film is doing, because karma, at least in the sense she needs, is a theory of desert: it holds that one's circumstances are one's due, that suffering is merited by prior action, and that the order of things is in the business of settling accounts. Groundhog Day never once suggests that Phil's imprisonment is deserved; it supplies no prior offense that could merit it, no mechanism that is keeping score, and no proportion between what he does and what he gets. She replied that I was being pedantic — that the loop plainly functions as a moral gymnasium, and that whether we call the payoff desert or growth is a distinction without a difference.

We agreed to settle it by writing it up properly, and that is the task I would like help with. Read Groundhog Day through the philosophy of karma understood as a theory of moral desert, taking both the film and the doctrine seriously enough that neither is reduced to an illustration of the other. In particular, please keep apart three strands that ordinary talk runs together: karma as a causal law that explains how character and circumstance are produced across a stream of lives; karma as a system of desert, on which one's present condition is one's due; and karma as a theodicy that justifies suffering by appeal to a hidden moral order. Which of these, if any, does the film actually dramatize, and where does it refuse the one it appears to invite? I am especially interested in the scenes that pull in opposite directions — the suicide montage, where the loop looks like a punishment with no crime; the stretch in which Phil learns piano, ice-sculpting, and French, where the very repetition that was torment becomes cultivation; and the final day, where his goodness is presented as the price of his release. Is Phil's eventual virtue genuine, or is it the by-product of an incentive that would make it not virtue at all — and does that bear on whether his release is deserved? Where does the film resist the reading altogether, and what does it add to the philosophical debate that the karma literature, which tends to assume rather than dramatize the link between act and consequence, tends to miss?
