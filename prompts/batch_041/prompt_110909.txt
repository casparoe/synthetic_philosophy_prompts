Having spent my working life in the physical sciences, I have only recently begun to read philosophy, chiefly by sitting in on a small reading group at my former university. Last month two of its members became locked in an argument that I was unable to referee, and I would value your help with it.

The case under discussion was this. A woman was advised by her physician to postpone conception for several months on account of a transient condition; waiting would have been inconvenient but perfectly feasible. She conceived immediately instead. Her daughter, whom I will call Anna, was born with a moderately serious but manageable impairment that would not have arisen had the mother waited. Anna is now grown; she is glad to be alive, does not consider her life blighted, and yet maintains that her mother acted wrongly and that she holds it against her.

The first of my two acquaintances, whom I shall call the rigorist, held that the mother wronged Anna. The second, the pragmatist, held that Anna cannot have been wronged at all. Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

Rigorist: The mother understood the risk and chose to run it. She should not have gambled with the welfare of the child she was about to have, and Anna has a legitimate complaint.

Pragmatist: But had the mother waited, Anna would not exist. The alternative was not Anna unimpaired; it was a different child, or no child. Anna therefore cannot be worse off than she would otherwise have been, and by her own account she is glad to be here. One cannot wrong a person by conferring on her a life she endorses.

Rigorist: I never claimed she was worse off. Not every wrong consists in leaving someone worse off. One can be wronged by a betrayal, or by being used, without any measurable loss. Anna herself distinguishes being glad that she exists from approving of how she came to exist, and I see nothing incoherent in her holding both at once.

Pragmatist: The incoherence shows up the moment you ask what she would give up. If she would not trade her existence for nonexistence, then in what sense is she wronged? A grievance she would not undo is not a wrong to her; it is a feature of her history that she dislikes.

Rigorist: That standard is far too high. I can be wronged by a betrayal I would never undo, precisely because undoing it would undo the good that followed. The wrong lies in the act, not in a ledger of outcomes. And we do not ordinarily require a victim to prove that she would rather never have existed.

Pragmatist: The betrayal case has a victim who exists independently of the betrayal. Here the supposed victim exists only because of the act you condemn. That is the disanalogy, and it is decisive.

Rigorist: Then perhaps the wrong is not that Anna was harmed but that she was disrespected — brought into being on a wager about her own welfare, which is a way of treating her as a means rather than as an end.

Pragmatist: There was no "her" to treat as a means before the act. You keep reaching for a victim who was not yet there to be one.

I have no idea who has the better of this, and I would rather not guess. Please give me your verdict as five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
