I'm in a reading group on social explanation, and a classmate has worked up an argument that I cannot decide whether to admire or to throw out. It is supposed to show that methodological individualism is not merely one option among others but the only option that is even intelligible, and he thinks it does this by exposing the holist's talk of institutions, structures, and markets as either shorthand or superstition. The trouble is that when I follow it out it seems to prove far more than he wants: it appears to show that there are no genuine explanations anywhere above fundamental physics, so that biology, psychology, and economics alike are at best bookkeeping devices that describe patterns without explaining anything. He is untroubled; I am not. The argument runs as follows. (1) Every event, social or otherwise, consists in some change in the states or properties of individual things; there is nothing else for it to consist in. (2) To explain an event is to cite what brought it about, which is to say, to cite the causes of the change it consists in. (3) Therefore any adequate explanation of a social event must ultimately cite facts about individuals. (4) If an explanation cites a fact that is not itself a fact about individuals — that an institution required it, that a structure produced it, that a market cleared, that a norm spread — then either that fact is a fact about individuals in disguise or it is not a fact at all. (5) But there are social explanations we cannot paraphrase into individual terms without losing precisely what explains: for instance, explaining why a currency lost its value, or why a rumour outran its correction, by citing features of a network or a distribution of beliefs that no individual intended and that no individual-level description captures. (6) Therefore such explanations either are not genuine explanations or cite facts that do not exist. (7) But they are genuine explanations — the currency really did lose its value for that reason. (8) So one of (1), (2), or (4) must be false.

He reads (8) as a proof that individualism must give way to holism; I notice that the same machinery would just as well show that a cell biologist who explains a division by citing the cell cycle, or a psychologist who explains a choice by citing a preference, is trading in non-facts, and that cannot be right. He also insists that any legitimate social explanation has the shape of a little diagram — a macro condition, the individual dispositions it produces, the individual actions that follow, and then the aggregate outcome — and treats any explanation that skips a stage as incomplete by definition. I suspect the damage is done somewhere in the passage from what things are made of to what explains them, and possibly again in that either/or in (4), but I cannot say which step is the culprit or why it looked so innocent. I would rather keep (5) and (7) than surrender them, and I am willing to give up (1) if I must, though I want to know what that costs.

Tell me which step or inference to reject, and be specific about the mechanism of the failure — whether it is an equivocation, a scope or quantifier slide, or a premise that is simply too strong. Then explain why the offending step looked fine, and price the alternatives: if I give it up, what do I owe the individualist, what do I owe the holist, and what, if anything, must I now say about explanation in the special sciences generally? Assume I have no library at hand; argue from the content of the steps rather than sending me to the literature.
