I'm midway through a metaphysics unit on dispositions, and I keep meeting the same picture at the front of every textbook: that ascribing a disposition is just a way of stating a conditional. To call a glass fragile, on this reading, is to say that if it were struck it would break; to call a lump of sugar soluble is to say that if it were put in water it would dissolve. The book does not offer the picture as a loose gloss but as an analysis, and it uses it to license a deflationary moral — that dispositions are not a distinctive kind of property at all, only patterns of counterfactual dependence, so nothing metaphysically special has to be added to the world to make room for them. My study partner says the analogy is carrying that whole argument and cannot bear the weight, and before I commit to a line in my essay I want to know whether they are right on the analogy's own terms.

The cases we keep circling are the ones where the conditional and the disposition come apart. There is a wire that is live, but wired to a cut-out that would break the circuit the instant anything touched it: the wire is disposed to conduct, yet it is not true that if it were touched it would conduct. There is a glass that really is fragile but is packed in foam: if it were struck it would not break, though nothing has happened to the glass itself. And there is a case I proposed in seminar that nobody seemed to know what to do with: a glass with a coating that is inert until the moment of impact, at which point it stiffens instantly and absorbs the blow, so that if the glass were struck it would not break, and the only thing standing between it and breaking is a feature that exists only because the striking occurs. My partner says the first two cases show the if-then reading is simply false, and the third shows it cannot be repaired by a rider about nothing interfering: any such rider must either be filled in by naming the dispositions in advance, which is precisely what the conditional was supposed to deliver, or it swallows every case and becomes vacuous. A classmate replies that these are two different kinds of case — one takes the disposition away, the other merely blocks its exercise — and that the reading only ever needed the second kind to be handled, which the rider can do. I cannot tell whether that distinction is a genuine resource for the analogy or a relabelling of its failure.

So please audit the analogy rather than adjudicate the literature. Start with the correspondence table: what is supposed to answer to the antecedent, to the consequent, to the background conditions, and to the rider about other things being equal, and at which of those joints the correspondence actually breaks. Then separate the breaks that matter for the deflationary conclusion from the ones that are just the ordinary distance between a slogan and a theory, and say which side of that line my invented case falls on. Then tell me what becomes of the argument once the rider is added — does it rescue the analysis, demote it to a merely extensional claim about which counterfactuals happen to hold, or make it circular in the way my partner alleges? And if the if-then picture is the wrong analogy for what a disposition is, say what a better one would be, and whether it survives all three cases or only appears to because it has not yet been put under the same pressure. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need the mapping and the argument.
