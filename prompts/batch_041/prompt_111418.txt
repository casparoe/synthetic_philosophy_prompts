I'm in my third year and taking a philosophy of physics module — we just finished the Bell's theorem block and I'm writing the essay now. The thing driving me up the wall is that everyone in the seminar says 'nonlocality' as if it's one thing. My tutor reckons the word 'local' is doing about six different jobs in the literature and that most of the shouting is people who mean different jobs by it. So I went back through my notes and collected the things that actually get called 'locality', and I want you to sort them for me. In my own words: (1) no signalling — nothing I do on one wing can shift the statistics on the other wing when the two events are spacelike separated; (2) parameter independence — given the complete hidden state of the pair, the setting on one wing (i.e. which observable gets measured) makes no difference to the outcome recorded on the other; (3) outcome independence — given the complete hidden state, the outcome on one wing makes no difference to the outcome on the other; (4) the factorisation condition Bell actually writes down, where the joint probabilities split into a term for each wing that depends only on that wing's setting plus the hidden state; (5) seperability — distant systems each have their own definite, separately specifiable states, so the state of the pair is fixed by the states of the parts; (6) relativistic locality — no privileged foliation, so we never have to say that of two spacelike separated events one happened 'before' the other.

What I want: rank those six by how much would actually be lost if we had to give that one up. So something at the top of your list means giving it up is giving up a real part of the world being local, and something at the bottom means it turns out to be an optional extra assumption rather than part of what we meant. Then tell me which of them Bell's theorem really does force us to drop — my essay currently says it forces us to drop signalling, and I'm now fairly sure that's wrong, so don't be polite about it. Be opinionated rather than hedging every clause.

And the thing my tutor always marks me down on: say what would change your ranking. Which argument or example would have to come along to move something up or down, and where is your own reasoning most likely to be shaky — e.g. if the ranking leans on a reading of 'local' that people dispute, say which reading and why you went with it anyway.
