Ok so I'm an editor at a small religious publisher and I've been handed the worst job in the buiding: we're doing an annotated edition of the gospels where sayings that probably go back to Jesus get marked in a different colour, and every single saying gets a footnote saying how confident we are and on what grounds. There are about four hundred sayings in the running, I get maybe ten minutes per saying, and whatever I decide has to survive a review meeting with our historical consultant (a serious secular historian), a theologian from our own tradition, and the publisher, who is the one who actually signs off.

I've read enough to know the standard toolbox — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, Semitic traces — and I've also read enough to know the toolbox is a mess. Dissimilarity looks self-defeating to me: if a saying is unlike both the Judaism of the time and the early church, who exactly was motivated to hang onto it? Embarassment has the same smell — the earliest Christians cheerfully preserved things that made them look bad (the crucifixion itself, Peter's denial, women as the first witnesses), so it doesn't seem to discriminate. Multiple attestation sounds like counting sources, but our sources probably share traditions, so I suspect it's usually double counting with extra steps. Coherence needs an authentic core to cohere with, which is the thing I'm trying to find. And the Aramaic argument cuts both ways — a translator can produce translation Greek, and a Greek author can write in a Septuagint-flavoured style on purpose. I also keep hearing that instead of asking whether a saying is weird enough, we should ask whether it's plausible in two directions at once: at home in a first-century Galilean Jewish setting, and at home in a movement that was carrying sayings forward and reworking them. That sounds right and I have no idea how to turn it into a test.

So what I want from you is a procedure I can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, what the output labels are and what each label is actually claiming (that these exact words are his? that something behind them is? that the saying is early even if it isn't his? that the story is early and the saying was added?), where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it has to hand off to a human because the call is theological rather than historical. Build in the fact that the tradition was carried for decades inside communities that were reshaping it — I don't want a procedure that pretends we can peel the church off the top of the Jesus layer like a sticker. And put one step ahead of all the others: some of what's in the running may not even be in the earliest recoverable text, which is a different question from whether he said it, and I want the procedure to say so before it starts weighing historical evidence.

Then run it on three cases: (1) the render-to-Caesar line, which everyone cites as multiply attested and which I think is really one source three times; (2) the let-the-dead-bury-their-own-dead saying, harsh and off-putting and serving nobody's agenda that I can see; (3) the story about the woman caught in adultery, which I'm told isn't in the earliest manuscripts of John at all — tell me what my procedure does with that one, and whether the answer it gives is a no or just a different question. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me something I won't want to hear, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure — how much independent attestation is enough, what base rate we're implicitly assuming for how much of the tradition is authentic, how much weight plausibility gets versus oddity. Finish with what the procedure can't do. My suspicion is that these criteria can sort the tradition into rough bands and never settle who he was, and I'd rather you say that plainly than dress it up.
