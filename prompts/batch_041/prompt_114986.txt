Ok so I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy degree, but I've been reading alot about moral disagreement and I ended up writing a long blog post that got me invited to talk at a small interdisciplinary ethics workshop next month — 15 or so people, mostly phil grad students and a couple of faculty. This is very much not my home turf and I'd definately like to not get shredded.

Heres my thesis. When two people (or two groups) hold genuinely different moral views, we usually treat compromise as a sad second-best: everybody gives up something they believe in, and the result is worse for both sides than getting your way. I want to argue the opposite — that compromise between value systems can be a straight up gain from trade, the same way two countries trading is a gain for both. The trick is that the deal doesn't have to be judged in a shared currency. Each side judges the deal only by its own ranking, and if the package beats the status quo on both sides' own rankings, nobody has been asked to think their values are less true; they've just been asked to accept a package that, by their own lights, is better than what happens if they keep fighting.

I've been calling the set of such deals the compromise surface: every point where each side prefers the deal to the walk-away. And I claim three things: (1) the surface is usually non-empty; (2) it's bigger than people assume, because the two sides care about different margins — the utilitarian can get more total welfare, the deontologist can get the thing she thinks is absolutely forbidden to happen, and neither has to endorse the other's reasoning; (3) treating compromise this way is more respectful than splitting the difference, because splitting the difference requires both sides to treat their convictions as only partly correct.

The room: it's an ethics / political theory crowd. I know there's at least one hardline consequentialist who'll say I'm dressing up act-consequentialism in a nicer suit; one Kantian-adjacent person who thinks treating a moral commitment as tradeable is already to have betrayed it; a political theorist who works on modus vivendi and compromise and who I suspect thinks my whole framing is naive about power, because compromise between unequal parties isn't trade, it's extortion; and a game theorist who will say this is the Coase theorem with extra steps and that I've ignored transaction costs, holdouts, and the fact that the status quo point is itself the product of past coercion. Probably also someone who says some things shouldn't be on the table at all — you don't trade away someone else's rights.
