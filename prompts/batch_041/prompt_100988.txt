I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about what she calls the unilateralist's curse, and I've come away with a knot of verdicts that I can't get to agree with one another. The structure she's interested in is this: suppose a number of people can each, independently, take some action, and the action only has to be taken once for it to have been taken by the group — publishing a result, releasing a dataset, pressing a button that changes something for everyone, leaking a document. Each person privately judges whether it's worth doing. If everyone who judges it worth doing simply does it, then the action happens so long as a single person judges it worth doing, and once the pool is large enough that is close to certain, even if each individual is more likely than not to be judging correctly. Her conclusion is that a prudent person should grow *more* conservative as the pool of possible actors grows: you should demand a higher standard of yourself precisely because someone else may act on a lower one. My resistance to this keeps surfacing as verdicts that don't line up, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the cases, with my honest reactions. (1) I hold documents showing that a company is poisoning a town, and no one else has them; leaking is clearly right, and the fact that I'm the only possible leaker only strengthens the case. (2) Same documents, but thirty colleagues also hold them and are as likely as I am to leak; leaking is still right, and in a way it feels *more* defensible, since the information is coming out regardless and my silence buys nothing. (3) I'm the only person who could publish a technique that I put at sixty per cent likely to do more good than harm; publishing seems permissible to me. (4) A hundred people, myself among them, each independently put the same technique at sixty per cent likely to be net beneficial and could each publish it; here my gut says I should not publish, and that no one in my position should, because the group is nearly certain to publish something harmful and I don't want my hand on it. (5) Now change (4) so that I'm at ninety per cent and the other ninety-nine are at sixty; I feel I should publish — I'm the one who has actually looked closely, and the others will publish their bad versions whether I move or not, so my refraining protects no one. I notice that (5) leans on exactly the marginal-impact reasoning that (4) rejects, that (2) leans on it while (1) doesn't, and I can't tell whether that's a principled difference or just my sympathies shifting with the stakes.

My own tentative principle is this: do the thing only if you would still endorse its being done in the world where everyone who is as confident as you are also does it. It seems to get (4) and (5) right, and (3) right, but it says nothing about the crowd of less-confident others in (5), who are doing most of the work in that case, and it treats (1) and (2) as equivalent when my verdicts about them differ at least in tone. I'd like you to tell me whether there's a version of this principle that survives contact with all five cases; if there isn't, which verdict I should give up and what I lose by giving it up; and what the surviving principle predicts for two cases I haven't thought through — a person who is one of a hundred, at sixty per cent, whose action is fully reversible, and a person who is one of a hundred, at sixty per cent, whose action would pre-empt the others by making their versions pointless. Please state the principle precisely, say which clause each of my cases forces, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save.
