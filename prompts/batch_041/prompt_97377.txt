I spent thirty-five years as a field ornithologist and population ecologist, and since retiring I have been working through philosophy of science on my own. One topic has me stuck: the raven paradox. I have read enough by now to see that there are two camps, and I have watched them argue past each other at a seminar series at my local university. I want you to write the document they should have produced together.

Camp A, as I understand it, holds that Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition are both correct as stated, that logically equivalent hypotheses are supported by the same evidence, and that a green apple therefore really does support the claim that all ravens are black; the only error is the surprise we feel, which is a fact about us and not about support. Camp B holds that support is a three-place relation among evidence, hypothesis, and background information, that the report of a green apple carries its provenance with it, and that whether such a report supports, does nothing, or undermines depends on how the observation was obtained; on this view the equivalence condition must not be applied to arbitrary reformulations of a hypothesis. I may have caricatured both. If so, sharpen them, but keep them as two positions a competent person could hold and defend.

I want the joint statement to do real work on cases that I have not seen handled well, and that matter to me because they are the sort of thing that actually happens in field work.

The camera-trap case. A station in a study plot logs every object its sensor triggers on. The hypothesis H is that every raven in the plot is black. The sensor is contrast-triggered, and the calibration data say that conditional on H the triggers are uniform across objects, while conditional on not-H a non-black raven is several times more likely to trigger than any other object. The log eventually returns one green apple (non-black, non-raven) and later one black raven. What does each side say the green apple does to credence in H, and what does each say the black raven does? The propositional content of the apple observation is the same as in the textbook case; only the selection procedure differs.

The informant case, which I have set up to point the other way. A colleague reports objects to you. You give prior weight one-half to the colleague being neutral, reporting a uniformly random object from the plot, and one-half to the colleague being a partisan who, if H is false, never reports the counterexample and instead reports a uniformly random non-black non-raven, and who, if H is true, reports a uniformly random object. Now the report is: here is a green apple. State the direction of the effect on credence in H under this model, and say what this case and the camera-trap case jointly show about how much the evidence is allowed to include.

The finite plot, so that we have numbers rather than intuitions. The plot contains exactly 10,000 objects, exactly 40 of which are ravens. Specify a prior over the colors of the ravens, sample one object uniformly at random, and compute the likelihood ratio for H against not-H for each of the two outcomes, a black raven and a green apple. Then repeat the computation for a second plot in which a separate census has established that at most three of the 10,000 objects are non-black. I expect the two camps to agree on the arithmetic and to disagree about what the arithmetic is evidence for, and I want to see whether that is what happens.

Then the document itself. A numbered section of agreed claims: everything both sides can sign without reservation, including whatever is a theorem about Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition taken together, whatever is true of the arithmetic, and whatever the two selection cases settle once their models are fixed. Then a disagreements table, one row per sub-question, with the disagreement written twice, once in wording that Camp A would accept as its own position and once in wording that Camp B would accept as its own, plus a column saying whether I should treat the row as a genuine disagreement or as a verbal one. Then the cruxes: for each genuine disagreement, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side, and for each crux a case, computation, or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and in a form that could turn out false. Tell me as well, for each side, what that side itself would count as evidence against its own position, and whether there is anything both sides agree would move the debate rather than merely being absorbed into the existing positions. Include, among the cruxes, whether any of the disagreement survives once the measure of support is fixed — qualitative support, a difference of probabilities, a likelihood ratio — and what each side says happens in the limit of an unbounded population.

Two constraints on the writing. Every formulation attributed to a side must be one that side could sign without feeling the deck is stacked; if you cannot find such a formulation, say so and explain what the obstacle is. And I do not want references, reading lists, or citations of any kind. I want the substance, and I would rather have your own reconstruction of the two positions than a survey of who said what.
