For a seminar paper on diachronic rationality I have been collecting the standard arguments for various coherence constraints, and I keep running up against a summary of the money pump that a friend's blog post gives. I want to know how much of it I can rely on, so I will set it out exactly as she wrote it. "The money pump is the decisive argument against intransitive preferences. Suppose you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, and that you begin holding C. A trader offers to give you B in exchange for C for a small fee; because you prefer B to C you accept, and you now hold B, a little poorer. She then offers A for B for another small fee, and since you prefer A to B you accept again. Then she offers C for A for a third fee, and since you prefer C to A you accept. You are back holding C, exactly where you started, and three fees poorer. Since you would rather have kept your money, and since the trader could have made the fees as small as she liked, your own preferences have been turned against you, and the only way to escape is to have transitive preferences. That is why transitivity is a requirement of practical rationality, and, more broadly, why rationality requires one's preferences to be stable over time: the smoker who prefers a cigarette in the evening but prefers, come morning, not to have smoked is caught by the same pump, only stretched across a day. The argument is the practical counterpart of the Dutch book argument, and it shows that coherence of preference is demanded by rationality in just the way coherence of credence is."

What I want from you is an accuracy audit rather than an exposition, since I already know the rough shape of the argument and do not need it re-derived. Go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a decision theorist or a philosopher of practical rationality would accept without hesitation, which are the sort of thing they would wince at even where there is a defensible reading, and which are simply wrong. I am especially unsure about the slide from "your preferences must be transitive" to "your preferences must be stable over time," which feels like two quite different constraints wearing one coat, and about whether the comparison with the Dutch book argument is doing any real work or merely borrowing its prestige. I also want the mechanics pinned down, because a classmate of mine thinks the whole thing is a parlour trick: she says that anyone who sees the sequence coming can simply decline the first trade, or refuse to pay for the swaps at all, and that if the trader instead bundles all three exchanges into a single take-it-or-leave-it offer then the argument changes character entirely, since the agent who accepts the bundle is immediately worse off while the agent who refuses it is not obviously irrational. I cannot tell whether that is a refutation of the pump, a restatement of it, or a separate argument that happens to share its conclusion, and I cannot tell what it leaves of the post's claim that transitivity is a requirement of rationality. Finish by telling me whether the post's conclusion survives your corrections in some weakened form, and if so what that weakened form is, and if not, what the pump does establish about diachronic rationality, if anything.
