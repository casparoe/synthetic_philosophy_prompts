For some time I have been trying to isolate a family of argumentative moves that recurs whenever scepticism or relativism comes up, and I cannot decide whether what I am noticing is one move or several, or whether it already has a name. The shape is always the same: a position is set aside not by evidence against it but by the observation that anyone who holds it must, in holding it, rely on something the position denies. The sceptic who argues that no belief is justified leans on the standards of justification he is impugning; the relativist who says that a claim is true only relative to a framework states that very claim as though it were true without qualification. I have no quarrel with the observation that such positions are awkward to hold. What puzzles me is that the charge seems to come in strengths that its users never distinguish, and that the strength tends to climb as the argument proceeds.

I can separate four strengths, though I am not confident the list is right or complete. First, the position cannot be consistently stated: the sentence, taken at face value, denies what the act of uttering it asserts. Second, the position cannot be consistently believed or genuinely doubted, because doubting that p requires one to take for granted things that the doubt is meant to suspend. Third, the position cannot be defended in argument, since any defence must employ the very resources it disowns. Fourth, the position is false, because what it denies is a precondition without which the activity by which it is reached — thinking, experiencing, asserting — would not be possible at all.

I have been told that what I am after is 'performative contradiction' or 'self-refutation', but those labels seem to cover only the first two strengths, and they do not help with the thing that actually troubles me, which is the passage from the third to the fourth. In most of the arguments I meet, what has been shown at best is that the position cannot be defended; what is concluded is that it is false. I would like to know whether that passage has a name of its own, and what would have to be added to a third-strength point to make a fourth-strength conclusion legitimate rather than merely rhetorical. I am equally unsure how the strengths relate to one another: whether the second collapses into the first, whether the first is ever more than a pun on a word, and whether the fourth is ever actually achieved by anyone or is only ever gestured at.

So: does this family of moves have an established name, and does the distinction among the four strengths — and the slide between them — have one? I am not looking for a bare label. What I want is the carving: exactly what each strength claims, which of them is ever established and by what, where the boundary between a genuine fourth-strength result and a rhetorical one falls, and whether the whole thing is best understood as one device with four settings or as four devices that happen to share a surface form.
