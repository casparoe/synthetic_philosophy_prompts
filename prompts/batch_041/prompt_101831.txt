After thirty years in experimental physics, I retired and have spent the last several years trying to learn philosophy properly. Next month I am presenting a paper to the philosophy of science reading group at the university where I now have an emeritus affiliation. The group is small and quite specific: four or five Bayesian confirmation theorists who treat projectibility as a matter of prior probability and conditionalization; two older entrenchment theorists who think the grue problem is solved by the fact that green is an entrenched predicate and grue is not; one historian of science who is allergic to a priori solutions and will want to know what working scientists actually do; and one metaphysician who will press me on dispositions and laws. I am a natural scientist, not a philosopher, so I want to be ready for the questions they will actually ask, in the vocabulary they will use.

My thesis is that the grue puzzle is not fundamentally a problem about induction or confirmation but about the metaphysics of projectible predicates. I argue that a predicate is projectible just in case it is invariant under a broad class of interventions: interventions on the sampling frame, on background conditions, and on the time and manner of examination. Green passes this test because its extension is stable under such interventions and because it tracks a causal disposition of the objects examined. Grue fails because its extension is defined by a discontinuous dependence on the time of examination; any intervention that changes when objects are examined changes the predicate's application in a way that no underlying causal disposition explains. The apparent symmetry between green and grue is therefore an artifact of treating predicates as mere functions from objects to truth values, rather than as devices for tracking causal structure. I call this the intervention-invariance criterion, and I want to defend it without appealing to any special faculty of intuition or to the history of word use.

Please prepare me for the question period. I need the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the relevant member of the group would phrase it. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft, along with what I should concede up front and what I should hold. I especially want to know what the Bayesians will say about whether intervention-invariance is just a disguised prior, what the entrenchment theorists will say about circularity, what the historian will say about actual scientific practice, and what the metaphysician will say about whether I have earned the right to talk about causal dispositions. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. I do not need a reading list or citations; I need a Q&A prep sheet.
