I run the forecasting desk at a mid-sized public-health analytics unit: six analysts, a quarterly forecast cycle, and a standing commitment that our published numbers are what regional planners use to decide where mobile testing capacity goes. A statistician two desks over has spent the past month telling anyone who will listen that our forecast register is incoherent — that if he were allowed to place a handful of bets at the odds we publish, he could guarantee himself a profit, and that he would happily demonstrate this for a £50 stake against any of us. I have read enough to recognise the shape of the argument: a set of odds that can be assembled into a sure loss is supposed to be defective, and the defect is supposed to lie in the odds rather than in the person holding them. I cannot settle this by argument and I do not want to. What I want from you is a coherence audit — a procedure my analysts can run on their own registers, once a quarter, in under half an hour, with a spreadsheet and no probability theory beyond what I can teach in a single lunchtime session. I will sign off on the results, and an analyst whose register fails has to be told to their face, so the procedure needs to produce findings I can actually deliver, not a score.

Here is what a register contains, so that the procedure can be built around it. For each question we track, an analyst publishes a single probability; sometimes, when they are unsure, a range instead. Questions come in families: partitions whose outcomes are supposed to be mutually exclusive and exhaustive, conjunctions and disjunctions built from those, conditionals of the form "if X happens, Y is likely", and — the part I care most about — a reflection item, in which each analyst also publishes a forecast of what their own forecast for the same question will be after the next data release. I want the audit laid out as a fixed sequence of tests, ordered so that the cheap and decisive ones come first, with each step stating what it checks, what counts as a failure, what a failure does and does not license us to conclude, and where the test simply cannot decide and a person has to. I want the points where judgment cannot be engineered away marked as such rather than hidden — in particular, I suspect most of the work is done by whoever decides which propositions are genuinely mutually exclusive, and by the question of what to do with an analyst who gives a range, or who says of some event that they would not bet on it at any odds. I also want it to say plainly what a clean audit does *not* establish. My worry is that I will start treating a passed audit as a certificate of quality, when as far as I can tell the whole apparatus tests internal consistency and says nothing at all about whether our numbers are any good. I do not need the case for probabilism made from first principles, and I do not want citations; I need the procedure.

Finally, run it end to end on three registers, and be willing to reach conclusions I will not enjoy. The first is tidy: a four-way partition summing to 1.06, together with the analyst's own conditional forecasts for two of the branches. The second is the awkward one: an analyst who gives ranges rather than points, refuses to name odds for the tail event, and insists that this makes them immune to the statistician's game. The third is the one I expect to hurt: a register that is internally impeccable — the partition sums to one, every conjunction respects its bounds — but whose reflection item has the analyst promising to move from 0.4 to 0.75 when the next data release arrives while also describing that release as uninformative, and whose conditionals imply they should already have moved. Tell me what the procedure says about that analyst, whether the £50 demonstration is legitimate against any of the three registers, and what I should say to my desk on Monday. I am content to be told that the sceptic is right about numbers I am attached to; I would rather learn it from you than from him.
