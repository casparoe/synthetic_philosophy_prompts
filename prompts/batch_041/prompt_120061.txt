I've been arguing about this for weeks with a friend who works on population ethics, and I've discovered that my verdicts on the familiar torture-versus-dust-specks tradeoff don't hang together. I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher who works on axiology and the aggregation of small harms, think the cases through carefully before you commit to anything, and then tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. Here they are. (1) One person tortured for fifty years, versus a single dust speck in the eye for each of 10^18 people: prevent the torture, and no increase in the number of specks would change my mind. (2) The same comparison, with the one person instead in severe, disabling but non-torturous chronic pain for fifty years: still spare them, though less firmly. (3) One person having one genuinely bad day — fever, aches, vomiting, missing something that mattered — versus one dust speck for each of 10^12 people: here I'd give the one person the bad day, since a trillion specks seem worse in aggregate than one bad day. (4) One person enduring a single minute of an itch, where I stipulate that each moment of the itch is exactly as unpleasant as a dust speck, versus one speck each for 10^15 people: give the one person the itch, the aggregate wins. (5) Exactly the same numbers and the same stipulated per-moment unpleasantness, but now the one person's minute is mild social embarrassment rather than an itch: spare them, and I don't think any number of specks justifies it. (6) One person's name is placed, without their knowledge and with no consequence that ever reaches them, on a list circulated to a handful of clerks as someone unreliable with money, versus one speck each for 10^15 people: prevent the list. (7) One person lives an entire life of unrelieved misery, versus one bad day for each of 10^6 people: give the million people the bad days — I won't trade a whole population's bad days for one ruined life — though I notice I can't say at what multiple of bad days I would flip. (8) One person getting a dust speck every day for fifty years, versus one speck each for 10^9 people: give the one person the daily specks; concentrating trivial harms in one person seems better than spreading them over a billion.

The best I can do on my own is something like this: harms above a severity threshold cannot be traded against any number of harms below it, while harms below the threshold aggregate freely; and the number of people affected matters over and above the total, so I prefer concentrating trivial harms in fewer people. That story doesn't explain (4) and (5), where felt intensity and the number of people affected are stipulated identical and I still go opposite ways, and it doesn't tell me whether (2) sits above or below the threshold, or whether (7) commits me to the view that the badness of a life has a ceiling. So: is there a single principle, or a small set of them, that gets all eight verdicts, or close? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for cases I haven't given — in particular (a) 10^20 specks against one bad day, (b) one person blinded against 10^9 people each breaking a finger, and (c) one person enduring eighty years of a mild chronic irritation they would pay everything they own to be rid of, against 10^12 people each getting one speck. Then tell me honestly which of my eight verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which one I should give up.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question. And tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to a different principle, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
