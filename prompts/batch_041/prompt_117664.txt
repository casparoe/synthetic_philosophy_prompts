In my second-year seminar on rationality we've spent two weeks on a puzzle the lecturer calls the cold room, and now two people in the group are at war over it. The setup: a research team will pay £400 to a food bank in your name if, at the moment you sign the consent form, you genuinely intend to come back next week and sit in a 4°C room for an hour. You never actually have to do it — you can bin the whole thing the second you've signed, no penalty, nobody minds, nothing at all is at stake for you. Their scanner is right about your intention about 95% of the time, and they only pay if it says you meant it.

Sam: you just decide you're doing it, sign, take the money for the food bank, then don't turn up. Intentions aren't promises, and changing your mind isn't lying.
Alex: you can't decide to intend something you already know you won't do. That's like deciding to believe the moon's made of cheese.
Sam: people sincerely intend things and then don't do them all the time — I sincerely intend to go to the gym every Tuesday.
Alex: sure, but you don't know in advance that you'll skip. Here you do, and you can't intend what you know you won't do.
Sam: I don't know it for certain, I might surprise myself. And anyway the scanner's only 95%, so even if my intention's rubbish the food bank gets paid one time in twenty.
Alex: then you're not getting it for intending, you're getting it by luck, which has nothing to do with the puzzle.
Sam: fine — so I just sit there trying to mean it?
Alex: you can't try to have an intention, same as you can't try to fall asleep; the trying is what stops it happening.
Sam: then I'll rig myself — don't rehearse the escape, tell my mum I'm going, write it in my diary in pen. People use committment devices all the time.
Alex: a commitment device only works if you sincerely intend to keep to it, so you've just moved the same problem one floor up.
Sam: even so, if I cant get the money that's not me being irrational, it's just a fact about how minds work.
Alex: the whole point of a puzzle about rationality is that a rational agent collects, so if you can't, something in your self-picture is broken.
Sam: and it's for a food bank, so the stakes obviously justify messing with your own head a bit.
Alex: 'should' implies 'can'.

They want me to referee and I honestly can't. Please go through it move by move — who's right on which point, not just who wins overall — and say what both of them are missing. British English please, its for our seminar write-up.
