Two people in my lab have been going at each other for like a week now about one of the Bongard-style problems in the set we're using to test concept induction, and I've been drafted to break the tie at our next meeting, which I'd rather not do blind. The setup, roughly: each problem is twelve little hand-drawn panels, six on the left, six on the right, and the solver has to say what separates them. In this one, every left panel is a polygon with exactly one edge drawn thicker, every right panel has two thick edges — that was our intended rule. But we drew the left ones so they all happen to be convex and the right ones concave, without noticing, and now both rules fit all twelve panels perfectly. So call it convex-vs-concave vs odd-vs-even thick edges, and nothing in the panels rules either one out.

Labmate A says the problem is junk and has to be cut: if two rules fit every example and disagree about new panels, the thing has no determinate answer, and whatever "solution" a model gives is just an artifact of which rule it happened to reach first.

Labmate B says that's backwards — every problem of this kind is like that, you can always gerrymander some rule that fits the training cases, and the real question is which rule a reasoner prefers and why, so the problem is a perfectly good probe of inductive bias rather than a bug.

A comes back: fine, then it isn't testing concept formation, it's testing which of two equally good rules the model's architecture happens to favor — a fact about the model, not about the concept. And you can't even score it, because odd-vs-even isn't more wrong than convex-vs-concave; nothing in the panels picks one out.

B: there you go again, you're assuming "no unique rule from the data alone" means "no right answer," which is only true if you think concepts are supposed to be read off the data with no prior at all. Nobody believes that. Humans get the intended reading instantly and don't even see the other one — that asymmetry is the phenomenon, and the problem measures it.

A: or it measures which reading we accidentally baked in, i.e. which one our own prior favors, and then we're just scoring the model against our blind spot.

I genuinely don't know how to call this one. Can you sort out who's right about what — is A right that the problem is defective, or B right that this is normal and fine — tell us what we should actually do with the problem, and say where each of them is missing something?
