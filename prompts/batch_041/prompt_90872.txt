I'm running a 90-minute session on social choice for a grad seminar, about 14 people, mostly philosophy and politics students with maybe two economists. I want an in-class game that makes Harsanyi's aggregation theorem land as a trap they walk into themselves rather than a formula I put on the board. What I want them to feel: they accept three things that each seem obviously fine alone — that any one person's preferences over gambles can be scored by an expected-utility number, that the group's ranking of gambles should follow those same expected-utility rules, and that if literally everyone prefers A to B then the group does too — and then discover that together those three already force the group's ranking to be nothing but a weighted sum of everyone's personal scores, so that the moment you add the veil-of-ignorance move (you don't know which person you'll be, and you treat each identity as equally likely) you've bought plain utilitarianism without adding a single extra premise.

Design it so they build this out of their own choices. My instinct is two phases: first each person gets a character with a stipulated utility scale over a few concrete outcomes and ranks some lotteries over social states selfishly; then, before anyone learns which character they'll actually be, the group has to agree on one rule for ranking lotteries that (a) can accommodate everyone's phase-one rankings whenever those are unanimous and (b) stays put when you break a compound gamble into its parts. The facilitator then audits that rule against a few probe lotteries and shows the only thing left standing is a weighted sum, with the weights set by how likely each identity is. Phase three, the sting: apply their own rule to a case where a big aggregate gain rides on one person getting crushed, and let them watch it come out utilitarian. Then a debrief where they have to say which of the three premises they'd rather give up and what each retreat costs — Rawls giving up the equal-probability picture, somebody else giving up expected-utility bookkeeping on the group, somebody else refusing to score peoples preferences at all.

Give me the actual materials (character sheets, lottery cards, how to make the stakes real but trivial — candy, coffee money, who gets the good chair), a run-of-show that fits 90 minutes, and what predictably goes wrong the first time: my guess is one student refuses to give consistent lottery answers and breaks the audit, and another objects early that nobody is actually assigned an identity at random so the whole vNM thing doesn't apply — which is the Rawls point arriving before I want it, so tell me how to park it and keep going. And be honest about what the game can't do: it can't prove anything, the utilities are stipulated by me rather than read off real people, and the equal-probability step gets smuggled in by the mechanics — so I have to supply the caveat that all of it is conditional on axioms nobody is forced to accept.
