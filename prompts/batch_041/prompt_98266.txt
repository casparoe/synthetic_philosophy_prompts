I'm collecting fiction that teaches philosophy by ambush, so write me a short story engineered to make the Knobe effect happen to the reader instead of just explaining it. In case its fuzzy: a chairman is told a new program will raise profits and, as a side effect, harm the environment; he says he doesn't care about the environment, he just wants the profits, and goes ahead — and nearly everyone says he harmed the environment on purpose. Swap in a good side effect (profits, plus the river gets cleaner) with the same indifference, and people get much stingier about saying he cleaned the river on purpose. The "on purpose" verdict tracks the badness, not the guy's psychology.

So give me a story with a single character and one decision that plays out in two mirrored halves — same setting, same "I only care about the one thing" line, same shrug at the side effect — except in the first half the side effect is a harm and in the second its a benefit. Make the two halves as close to word-for-word identical as you can, keep them back to back, and don't let the narrator nudge us toward a verdict; the "did he do it on purpose?" question should come out of someone's mouth in the dialogue. Around 400 words, and make it read like a real scene, not a lab vignette.
