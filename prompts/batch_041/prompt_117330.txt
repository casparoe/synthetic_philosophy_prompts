I write about science and ideas for a weekly, and I'm putting together a piece on the Euthyphro dilemma — specifically on whether the most sophisticated contemporary defences of divine command theory survive a generalisation of the dilemma that I have not seen put in quite this way, and which I would rather test in dialogue than in my own prose. The familiar dilemma, as Socrates puts it to Euthyphro, asks whether the holy is loved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is loved; in the monotheistic key the question is whether God commands what is good because it is good, or whether it is good because God commands it. The defence I want pressure-tested is the one that says the dilemma assumes God's will and God's goodness can come apart, whereas on the best view they cannot: the commands flow from a nature that is necessarily good, so they are neither arbitrary nor answerable to a standard outside God. My objection is this. If that defence works, it seems to work by identifying the goodness of an act with its being commanded by God — one property under two descriptions. But an identification of that shape appears to be available to any theory that grounds the good in someone's will or some community's practice: the good simply is what the sovereign wills, or what a settled tradition ratifies, under a second description. So either the identity move rescues every theory of that form, in which case it cannot be what distinguishes the theistic one, or it rescues none of them, including the theistic one. I want a dialogue between two philosophers who take this seriously and who are each other's equals — a defender of the nature-based divine command theory, and a critic who thinks the generality point is fatal — in which they engage each other's actual moves rather than trading positions past one another.

The obvious first reply, that the sovereign's will is contingent and could have been otherwise while God's goodness is not, should be made and then genuinely tested; I want to see whether that asymmetry does any real work or whether it merely relocates the difficulty onto necessity, which a Platonist could claim without God. Keep the standard catalogue of replies to a minimum — I don't need the arbitrariness objection or the abhorrent-command problem rehearsed at length, and I certainly don't need citations or reading lists; I want the argument, not the literature. Let the exchange run to a dozen or so turns, with each philosopher answering the point just made, and let neither be a fool: the theist should get the strongest version of their view, and the critic should have to work for the conclusion. Close with a short editorial note in your own voice saying which side, if either, is left with the harder remaining task, and naming the single question I should put to each of them if I get them on the phone. Write it in British English — I'm filing to a London desk, and the copy editor is a tyrant about these things. And please don't end on a handshake; I would rather have a real disagreement left standing than a synthesis nobody earned.
