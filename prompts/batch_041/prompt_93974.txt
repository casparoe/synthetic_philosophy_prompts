I have been asked to give this year's commencement address at the college where I teach, and I have spent a month failing to draft it. My research for the past dozen years has been on the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and I have come to accept most of the empirical core of that critique. What I have not accepted is the conclusion my colleagues draw from it — that because character does not show up in behavior the way we once assumed, there is nothing honest left to say to a graduating class.

The occasion: a lawn, several thousand people, a third of them parents and grandparents with no philosophical training; a scattering of my colleagues who know the literature and will be listening for whether I have quietly gone soft; and the graduates themselves, who have already sat through a week of speeches about passion and purpose. I have eleven minutes.

What I believe, and what the address must be consistent with:

- In the best-known version of the hurry experiment, students on their way to deliver a talk about the parable of the Good Samaritan walked past a man slumped in a doorway, and whether they stopped tracked how rushed the experimenters had made them feel far better than anything about their convictions. The wider pattern from the honesty and helpfulness studies is the same: someone who behaves well in one setting is often unremarkable in the next, and correlations between a single behavioral measure in one situation and behavior in another rarely rise much above .2 or .3.
- This is a genuine problem for one specific picture — character as a set of global, context-free dispositions that surface reliably whatever the circumstances, and that can be strengthened by exhortation the way a muscle is strengthened by repetition.
- But the same research tradition has a second half that commencement season never mentions. Within a person, the if-then patterns can be strikingly stable: one person grows more generous as the pressure rises, another collapses. Behavior aggregated across many occasions does predict. Settings differ enormously in how much room they leave for individual differences to show at all — a fire drill tells you almost nothing about anyone, an unstructured afternoon tells you a great deal. And much of what passes for character turns out to be situation selection: which rooms you enter, what you arrange in advance, how you build a life in which the decent thing is also the easy thing.
- The skill analogy survives in some form as well. A skill is not a guarantee of a fixed output independent of conditions; it is a capacity whose exercise depends on conditions, and which includes the know-how to manage them.

The address therefore has to do three things at once: satisfy the occasion's demand that the graduates leave with something to carry, say only what I believe, and avoid smuggling the global-trait picture back in under a new name. The last is the difficult one. I do not want to be the speaker who announces that character is not about who you are but about what you do, and thereby merely relabels the thing the evidence refuted.

Please write the address, about fourteen hundred words. Do not name or cite any of the research described above; the graduates should receive this as an observation about their own lives, not as a literature review. Avoid the standard moves — follow your passion, trust your conscience, be the same person in every room, become the person you want to be — not because they are clichés but because each of them assumes something I have just said I do not believe; if the address works, the audience should feel their absence without being able to name it. Include one moment where you voice the objection I will certainly hear from the parents — that I am telling their children character does not matter — and answer it without either capitulating or lecturing. End with advice that would be different if the global-trait picture were true: something that only makes sense given what I actually think.

Then, in a short note to me kept separate from the address itself, say which account of character the address committed me to, and identify the single sentence in it that I would most need to be able to defend if a graduate quoted it back to me in ten years.
