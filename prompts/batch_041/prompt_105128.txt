Value theory has a dispute that has run for decades without either side yielding — the problem of the "wrong kind of reasons" for a fitting-attitude analysis of value — and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than one more position staked out inside it.

The setup is familiar. If being good is analyzed as being a fitting object of a valuing attitude, then reasons divide into those that bear on the object and those that bear only on the attitude or its consequences. The stock illustration is an all-powerful agent who will punish you unless you admire him: you have reason to want to admire him, and perhaps reason to bring it about that you do, but nothing about him makes admiration correct. Those who accept the distinction say that only right-kind reasons can confer fittingness, and that wrong-kind reasons must therefore be reasons of another sort — reasons to want the attitude or to produce it, not reasons for it. Those who reject it say the maneuver either presupposes the notion it was meant to explain or collapses into a gerrymandered list, because every candidate criterion — the reason's source, its content, its direction of fit, the attitude's constitutive aim — appears to admit counterexamples. The exchange has settled into each side offering cases the other must rule marginal, and trading intuitions about which considerations are "really" reasons for the attitude.

Suppose a colleague proposes a sharper criterion: a consideration is a right-kind reason for an attitude just in case it bears on whether the attitude meets its own constitutive standard — admiration is correct when its object merits it, and the threat bears on nothing but your welfare, so it is wrong-kind by construction. The rejoinder writes itself: the criterion is circular, since "meets its constitutive standard" already means "is fitting," and the original problem was precisely to say what fittingness amounts to without presupposing it. My own hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about the extension of a predicate but two groups answering different questions under the single word "reason" — one asking what makes an attitude correct, the other asking what counts in favor of having it — and that the felt circularity is a symptom of that, not a refutation.

Test that hypothesis against the actual shape of the disagreement and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: verbal, a clash of starting intuitions, a substantive disagreement about the nature of fittingness wearing a terminological mask, or evidence that the founding question was ill-posed. If it is substantive, say what would move it — a distinction, a criterion that escapes the circularity charge, a formal result about the structure of fittingness, or a change of question. If it is not, explain why the participants cannot see that, and describe what a distinction that did the required work would have to look like. I don't want a survey of who said what; I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it.
