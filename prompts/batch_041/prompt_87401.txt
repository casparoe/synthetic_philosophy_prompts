Two nights ago I lost an argument — or at least failed to win one — with a friend who cuts documentaries for a living. She has spent the past year assembling a film about a famine, and she told me that the hardest part of her job is that ninety seconds of invented footage can make an audience weep, while the real thing, screened at length, mostly makes them look away. Then she pressed me: if the weeping at invented footage is the same weeping, the old puzzle about how we can be moved by what we know never happened isn't a puzzle at all, and if it isn't the same weeping, I owe her an account of the difference. I have been developing a paper on exactly that question, and I present it in ten days to a small interdisciplinary workshop, so what I want from you is not a defense of my view but a question-period preparation sheet. The room is mixed and I know roughly what each part of it thinks. Five or six analytic philosophers of mind and aesthetics: the strongest of them holds the make-believe line — that what we feel for fictions is not emotion proper but the pretend version of it, real enough as a feeling, with a merely imagined object — and two others who insist the real problem was never about belief in the fiction's existence but about the rationality of caring about what one knows to be invented. Three psychologists who run laboratory work on narrative engagement, measuring skin conductance and post-film mood, and who will be impatient with any distinction I cannot operationalize. A narratologist who thinks the entire debate is a philosopher's artifact, produced by treating texts as though they arrived with a frame around them. And a historian of the novel who will want to know what my account says about the eighteenth-century reader who wept over a character in a letter-novel and then wrote to the author as though the character were a person.

My thesis is this. The axis everyone argues along — real versus unreal, believed versus merely entertained — is the wrong one. What matters is the difference between affect that is extinguished the moment the frame is acknowledged and affect that crosses the frame: the flinch at the jump and the sob at the deathbed, which stop when the lights come up, versus the mood that persists for a day afterwards, the way a book quietly rearranges what one notices in the actual world, and the responses one would not retract on being reminded that none of it happened. Call the first frame-bound and the second frame-crossing. I claim that frame-bound affect is not an emotion in the full sense but a rehearsal response that commandeers the machinery of emotion without supplying it an object, and that the make-believe account is essentially right about it. Frame-crossing affect, by contrast, is a genuine emotion with a genuine object — but the object is not the character and not the invented event; those are anchors, vivid particulars that make some real feature of the world salient (that children die of preventable causes; that loyalty can cost a person everything). Since that object is real, no paradox arises for it. On my account the paradox was never about fiction at all; it was about a class of emotions whose object has been misidentified because the anchor is more vivid than the object.

Prepare me for what this audience will actually say. Give me the six or eight objections I will really get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, each phrased the way that particular objector would put it — the psychologists' version of the operationalization worry is not the make-believe theorist's version of it — with a reply I can deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first thirty seconds, before anyone has to drag it out of me. Tell me the one reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the hour. And tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks: I have a suspicion about what it is, and I would rather hear it from you now than from the historian.
