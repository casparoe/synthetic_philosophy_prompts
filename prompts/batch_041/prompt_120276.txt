I'm a high school student finishing a philosophy elective, and my final paper is on the ethics of selecting traits in future children. I understand the broad shape of the position usually called procreative beneficence, and the liberal-eugenic program built on top of it: that when parents are deciding which of the possible children they might bring into existence, they have reason to choose the one whose life is expected to go best, that this is not merely a permission but something close to a requirement, that it reaches past disease and disability into ordinary traits, and that the standard is the future child's own expected well-being rather than any improvement of the species or the gene pool. I also know the usual objections: the non-identity problem, the disability-rights complaint about what such reasoning expresses, the worry that a best possible life is not a well-defined target, and the attempt to draw a principled line between curing disease and enhancing. What I want from you is not a summary of that literature.

I have spent years breeding competitive Pokémon — natures, IVs, egg moves, hidden abilities, and the recurring question of whether one more batch of eggs is worth the hours it costs to hatch them. That is the vocabulary I actually think in, so I want the procreative-beneficence position restated in it, as faithfully as you can manage. Please take the role of a philosopher of bioethics who also knows breeding mechanics well enough not to be sloppy about them; think carefully before writing, and open with a short brainstorming section in which you list the structural features of the position — the choice set, the standard of comparison, whose interests count, the modal status of the children who are not chosen — and for each one ask whether the breeding framework has anything to match it. Then give me the systematic translation, element by element: what the position looks like in breeding terms, and for each element a clear verdict on whether it carries over cleanly, carries over only with distortion, or has no expression at all.

Finish with the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Tell me what in the philosophical position has no counterpart in the breeding framework, and for each item say whether the absence is a limitation of my vocabulary — a game has no persons, no interests, no claims, so of course it cannot say certain things — or a sign that the original position was less determinate than it appears and quietly borrowed something from ordinary moral thought that it cannot generate on its own. I am especially interested in three things: the non-identity material, the difference between a child who is merely not disadvantaged and the best child available, and the way the breeding frame treats an unhatched egg as a set of stats — I want to know whether that last one is a distortion of the philosophy or an uncomfortably accurate picture of it. I would also like you to say whether the framework can express the difference between one breeder's private choices and a competitive community whose shared tier lists quietly become a standard everyone breeds toward, since that seems to be where the liberal part of liberal eugenics does its work. Please do not assume throughout that the philosophical position is correct and the game vocabulary merely deficient; if the translation exposes a gap in the position, say so plainly.
