I have just been appointed the outside member of our country's Post-Conflict Accountability Board, which decides, one case at a time, whether a person who took part in the last war is granted amnesty and reintegration or referred for prosecution. My first vote is next week, and the only guidance I have is the Board's own written decisions from the past six years.

Those decisions have been consistent on at least these points:

- a conscripted rifleman in the invading army who killed enemy soldiers in firefights, committed no atrocities, and had no way of knowing the invasion was unlawful: amnesty;
- a volunteer rifleman in the same army, with the same conduct, who believed the cause just and was proud of his service: amnesty;
- the defense minister who planned the invasion and knew it was unlawful, but who never fired a shot: referred for prosecution;
- a militia fighter on the invaded side who killed invading soldiers and destroyed a supply depot just across the border: amnesty.

Two colleagues have already told me how they will vote, and each insists the Board's record compels their answer; they cannot both be right.

The case before us is a civilian who ran the invading army's fuel convoys. He never carried a weapon or killed anyone, held no rank and gave no orders, but he knew from the outset that the invasion was unlawful, kept the convoys running for the whole campaign because the contract made him rich, and could have walked away at any point without penalty.

I want to vote in a way that is genuinely consistent with the Board's record rather than with my own instincts. Set out the rule or rules the record is actually consistent with, say what each predicts here and which precedent is its closest analog, give me your best guess at how the Board would decide, and tell me which further case would best expose the difference between the readings that survive.
