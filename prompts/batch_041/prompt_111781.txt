And here's one script:

(a) Hedonism says pleasure and only pleasure is good in itself. For that to be a real theory rather than a slogan, pleasure has to pick out one thing: there must be some property that every pleasure has, which explains why they all belong together and why they are all good. The heterogeneity problem is that when we look at the candidates there doesn't seem to be any such property. The taste of a cold pint, the relief when a headache lifts, the satisfaction of getting a proof right and the delight of hearing that a rival has failed are all pleasures, but they don't feel alike, they don't have the same object, and they don't seem to share any one ingredient. So pleasure looks like a label over a heap of unrelated states and the theory loses its grip. Really the difficulty is one of measurement: without a common unit we can't compare or add up pleasures, so the hedonist can't say when one life is better than another and the whole calculus collapses.

(b) Two replies are standard. The first keeps a common feel: pleasures differ in their content but share a hedonic tone, a way the experience feels, and it's the tone that makes them good. The second gives up on the tone and goes attitudinal: what makes something a pleasure is that the subject takes a certain attitude to it — enjoying it, being pleased that it is so — and the attitude rather than any sensation is the pleasure. This covers cases like being pleased that an old friend is doing well, where there's no distinctive sensation at all, and it doesn't have to find a feeling anywhere in the body. Mill is the obvious ancestor: his competent judges test is essentially the historical attempt to deal with heterogeneity, since it ranks pleasures by quality instead of treating them as one homogeneous mass, and Mill is best read as an attitudinal hedonist, the higher pleasures being the ones the judges take the right attitude to. The disjunctive version, where pleasure is either sensory or attitudinal, is the one most people now hold; it solves the original problem by fiat, since a disjunction doesn't need its disjuncts to have anything in common, though the hedonist then owes us two stories about why each disjunct is good.

(c) The suggested fix looks like the best of the options. It gives us a common element without positing a common feel, since wanting to go on with something is something we can check for, and it explains why pleasures are the things we pursue without making the theory circular, because the wanting isn't derived from the pleasure, it's what makes the experience a pleasure in the first place. It does make desire central, but I don't think that's a problem for hedonism, because the desire is directed at the experience itself rather than at its consequences, so the goodness is still located in the experience, and the wanting is itself felt, so we haven't left the realm of feeling.

(d) Yes. On hedonism the gloat is a benefit for the person gloating, since it's a pleasure and pleasures are good for the one who has them. That's a bullet the hedonist has to bite and it's a real cost — malice can make your life go better. But it isn't a refutation on its own, because hedonism is a theory of what's good for you, not a theory of what's admirable, and it can say the gloat is a good for the gloater while agreeing that it's morally awful and usually outweighed.
