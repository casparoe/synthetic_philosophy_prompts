I'm working through Jevons's Theory of Political Economy (first edition 1871), and I've hit a passage near the start of the chapter on utility that I can't get to sit still. He writes: "PLEASURE and pain are undoubtedly the ultimate objects of the Calculus of Economics. To satisfy our wants to the utmost with the least effort—to procure the greatest amount of what is desirable at the expense of the least that is undesirable—in other words, to maximise pleasure, is the problem of Economics." Shortly afterward he says that "In the science of Economics we treat men not as they ought to be, but as they are," that anything an individual desires and labours for "must be assumed to possess for him utility," and, in glossing Bentham's definition, that this holds "provided that the will or inclination of the person immediately concerned is taken as the sole criterion, for the time, of what is or is not useful."

My difficulty is that these two stretches of text pull in opposite directions. The first sentence reads like a single maximisation — one quantity of pleasure to be made as large as possible — which would seem to require comparing one person's pleasure against another's. But the definitions that follow make each person's own inclination the sole criterion of usefulness for that person, which looks like it rules out exactly that comparison. He then builds the theory of exchange out of each trader's own schedule of utilities, as though no common measure across minds were needed anywhere. So I want a close reading rather than a general survey: is "pleasure" in the opening sentence a single homogeneous quantity, or a shorthand for a family of quantities, one per person? Does calling economics a "Calculus of Pleasure and Pain" commit Jevons to interpersonal comparisons, or is the phrase carrying less weight than it appears to? And if the latter, what becomes of the stated "problem of Economics" — whose pleasure is being maximised, and by what operation? If Jevons is best read as never needing interpersonal comparisons, say so and show me where the appearance to the contrary comes from.
