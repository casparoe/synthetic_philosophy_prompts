Construct a thought experiment in which the contractualism associated with T. M. Scanlon—the view that assesses a principle by asking whether any individual could reasonably reject it, and that on its standard reading refuses to aggregate complaints across persons—and a complaint-aggregating version of contractualism—one that adds up the complaints of different individuals to decide whether a principle could be reasonably rejected—deliver opposite verdicts about the same standing institutional policy, while a third, mixed version that permits aggregation only among complaints of the same kind, and treats complaints of different kinds as non-aggregable, delivers a verdict different from both. The setting must be a realistic institution—a hospital, a university, a city agency, or a workplace—not a trolley, a transplant, or a one-off emergency rescue. Keep the case to one paragraph. The policy must impose a modest burden on each of a large number of people in order to avert a severe burden on a smaller number, and the numbers must be arranged so that no single burdened person has a complaint stronger than the complaint of any single person who would be spared, even though the cumulative burden on the many is plainly greater. The policy should also be one that a rule-consequentialist would plausibly endorse, so that the anti-aggregation verdict does not rest on the policy being obviously bad. The burdens should differ in kind—for example, material welfare for the many and a symbolic or relational good for the few—so that the question of aggregation turns partly on commensurability. After the case, do all of the following: (1) state the policy, the affected parties, and the burdens precisely; (2) explain why a Scanlonian who refuses to aggregate complaints across persons must reject the policy; (3) explain why a contractualist who aggregates complaints must accept it; (4) explain why the same-kind aggregation view gives its own verdict; (5) identify the exact feature of the case that makes the three verdicts come apart, and say what happens if that feature is removed; (6) say how a defender of the anti-aggregation reading would try to show that the case is not really a counterexample. Begin with the case itself, with no preface or commentary, and then give the demonstration.
