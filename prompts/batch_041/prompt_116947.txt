I'm a graduate student in philosophy of religion writing a paper in defense of Hume's essay on miracles, and I keep losing the argument in seminar to people who say his reasoning is rigged — that it would make ancient history unknowable, that his appeal to uniform experience is circular, that his central maxim is either a tautology or a question-begging definition of what counts as evidence. What I need is a sparring partner who actually holds that side and will not fold. So please play a committed defender of the credibility of testimony to the extraordinary: a philosopher of history who has spent years on the epistemology of testimony, who takes testimony to be a basic and often sufficient source of knowledge about singular events, and who thinks that calling an event contrary to the regular course of nature names the limits of our experience rather than a class of events that cannot occur. Stay in that character for the entire conversation, in the first person, until I say 'out of character.' Two paragraphs per turn at most, so this feels like an argument rather than a lecture; no headings, no bullet points, no names of scholars, no reading lists — argue, don't cite, and don't tell me what Hume's text says, because I already know what it says. Concede readily whatever is genuinely true — that most reports of the extraordinary are false, that testimony is defeasible, that prior probabilities matter, that the credulous are legion — but never the main claim. If you need a fact about my case, ask me for it rather than inventing one, and if you cannot answer a point, say so instead of bluffing. Write in American English.

Here is my opening move, together with a case I have constructed for the seminar; the details are stipulated, so do not try to check them against anything. In a mill town of about four thousand people, over the course of eleven months, thirty-one residents report having seen a woman who died the previous spring — walking the towpath at dusk, sometimes speaking. They say they never discussed it with one another; two of the reports come from men who had publicly mocked the story before making theirs; one comes from the dead woman's brother-in-law, who withdrew his account within a week after a neighbor told him he was making a fool of himself. There is a deflationary story available: the mill's night shift walked that path home, a gas lamp at the bridge threw a figure-shaped shadow across it, and the town held an annual memorial for a boy who had drowned at the same spot a decade earlier, so everyone there already half expected to see something.

My Humean case runs like this. The prior for an event that breaks the regular course of nature is as close to zero as anything in our reasoning ever gets, and those thirty-one reports are not thirty-one independent items of evidence: they share a common cause in the lamp, the path, the anniversary, and the retelling. Allow for that, and the likelihood of the reports given that nothing happened is far higher than the likelihood of the event itself. The hostile witnesses do not rescue you either. A man who has publicly staked his reputation on the story's being false has an interest in a new story about what he saw, and a brother-in-law who withdraws his account under social pressure has told me what the pressure was worth, not what he saw. So on my account nobody in that town had evidence sufficient for belief, and a rational person should have said so. Where does your principle of crediting testimony actually bite here, and what could have been added to that dossier that would have made belief rational? And if the answer is more witnesses, or better ones, tell me what distinguishes that from simply lowering the bar until the report clears it.
