I am trying to get clear about the ethics of inequality, and I want the specification before the verdicts. So: write out the desiderata that any adequate theory of distributive justice in this area — the family of views, sometimes called luck egalitarianism, on which what makes a distributive inequality unjust is that it traces to unchosen circumstance rather than to choice — would have to satisfy.

Give the requirements as a list. For each, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as either a condition the theory cannot fail without ceasing to be a candidate at all, or an insistence of one camp within the family. I have in mind the cases on which such theories are usually tested: the person born with a costly disability; the person whose expensive tastes were chosen and the person whose expensive tastes were not; the person who could work and prefers not to; the diligent person of modest talent; the gambler who loses a fair bet; the person whose disadvantage is the upshot of other people's choices to exclude them; and, the case I care most about, the person whose willingness to run risks, and hence whose eventual bad luck, was itself something they never chose. That last case seems to me to threaten the enterprise, so say whether a theory can keep a robust notion of responsibility for one's choices without claiming that people author their own characters, and what it would have to give up in order to do so.

Two constraints on the list. First, assume the theory is meant to guide what a liberal state owes its citizens, and that the state can observe what people do and what they end up with but not whether what they did was genuinely their own doing; any requirement you state has to be one the theory can state without asking officials to read minds. Second, tell me what the theory owes to those whom their own choices leave badly off, since I suspect that the requirement of responsibility and the requirement that no one be abandoned pull against each other, and I want to know whether that tension is real or only rhetorical.

Finally, say which single requirement you would expect to do the most work in ruling out otherwise attractive theories, and flag any pair of requirements that I should expect to be jointly unsatisfiable; if the list cannot be satisfied as a whole, that is itself the most useful result I could get from it. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them rather than on scoring the familiar contenders; I will do the scoring once I have the list.
