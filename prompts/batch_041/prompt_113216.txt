I am a novelist at work on a book about a citizens' assembly: a few dozen people chosen by lot, given briefing materials vetted for balance by a body appointed by whoever commissioned the exercise, seated in small groups with trained facilitators, asked to set aside their prior positions and to remain open to changing their minds, and finally asked to publish a recommendation on some contested question, usually with the vote breakdown attached. In the book's fictional country the recommendation binds no one; the government that commissioned the assembly may ignore it, and sometimes does. What puzzles me is that everyone in the story treats the assembly's report as a weightier expression of public judgment than a referendum with many times the turnout, and that nobody involved claims the members are wiser or better informed than anyone else, only that the conditions under which they decided were better. I want a character of mine to be able to articulate what the practice is committed to, and another character to attack it. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing paragraph: what theory of political authority the practice presupposes; what it presupposes about the nature of people's opinions; which features of the design the presupposed theory strictly requires and which merely suit it; which rival accounts of legitimacy it quietly rules out; and what would have to be redesigned, in the selection, the facilitation, the advisory status, or the publicity of the vote, if we replaced the presupposed theory with straightforward preference aggregation.
