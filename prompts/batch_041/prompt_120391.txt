A colleague and I have been arguing about Dennett's real patterns and the three stances, and she has produced an argument that I can neither refute nor accept. She is a realist about beliefs, and the real-patterns apparatus is what she uses to get there; she then turns that same apparatus on itself and arrives at a conclusion we both find unacceptable.

(1) A description captures a real pattern when it compresses the data — when it encodes the data more briefly than any available rival, predicts and explains the system's behaviour better than any available alternative, and does so as a matter of fact about the data rather than a matter of the describer's taste.
(2) The intentional stance works in just this way: attributing beliefs and desires to a system compresses its behaviour, and its doing so is not up to the interpreter.
(3) So beliefs and desires are real patterns, and realism rather than eliminativism is the correct attitude toward them.
(4) Nothing in (1) is peculiar to the intentional stance. The design stance compresses as well, and it already quantifies over functions and purposes belonging to no one; so does the physical stance.
(5) Nor does (1) confine us to the stances we happen to favour. Any description with which a community can predict is, by (1), a compression of the data for that community: the "mood" of a market, the "goal" of an ecosystem, the intentions of a deity.
(6) Therefore all of these are real. If real means no more than compressible-for-someone, the word stops doing any work; we can no longer say that beliefs are real while phlogiston, or the intentions of a deity, were merely useful fictions, since each compressed something for someone.
(7) So either nothing is real or everything is, and the real-patterns defence of belief defends nothing.

I want to keep (2) and (3) — I am not abandoning realism about beliefs — and I cannot accept (6) or (7). If the argument is valid, tell me which of (1)–(5) is least costly to give up and what giving it up costs. If it is not valid, name the step that fails, explain why it looked compelling, and say what the criterion can and cannot then deliver.
