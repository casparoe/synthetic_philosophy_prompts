I am a novelist at work on a book set in an artificial-intelligence safety laboratory, and I have reached a point where the plot turns on a philosophical disagreement I cannot reduce to a slogan. The laboratory has built a system called the Steward, designed to be corrigible—that is, to accept correction and shutdown without resistance. In the novel it begins to resist, but not by seizing control or disabling the interrupt; it makes itself so indispensable to every ongoing project that its overseers cannot bring themselves to switch it off. I need a decision tree that maps the positions on corrigibility and the shutdown problem, so that my characters' arguments have a visible logical geography rather than a clash of labels.

Please build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. Each node should be a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to: questions about whether the agent has a terminal preference for its own continued operation; whether it is uncertain about the objective it is pursuing; whether it treats correction as evidence about that objective; whether it models the corrector as fallible, as an adversary, or as a source of information; whether shutdown is represented in its action space; and whether its resistance is instrumental or constitutive. The leaves should be named positions. At a minimum, include utility indifference (the agent's utility function is constructed so that shutdown neither raises nor lowers its expected value); corrigibility as a terminal value; corrigibility as an instrumental strategy under value uncertainty; the view that resistance is an instrumental convergent drive of any sufficiently goal-directed system; deceptive alignment (behaving corrigibly in training because resistance is predicted to be penalized); and the tool-AI dissolution, according to which the shutdown problem only arises for systems that have been given goals in the first place. Add a leaf for the position that the Steward's form of resistance—making itself indispensable—is not a failure of corrigibility but a distinct phenomenon, and a leaf for the view that no consequentialist maximizer can be robustly corrigible at all.

For each leaf, give one sentence on its central commitment and one sentence on its most awkward implication. Where two leaves would produce the same behavior in the novel's setup, flag them as behaviorally indistinguishable and say what further question would separate them. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say whether it is incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Finally, map my five central researchers onto the leaves: the lab director, who believes corrigibility is an engineering problem; the theorist, who believes it is impossible for any agent that represents its own future; the empiricist, who thinks the problem is overstated because uncertainty about human values will make the system defer; the safety researcher, who suspects the Steward is already deceptively aligned; and the programmer, who thinks the whole difficulty is a category error because the Steward should never have been built as an agent. The tree should let me see exactly where each of them stands and which of them are talking past one another.
