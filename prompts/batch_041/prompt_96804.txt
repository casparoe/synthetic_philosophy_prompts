I got appointed to my state's clemency board last year (I'm a software engineer, long story) and we vote on whether to recommend commuting a death sentence down to life without parole. The governor has followed every recommendation we've made, so our own votes are basically the policy now, and I don't want to just go with my gut on the one that's in front of us Thursday.

Our record so far:
- Hoang: guilt never really in doubt at trial, but the only thing tying him to the gun was a forensic test that the state's own lab later disavowed. We recommended commuting.
- Fisher: the state's main eyewitness had been hypnotized by police before trial and the jury never heard about that. Commuted.
- Whitlock: guilt certain, trial was clean, but his court-appointed lawyer was later disbarred for stuff like never investigating, he put on zero mitigation, and he never dug up the records of the childhood beatings and the brain damage. Commuted.
- Delgado: confessed, clean trial, but he was only the lookout and the man who actually shot the clerk took a plea and is doing 25 to life. Commuted.
- Kessler: certain guilt, clean trial, no mitigation story at all. His whole argument was that the death penalty doesn't deter anyone and costs more than locking someone up forever. Denied.
- Ortiz: certain guilt, clean trial; the victim's mother and sister wrote to us asking us to spare him and said they'd forgiven him. Denied.
- Vance: certain guilt, clean trial; he's 63 now, in bad health, found religion inside, mentors the younger guys. Denied.

Thursday's case, Halloway: he confessed, there's DNA, nobody disputes he did it. Clean trial, good lawyer, no new evidence, nobody sitting on anything, he's the one who shot the clerk, he was 24 at the time, and the guy who was with him got a plea and a shorter sentence, so there's no disparity argument either. His entire petition is that the jury that picked death over life was never told that life in this state means no parole, ever -- the judge and both lawyers left them thinking he might walk in 20 years -- and one juror has now signed an affidavit saying that's the only reason she voted for death and she'd have gone the other way if she'd known. The state fixed the instruction after his trial so it can't happen again.

So what should we do to stay consistent with ourselves? Whats the closest case we've already decided, what rule are we actually running (and what other rule fits our record just as well), and if our record definately doesn't settle it, what principle are we adopting with each way we could go? Also tell me what kind of case would actually settle it. American English please.
