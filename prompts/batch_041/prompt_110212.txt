I'm a third-year student in a machine learning ethics seminar, and for next week's response paper I have to stage a disagreement rather than summarise one. Please write a dialogue between two members of a university committee that is deciding whether to use a model to screen applicants for a support programme. One of them treats the impossibility results about group fairness as the end of the story: unless the base rates are equal or the predictor is perfect, you cannot have the score mean the same thing in each group — the same probability of the outcome given the score — and also have equal false positive and false negative rates across groups, so the committee is really just choosing whose errors it would rather make, and every technical fix is a value judgement wearing a lab coat. The other thinks that is a counsel of despair, because the impossibility is conditional on a fixed feature set and a weak predictor: collect better data, and allow the final decision to be randomised, and you can buy back each of the things you were told to trade off, so the trade-off is an engineering problem that shrinks as the model improves rather than a permanent limit. Let them argue. Have the second speaker pressed on whether the two desiderata can be had together rather than one at a time, and have the first pressed on whether the impossibility claim survives once the label is itself produced by the institution doing the predicting — the committee's decisions partly determine who succeeds later, so the base rates are not a fixed fact about the world. Both should be sharp, and neither should be a fool. I don't want a list of known results; I want them answering each other's actual moves. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
