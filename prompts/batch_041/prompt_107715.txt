I keep running into a fairness rule in two-person negotiations and I don't know if it has a name. The rule is: figure out the best each side could possibly get if the other side went along, then give each side the same fraction of that best-case amount. So if I could at most get 100 and you could at most get 50, we split whatever is actually on the table in a 2:1 ratio, no matter how big or small the total pie is. It's not "split the difference" and it's not the rule where you maximize the product of what each side gains over their fallback — under that product rule, if your outside option improves, my share can actually go down, which seems wrong, whereas under the fraction rule my share as a fraction of my best case stays put. But I'm not sure the fraction rule is even well-defined when the best-case amounts depend on what counts as the fallback, or when the feasible set isn't a nice convex blob. Does this rule have an established name? And if so, how exactly is it different from the product rule and from equal-split? I care less about the label than about the precise boundaries: what does the rule assume about the disagreement point, what happens when the best-case amounts change because the other player's options change, and are there cases where the rule gives an answer that isn't on the efficient frontier? Also, is there a version for more than two parties, or does it fall apart? I've tried to work out a seperate example with three people and got confused about whether the ratio should be to each person's own max or to some joint max.
