My town's historical society voluntold me into writing the official centennial book — 150 pages, glossy, sold at the fall fair — and I've got two people pulling me in opposite directions. The retired teacher who's "advising" (really: hovering, and he controls the small budget the society approved) keeps saying the book has to be about the founders, the mill, the two fires, the war dead, the mayors — the stuff already carved on the plaque and sitting in the old newspaper clippings — because that's what a town history is and that's what people will buy. Meanwhile I've spent four months in the library basement with the actual stuff nobody's touched: a midwife's daybook from the 1890s where she bills in eggs and notes who never paid, a bundle of small-claims complaints over a stolen pig and a fence moved two feet, a boarding-house ledger, that kind of thing. This is the material that actually moves me and I think it says more about how people here lived than another chapter on the mill owners. But here's what I can't get past: my advisor says a book built on that is just a scrapbook, that you can't say anything about a whole town or a whole era from one midwife and a stolen pig — that it's anecdote, not history, and that at least the mayors and the fires are "representative" because they're what everyone lived through. And honestly maybe he's half right? When I read a book that does what I want to do, it feels like one small case is somehow standing in for something enormous, and I can never tell whether that's a real inference or just the author's writing making me feel that way.

It gets worse. There's one entry in the midwife's daybook that stopped me cold — a woman who came to her at night, something awful, and she wrote a single line about it and never mentioned it again. I want to open the whole book with that entry. My advisor says that's the exact mistake: the interesting case is by definition the unusual one, so leading with it distorts the town and proves his point about scrapbooks. But my gut says the opposite — that it's precisely because it's a one-off that you learn what the everyday was braced against.

So what I want from you is something I can actually act on, not a pep talk. Help me decide: is his claim that the big public events are "representative" actually true, or is that word doing something sneaky — representative of what, and to whom? Can the small basement case really speak for a whole era, and if so, what has to be true of that case for the inference to hold — what makes it the right kind of small case rather than just a fluke? Should I open with the shocking entry or bury it, and does the answer change depending on whether I'm trying to describe the town, explain it, or argue with the version everyone already believes? And at the next meeting, how do I answer him without just sounding like I'm defending a scrapbook — give me the strongest version of his objection and tell me whether it survives. I'm a school nurse, by the way, not a historian, so don't assume I know the jargon.
