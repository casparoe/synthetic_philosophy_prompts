I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of markets — eighteen students, one 90-minute session, no economics prerequisites — and I want to stage the no-trade theorem so that the room runs into it before I ever name it. In the Milgrom–Stokey form the result says roughly this: if traders share a common prior, if the initial allocation is already ex ante efficient, if there are no noise traders or other non-rational interference, and if the whole informational structure of the market is common knowledge, then traders will not trade — not even the ones holding genuinely valuable private information — because any offer is itself a public signal about the offerer's private information, and everyone will draw the inference in time to refuse. I do not want to present this as a proposition. I want eighteen people to walk into it.

What they should come away seeing: first, that their own willingness to accept a trade was, in the moment, the very evidence that should have stopped them; second, that the theorem does not say trade is irrational but that any informational trade must violate one of its hypotheses, so that naming which hypothesis you violated is the entire content of the result; third, that this stands in unresolved tension with the other thing I teach about markets — Hayek's picture of prices as an aggregator of knowledge that no single participant possesses — and with the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox that a fully revealing price leaves no one a reason to gather the information it reveals.

Design the activity with the following constraints in mind. The common prior has to be genuinely credible: every participant must know the data-generating process and know that everyone knows it, so that when they trade they cannot excuse themselves by saying they simply disagreed about the odds. Participants must hold private signals, and the rules must guarantee that a person's willingness to trade at a given price carries real information about their signal, so that accepting is an act of ignoring an inference rather than merely taking a gamble. The activity must include a second phase in which an exogenous, non-informational reason to trade appears — a liquidity need, a hedging motive, a fee, a forced reallocation — so that participants feel the difference between the trade the theorem forbids and the trade it permits; the contrast, not either phase alone, is the point. At least one participant who is correct about the hidden state should still lose points, because the price moved to absorb their information; the design should make that outcome visible and, in hindsight, unsurprising. There is to be no lecture before the game — the name and the statement of the theorem come only in the debrief. Give me rules, materials, a payoff scheme in points that needs no real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the theorem's hypotheses — common prior, common knowledge of rationality, ex ante efficient allocation, absence of noise traders — and for each episode of trade ask the participants to say which hypothesis they implicitly treated as false. Have them confront the three escape hatches: differing priors, which under a common prior would make them irrational rather than merely uninformed; a failure of common knowledge of rationality, each suspecting the others of overconfidence or of being noise; and the presence of genuine non-informational motives. Be honest that only the last two let them keep calling themselves rational. Bring out the lemons-style inversion as well, that a counterparty's eagerness to sell is itself bad news about what is being sold. If time permits, pivot to the Myerson–Satterthwaite result to show that no-trade has a second and independent source in bilateral bargaining under private values, so that the room does not leave believing it has understood why markets trade from a single theorem.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the failure modes I should pre-empt in the rules rather than discover live — and which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will therefore have to supply in my own voice. I expect the honest answer to include that a one-shot classroom game can never establish the common knowledge of rationality the theorem requires, since the game probably works only because that common knowledge is absent; that linear points cannot represent the risk-sharing route to mutually beneficial trade; and that the activity will produce anecdotes rather than an equilibrium. Tell me what else I am missing.
