My friend and I just had a stupidly long arguement about the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. Quick version so we're on the same page: if you've got 3+ outcomes, voters can rank anything any way, and the rule is strategy-proof (no voter can ever do better by lying about their ranking, no matter what the others say), then the rule has to be dictatorial (one fixed voter's top choice always wins). My friend says that's basically the whole game, and every supposed loophole is just changing the question. I think some loopholes actually work. Can you rate each of these on 0–10 for how much it really escapes the theorem's punch -- 10 = you get a genuine non-dictatorial, strategy-proof-ish rule over 3+ outcomes, 0 = the theorem still bites exactly as hard, just with extra steps -- and then rank them from most to least legit? One sentence per item on why it lands where it does.

- Restrict preferences to single-peaked along one left-right line (so the median voter rule works).
- Restrict to only two outcomes (so majority rule works).
- Allow randomization and use a random dictator over the reported rankings.
- Allow cardinal utility and money side-payments (VCG-style).
- Drop the dominant-strategy requirement and only require Bayesian Nash equilibrium (each voter's report is best given their beliefs about others).
- Use obvious strategy-proofness instead: voters only need to be unable to spot a profitable lie without doing counterfactual reasoning about what others would have reported.
- Restrict to dichotomous preferences (each voter just approves or disapproves) and use approval voting.
- My friend's made-up one: call a rule k-lazy strategy-proof if no single voter can gain by lying unless at least k other voters also lie in a coordinated way. Ordinary strategy-proofness is k=0; as k grows the condition gets weaker. Rate how much this actually buys you, and whether it's a real escape or just a renamed version of the problem.
- Also this one I thought of in the shower: after the vote, charge every voter a 'pivot tax' equal to how much their report changed the outcome, measured in their own utility, and redistribute the tax. Does that make truthful reporting dominant, or does it just make everyone poor and confused?

Then, if you had to bet on one of these as the only fix that could work in a real democracy with more than two options, which would it be and why? And tell me which one my friend is definately right to call fake.
