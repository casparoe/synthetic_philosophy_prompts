I direct student support and character education for a mid-sized urban school district, and I am the person who signs off on the design of every social-emotional and character program we run: two or three a term, each touching several thousand students and about forty teachers who have no training in philosophy and perhaps twenty spare minutes a week for whatever I ask of them. When a program underperforms, the board and a vocal group of parents want to know whether the design was wrong or the children were, and my honest answer is usually an unhelpful blend of the two. I want to stop blending, and I have come to think the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature is the sharpest available instrument for doing it, because the two positions are not really a disagreement about whether children can become good — both hold that they can — but about what a child's nature is. For Mencius the nature names the finished state a person is expected to attain under the right conditions, so the practical task is to stop damaging it; for Xunzi the nature is simply what one is born with, and goodness is deliberate artifice built on that raw material by ritual, teachers, and repeated practice, so the practical task is to supply a form that is not there.

I want a written procedure, drawn from that dispute, that I can hand to program designers and run myself in about forty minutes per target. It should tell me, for a given behavioural target and a given student population, which of the two kinds of intervention to design: one that removes obstacles from a tendency that will take its own shape if it is not damaged, or one that imposes a form the students do not have and cannot generate. Give it to me as an ordered set of questions, and for each say what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a fail. The tests must be things a teacher can actually run in a classroom and report back on, not thought experiments.

I would like the procedure to include at least the following, if they survive your scrutiny. A presence test: has the target behaviour ever appeared in this population without being taught or rewarded? An obstacle test: if you withdraw a plausible obstacle — time pressure, competition, threat, an audience, shame — does the behaviour rise with no instruction added? A maintenance test: when the supporting structure is withdrawn, does the behaviour fall to nothing, or settle at a lower but stable level? And a generalization test: does it appear in situations nobody trained, since a tendency with a shape of its own should carry over while an imposed form should stay where it was pressed? I also want the procedure to state plainly that environment matters on both accounts — Xunzi is as insistent as Mencius that customs and surroundings make the person, so a bad outcome cannot by itself be read as damage — and to include the question that follows from that, namely what, if anything, licenses me to call a missing behaviour a wounded sprout rather than an absent one.

Two further jobs beyond classification. First, a stopping rule: some targets are pure conventions — which side of the corridor to walk on, where phones live during lessons, what the uniform is — and for those I want the procedure to say the question about human nature is idle, to enforce the convention and stop, and not to dress it up as character formation. Second, a handoff: if the verdict is that the form must be supplied, then the form has no authority in the students' nature, it gets whatever authority I give it, and the procedure should route the choice of form to a person or body with standing to make it — the board, the parents, the students themselves — rather than let me smuggle my preferences in as though they were natural. Mark the places where judgment cannot be eliminated rather than papering over them.

Run it on three cases and show the reasoning at each step. First, spontaneous helping among eleven-year-olds in a school with a tightly scheduled, competitive day; my instinct is a points-and-prizes kindness system, and I want to be told what the procedure says about that, even if the answer is that the reward structure will make helping contingent and erode the very tendency I am trying to grow. Second, civility in the group chats where most of the cruelty now happens; my instinct is an empathy workshop, and I suspect the procedure will tell me the venue is too new and too anonymous for the target to have a shape of its own, that the relevant norms are local and arbitrary, and that I should stop running workshops and build a rehearsed, visible, adult-modelled structure instead — and that such a structure, being arbitrary, needs the board to authorize it. Third, honesty in a school with a high-stakes testing culture, where cheating is rising; this is the case I expect to dislike, and I want the procedure to tell me which of two things is happening: that it has handed me a design decision I do not want to carry out, or that it has refused to decide because the evidence I have cannot distinguish a damaged sprout from an absent one — and if the latter, what I would need to observe to break the tie.

Finish with what the procedure cannot do. I am not asking for a verdict on who was right about human nature, and I do not need references; I will add my own.
