I need a speech and it's a wierd one, so bear with me. On Friday afternoon we're switching off the old data rack in the basement for good. It's been logging since the mid-eighties, it's got four thousand reels stacked behind it, and when I press the button the tapes get wiped and re-used — the lab is closing, the building goes to the estates department in the spring, and nobody will fund cold storage for a machine that half the faculty think is a fire hazard. There'll be drinks in the seminar room afterwards and I've been asked to say a few words, and I want those words to be about Maxwell's demon. Not as a bit of decoration I wave at the start and then drop — the demon is the actual argument I'm trying to make, and I want it done properly, because about half the room will know it cold and the other half will be my sister, two of the technicians, and a man from finance who thinks I'm peculiar.

The tricky bit is who's in the room. The bloke who built the rack in 1984 is coming, and he has spent thirty years telling anyone who'll listen that everyone who thinks the demon is beaten by the erasure of its memory is quietly selling something. He's in the front row, he's my friend, and I don't want to flatten him to make my point land.

So: about 600 words, four minutes spoken, not written to be read.

What I need from it:

- Actually do the demon. Trapdoor, one gas, one memory, and then the reset. Walk the room through one full cycle and say where the entropy actually goes. Don't skate past the awkward bit where a clever enough demon is allowed a reversible memory — I want the speech to face that head on rather than pretend the standard story has no gap in it.
- Come down on one side of the standing argument about *where* the cost is paid — at the measurement itself, or at the resetting of the memory — and give the strongest version of the other side, the version the other side would recognise as fair rather than as a straw man. If I'm going to disagree with him in front of everyone, I want to have earned it.
- No names, no citations, no "as so-and-so showed in such-and-such a year". Nobody in that room can check, and the ones who can will find it naff. No equations read out loud, one number at most, and none of that "entropy is just missing information" hand-waving that sounds deep and means nothing.
- The tape decision has to come *out* of the physics, not be bolted on at the end. By the time I say what happens to the reels on Monday, the audience should feel that wiping them is either the same move the demon can't get away with or the same move he can, depending on which way I've come down. That's the whole point of doing it this way.
- Land it as affection — for the machine, for him, for thirty-odd years of people logging rubbish at three in the morning. Not a lecture with a toast stapled to it. There's a long-running joke that the rack is haunted; you're allowed to use it once, no more.
- End the speech itself on a toast line. Then, on a new line, put "Verdict:" and name in a few words the account I came down for, so I can see at a glance whether you actually picked a side.

British English throughout. And keep it casual — this is getting delivered holding a plastic cup of warm white wine, not from a lectern.
