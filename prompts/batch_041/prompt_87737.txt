I am moderating the discussion period at a colloquium at which the speaker defends the following thesis. In the puzzle of choice without preference, traditionally illustrated by the case of Buridan's ass, an agent confronted with two options that are equal in every respect — or else incommensurable, so that neither is better than the other, worse than the other, nor equal to it — is not thereby paralysed, and does not act irrationally in simply picking one of them. The demand that the agent be able to give a reason for taking this one rather than that one is not a demand of practical rationality at all; the only genuinely irrational course would be to go on deliberating until the options expire. She regards the familiar reply that some unnoticed asymmetry always obtains between the two options, whether in their distance, in the quality of the hay, or in the agent's momentary disposition, as changing the subject, since it concedes that a perfect tie would be fatal.

Draft eight to ten questions that press her on the substance of this position rather than inviting her to restate it, and give a follow-up to each, keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question each on the following.

- The level at which the arbitrariness is supposed to have been removed. If the agent acts on a standing policy of picking whenever she is indifferent, is that policy itself backed by reasons, and if it is, in what sense were there no reasons bearing on the options themselves?
- Whether the thesis is conceptual or normative. If preference is by definition a complete ordering, then her case involves no preference at all rather than equal preferences, and the puzzle looks dissolved rather than solved.
- The description under which the options are individuated. The tie exists only under a fine description of the options, eating from this pile and eating from that pile, while under a coarse description, eating, the agent has an undefeated reason to act; ask whether the puzzle's sting therefore depends on a level of description that no one has argued for, and whether her account fares as well when the two equal options are both bad rather than both good.
- The incommensurability variant, where the options are neither better, nor worse, nor equal, and whether her account requires the act of picking to generate a reason to stay with what was picked.
- The principle that nothing is so without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise. Ask whether she accepts that her account requires abandoning it, and what she takes the cost of that to be.
- Responsibility. Whether the agent is answerable for which option she took or only for having settled on one of them, and whether picking is an action at all rather than something that merely happens to her.

Include as well at least one question on whether randomising is permitted on her account, and on what basis it is permitted or forbidden if picking is not.
