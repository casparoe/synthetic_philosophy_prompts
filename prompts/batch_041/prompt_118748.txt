I'm a science journalist — features, not academic philosophy — and I'm working on a piece about why the DSM gets torn up and rewritten every decade or so, and whether that's a sign of a field failing or just of a young science. At a conference last month a psychiatrist on a panel said, roughly, that psychiatric categories are like the periodic table: chemistry's table looked like a mess once too, then the underlying structure got filled in, and the same thing will happen for psychiatry, we just need better data and more time. It's a nice line. I've heard a version of it maybe a dozen times since, and I nodded along.

Then I went and read some Ian Hacking for background — the looping effects material, the distinction between interactive kinds and indifferent kinds, making up people — and now I'm worried the periodic table line is doing alot of hidden work that nobody in that room would have caught, including me.

So before I quote it I want to know how far the analogy actually holds. Could you:

- lay the correspondence out explicitly — what plays the role of the table, of the elements, of atomic number, of Mendeleev; who or what is the chemist doing the classifying; and what corresponds on the psychiatry side to the fact that the table predicted elements nobody had found yet;
- then say where the mapping breaks, and sort the breaks into the ones that actually matter for the claim being made (categories will settle down once we know more) versus the ones that are just surface differences I shouldn't make a fuss about in print;
- then the Hacking part in plain language: does the looping effect mean the target of the classification is changing in response to being classified, and if so, does that kill the convergence claim or only complicate it? Does the analogy survive better for something like high blood pressure than for something identity-charged like autism or ADHD, where people actively take up the label and build lives around it?
- Someone else on that same panel said the real issue is just the observer effect — you can't measure a thing without disturbing it. I suspect that's a different claim, and maybe a lazier one. Tell me which is closer to Hacking and why the difference matters for my piece.
- And if the periodic table is the wrong picture, is there a better analogy that genuinely earns the convergence claim — or one that earns the opposite? Friends keep offering me the map-and-territory thing and the law analogy, neither of which I find convincing, but I don't know enough to say why they fail.

I don't need a lit review or citations, I need to write three paragraphs a philosopher wouldn't wince at. Probaly also worth flagging if I'm about to overcorrect in the other direction and imply these disorders are just invented, which I don't think Hacking even says.
