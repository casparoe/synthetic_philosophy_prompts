A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether it makes any sense to cry at a novel, and we ended up stuck in the oldest puzzle in the philosophy of fiction. We know Anna Karenina never existed; we know the reunion at the close of Ian McEwan's Atonement is invented; and the emotions arrive anyway, fully formed and seemingly genuine. Her position is that this is straightforward confusion — you cannot literally pity someone you know to be a fiction, so what we feel must be make-believe pity, a quasi-emotion we play at rather than suffer, and Radford was right to find the whole thing incoherent. Mine is that the feelings are as real as any others and that the puzzle is an artifact of a bad theory of emotion, one that wrongly assumes every emotion must take as its object something believed to exist. We got nowhere, mostly because we kept recycling the same handful of examples.

So I would like a sustained reading of Atonement through the paradox of fiction, and I want it to take both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously. Some things I would want it to address. Briony is a novelist who lies, and her lie is not merely an aesthetic event: her false testimony sends Robbie to prison and destroys two lives, which seems to put pressure on any solution that treats fiction as an emotionally and practically inert realm of make-believe. Later, the reunion we have been reading toward is revealed to be Briony's invention, offered as a kind of atonement; the novel then asks whether a fiction can accomplish anything real, and whether a fabricated happiness is a gift or a further wrong. And there is the reader's own case: we are moved by the reunion before we learn it was invented, and — this is the part I care most about — many readers are moved again after, now by the fact of the invention itself. That second-order emotion, directed not at the characters but at the fictionality of their story, seems to be a datum neither the quasi-emotion view nor the thought theory handles comfortably, and I would like to know what the novel shows about it.

Two further constraints, since I do not want the standard essay back. First, distinguish carefully between the occurrent pang while reading, the standing attachment to a character that persists between sittings, and the meta-emotion about the fiction as such; I suspect the paradox looks different depending on which of the three is on the table, and I want the reading to say which one each scene is really about. Second, bring in the contrast with imaginative resistance: readers can be moved to tears by a fiction while flatly refusing to imaginatively accept a moral claim the fiction asks them to accept. Why the will should balk at the one and not the other is, I think, the sharpest available test of any account of fictional emotion, and Atonement — a novel that both solicits our sympathy and asks us to sit in judgment on a girl who makes things up — is unusually well placed to illuminate it.

Please read the novel, not merely illustrate a thesis with it. Where the philosophy illuminates a scene, say so; where the novel resists the reading or cuts against the theory, say that too, and do not distort either side to make them fit. End by saying what Atonement adds to the debate that argument alone does not. No citations or reading lists, and no quoted passages — I want the reading, not the apparatus. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which scene, counterexample, or argument would push you to a different verdict, and where in your own reasoning you think it is most likely to fail — in particular, whether your reading quietly assumes a theory of emotion it has not defended, or treats the twist as a trick rather than as something the novel has been building toward all along.
