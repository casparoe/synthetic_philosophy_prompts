You are a philosopher of epistemology and the theory of evidence, and you are good at exactly one thing that I need now: taking an argument apart step by step, rather than surveying the literature around it. Think carefully before answering, and begin by briefly laying out the two or three candidate diagnoses you take most seriously before you commit to one.

A colleague circulated the following at a workshop. Suppose she is confident she has found the error in a long-standing proof. (1) Her confidence ought to track her evidence. (2) The outside view tells her that people in her reference class -- competent people who have just convinced themselves of a striking new result -- are wrong far more often than they feel, and it tells her why: the very sense of insight she is now enjoying is among the causes of their errors. (3) Whatever she does next -- rechecking the derivation, hunting for a counterexample, trying the idea on a colleague -- is precisely what members of the reference class do, and their doing it is already priced into the base rate; so her check, and its result, carry no information the outside view has not already absorbed. (4) Any consideration she can adduce for her result is therefore either already included in the reference class's error statistics or produced by the same process that produces their errors. (5) Hence the inside view never supplies evidence that the outside view has not already discounted. (6) Her credence should simply be the base rate for her class, with nothing added; her own reasoning deserves no weight. (7) Generalizing, she should defer to the base rate even about how to reason: the base rate of people who set their private judgment against the field's is bad, so she should not dissent. (8) But that cannot be right -- it would have told every correct dissenter in the history of her field to recant, and it would equally forbid the outside view itself, whose credentials, namely that base rates predict better than hunches, were established by people who trusted their own reasoning against their contemporaries' hunches.

I want to keep (2) and (8); the outside view strikes me as genuinely humbling, and the self-undermining point strikes me as decisive. So I suspect something slips in the middle, but four steps is a lot of middle, and I cannot say which one gives or what giving it up costs. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling, and what the repair costs -- and if the repair forces me to weaken (2), say precisely how much of it survives.
