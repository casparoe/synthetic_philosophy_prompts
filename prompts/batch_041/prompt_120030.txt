I've been sitting on this argument for like three weeks now, ever since a long fight with a friend of mine (an econ grad student, so he definately should know better) about whether to take a certain bet, and I keep flip-flopping on whether I've found something real or just tangled myself in knots. Some background on me: I've read the rationalist blogs since forever, I basically treat all my beliefs as probabilities and update them mechanically, and Aumann's agreement theorem has always been my little security blanket — whenever two smart people disagree about something factual I tell myself, okay, they must not share priors or they don't have common knowledge of each others posteriors, so it's fine. But then I wrote out the argument below and now the security blanket looks like it might smother me. Here it is, numbered so you can see where it goes off the rails:

(1) If two agents share a common prior, and it's common knowledge between them that one assigns probability p to some event and the other assigns probability q to that same event, then p = q. That's just the theorem.
(2) When two people openly hash out a disagreement, each knows the other's view, knows the other knows, and so on up the tower — the disagreement is common knowledge between them.
(3) My friend and I are both rational (good Bayesians, we both update on evidence) and we came into this argument from basically the same starting point — same priors, or close enough for jazz.
(4) So by (1)–(3) our posterior probabilities must be equal, i.e. we can't actually be disagreeing.
(5) But we are disagreeing, openly, after hours of it, and neither of us is lying or posturing.
(6) So one of us isn't rational after all — and since I've gone over my own reasoning a dozen times and can't find the bug, it must be him.

The conclusion is obviously insane. If I accept it I get to write off every person who's ever disagreed with me as the broken one, which is exactly the move the blogs taught me to be suspicious of, and it gets worse: it says any two people who actually understand each other and are both being rational simply cannot disagree, full stop, which would make the whole practice of arguing — of talking to people who see things differently — pointless. And here's the part that really spooks me: the argument applies to itself. My friend thinks the bad step is (3), I've got a sneaking suspicion it's (2), and now we have a disagreement about where the disagreement goes wrong, which by the argument can't be common knowledge if we're both rational. So either the argument refutes itself, or it eats itself and one of us is being irrational about which premise to drop.

I'd most like to keep (1) (it's a theorem, c'mon) and (5) (we really are disagreeing). (2) feels like it has to be true — we're not mind readers but we've both said our views out loud plenty. So which step do I actually give up, and what's the price? I want to know why the step looked fine in the first place, and what each of the standard moves costs me — like, if I give up (3) and say we just have different priors, is that a real answer or is it just a fancy way of saying "we're both irrational but at the level of priors"? And if the fix is some distinction I'm blurring, spell it out in small words, cause I clearly keep stepping on it.
