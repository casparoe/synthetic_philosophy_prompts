I am trying to get my own position on climate ethics straight before a seminar, and what I want is a consistency audit rather than a tour of the literature. Here is where I have landed.

(1) Wronging requires a victim: a policy is unjust only if it wrongs someone in particular, an actual person, present or future, who ends up worse off than they would otherwise have been.
(2) Because our climate choices change who is born and when, most people who would suffer on a high-emissions path would never have existed at all on a low-emissions path, so as long as their lives are worth living they cannot have been wronged by our choice.
(3) Even so, I am confident that a path which leaves future generations much worse off than we could have left them is unjust, and that we owe them something close to the consideration we owe our contemporaries.
(4) I reject pure time preference: future welfare counts for exactly as much as present welfare, and not less merely because it lies in the future.
(5) For actual policy I would still run cost-benefit analysis with a positive discount rate on consumption, since that rate is tracking opportunity cost and the likelihood that future people will be richer, not the lesser worth of their welfare.
(6) I am a proceduralist about political legitimacy: only present-day people can authorize policy, and I do not accept that anyone can legitimately stand in for the unborn.

Tell me which of these pull against each other and which only appear to; rank the real tensions by how much they cost me; and for each one say what the cheapest repair is and what I would be giving up by making it. I would also like to know where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and which single argument, if it went through, would make you tell me that a different item on the list is the one that has to go.
