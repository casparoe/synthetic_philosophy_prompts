I am a retired field entomologist. For most of my working life I studied solitary wasps and social insects; since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind and action. I have encountered a pattern that I can describe but cannot name, and I would like to know whether there is an established concept for it -- or, failing that, whether it can be given a precise definition that distinguishes it from the concepts that lie nearest to it.

The pattern concerns the ascription of rigidity and flexibility. An animal's behavior can appear flexible or intelligent at one level of description and purely mechanical at another, and the verdict can reverse again when the level changes. I first noticed this in a hunting wasp. If I displaced her prey a few centimeters while she was inspecting her burrow, she did not simply adjust; she dragged the prey back to the original position and began the entire sequence again. At the level of the sequence, she was rigid. At the level of the individual movements, however, she was responsive: she compensated for uneven ground and wind, and she could switch from dragging to flying if the load was light. A colleague who studied ant navigation presented the mirror image. His ants followed a route that looked fixed, yet when the route was blocked they improvised, and when the food was moved they updated. But when a single landmark was removed, they would run the old path until exhausted. Which of these animals was flexible? It depended on whether the goal was described as bringing the prey to the burrow, completing the inspection sequence, reaching the food, or following the landmark vector.

The same ambiguity, it seems to me, appears in how we describe ourselves. A person may be rigid about a principle and flexible about the means of satisfying it, or flexible about the principle and rigid about the means. We call the first principled and the second stubborn, but the difference seems to lie in the level at which we locate the fixed point, not in the amount of mechanism. When philosophers ask whether we are free or merely complicated machines, I am never certain at which level the question is meant to be answered. If every level of our behavior is flexible only relative to a fixed rule at the level below, and every level below is flexible relative to a fixed rule below that, is there a bottom level that settles the matter? Or is mechanical always a description relative to a chosen grain?

Is there a name for this? I do not mean the general claim that behavior can be described in many ways, nor the observation that an organism can be rigid in some respects and flexible in others. I mean the specific phenomenon in which the charge of rigidity or flexibility is not a property of the agent as such but of the agent-under-a-description, so that the same episode can be a paradigm of mechanical repetition at one grain and a paradigm of flexible adjustment at another. If an established term exists, what is it, and what does it capture that instinct, habit, reflex, compulsion, and automatism do not? If no such term exists, I would like one coined, with a definition, a statement of what it excludes, and some boundary cases. In particular, I would like to know what would have to be true of a system for it to be rigid or flexible without relativization to a description -- whether that is a coherent demand, or whether it is the wrong way to pose the question about free will.
