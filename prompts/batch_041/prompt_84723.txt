A committee moved the boundary of a condition I had always been told I did not have; nothing about me changed but the label. The condition is diagnosed by no test: the criteria are a list of symptoms, some fixed number of which suffice, so my sister and I carry the same diagnosis while sharing almost nothing on that list. Meanwhile the clinic that diagnosed us is recruiting for a study to find the biological marker of that very condition, and the committee presents its work as tracking a disease that was always there. A friend on the research team adds that the criteria do not so much report something about me as confer a status, and that the status changes how I act, which changes who meets the criteria. I had assumed a diagnosis names a joint in nature. What do these practices presuppose about what the label picks out — that the symptoms are fallible indicators of one underlying condition that exists independently of the list, or only that the list is a serviceable way of sorting a continuum? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits with, say whether the boundary-setting, the marker hunt, and the status-conferring reading can be held together, and tell me what would actually have to change in the clinic if the category were only a device for grouping heterogeneous cases.
