I took early retirement from a chemistry department a few years back — thirty-odd years of atmospheric work, mostly — and I've been spending the time since reading philosophy, badly and happily. The thing I keep tripping over is the cluelessness objection to consequentialism: the thought that because what we do ramifies indefinitely, we can't know whether any given act leaves the world better or worse, so the theory can't tell us what to do. Fine, but everyone argues about it with toy examples, and I happen to have lived through a real one. In the 70s and 80s the case against CFCs was ours — the ozone chemistry, the hole over Antarctica, and the polar stratospheric cloud chemistry that nobody had put in the models until the hole was already there — and then the Montreal Protocol actually phased the stuff out, which is about as clear a win as environmental policy ever gets. Except the substitutes we reached for, HCFCs first and then HFCs, are either mildly ozone-eating or ghastly for the climate, the HFCs hundreds to thousands of times more potent than CO2 per molecule, and now there's a whole follow-up treaty to get rid of those too. So the fix for one problem may have made another one worse, and nobody at the time could run the numbers out to the end. Or could they? That's part of what I want to know. I've also seen a distinction drawn between simple cluelessness, where the effects are knowable in principle and merely hard to add up, and complex cluelessness, where they propagate indefinitely so no computation ever closes the question — I'd like to know which one this is, if either.

What I want is a case analysis in both directions — the theory read onto the episode, and the episode read back onto the theory — and please be disciplined about it rather than gesturing at unintended consequences. Specifically: which kind of cluelessness this is, and whether the theory even needs the strong kind to make its point; what the decision-makers could actually have known ex ante and what they couldn't, given that the HFC problem strikes me as possibly a case of knowing and weighting wrongly rather than not knowing, and I suspect those get lumped together when they're seperate failures; whether cluelessness, if it's real, is a problem for the criterion of rightness, for the guidance function, or only for how confident we're entitled to feel; and what the case shows back to the theory — does it confirm the objection, is it an anomaly the objection mishandles, or is it a case the objection never anticipated. Say plainly where the theory goes silent instead of forcing it to speak, and don't pad it out with the standard stuff about expected value, I've read that. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, with no introduction and no conclusion — I want to see whether that structure forces you to be honest about which parts you actually know.
