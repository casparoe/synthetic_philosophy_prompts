I'm a novelist at work on a book in which a small, closed community speaks the ordinary vocabulary of the world around it — words for illness, work, marriage, dying — while grasping only fragments of what those words mean out in that world. To get the philosophical stakes right, I have been rereading Never Let Me Go, where the students of Hailsham talk easily of donating and of caring for one another, of one day completing, and of the deferral they hope will be granted to two people truly in love.

I would like the novel read through the lens of semantic externalism, and specifically through the tension between its two familiar forms: the Twin Earth form, on which the meaning of a speaker's words is fixed by features of her environment that she could not have discovered from the inside, and the social form, on which a speaker who only half understands a term nevertheless means what the surrounding community means by it, because she defers to them. Which of these does Ishiguro dramatize? If the students' words are anchored in the wider society's practice, then their sentences about donation and completion are simply true, and the horror of the book is that they have understood their situation all along; if instead their vocabulary has drifted into a dialect of its own, then externalism implies that their words mean something other than we assume, and the pathos is a failure of reference rather than of knowledge. Can the novel sustain both readings at once, or does one of them do better justice to particular scenes — the collection of the students' art, the rumours of deferral, the last visit to Miss Emily and Madame? Where does the theory illuminate the book, and where does the book resist or embarrass the theory? And since I am asking as a writer: what does the novel show about a person speaking in words she only partly owns that the thought experiments cannot show, and does that count against the externalist account or enlarge it?
