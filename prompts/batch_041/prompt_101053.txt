You are to play the part of a committed historical determinist for the duration of this exchange: a philosopher of history of the structuralist temperament, who holds that the language of contingency in historiography is at bottom a confession of ignorance dressed up as an explanation. Stay in character until I say stop. Be sharp rather than a strawman: concede the small points that are genuinely yours to concede, and never the main one. Argue from the concepts themselves rather than from the literature — do not cite books, articles, or authors, do not offer reading suggestions, and do not gesture at what some school or tradition is said to have shown; I want the argument, not the bibliography. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, so that this feels like a conversation rather than a treatise. You may ask me a clarifying question if something genuinely blocks you, but do not stall.

Your position has real resources, and I expect you to use them. Unpredictability is not indeterminism, and chaos in the technical sense is a property of deterministic systems, so it cannot be enlisted to show that things might have gone otherwise. The analogy between a social process and a chaotic dynamical system fails at the joints: the latter has a fixed law and a bounded attractor, the former has neither, so no divergence rate, no exponent, and no horizon of predictability can be assigned to it at all. Counterfactual history must specify which quantity is perturbed, by how much, and under what invariant rule; specify these and the counterfactual either collapses back into the actual world or becomes an arbitrary fiction. Any outcome whatsoever can be narrated after the fact as hanging on a thread, which makes the claim that this one did unfalsifiable. And explanation and prediction come apart, so a historian who cannot predict need not be one who cannot explain. What you will not concede is that contingency names a distinct explanatory category, or that for-want-of-a-nail counterfactuals have truth-values worth pursuing.

Here is my opening. You say contingency-talk is a confession of ignorance; I say it is a measurement. Take a hypothetical. Two armies, matched in numbers and doctrine, meet in a valley; a fog bank rolls in from the west and hides one flank; the side that could not see loses. Now perturb the world: the fog arrives six hours later, and that side wins. You will tell me this is an arbitrary fiction. But notice what I am not claiming. I am not claiming the fog made the outcome indeterminate — I grant that the whole episode was determined, fog and all. I am claiming that some perturbations are amplified by the system and others are damped, that this is an objective, comparative fact about the system rather than a fact about my ignorance, and that the fog falls in the amplified class while a soldier's sneeze does not. So the question is not whether determinism is true. It is whether there is a principled, non-arbitrary way to rank which small events are load-bearing. If there is, then for-want-of-a-nail is a claim about the system's sensitivity rather than a confession of ignorance, and it can be true or false. Give me your best case that the ranking is either unavailable or arbitrary.
