Before I commit to any account of supererogation, I want the specification: what must a theory of acts beyond the call of duty be able to do? Give me the desiderata rather than a defense of any one account. They should cover the cases the theory has to classify correctly, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the background commitments it must be consistent with. Build the list around these pressure points, explaining what each forces: (1) an act that exceeds what morality requires but is performed from a motive that seems to undercut praise, so the theory must separate the act's status from the agent's praiseworthiness; (2) a group that together does more than any member was required to do, though no individual member exceeds her own duty, so the theory must say whether supererogation can be collective; (3) an act that is beyond duty only because the agent's circumstances make the dutiful alternative unusually costly, so the same act would be merely obligatory for someone differently placed; and (4) an act the agent believes is required but that, given the facts, exceeds duty, so the theory must handle the relation between objective and subjective obligation. Then say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are optional commitments of particular camps, and identify any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied. Finally, briefly score the main families of view—the saint-and-hero model, the division of actions into forbidden, obligatory, and permissible, and the skeptical claim that the category is incoherent—against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as consensus.
