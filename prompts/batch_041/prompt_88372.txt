I am a novelist, and for the past six years I have also taught an upper-level seminar, cross-listed with the philosophy department, on selfhood and character. I am writing the final examination now and need one essay question on Confucian role ethics, together with a model answer and a marking scheme I can hand to my two graders.

My difficulty is a familiar one. My students arrive able to recite the received picture of the view: that a person is not a private interior substance who subsequently enters into relations, but is constituted by those relations and by the roles they carry; that the virtues are the excellences of a role well performed; that practice and ritual, rather than introspection, are what make a self. They can also recite the standard objections — that the view dissolves the individual into a web, that it makes morality hostage to whatever a given society assigns, that it leaves no room for the dissenter. What none of them can do is follow the view into a case where the roles come off.

I have been using a case from my novel-in-progress in lecture, and I want the exam question built on it. Marta has run her family for thirty years — not as a job but as what she is: the one who keeps the calendar of obligations, hosts the holidays, arbitrates between siblings, remembers the dead, and holds the family's account of itself. No one elected her, and her authority is of the kind that is simply assumed, by everyone including her. In the novel she learns that the family's prosperity and its much-prized cohesion rest on a wrong done decades ago to one of its own members, a wrong that was afterward absorbed into the family's story as a misfortune. She wants to lay the role down: to stop hosting, to stop arbitrating, to let the calendar lapse. Her family's answer is not that she should not but that she cannot — that what she proposes is not a decision a person makes but a kind of self-destruction, or a betrayal that would prove she had never really been what she seemed. I have also been writing a second version of the scene, which I want the question to cover: she lays nothing down, keeps every practice, and has inwardly renounced the role entirely, performing it the way a stranger would. I have set the case in a contemporary Western family on purpose; if the view is merely a report on one society's arrangements, the students should have to say so, and if it is an account of persons, they should have to show what it can do here.

I have been teaching the students to work with a distinction I coined for the novel, and I want them held to it: the difference between a role one holds and a role that holds one. The first is a station with duties attached, which a person can vacate; the second is a constituent of who one is, which cannot be vacated without loss of self. The exam question should make them say which of the two role ethics is actually committed to, whether it needs both, and what follows for the two versions of Marta's case.

What I want the question to force is the crux: whether the theory's central claim — that roles constitute the self — can survive the possibility of a justified renunciation, or whether making room for that possibility quietly reinstates the very thing role ethics was built to replace. A student who answers by listing the objections I mentioned above, or by saying that role ethics is communitarian and Marta is an individualist, has not answered it.

Please write me (1) the essay question itself, phrased for a closed-book, sixty-minute exam and answerable in about eight hundred words; (2) a model answer of roughly six hundred and fifty words, written the way I would want a strong student to write it, in plain prose and without leaning on the names of scholars or the titles of works — my students are not expected to produce a bibliography, and I do not want the model answer trading on attribution; and (3) a marking scheme organized around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (the total should be twenty) and, for each point, a one-line note on the mistake candidates typically make, plus a short descriptor that separates a top answer from a merely competent one.
