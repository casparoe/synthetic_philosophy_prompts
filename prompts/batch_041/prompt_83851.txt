I'm supposed to hand out a one-page "where I stand" sheet for my concepts seminar next week and I want to make sure it isn't a mess before it goes out. Here's what's on it: (1) Hofstadter and Sander basically have me — categorization just is analogy-making, and a concept is a fluid, context-dependent halo of analogies with no fixed core you could write down as a definition; (2) I'm a scientific realist about natural kinds — there's a determinate fact about whether some stuff is gold or some animal is a tiger, and chemisty and biology have been progressively getting those facts right; (3) the best computational account of concepts going is the distributional one, where each word gets a single vector and similarity is just distance in that space; (4) a system has a concept only if it can deploy it on a case it's never seen, so getting the labels right on a benchmark tells you nothing about whether it has one. Two verdicts I'm attached to and would rather not give up: a kid who calls a zebra "a horse with stripes" has the zebra concept, just a thin one; and I don't think there was ever a fact of the matter about whether a whale is a fish — we redrew a line, we didn't discover anything. So which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, how bad is each real one, and what's the cheapest fix plus what I lose by taking it? Rank them by severity. And if I only get to change one thing, which change cleans up the most?
