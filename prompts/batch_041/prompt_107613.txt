I am a high school student writing an independent-study paper on the reliability of introspection, and I would like your honest credence in one specific claim, together with a decomposition of how you arrive at it. Please assess exactly the claim below rather than a neighboring claim that is easier to defend or to attack.

Claim: For ordinary adults making everyday evaluative judgments — which of two near-identical options they prefer, why they trust one acquaintance rather than another, why one argument strikes them as persuasive — a person's introspective report of the basis of that judgment is more often accurate than not, more accurate than a well-informed third party's inference from the same behavior, and more accurate than the person's own confidence would lead one to expect.

I want accuracy scored causally, not justificatorily. That is, I am treating a report as accurate only if the consideration it names is one whose manipulation would actually change the judgment. A reason that merely sounds good in hindsight, and that would have been offered no matter which way the judgment went, counts as inaccurate under my definition even if the person sincerely endorses it. I realize this is a demanding reading, and I would like you to keep it fixed rather than quietly relaxing it into something more forgiving.

Please give me the following.

1. A number between 0 and 1 for the claim as stated. My own guess is around 0.35, but I do not trust it and would rather see your reasoning than your agreement.

2. The sub-questions the number depends on. I would suggest at least these, though I expect you to add or replace as you see fit: whether judgments of this kind have a single determinate cause that a report could be right or wrong about, or are instead overdetermined and assembled after the fact; whether the laboratory findings on poor introspective access — for instance, cases in which people confidently justify a choice that was experimentally reversed without their noticing — extend to everyday judgments made with no experimenter watching; whether confidence and causal accuracy come apart in the way the claim asserts; and whether the third-party comparison is fair, since a careful observer working from the same behavioral evidence might well outperform the person. For each sub-question, give a rough probability and one line of justification.

3. How those sub-questions combine into your final number, and which single term that number is most sensitive to.

4. What evidence or argument would move that term the most, roughly how far, and in which direction.

5. Finally, if you think the causally-scored reading is the wrong way to measure introspection in the first place — that first-person reports ought to be judged by their role in the person's reasoning rather than by their causal pedigree — say so, and tell me how much of your number is really an artifact of my definition rather than a finding about introspection itself.
