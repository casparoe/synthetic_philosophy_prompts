I have just joined the standing review panel of the small research consortium I work for — the body that must approve or refuse any action a member proposes to take unilaterally and that cannot be taken back: a publication, a data release, a disclosure, a deployment. The panel has met for years and its minutes are terse but consistent. My own group's proposal is the first item at the next meeting, so I have been reading the record backwards, trying to work out what it commits the panel to.

What I have reconstructed:

Case 1. A member proposed to post a preprint showing that a widely used clinical risk score performs much worse out of sample than its developers claim. Approved. The minute notes that the analysis is reproducible, that the field will converge on the finding regardless of what any one group does, and that the worst case is damage to a few reputations.

Case 2. A member proposed to publish, without first notifying the manufacturer, a working exploit for an industrial control device deployed at thousands of sites and not patchable in the field. The member's own assessment was that disclosure would force a redesign and was therefore net positive. Refused. The minute says that one reader with bad intentions would be enough to cause harm that no subsequent action of ours could undo, and that the fact that other members would probably have made the same call is not a reason to make it.

Case 3. A member proposed to begin, on their own land, a contained trial of an engineered organism carrying a kill switch, arguing that the trial was reversible by design. Refused. The minute says the kill switch is untested, that an escape would be effectively permanent, and that the panel could not defend a rule under which every member ran their own contained trial.

Case 4. A member proposed to release a corpus of public records with personal identifiers stripped, over an objection that some of the individuals named would be embarrassed. Approved. The minute says the harm is minor, the material is public in principle, and no member's doing the same would make things worse.

Case 5. A member proposed to announce, ahead of the group, that a widely used safety practice in our field rests on a misreading of the evidence. Approved, even though the panel recorded that the member had underestimated the professional cost to two colleagues. The minute says the claim is either right or it is not, and the field will settle it within a year either way.

My item next week: my group has spent two years on work that, if published, would be a modest but real increment in the accessibility of something harmful. As far as I can tell, roughly a dozen other groups are within a year of publishing pieces of the same thing. Each piece on its own is small; a reader who assembled them would be considerably better equipped than a reader who had any one of them. My own honest estimate is that publishing is net positive: the work is good, the barrier is coming down regardless, and being the group that withholds will cost us more than it buys. The publication cannot be recalled. No single publication, mine included, would by itself do serious harm; the worry is about what the set does.

My working hypothesis is that the panel has been running some version of the unilateralist's curse — treating a proposal as suspect when a single actor can produce a harm the group cannot undo, and when the world in which every similarly placed member acts is worse than the world in which none does. If that is the right reading, my case is awkward, because the two halves of that test come apart in it: the every-member-does-it half points toward refusal, the single-actor half toward publication.

So: predict the panel's verdict. Say which rule the record actually supports — a conformity rule of the everyone-does-it kind, a single-actor-sufficiency rule, a marginal-impact rule of the sort Case 1 seems to invite, a reversibility rule, or something else — and which of these the record leaves genuinely open. Identify the closest analog among the five cases and defend the choice, since Case 1 and Case 2 each look close in different ways. Name one further case the panel could be asked to decide that would separate the rules you think are still live. And tell me whether the fact that a dozen groups share my reading of the evidence makes the curse more pressing or less: I have heard it said that the curse is a product of independent error and therefore weakens when judgments are correlated, but shared error seems to me the more frightening case.

Be explicit about how confident you are in the verdict you predict and in the rule you attribute to the panel, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
