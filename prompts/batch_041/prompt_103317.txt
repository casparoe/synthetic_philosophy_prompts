Write a satirical piece in the form of the customer-reviews page for a product sold on a fictional business-to-government procurement site. The product is the Lange Board Mk. III, a computational apparatus marketed to national planning authorities as a way of discovering equilibrium prices for every good without a market: the central board announces a price vector, enterprises and households report the supplies and demands they intend at those prices, the board adjusts the vector, and the procedure iterates until excess demands vanish.

Include the listing's product description, briefly, in the flat register of an online storefront. It should advertise the standard claims: that prices are fixed parametrically by the board rather than by anyone's bidding; that managers need follow only two rules, producing at minimum average cost and expanding output until marginal cost equals price; that households receive their incomes and spend them as they please; and that the procedure converges on the same allocation a competitive market would have reached, only without the waste.

The reviews should come from a range of purchasers and users, each in a distinct voice, each unconsciously testifying to a different part of the objection without ever articulating it:

- a bureau economist delighted that the machine converges in eleven thousand iterations, and untroubled that the demand schedules it converges on were supplied by his own bureau;
- a factory director who can comply with the marginal-cost rule only by guessing at next quarter's costs, and who notes that the machine has never once asked him what he knows about his suppliers, his workers, or the substitutes available to him;
- a household respondent who reports that her stated preferences shifted after the board announced the prices, and who is told that this is a rounding error;
- a mathematician who admires the fixed-point argument behind the convergence claim, and mentions in passing that the existence proof presupposes that the demand correspondence is already known;
- a reviewer who compares the Mk. III unfavorably with an earlier arrangement that at least made its mistakes legible, because errors there showed up as losses and closures rather than as a printout saying the solution had been reached.

Several reviewers should observe that the device's failure mode is silent: it reports convergence whether or not the numbers fed into it meant anything. One reviewer should be entirely satisfied, and his satisfaction should be the most damning item on the page.

Keep the whole piece deadpan. The comedy must come from reviewers describing, in the idiom of ordinary product feedback, exactly the informational and incentive difficulties that make such a scheme unworkable, with no reviewer and no narrator naming those difficulties or winking at the reader. No reviewer may cite an economist or a work of economics; the objections have to surface through practical complaints about the product. Do not let the piece close with a moral or an explanation of the joke. The object of the satire is the position that a planning board can replicate the allocative work of market prices by iterating on announced prices, along with the habit of treating the informational problem as a technical detail that more computation will eventually settle. A reader who knows the debate should recognize every objection as a real one and find none of them invented; a reader who does not should still be able to follow the joke.

About 700 words. Begin directly with the piece — the listing and then the reviews — with no preface, no framing remarks, and no commentary afterward.
