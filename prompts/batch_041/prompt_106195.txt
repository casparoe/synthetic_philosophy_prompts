I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and next month I'm recording a live episode of my interview series in front of an audience of a few hundred people who are curious, literate, and have no biology beyond school. The subject is what heritability does and does not mean. I have forty minutes and one guest, and I would rather the forty minutes not become the usual set of reassurances. I understand the material well enough to know that the hard part is the order of the pieces, not the pieces; what I want from you is the plan, not the explanation.

Work out the sequence in which the ideas have to arrive, so that nothing is used before it is available, and say what work each step is doing and what it depends on. Decide what single example should carry the whole arc. I have been assuming it should be myopia, which is strongly heritable and yet has gone, within a generation or two, from a minority condition to the ordinary condition of young adults in several East Asian cities; tell me whether that example can bear the weight, and if it cannot, what should replace it, and what any candidate example must have and must not have. Then map the misconceptions. The audience will arrive with something like "heritability of 0.8 means 80 per cent of my height comes from my genes," and they will leave, unless I am careful, with "so nothing can be done about it." Tell me where each misconception enters, where in the sequence I should head it off, and what happens if I let it run.

Three harder points have to land somewhere in the arc, and I want to know where, and why they cannot come earlier: that a heritability estimate is a property of a population at a particular time and moves when the range of environments moves; that genes and environments are correlated rather than independent, so the variance decomposition is not a clean causal split; and that an estimate computed inside a group leaves the question about differences between groups untouched. I would also like one question I can put to the room at the end that would show whether the idea has landed rather than merely sounded familiar, and a short list of what I should openly decline to go into at this level, with the sentence I use to decline it.

Give me the response in five titled sections, in this order: "The arc" (roughly 400 words — the ordered sequence, each move in a sentence or two, with what it depends on); "The carrying example" (roughly 250 words — your verdict on myopia, the replacement if you have one, and the features any example needs); "Where the misconceptions break" (roughly 400 words — each misconception, the moment it appears, and the point in the arc where I head it off); "The closing question" (roughly 100 words — the question, and what different answers from the room would tell me); and "Off the table" (roughly 100 words — what I leave out and how I refuse it).
