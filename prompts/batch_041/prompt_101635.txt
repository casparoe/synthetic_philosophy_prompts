I have recently become the editor of a mid-sized journal in comparative social science, and with the job I have inherited a genre of submission I do not yet know how to handle consistently: papers that explain an outcome — a regional homicide rate, a national savings rate, the uptake or non-uptake of a voluntary pension scheme, a pattern of tax compliance — by appeal to a culture. A culture of honor, a culture of deference to elders, a culture of thrift, a culture of fatalism. My screening assistant is a second-year graduate student with no training in the philosophy of social science, and the guidance I have managed to give her so far amounts to "does this feel like a just-so story?", which is neither fair to authors nor reproducible from one manuscript to the next. The obvious reference point for all of these papers is the Weberian paradigm — the claim that ascetic Protestantism helped bring about the spirit of capitalism — and part of what makes it the paradigm is exactly how much the causal claim was hedged: the economic conduct in question was, on Weber's own telling, largely an unintended by-product of a religious discipline rather than anything the believers were aiming at, and that is the feature nearly every imitation quietly drops. What I want from you is not an essay about Weber and certainly not a reading list; it is a decision procedure my assistant and I can actually run, in order, on a manuscript within the half hour we have before the first decision has to be logged.

The procedure should lay out the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which it stops with a reject or a revise-and-resubmit, and the point at which it hands the decision to me because what is at stake is a value judgment rather than a question of evidence. Three distinctions have to survive whatever simplification you impose, because they are the ones I keep losing when I try to improvise: first, the difference between a value being merely present in a population, a value actually figuring in the agents' own reasoning, and a value doing independent causal work once institutions and material incentives are controlled for; second, the requirement that the culture be measured without using the very outcome it is meant to explain; and third, the difference between an outcome the agents intended and one that, as in Weber's own case, was a by-product they would have disavowed had they noticed it. Please mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the checklist will misfire.

Run the finished procedure on three hypothetical submissions, and do not soften the verdicts: (1) a paper arguing that a culture of honor explains why one region's homicide rate persistently exceeds a neighboring region's; (2) a paper arguing that Confucian filial deference explains why a voluntary pension scheme was undersubscribed in two provinces; (3) a paper arguing that ascetic Protestant values caused the early capitalist takeoff. I expect the procedure to be uncomfortable in at least one of these — if a submission can satisfy every step while its culture turns out to be little more than a redescription of the outcome, say so rather than patching the checklist to reach a verdict. Give me the result as a JSON object with this schema: a "procedure" field holding an ordered array of step objects, each with "step" (integer), "name", "question", "sufficient_answer", "if_insufficient" (one of "reject", "revise", "flag_for_editor"), and "judgment_call" (boolean); a "worked_cases" field holding an array of objects, each with "case", "per_step_verdicts" (array of strings, one per step), "recommendation", "decisive_step", and "what_the_procedure_misses"; and a "limitations" field holding an array of strings naming the things the procedure cannot do.
