Assume the role of a philosopher of medicine and biology who works on the metaphysics of health and disease, and think carefully before answering — I would rather have a slow, structured audit than a quick verdict. Some context: I spent my career as a physiologist and have only recently started reading philosophy, so I may be using some of these terms more loosely than you would. Here is roughly where I have landed, and I want to know whether it hangs together.

(1) Whether a condition is a disease is an objective biological fact, namely whether some part of the organism is failing to perform its natural function; values enter only when we decide what to do about a condition, not in classifying it as a disease. (2) A part's natural function is fixed by its evolutionary history — what it was selected to do. (3) But I also think we can read a part's function off species-typical statistics: we know the heart is for pumping because pumping is what hearts characteristically do. (4) I take conditions like major depression to be genuine diseases, no more value-laden than diabetes. (5) I think the treatment/enhancement distinction is real: medicine restores function, it does not improve on it. (6) I do not count aging as a disease, though I would happily take a pill that slowed it.

Work through these pairwise rather than treating every pair as an equal complaint. Tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to; for each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs; then name the single change that would resolve the most at once. Where a tension turns on a case, give me the case and say what each commitment forces you to say about it. Do not smooth over the disagreements — I want to know which of my views has to give.
