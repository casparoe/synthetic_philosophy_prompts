Design a Frankfurt-style counterexample to the principle that an agent is morally responsible for what she does only if she could have done otherwise — that is, a case in which an agent is genuinely responsible for her action even though no alternative was open to her. The case is meant to test that principle, so it must earn its verdict from the details rather than stipulate them: you may not simply assert that the agent is responsible and that no alternative was open; both must follow from the structure of the scenario.

Specifications:

1. The action must issue from the agent's own practical reasoning. She weighs reasons and acts on the balance she accepts; nothing overrides or bypasses her deliberation in the actual sequence.

2. No alternative possibility may be available — and not merely at the level of overt action. She could not have chosen, decided, or even begun to try to do otherwise. A case that leaves her able to take the first step down the other path (a case that leaves a "flicker") fails this specification.

3. The absence of alternatives must not rest on the assumption that the actual sequence of events was causally determined. Whatever forecloses the alternative is to be purely counterfactual: in the actual world it never operates, and the agent's action is not causally necessitated by anything outside her own reasoning.

4. The mechanism that would have blocked the alternative must not be triggered by a prior sign that already settles which way she will go. If it waits for such a sign, the case can be charged either with assuming the conclusion or with leaving a flicker; your mechanism must instead foreclose the alternative directly.

5. Keep it ordinary: no neurosurgeons, implants, mind-reading devices, or exotic physics. The blocking element should be a standing fact about the situation rather than a person or machine that acts at the last moment. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph.

Then, in order: (a) state exactly which alternative possibility you claim is absent, and demonstrate its absence rather than asserting it; (b) say which reading of "could have done otherwise" your case defeats, and whether it also defeats the weaker reading on which she needed only to have been able to choose otherwise; (c) identify the single feature of the case that does the work, describe a variant in which that feature is removed or reversed, and say what the variant shows; and (d) give the strongest objection a defender of the principle would raise against your case, and judge whether the case survives it. If you conclude that specifications 2 through 4 cannot be satisfied together, demonstrate why, and then supply the nearest case that satisfies as many of them as possible.
