Me and my friend Dave got into a long argument last night about whether Aquinas is basically a quietist, and I still can't tell who was right. His line was that Aquinas says sedition is always a mortal sin, full stop, so he'd tell you to just put up with a tyrant and shut up about it. I said that can't be the whole story, and I hauled out the bit from the Summa (II-II, q. 42, a. 2, reply to the third objection) where he writes: "A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states." Then he goes on — and I'm going from memory for this part, I don't have the exact words in front of me — to say there's no sedition in disturbing a government of that kind, unless the disturbance is so disorderly that the people end up suffering more from it than they did under the tyrant. So he definately isn't a pure quietist.

But here's what I actually want help with, because Dave and I just kept circling and neither of us could cash it out.

First: is the "no sedition in disturbing a tyrant" move a real argument or just a definitional dodge? He's already defined sedition as something opposed to the unity of law and the common good, so once a tyrant's rule isn't that unity, resisting him just falls outside the definition by construction. That feels like winning by stipulation. Or is there something substantive in the background — something about what makes a multitude a people in the first place, or about who holds the authority that a tyrant is abusing?

Second: that "unless greater harm" clause. Doesn't it quietly turn "sedition is always a mortal sin" into a consequentialist calculation? If the sinfulness of the disturbance depends on comparing the damage it does against the damage the tyrant was doing, then "always" is doing a lot of unearned work.

Third: how does this fit with what I half-remember from On Kingship, where he seems to say something much more permissive about the multitude getting rid of a tyrant — that if the community had the right to appoint a king it has the right to depose one, and that if some higher authority appointed him you go to that authority instead? And I have a vague memory of him being very wary of private individuals taking it on themselves to kill a tyrant. Are the two texts doing seperate jobs, or is there a real tension between the cautious Summa line and the more political On Kingship line? Also, does he distinguish between a tyrant who seized power in the first place and a legitimate ruler who later turned into one? Because that seems like it should matter a lot and I can't tell if he actually makes the distinction or if I'm importing it from later writers.

Fourth: who counts as "the multitude"? His line that those who defend the common good and withstand the seditious aren't themselves seditious sounds fine until you notice both sides in a revolt will say exactly that about themselves. Is there a criterion here, or does it just collapse into whoever happens to be right about the common good — which we only know after the fact?

I don't need a reading list or citations, and please don't just tell me to go read a commentary — I want the argument itself laid out, in plain terms, including the parts where it doesn't hold together. If the honest answer is that these texts can't be fully reconciled, say that instead of smoothing it over.
