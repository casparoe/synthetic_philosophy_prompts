I teach the upper-level metaethics seminar here and I'm putting the final together, and I could use your help drafting one of the essay questions. Every year the wrong-kind-of-reasons topic comes back around and every year I get alot of essays that retell the demon case (the one where a demon threatens to torture you unless you admire him), mumble something about "object-given versus state-given reasons" and stop, without ever saying what work that distinction is supposed to do. So this year I want the question built around a case I invented myself, so they can't have seen it anywhere, and I want it to force them to actually run their preferred solution through the case instead of reciting it. For context on what they'll have: the fitting-attitude analysis of value (the good is the fitting object of a pro-attitude), buck-passing, Parfit's object-given/state-given distinction, and the standard replies to the wrong-kind objection -- the "that's not a reason for the attitude at all, it's a reason to do something about your attitude" move, the "just restrict the analysis to reasons of the right kind" move, and the complaint that the restriction is circular because you can't say which reasons are the right kind without already knowing what's valuable. It's closed book, 70 minutes, and this essay is 25 of the 100 marks on the final, so it has to be answerable in that time without being trivial.

Here's the case I want them to work with. A foundation offers a young art critic a year-long fellowship worth a lot of money, and the money is released only if at the end of the year she sincerely believes that a certain neglected painter -- call her Vos -- is a major artist. She can't just decide to believe it, obviously, and everyone knows that. Now two versions. Version 1: prompted by the money she looks at the paintings far more closely than she otherwise would have, and the close looking reveals real merits she'd previously missed; by December she believes Vos is major, and she can trace the change in her belief to the merits rather than to the money. Version 2: the foundation's very public announcement of the fellowship turns Vos's paintings into a cause celebre -- they become the works that sparked a national argument about how arts funding works -- so there's now a genuine, object-given reason to think Vos's paintings matter historically, even though nothing about their aesthetic quality has changed. I want the question to make them (a) say precisely which claim of the fitting-attitude analysis the wrong-kind objection threatens, and in particular to notice that the standard version of the objection only bites if "fitting" is read as something weaker than "supported by reasons" -- so they have to say what that weaker reading has to be and why the analysis needs it; (b) run their preferred fix through both versions, including whether Version 1's belief is rationally defective just because of how it came about, and whether the correct thing to say there is that the money was a reason for an action (looking closely) rather than for the attitude, in which case they owe me an account of what's left of reasons for attitudes generally; and (c) come down on whether the objection shows the fitting-attitude analysis is false or only that it needs a principled restriction. Version 2 is meant to be the trap: the historical-significance reason is object-given, so anyone whose criterion is "object-given equals right kind" has to either swallow that the belief is fitting on those grounds or explain what's wrong with their criterion, and I want the question phrased so that neither escape route is free.

So what I need back is: the exam question itself (aim for something a student can read twice in three minutes and still be unsure how to start -- that's the sweet spot for this kind of thing), a model answer of about 600-700 words written at the level of a strong second-year grad student rather than a published paper, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks built from five must-hit points with the marks attached to each, and for each point a one-line note on the mistake that usually costs students those marks. Then, seperately, a short paragraph on what separates an A from a B here: the specific moves an A has to make that a B typically won't, and what the B answer that still scrapes 15 or 16 out of 25 tends to look like.
