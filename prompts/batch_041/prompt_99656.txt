Last night a friend of mine — a colleague in the same department, which is part of the trouble — and I argued for two hours over a single sentence on a wall label, and I would rather settle the question by method than by seniority. I am the staff historian at a small city museum, and I am drafting the explanatory text for a permanent gallery about a wave of anti-immigrant violence in our city in 1907, the trials that followed it, and the slow collapse of the industry that had drawn the immigrants there in the first place. The gallery needs about thirty labels of roughly forty words each, nearly every one of which makes a causal claim — this provoked that, this collapse was caused by that — and each must pass two reviews: a community advisory board of descendants and local residents, and an outside academic historian. There are no footnotes, the labels must stand alone, and I have maybe ten minutes per label. My friend's position is that "multiple causes" is a coward's formula, that a serious historian names the cause, and that a label listing four contributing factors is an abdication dressed as caution. Mine is that several episodes in this gallery are overdetermined — more than one condition was on its own enough, so the but-for test finds nothing and "the cause" is not a well-formed question — and that which cause you name depends on a contrast you have to choose, which makes the choice partly evaluative rather than factual. We were arguing about verdicts when we should have been arguing about method.

So please turn what is actually known about multi-causal explanation and overdetermination into a decision procedure I can run on each label, in order, without a philosophy degree. It should begin by forcing me to state the contrast I am implicitly drawing — caused rather than what? — since I suspect half of our fight was two different contrasts colliding. It should teach me to separate a condition that was necessary from one that was sufficient from one that is merely a non-redundant part of a sufficient set (I have met the term INUS and have never been confident I was applying it correctly), tell me what to write when two conditions each sufficed so that neither is a but-for cause, and tell me how to recognize genuine preemption — where the second cause would have done the work but the first arrived first — as opposed to ordinary hedging. It should hand me a short ranked vocabulary of verbs, from the strongest to the weakest, with the evidential bar for each, so that "caused", "made possible", "contributed to", and "was among the conditions" stop being interchangeable mood music. It should say when the honest verdict is that the evidence cannot distinguish two stories, and it should mark plainly where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to a person — I expect the choice of contrast and the choice of what the label is for to be such points, but tell me where else they occur — and where the procedure is likely to misfire. Then run it on three labels. First, the violence itself, where I have a mob, a rumor, a police decision to withdraw, and a newspaper campaign, and no obvious single cause. Second, the collapse of the industry, where I have a tariff change, a technological shift, a long strike, and a fire, and I want to be able to say which of these the gallery can responsibly call decisive. Third, a case where I expect the procedure to give an answer I will not enjoy: I would like to credit a single reformer with ending the trials, and I want you to tell me, honestly, whether any individual is causally necessary in an episode where the outcome was overdetermined — and what verb I am entitled to use if the answer is no. Finally, be explicit about which parts of this rest on settled ground among people who study causation and historical explanation and which are genuinely contested — whether causation should be analyzed in terms of counterfactual dependence at all, whether the selection of a cause is interest-relative, whether narrative explanation in history is a method or a confession of failure — and do not present a contested position as the consensus; where your procedure depends on a view that is disputed, say which step would have to be rewritten if a rival view were correct.
