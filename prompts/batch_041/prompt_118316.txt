Treat others as you would have them treat you is one of those proverbs that gets produced whenever a discussion of cooperation reaches an impasse, and I have just watched it used to close down a disagreement about the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: a friend insisted that cooperating is simply what the rule demands, adding that if everyone reasoned as she did everyone would be better off. I do not believe the proverb can bear that weight as it stands, but I cannot say precisely where the reasoning goes wrong, and I would like a map of the possibilities before I argue with her again.

Enumerate the distinct precise principles the slogan could be expressing, ordered from the nearly empty to the strong, and give a verdict on each. At a minimum I want these separated:

- a norm of reciprocity — cooperate because the other party will return the favour in a later interaction;
- the superrationality idea, on which an agent in a symmetric situation assumes that every agent who reasons as they do will arrive at the same choice, and evaluates each action under that assumption;
- a Kantian requirement to act only on maxims that could be willed as universal law;
- a plain instruction to give others' interests the same weight as one's own;
- an empirical prediction about which strategies prosper under selection.

For each, say what it actually commits you to, whether it is plausible on its own terms, and — what I care about most — whether it delivers the conclusion my friend wanted, namely cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against a stranger. I am especially interested in where the readings come apart. Does the superrational reading require me to care about the other player at all, or would a purely self-interested agent who reasoned superrationally still cooperate, and how does that separate it from the empathy reading? Does it collapse into the reciprocity reading once repetition is allowed? And does the negative form of the rule — refrain from treating others as you would not want to be treated — demand cooperation at all, or only forbid a narrower class of harms?

Close by saying which reading my friend most plausibly had in mind, and whether on that reading the slogan actually settles the one-shot case or merely sounds as though it does. Give me the menu with verdicts rather than an essay in defence of a single reading: I want to see at a glance which readings are platitudes, which are substantive, and which are simply false.
