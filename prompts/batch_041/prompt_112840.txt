Retired chemist here, spent most of my career on reaction kinetics, and I only started reading philosophy after I stopped working. So I'm definately going to mangle some terms. I keep getting into alot of arguments about the arrow of time and I'm starting to suspect everyone is quietly answering a different question. Before I read any more, I want the spec sheet, not another answer.

Write me the desiderata for a theory of the arrow of time: what does any account have to be able to do before I should take it seriously?

- The cases it has to get right. Coffee cooling and never spontaneously reheating; a glass shattering and not reassembling; us remembering the past but not the future; causes seeming to run one way; records and traces existing at all; the fact that we can meddle with the future but not the past; a demon sorting fast molecules from slow ones. Add whatever I've missed.
- The distinctions it can't collapse. I suspect some of these are separate arrows people keep lumping together — the thermodynamic one, the cosmological one, the causal one, the one about what we can know, and the psychological one about memory and anticipation. Does a theory have to derive all of them, or is it allowed to call some primitive and let the rest follow? And there's a difference between explaining why time has a direction and explaining why we think it does — which is it actually on the hook for?
- The things it can't contradict. The classical laws and most of the quantum ones are time-reversal symmetric, near as I can tell, though I gather a few particle processes aren't — does that matter or is it a red herring here? The second law is statistical rather than absolute, so it can be violated, just absurdly unlikely. And the early universe looks like it began in a very special low-entropy state.
- Something that bugs me: why do we demand an explanation for why entropy increases toward the future but treat the low-entropy past as a brute fact needing none? That lopsidedness in what we ask for seems like it should itself be a requirement — either justify it or don't do it.

Then the part I actually want. Flag any two requirements on that list you don't think can be met at once — especially anything like "the arrow has to be objective" colliding with "the arrow has to fall out of time-symmetric laws." If there's a contradiction buried in the list I'd rather hear it now than after five books.

And which single requirement does the most work at ruling out the popular answers?

Finish with a short scorecard against your list for the families I keep hearing: the one that pins everything on a special initial condition, the one that says the arrow is really about us and our memories, the one that says entropy is just about our coarse-grained descriptions, and the one that says there must be a fundamental time-asymmetric law after all. Doesn't need to be long — I just want to see where each one breaks.
