Her version, roughly verbatim: you take a circle, inscribe an equilateral triangle in it, then ask what the probability is that a chord chosen at random is longer than the side of the triangle. Bertrand showed in 1889 that you get 1/3 if you pick two random points on the circumference, 1/2 if you pick a random radius and a random point on it and take the chord perpendicular to the radius there, and 1/4 if you pick a random point in the disk and use it as the chord's midpoint. Three natural applications of the principle of indifference, three different answers. So that's a self-contradiction in the principle of indifference — you can't distribute equal probability over "equivalent" possibilities in a continuous space without generating inconsistent results — which means any argument of the form "there's no reason to prefer either option, so it's 50/50" is worthless, and it's basically the same kind of thing as Russell's paradox, a genuine contradiction that made people give up on the classical definition of probability. It's also why Bayesians can never justify their priors: either you make a prior up, or you use indifference and get garbage. A physicist called Jaynes sorted the whole thing out in the 70s: the answer can't depend on the size of the circle or on where the circle sits, because the rain of straws that draws the chords doesn't know any of that, so you impose scale invariance and translation invariance, and once you do, only the midpoint method survives — so the answer is 1/4. People have literally thrown straws at circles and confirmed it. So it's a dead issue now, and the only people who still bring it up are philosophers who haven't heard of Jaynes.
