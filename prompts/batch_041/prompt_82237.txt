I'm a novelist, and my current book has a trial in it, so I've been making everyone I know with any legal background read my chapters. Last weekend that turned into an argument at my kitchen table between Dana (a public defender, twenty years in) and Peter (her brother-in-law, teaches political theory and reads a scary amount of law), and I've been chewing on it ever since.

The case they were arguing about is one I made up, so there's no court record to check, just the setup. In the country my book is set in, the old regime had a statute requiring citizens to report anyone they knew to be 'sheltering persons of irregular status.' It was printed only in an internal ministry bulletin, never in the ordinary gazette, and 'irregular status' was defined by a regulation that got rewritten four times in a single year. My character Kolb turned in a family that had been hiding a young couple from across the border. The family got picked up, the couple was deported, and both died within the year. Kolb says he did it because he'd been told the penalty for not reporting was five years, and he kept the receipt for the informer's payment the ministry handed him, which was a standard payment anyone in his position was entitled to. Now the new government is deciding what to do with men like him.

Dana: The new courts should convict him. But there's no way around it, they'll have to pass a retroactive statute, and they should say out loud that that's what they're doing. We'd be punishing a man for something that wasn't a crime when he did it, and we should admit the cost instead of pretending it isn't there.

Peter: Why pay that cost if you don't have to? That statute was never law. It was never properly published, it existed to turn neighbors into informers, and the thing it required you to report was defined by a regulation nobody could keep up with. Convict him under the ordinary homicide and accessory provisions. No retroactivity, no announcement that the state is doing something shameful.

Dana: That's just retroactivity with a prettier name. Kolb did exactly what the rules in force told him to do, and he has a receipt proving the state thanked him for it. Calling the old statute 'not law' doesn't change what he could have known at the time, and it doesn't change that we're punishing him now for conduct that was lawful then. And you've handed judges the power to void any statute they find repugnant. Where's the line? Every criminal statute is a little vague at the edges, so on your test half the code is up for grabs.

Peter: The line is that a rule has to be able to guide you before you act. Public, prospective, clear, and actually followed by the officials who apply it. That's not my moral taste, it's a standard, and the old statute fails it on every count. A system that can't tell you in advance what's forbidden isn't just failing to be decent, it's failing at the thing law is for.

Dana: Notice what that does, though. Every official of the old regime who followed those same rotten rules was also not bound by law, so the shootings and the deportations broke no law either, because there was no law to break. And the people who obeyed the bulletin because they thought they had to, the ones who weren't monsters, get no protection from you at all. Also, if 'can it guide you' is the test, then my state's driver's license statute, with its seventeen exceptions, isn't law. You've swapped a line you can point to for a threshold nobody can find.

Peter: And you've kept a line you can point to that produces an admitted injustice every single time. Fuzzy at the edges beats exact and wrong at the center. Besides, think about the receipt. Under the old rules Kolb was owed that money as a matter of right. If the old statute was law, the state still owes him. If it wasn't, he was paid a bounty for a crime. You can't have it both ways.

I stayed out of it, mostly because I'm not sure I understand what they were definately disagreeing about, and by the end there was alot of table-thumping. There's a real disagreement in there somewhere, I just can't tell where the hinge is. Can you go through it move by move and call each one, on whatever scale you like as long as it's clear where you land between flat wrong and clearly right, and then say what each of them is missing, including anything they both missed. I don't need a syllabus or a reading list, just the adjudication. And write it in American English, all my editors are American and I've given up fighting about spellings.
