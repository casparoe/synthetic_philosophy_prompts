I've been in a reading group on moral psychology for about a year, mostly the moral foundations stuff, and we keep having the same fight without anyone moving an inch. One side says the research shows that political morality comes in flavors: liberals lean hard on harm and fairness, conservatives also weight loyalty, authority, and purity, and that's just a fact about moral taste, like how some people taste cilantro as soap. The other side says so what — a description of how people form moral judgments can't tell you whether the judgments are any good, and if you think it can you've smuggled in a premise. The first side says that reply begs the question by assuming some neutral standpoint from which to rank moralities. The second side says the first is confusing the causes of a belief with its justification. Then everybody nods at each other and we go home, and I've spent alot of evenings like this.

My hypothesis is that the two sides aren't disagreeing about any finding at all. They're disagreeing about what a descriptive science of morality is for — whether it's more like linguistics, which describes a grammar without ranking grammars, or more like medicine, which describes what's going on and thereby tells you what's wrong. The case that made me think this: we invented a population that has all the foundations but weights loyalty and purity way above harm, so they do genuinely awful things in the name of group and cleanliness. Does the theory have anything to say about them besides different taste buds? If not, hasn't it quietly committed to the view that no foundation beats any other, which is itself a normative claim?

Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a real disagreement underneath that I'm missing — a hidden empirical claim, or a disagreement about what counts as a reason in the first place? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a study, or just admitting the question is two questions wearing one coat?
