A workshop on consciousness has just heard a proposal that so far exists only in its author's notes. The proposal is this. Elementary physical entities have tiny experiences — there is something it is like to be each of them. When a collection of such entities enters a certain kind of dynamical relationship — their activities become mutually phase-locked, so that each entity's internal state is continuously and reciprocally determined by the states of the others and the whole settles into a stable pattern of mutual constraint — the many micro-experiences are said not merely to coexist but to fuse. The result is a single experience with a single subject. On this view the micro-subjects do not survive the fusion as so many separate subjects; they are absorbed into the larger subject, much as the separate motions of a medium are absorbed into a single wave. The fusion is supposed to be governed by a threshold: below a certain degree of mutual determination there are only many micro-subjects; above it there is one, and there is a fact of the matter about which.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers who have both stayed behind after the talk.

The first is the author of the proposal. She believes it dissolves the combination problem — the problem of how micro-experiences, if they exist, could add up to a unified macro-experience — and she is prepared to say precisely what she takes the phase-locking relation to accomplish.

The second is a critic who grants, for the sake of argument, that elementary entities might have experiences, and who is not a physicalist out to deny consciousness to the brain. His objection is internal to the panpsychist project: he holds that the proposal cannot work, because of the subject-summing problem — the observation that relating subjects to one another, however intimately, still leaves you with exactly that many subjects and no more, so that any fusion either secretly assumes a new subject or merely relabels the old ones. He also presses a second difficulty: even if a single subject could emerge, it is unclear how the micro-qualities (a faint warmth, a hint of red, a dull ache) would yield the specific unified quality of the resulting experience rather than a mere juxtaposition.

Make both characters equally competent and equally serious. Each should engage the other's actual points rather than talk past them, and each should be willing to concede something the other earns. The author should have real replies available to her — for instance, she may claim that the critic is treating subjects as countable things whose number is fixed independently of their relations, and that fusion changes what there is rather than merely how things stand; the critic should be able to say why that reply does or does not escape his objection instead of simply repeating it. The critic, for his part, should have at least one line of attack that the author cannot fully defuse.

At least once, have them descend to a concrete borderline case: two subsystems that have reached, say, ninety-nine percent of the author's threshold and are then nudged across it. Ask what changes at the instant of crossing, whether there is a moment at which there are one and a half subjects, and what the author's account says about a collection that oscillates around the threshold. Neither character should be permitted to wave this away.

The dialogue should not end in a tidy victory for either side. It should end with the two having located the precise point on which they disagree — ideally a disagreement about what it takes for a subject to exist at all, rather than about the details of the mechanism. Roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words.
