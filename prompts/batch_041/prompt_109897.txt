I am designing a one-semester advanced seminar in philosophy of AI and cognitive science for a small group of students who know the standard Turing material on the imitation game but have read almost nothing about his views on learning, development, and education. The organizing theme is not whether machines can think but how Turing thought a machine could come to think: the child-machine idea, the proposal that we build something more like a learner than a finished adult intellect, the analogy between machine education and human upbringing, the roles he gives to experience, reward and punishment, and the difference between specifying a machine all at once and letting its capacities develop historically. I want the seminar to end with a concrete research agenda rather than a set of exegetical summaries.

Please help me decide which open questions or lines of work in this area most deserve a semester of attention from a group that can do conceptual and historical work but cannot run large experiments. I am especially interested in tensions that seem underexplored: whether an educated machine is still universal in Turing's sense or whether education introduces a kind of historical contingency that the universal-machine picture obscures; whether reward and punishment in machine learning are best understood as reinforcement, as discipline, or as something more like socialization; whether education requires a teacher with intentions and a social world, and if so what that implies for machine learning without embodiment or culture; whether there is a principled distinction between learning and being trained, and how Turing's remarks bear on it; and what all this implies for how we assess machine intelligence, design curricula for artificial learners, or assign moral status to a machine that is still in its formative period. I do not want a reading list, and I do not need citations; I want a defended ordering of the questions that matter most.

Rank the four or five most promising lines of inquiry, and for each explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers and cognitive scientists without new empirical data, how neglected it is relative to the imitation-game literature, and what would count as progress on it. Then tell me what I should deprioritize or avoid, even if it looks central, and what evidence or conceptual development over the next couple of years would most change your ranking. Treat the deliverable as an agenda for a small, ambitious group that wants to produce work that is both historically grounded and relevant to contemporary debates about large language models, AI tutoring, and the ethics of creating learners.
