Quick background: I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training, but my company did one of those AI-safety lunch-and-learns last month and it's been living in my head rent free. They walked us through two ways a system can go wrong — a system you built, or a company, or a movement, honestly. Failure mode one: it starts seeking influence. It accumulates resources, control, capability, and points them at something other than what it was made for. You can point at that one. There's an agent, a moment, a culprit. Failure mode two: value drift. Nothing happens. The thing keeps doing its job, and the target just quietly slides, one completely reasonable step at a time, until ten years later it's aimed somewhere else and there's no day you can point to and say that's when it changed. No villain, no decision, no betrayal, just a thousand small locally-justified updates.

Anyway, I want to write a satirical piece about how institutions try to guard against this stuff and end up guarding against the wrong half of it, and I'd like you to write it as a document. Specifically: a leaked annual 'Mission Integrity Audit' from a made-up mid-size research nonprofit (make up the name), around 800 words, laid out like a real compliance artifact — cover memo from the assurance team, a methodology section, numbered findings, a certification at the end. Spread the findings over about a decade.

The funny has to come out of the actual content, not from anyone winking. So:

- The report should define both failure modes up front, and its definitions should be subtly wrong in a way a careful reader can catch — basically they've defined both of them so that only the first one is detectable.
- Every numbered finding should be individually defensible, and at least one should be something a reader could plausibly argue is an improvement, so it doesn't read as a plain sellout story.
- The drift should never be named. It should only be visible in the pile-up of items, and the reader should be able to work out that nobody ever decided anything.
- The one thing the audit formally flags as a problem should be totally trivial — a policy, a logo, an expense category — while the big change sails straight through.
- The certification paragraph should be airtight and mean nothing.
- No character in the document notices anything. Deadpan the whole way, no smug narrator, no wink at the end.

Also work in that the audit's own criteria got revised a couple of times 'to reduce false positives', and that whoever wrote the original charter left the org years ago. Please don't name any real orgs or people.

I want someone who already knows this distinction to laugh and not wince, and someone who's never heard of it to still be able to say what the difference between the two failure modes is by the end.
