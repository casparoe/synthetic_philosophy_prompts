I've been trying to get my head round the common prior assumption and I found a chapter in a decision theroy book that explains it like this (I'm paraphrasing, I don't have it in front of me):

'The common prior assumption is the claim that rational agents start from the same probability distribution over the states of the world, so any difference in their beliefs has to come from a difference in information. Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem makes this precise: if two agents are rational Bayesians with a common prior, they cannot disagree -- and in fact once each of them knows what the other one's posterior is, the two posteriors have to be identical. So whenever you find two experts with different opinions, at least one of them is being irrational, or they don't really share a prior. The same reasoning gives you the no-trade theorem: if everyone shares a prior, no trade will ever happen, because every trade would have to be a bet that the other side is a sucker to take. And it tells you what to do with persistent disagreement -- say, prediction markets pricing an election at 70% while a poll-based model says 55% -- namely, conclude that the common prior assumption is false, which is basically why markets can work at all.'
