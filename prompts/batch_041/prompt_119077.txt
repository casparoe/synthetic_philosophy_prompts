I've been on the governance and development beat for about six years now and I keep walking into the same argument wearing different clothes. There's an anthropologist I lean on who insists that corruption is basically a Western export — that in a lot of the places I report from, the official who steers a contract to his cousin or takes a envelope from a client isn't breaking his own moral code, he's keeping it; the binding obligations are the ones to kin and to patrons and to the people who showed up for him, and the norm of impersonal impartiality our auditors assume is just one local ethic we've quietly promoted to a universal. Then there's the economist I call when I need the other side, who says fine, call it a duty if you like, but the reason the road doesn't get built is that the money goes sideways, and that's a fact you can measure without importing anybody's values. I've written both versions of this story and I'm no longer sure the two of them are even disagreeing about the same thing.

So here's what I want. Take the anthropologist's position in its strongest form — the version where the insider's moral economy really is different and "corruption" as a universal evaluative category is either a mistake or a power move — and restate the whole thing in the vocabulary of institutional economics. I mean the actual machinery, not a vibe: principals and agents, agents with more than one principal whose demands conflict, monitoring and enforcement costs, transaction costs, rent-seeking, property rights over office, the gift-exchange idea that a payment above the going rate is a gift the recipient repays with loyalty and effort, and the old line that a bit of this greases the wheels of a rigid bureaucracy and is efficiency-improving. Go through it claim by claim and be honest about what survives contact. I read the development blogs and I can follow a principal-agent setup, but I'm not an economist, so don't hide behind notation and don't give me the fake reconciliation where the two vocabularies are just different lenses on the same facts.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue. What in the anthropologist's complaint simply cannot be said in that vocabulary at all — and when you hit something that won't translate, is that because economics has no way to be normative and is therefore missing the point, or because the anthropologist's claim was never as clear as it sounded and the translation is what exposes the hole? Don't lay out both options and leave me to choose. Pick one, commit to it, and tell me what it costs you to hold it. And write the whole thing as prose — no headings, no bullets, no bold, nothing like that, just the way you'd write me an email.
