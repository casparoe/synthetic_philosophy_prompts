I'm making a handout for a reading group on the socialist calculation debate — Mises and Hayek vs Lange — and I want it as a decision tree instead of yet another essay, because I'm convinced the arguement goes in circles for the boring reason that people are answering different questions under the same heading.

What I don't want is a tree organised around names. Every branch node should be a question someone can answer without already knowing which thinker it leads to, and every leaf a position plus the one commitment that's most awkward for it. Branch on what I think actually does the splitting:

- is the obstacle informational or motivational — i.e. you can't work out the right allocation, vs. you can work it out but nobody will act on it;
- if informational: is the missing knowledge tacit and non-transmissible, or just dispersed and in principle aggregable given enough communication and compute;
- if aggregable: is the bottleneck raw computation, the sheer volume and rate of change of the data, or nothing at all;
- is the standard being applied static allocative efficiency, or dynamic discovery and innovation;
- whether the position needs scarcity collapsed into a single scalar price per good, or whether a vector-valued signal (labour time, energy, emissions — pick your physical unit) would do the same job;
- and the one I care about most: does the position think the indispensable thing is a price *vector* — an informational device you could in principle hand to a planning board — or a market *process*, i.e. rivalrous discovery that you can't have without losers.

I also want the tree to be forced to take a stand on five cases that I think are the real tests and that nobody puts in the summary versions: (a) the planning that already goes on inside one big firm, where inputs get allocated without internal prices — if it works there, why not everywhere; (b) open-source software, which coordinates a lot of dispersed knowledge with no prices at all; (c) real-time electricity dispatch, which looks like central planning and mostly works; (d) a command economy bolting prices onto wholesale trade from above; (e) an accounting scheme that replaces money with a physical unit like labour hours or joules. For each case, say which leaves it supports and which leaf it embarrasses.

Leaves I want at minimum: Mises-style a priori impossibility; Hayek-style practical impossibility (a knowledge problem, not a logic problem); Lange-style trial-and-error market socialism; the mechanism-design dissolution, i.e. the line that no mechanism, market or plan, delivers efficient outcomes under dispersed private information, so the whole framing was off; labour-time or in-kind accounting; the modern 'big data / better compute makes it tractable' line; and at least one leaf for 'the market/plan dichotomy is a fiction, because real markets are already full of administered planning and real plans already run on prices.' If there's a leaf nobody occupies, say so and say whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If two leaves can't be seperated by any of the questions above, flag them rather than faking a split.

Six to nine leaves, aimed at someone who's read the Mises, Hayek and Lange pieces but none of the secondary literature.

Start with the tree itself — no throat-clearing, no 'here's a tree that...' — and stick any caveats at the end.
