I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and one recurring frustration is how often debates about language and social defaults stall because the parties are arguing past each other. I want to try an adversarial collaboration on a specific, well-known case: Hofstadter's Person Paper, the satire that substitutes race terms for gender terms in ordinary English sentences to make the reader feel the force of a default assumption. The question I want the collaboration to address is not whether sexist language is bad—both camps can agree on that—but whether the Person Paper's device is a legitimate and effective method for exposing and correcting default assumptions, or whether it is a rhetorically and epistemically defective move that proves less than it seems.

Define the two camps as follows. Camp A, the reversalists, hold that the Person Paper's term-reversal is a uniquely illuminating diagnostic: by swapping the default group, it makes visible the unmarked assumptions that ordinary language carries, and the discomfort it produces is evidence of those assumptions' grip. They think the satire can be a legitimate tool of moral and conceptual persuasion, and that language reform is a reasonable response to the defaults it reveals. Camp B, the satire skeptics, hold that the device is defective in several ways: it presupposes the wrongness of the analogized hierarchy, so it can only persuade those already convinced; it conflates a semantic claim about what a term means with a pragmatic claim about what hearers assume; it may reinforce the very associations it aims to disrupt; and it mistakes a surface feature of language for the deep cause of sexism. They think its value is at best diagnostic for the already-sympathetic, and that language reform is often cosmetic.

Write the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps accept—about language, defaults, the Person Paper's device, and what would count as evidence for or against its efficacy. These should not be vacuous; they should be the substantive common ground that makes the remaining disagreement precise.

2. A precise statement of each remaining disagreement, phrased in neutral wording that each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view. Where possible, separate genuine disagreements from verbal ones—for example, if one camp means by default assumption a descriptive fact about processing and the other means a normative fact about what is treated as normal, say so and say whether that dissolves or relocates the dispute.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I expect at least these, but add others if they are real:
   - Does the satire's persuasive force depend on the audience already accepting the wrongness of the analogized case? If so, is that a defect or a feature of all moral argument?
   - Is the race/gender analogy apt in the relevant respect? What is the relevant respect—social construction, historical contingency, legal status, visibility, relation to biology, or something else? Does the analogy do argumentative work or only rhetorical work?
   - Does the satire target semantics, pragmatics, or discourse norms? Is the claim that a term like mankind is gender-neutral a semantic claim, a pragmatic default, or a normative proposal?
   - Can lexical replacement change attitudes, or does it trigger a euphemism treadmill that leaves the underlying default intact?
   - Does the device risk imaginative resistance—readers refusing to inhabit the reversed world—and if so, does that blunt the satire or reveal its limits?
   - Does the satire risk reinforcing the associations it critiques by putting them on display?
   - Is the Person Paper best evaluated as an argument, a provocation, or a thought experiment, and does that change what would count as a fair criticism?

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or possible observation that both camps agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For example, if both agree that a study showing the satire changes minds among the unsympathetic would support Camp A, and a study showing it only changes minds among the already-sympathetic would support Camp B, then state that and record what each side predicts. Do not invent empirical results; describe the test and the predictions.

5. A final section on what, if anything, both camps agree would actually move the debate, and what would count as a stalemate.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the formulation acceptable to Camp A, the formulation acceptable to Camp B, the test or case both accept, Camp A's prediction, and Camp B's prediction. I will add citations myself; do not include a bibliography or references to specific papers, and do not attribute any verbatim quotation to Hofstadter or anyone else unless you are certain of it. Paraphrase the satire's device rather than quoting it.

One more thing: I want the document to be usable by both camps, so avoid formulations that are slanted toward one side. If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why, and say what the parties should do about it.
