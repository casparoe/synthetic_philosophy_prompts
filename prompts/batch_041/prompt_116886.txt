Write a syndicated advice column, between 700 and 850 words, in the flat, reassuring register of a mid-market lifestyle magazine, set in a world in which David Lewis's analysis of convention — the account offered in Convention (1969) — is the whole truth about social norms, and in which everyone knows it. Give the column and the magazine invented names; invent three or four letter-writers and their troubles. The columnist has absorbed the analysis completely and must never break frame: no winking, no authorial aside, no paragraph in which the column concedes that the framework has limits, no narrator standing outside the world to signal that a joke is in progress. The comedy must come from the analysis being applied correctly to the cases where it is powerful and, in precisely the same tone, to the cases where it has nothing to say, with the columnist showing no sign of registering any difference between them.

The machinery the columnist must handle accurately, and must never soften or overstate:

- A convention is a regularity in behaviour that a population conforms to in a recurrent situation, where each conforms partly because the others do, where everyone expects everyone else to conform, and where this is common knowledge — known to all, and known to be known to all.
- Nothing needs to have been agreed, promised, decided, or legislated; there need be no statable rule, and typically there is none. A convention can be older than anyone's memory of how it began.
- Conventions are arbitrary: a different regularity would have solved the same problem just as well, and the one in force is fixed by salience, precedent, or accident.
- Conformity is sustained because unilateral deviation goes badly for the deviator, not because deviation is forbidden.
- Conventions change without anyone choosing the change. A new regularity takes hold when a salient precedent appears and the others then have reason to follow it; no one can establish a convention alone, and no one has to authorise its death.
- The analysis explains convergence among people who have some interest in converging. Where interests genuinely conflict, or where the question is not what is expected but what is owed, the apparatus has nothing to say.

Requirements:

1. At least one letter should be a textbook coordination problem — the kind of thing the analysis handles beautifully, where the columnist's reply is genuinely useful and even quietly wise.
2. At least one letter should concern something that is not a coordination problem at all — a betrayal, a cruelty done to someone who expects nothing of you, a wrong that no redistribution of expectations would repair. The columnist must answer it in the same calm vocabulary and with the same confidence, and must not flag that anything has gone wrong.
3. One letter should come from someone who wants to change a convention, or to stop observing one that is fading, and who asks whether they may simply begin. The reply should be exactly right about how conventions do and do not change: you cannot make one alone; you can only supply a precedent that others find salient; if they follow, the convention has already changed; if they do not, you have merely made yourself strange.
4. The moral vocabulary of the letters — ought, owed, fair, deserved, betrayed — should be answered every time in the vocabulary of expectation, salience, and cost. The columnist should translate it away silently rather than argue with it, and every answer must be consistent with every other.
5. The final exchange should slide, without comment, from the first kind of case to the second, and the column should end there.

The satire's target is a habit of speech rather than a philosopher: the move of redescribing every social expectation as a convention and thereupon treating it as arbitrary, unowned, and immune to criticism. Keep it fair to Lewis — a specialist should see that the author understands the analysis and is not manufacturing its blind spots — and keep it legible to a reader who has never heard of him. That reader should finish the column with a live sense of the question the columnist cannot ask: what, if anything, stands outside the machinery.

No citations, no footnotes, no attributions, and nothing in quotation marks from any real text.
