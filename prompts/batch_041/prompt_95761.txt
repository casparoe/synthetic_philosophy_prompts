I'm in a small reading group going through the Wealth of Nations a book at a time (we're four econ grad students and one historian, so no one is an expert on Smith, which is sort of the point). Last week we hit Book IV chapter 7, on colonies, and I got into an argument I couldn't finish. Someone claimed Smith is basically an anti-imperialist, someone else said no, he's only against the mercantile system and would have been fine with colonies under free trade, and I said something in between and then couldn't defend it. The passage that broke me is this, from the section where he's asking what Europe got out of the discovery of America:

"The exclusive trade of the mother countries tends to diminish, or, at least, to keep down below what they would otherwise rise to, both the enjoyments and industry of all those nations in general, and of the American colonies in particular. It is a dead weight upon the action of one of the great springs which puts into motion a great part of the business of mankind. By rendering the colony produce dearer in all other countries, it lessens its consumption, and thereby cramps the industry of the colonies, and both the enjoyments and the industry of all other countries, which both enjoy less when they pay more for what they enjoy, and produce less when they get less for what they produce."

(That's from an online edition with modernised spelling, in case the punctuation looks off to you.) What I can't get straight is how the pieces fit together. He's just told us the colonies did derive real advantages from Europe's policy, and now he says the exclusive trade keeps those advantages below where they'd otherwise be -- fine -- but then he describes the harm as falling on everyone at once, the colonies and all the other European nations and even the mother country's own consumers, which makes me wonder whether the complaint is really about empire or just about monopoly, and whether it's a complaint about aggregate welfare or about injustice to the colonies. And the "dead weight on a spring" bit I can picture but can't state precisely; I don't know if the spring is industry, or demand, or the division of labour, or something else, and whether "dead weight" is doing any real analytical work or is just rhetoric. Also the last clause, about countries that enjoy less when they pay more and produce less when they get less, seems to be saying the same thing twice, so I suspect I'm missing a distinction.

Help me read this properly. I'd like it laid out as: first, a section called "The plain sense" (about 100 words) that just says what Smith is asserting in the passage, no evaluation; then "The mechanism, step by step" (250-350 words) walking through the causal chain he's relying on, making explicit what has to be true about prices, demand and colony trade for the argument to go through; then a section called "What the metaphors are doing" (150-200 words) on "dead weight" and "great springs", including your honest read on whether they carry argumentative weight or are decoration; then "Empire, or just monopoly?" (200-300 words) on whether this passage supports the anti-imperial reading, the free-trade-with-colonies reading, or neither, and what in the text decides it; then "Where I'm probably still confused" (100-150 words) naming the two or three things in my framing above that are most likely mistakes on my part. Keep it plain and argumentative, no citations needed, and don't tell me what other scholars say about Smith on empire -- I want the passage worked through, not the literature.
