You're a political philosopher who actually knows how the EU works on the ground — not the brochure version, the plumbing. Take your time, brainstorm a little before you commit to anything, and then give me a careful answer. Write it in American English.

Here's what's bugging me. The EU has an elaborate, almost liturgical procedure for protecting national parliaments, and through them the voters back home. Under the Lisbon rules, when the Commission puts forward a proposal, every national parliament gets eight weeks to send in a reasoned opinion saying the proposal breaks the subsidiarity principle — the idea that decisions should be taken as closely as possible to the citizen. If enough of them object, a third of the votes, or a quarter for certain police and justice proposals, the Commission is formally required to review the proposal. And then it reviews it and can keep it anyway. As far as I know, the escalation built into the rule — where a majority of national parliaments object, the Commission still won't budge, and the Council or the Parliament can then kill the proposal outright — has never once been triggered. So the whole apparatus comes down to: we are listening, formally, and you cannot stop us.

Meanwhile the actual legislating happens somewhere else. Under the ordinary procedure, the overwhelming majority of laws now get agreed at first reading, and they get agreed in closed-door sessions where a handful of negotiators from the Parliament, the Council, and the Commission hash out the final text. No meaningful public record, no recorded votes on the compromises, and most members of the European Parliament find out what's in the deal at roughly the same time the rest of us do. So we've built a transparant-looking formal channel for national parliaments to object, and then we settle the substance of the law in a room nobody can watch. I'm probaly missing something obvious here.

What I want from you:

1. What does this arrangement presuppose about democratic legitimacy and about sovereignty? Reconstruct the implicit theory rather than gesturing at the usual line about a democratic deficit — I've heard that one. Does the arrangement presuppose that there's one European people who are the ultimate source of authority, or many peoples, or nobody in particular? Does it presuppose that legitimacy comes from consent, from being answerable to someone, or from delivering results? Does it presuppose that sovereignty is the kind of thing a state either has or doesn't, or something that can be pooled, divided, and lent out?

2. Be precise about the difference between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely happens to fit comfortably with. Plenty of theories might license this setup without being presupposed by it. Say which ones.

3. The two halves of the puzzle — the formal subsidiarity channel that can't bind, and the informal closed room that does bind — do they presuppose the same things, or different things? If they pull in different directions, which one is doing the real work?

4. Where is this practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible views would all license it?

5. Now flip it. Suppose we replaced whatever view is being presupposed with a rival one — pick whichever rival you think is most defensible. What would concretely have to change? Would you keep the closed-door deals and drop the subsidiarity theater, or the reverse, or neither? And does the exit clause, the fact that a member state can now walk out, while the treaties still describe the whole project as an ever-closer union of peoples, make the picture better or worse for whichever theory you land on?

Don't give me a reading list or tell me what scholars have said; I want the argument itself. And don't be diplomatic — if the arrangement is incoherent, say so, and if it's actually fine under some view I haven't thought of, say that too.
