I teach a graduate seminar on embedded agency, and I need a joke to open the next session. By embedded agency I mean the family of problems that appear once you stop picturing the agent as standing outside the world it acts on: the Cartesian boundary between agent and environment, the fact that an action space is typically something the agent must itself discover rather than something handed to it, the impossibility of a world-model that contains a complete picture of the modeller, and the reflection problem of an agent that has to certify its own successor without any increase in proof strength. The students will know this material; the joke has to reward that.

What I want:

- The humor should turn on the substance rather than the trappings. Someone who has genuinely worked on these problems should laugh because the punchline is a real consequence of the setup, while someone who has not should be puzzled rather than amused. No robot jokes, no rogue-AI jokes, nothing that would survive intact if you swapped the subject matter out.
- It should not depend on any particular paper, author, or piece of terminology that I would have to attribute. I want to be able to tell it without footnotes.
- Build it on a specific tension I am stipulating, so that it cannot be a joke I have already heard retold. Call it the Self-Model Dilemma. An agent whose world-model includes its own present deliberation is always modelling a deliberation that has already moved on, so the model is permanently one step behind the thing it was supposed to inform. But an agent whose self-model is complete enough to predict its own output has no deliberation left to perform: if it already knows what it will decide, the deciding is a formality and the deliberative process has no work to do. Incomplete self-model, useless model; complete self-model, no agent.
- Keep it short — a two- or three-line exchange, or one compact scene, deliverable in under thirty seconds.

Then add a sentence or two naming which horn of the dilemma the punchline actually lands on, so that I can tell whether the joke is doing the philosophical work I want or merely gesturing at it.
