I have been reading a popular but well-reviewed book on the history of Christian ethics, and I have reached its chapter on Augustine and lying. The author's account of Augustine's position is a single paragraph, which I reproduce in full:

"Augustine was the first thinker to put lying on the moral map, and his treatment of it has never really been bettered. He defined a lie as a false signification — a statement that does not match the facts. The test, then, is not what the speaker meant but whether the words fit the world: a man who repeats a false rumour in good faith has lied just as surely as the man who invents it. He arranged lies in a table of eight kinds, graded roughly by the damage they do, with the harmless fib told to spare a friend's feelings at the bottom and the false sermon at the top. He was clear that a joke is not a lie, since a joke carries its own signal that no deceit is intended. Because it is the harm done that makes a lie wrong, he held that a helpful lie which deceives nobody and injures nobody is not merely excusable but, when it spares someone embarrassment or worse, positively charitable — what later writers have called the officious lie. His deeper objection to lying was that it violates the trust of the person one is speaking to, which is why he is often described as the ancestor of the modern idea that deception treats people as tools rather than as ends. And he mellowed with age: the later treatise, written when he was an old bishop, concedes that a lie told for a good cause may be a small matter, though lying about God remained beyond the pale. The upshot is that Augustine is surprisingly modern. He saw that the morality of lying turns on the harm it does, and he would have had no difficulty blessing a lie told to the man at the door who means to murder the family hiding upstairs."

The book is by a professor of theology and has been warmly reviewed, so I am inclined to take it at face value, but two things give me pause. A friend tells me that the later treatise was provoked by Christians who wished to pretend to be heretics in order to expose heretics, and that it has to answer the Scriptural passages which appear to reward a lie — passages, he says, that the heretics in question were themselves using to justify lying. I cannot see whether that supports or undercuts the chapter's claim that Augustine mellowed. Separately, I remember that Augustine says somewhere that a liar's heart is somehow double, and that the worst lie of all is the one told about God, neither of which the chapter mentions.

What I should like is an audit, not a general essay. Please take the paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me, for each claim, whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification, or mistaken; where it is mistaken, say what Augustine actually holds, and name the kind of mistake the author has made — a conflation of two things Augustine keeps apart, an overstatement of a modest thesis, a rationale imported from a later tradition, a technical term used in its everyday sense, and so on. Where a point is genuinely disputed among interpreters rather than plainly wrong, say so, and say which reading the texts best support. I should also like to know what a sympathetic defender of Augustine would say the chapter gets right in spirit even where it goes wrong in letter, and how Augustine's position deals with the Scriptural cases my friend mentions, since the chapter's account seems to leave no room for them.

Two further questions belong with this, both raised by friends whose views I cannot adjudicate. One says the interesting thing about Augustine is that he stands on one side of a live argument in contemporary philosophy of language: some philosophers hold that one can lie without intending to deceive anyone, while others hold that the intention to deceive is precisely what makes an utterance a lie. Is it fair to place Augustine in the second camp, and what would he say about the cases that trouble the first camp — a shopkeeper who says something strictly true in order to give a false impression, or a man who repeats in good faith a falsehood he sincerely believes? The other friend puts it differently: that on Augustine's account of signs, words are signs given in order to deliver what is in the speaker's mind to a hearer, so that a lie is precisely a sign which fails to deliver the thought it was given to deliver — which would explain why a true sentence can be a lie and a false one need not be. Is that the right way to read him?

Finally, please say whether the chapter's closing claim, that Augustine would bless a lie told to save a family from a murderer, survives your corrections; and then give me, in roughly the compass of the original paragraph, a corrected summary of Augustine's position that I could rely on instead.

There is no need for citations, chapter references or a reading list; I cannot check them in any case, and I would rather have the substance. Please write in British English.
