I'm a high school student writing a formal essay for my school's philosophy journal, and I want to argue that higher-order theories of consciousness are mistaken, and that a mental state is conscious in virtue of its own intrinsic character rather than in virtue of being represented by some further, higher-order state. Please construct the strongest case for that conclusion. I would like the argument to turn on a scenario I devised rather than on the familiar objections: suppose a creature whose first-order perceptual states are reliably targeted by a higher-order state that classifies them as belonging to a kind for which the creature possesses no concept at all, so that, by the higher-order theorist's own account, the creature undergoes experiences whose character it could never recognize, report, or use. Argue that the higher-order theorist is then forced to choose between admitting conscious states that make no difference to the subject's life and abandoning the claim that the higher-order representation is what makes a state conscious. Then take seriously the most natural reply — that a misrepresenting higher-order state simply yields a different conscious experience rather than a problematic one — and explain why it does not rescue the theory. Keep the essay self-contained and argue from the ideas themselves; do not include citations or a reading list, as I will add those myself.
