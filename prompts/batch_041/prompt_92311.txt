Alright, so I'm about 200 pages into a novel. My guy is a Hamburg reservist, mid forties, a shop manager, drafted late and shoved into one of the police battalions in Poland in '42 — not a fanatic, that's kind of the whole point of him. And I've written the scene everyone told me I had to write: the morning in the forest, the major stands up and says that anyone who doesn't feel up to it can step out, and my man doesn't step out. Fewer than a dozen of the 500 do.

Anyways. Every time I bring this up in workshop somebody says the same line, and it's always meant to end the conversation rather than start one: 'there but for the grace of God go I.' Sometimes it's the cheaper cousin, 'anyone would've done the same in his shoes.' It shows up whenever I ask the question I actually care about — is this man a monster, or just a man having a very bad few years — and the line is supposed to mean the question is naive, or vulgar, or that I should stop navel-gazing and go write.

I dont believe it means one thing. I think it's doing four or five different jobs and people slide between them without noticing. So pull it apart for me. I want the whole menu: every precise claim that sentence (and its cousin) could actually be making, from the flatly trivial to the ones that are real theses about how human beings work. For each, say whether it's plausible, and — this is the part I need — whether it's the kind of claim that would actually license what the workshop is using it to license, which is 'so you can't judge him.' A few of them I suspect are really claims about the speaker's own self-image and have nothing to do with my character; a couple might make him worse rather than better, and I want to know which.

Related, and maybe the same question: does it matter whether the claim is about what most people would have done in that situation, versus about what actually caused this particular man to act, versus about what his behaviour tells us about his character? Those feel like different animals to me and everyone keeps swapping one for the other mid-argument.

I've read Browning and I've read Goldhagen, so please don't just rehash that fight at me — I need the sentence untangled, not the historiography. And I need to know which reading would tell me what scene to write next: if it's the situation, maybe there's no scene of choice to write at all; if it's character, I have to go back to chapter 3 and plant something. Give it to me as a numbered menu, each entry a sentence or two, with your verdict attached.

Last thing — the phrase is religious, isn't it? I have a hunch the original use isn't exculpatory at all, more like a confession, and that people have quietly flipped it into an excuse. If that's right, say so, because it changes what I do with the ending.
