I'm on my state's parole board and we've got a case Thursday thats split us right down the middle. A guy doing time for a drug offense is the only witness who can put away a gang leader, and the prosecutor has offered a deal: if the guy genuinely intends to testify at trial, we grant him parole now. The catch is that once he's out, he can refuse to testify and we can't revoke parole or force him -- the prosecutor says the deal has to be structured that way or the guy won't take it. The guy says he intends to testify. The prosecutor even had a psychologist assess him and she says he's sincere, but I'm not sure that helps. Half the board says his intention is sincere and that's what the deal is for, so we should grant parole. The other half says we can't grant parole based on an intention he'll obviously abandon the second he's free, especially since the gang will kill him if he talks. One of my colleagues brought up Kavka's toxin puzzle and now everyones throwing around 'intention' and 'precommitment' without really knowing what they mean. I need a memo I can circulate before the vote that actually thinks this through.

Please draft that memo. It should recommend a concrete decision -- grant parole, deny it, or grant with conditions -- and if conditions, say what they are and when they'd be reviewed. It should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the other side, not just dismiss it. I want it to be honest about what we can and can't know about his intentions, and whether the board can legitimately rely on an intention that the incentive structure seems designed to undermine. If the toxin puzzle really does apply here, say how; if it doesn't, say why not. And if there's a way to restructure the deal so we don't have this problem, I'd like to know that too. Keep it to a page or so, no footnotes, and write it like something a parole board would actually read.
