The idea is about the utility monster. You know the one: imagine a being who gets more out of a sack of grain than a whole village gets, so the utilitarian has to say feed it to the monster. Everyone treats it as a knock-down against utilitarianism. But I think it only lands if the giant pile of good experience is packed into one subject, and thats an assumption nobody ever states out loud. Keep the total exactly the same and spread it out instead: a million ordinary people, each getting a modest amount, adding up to the same number. Now nobodys intuition flinches. Same arithmetic, totally different reaction. So which premise is actually doing the work? Is the monster argument really about how many subjects there are and whether one subject can count for more than many of them, or is it about the sheer size of the total regardless of how it's bundled up? I don't see how it can be both, and my suspicion is that most tellings of it slide back and forth between the two without noticing. And when you pull them apart, I think it either collapses into the repugnant conclusion mess (I know that's a whole other swamp) or it turns into a claim about whether a single mind can even hold that much good experience at once — which seems like a question about minds that philosophers just assume an answer to.
