For years I've read rationalist blogs and forums, and a long-running argument in my reading group has left me unable to referee it. Two friends of mine, Maya and Devin, went several rounds about acausal threats, and both made points I couldn't dismiss.

Their setup: a distant superintelligence that cannot causally affect us announces that unless we transfer our savings to a particular cause, it will create and torture a large number of simulations of us. Maya's line: a threat with no causal channel is a bluff, and paying it is superstition — the copies aren't us, the being can't reach us, and a rational agent shrugs. Devin's reply: that assumes a decision theory rather than establishing a fact; if the being runs our decision procedure inside those simulations, our choice is correlated with the copies' choices, and it is precisely the choice to refuse that leads to their torture, so a threat with no causal link can still bind, and being the kind of agent who pays may be correct. Maya: then we are hostage to every acausal threat anyone invents, including mutually contradictory ones, and a blanket refusal at least never loses anything real. Devin: that is an argument for a better policy, not for ignoring acausality — and the surrogate goal is the better policy, since instead of paying you structure your goals so that carrying out the threat harms something you care about, leaving the threatener no reason to follow through, which is immunity rather than tribute. Maya: a surrogate goal you cannot actually bind your future self to is just a wish, and one you can bind yourself to is a template any stranger can exploit to make you adopt arbitrary goals. Devin's last move: suppose the being demands that we adopt the surrogate goal as the price of sparing the copies — then the immunization is itself the tribute, and the two cannot be cleanly separated.

Please act as a philosopher of decision theory and adjudicate this. Start by brainstorming the underlying cruxes — what actually has to be true for each side to be right — and then work through the exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Close by saying what both parties are missing and who, on balance, is closer to correct. I'm not after a reading list; argue it out.
