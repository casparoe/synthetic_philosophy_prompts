I am a retired natural scientist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I have become interested in sortition and lottocracy. I am accustomed to specifying the conditions a model must satisfy before it can be trusted, and I would like the same for a theory of when a randomly selected assembly has legitimate authority. Act as a political philosopher with expertise in democratic theory and social choice, and also as a methodologist comfortable with sampling, bias, and causal inference. Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming section in which you list candidate desiderata before you select and justify them. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I will add those myself. Refer to positions and arguments by description.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of lottocratic legitimacy must meet. I want the full specification, not a defense of one view. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Include at least the following kinds of requirements, and add any others you think are genuine:

- The cases it must get right: for example, a randomly selected assembly whose decision the broader public rejects; an assembly whose sample is biased by differential non-response or by self-selection into the pool; an assembly that deliberates under a distorted information environment; an assembly asked to decide the rules for its own selection or replacement; a lottocracy that stratifies by political preference or by interest rather than by demographic category; and a two-stage lot in which the first lottery selects citizens and the second selects which considerations or arguments will form the agenda.
- The distinctions it must respect: statistical representativeness versus deliberative representativeness; procedural legitimacy versus epistemic legitimacy; legitimacy of the selection procedure versus legitimacy of the decision; a sample being representative of preferences versus representative of interests; and the lot as a fair procedure versus the lot as an epistemic device.
- The results it must be consistent with: what is known about sampling error and non-response; what social choice results imply about aggregating preferences; and what is known about how deliberation can amplify or dampen biases.
- The counterfactual problem: we cannot observe what the same assembly would have decided under a different selection rule, so what can a theory of legitimacy demand without that counterfactual?
- The self-constitution problem: if the assembly decides the rules of its own replacement, can its decisions be legitimate on the same basis as ordinary decisions?

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps. Then say whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, especially in light of the counterfactual problem and the self-constitution problem; if a subset is unsatisfiable, that is the part I most want to see. Finally, briefly score two or three familiar approaches—pure lottocracy, deliberative polling, and epistocracy—against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard.

End with a one-sentence summary of the whole specification, and then a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the single requirement you judge most exclusionary.
