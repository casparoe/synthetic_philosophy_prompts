I am a law-and-economics scholar with a working command of entitlements, property rules, liability rules, inalienability rules, externalities, transaction costs, consent, agency, and the distinction between harming someone and merely failing to benefit them. I have no theological training, and I am not moved by arguments whose force depends on premises about God's purposes. I want to understand Hume's case that suicide is not a violation of duty by seeing it restated as faithfully as possible in my own vocabulary. Treat Hume's discussion as having three branches: the alleged duty to God, the alleged duty to society, and the alleged duty to self. For each branch, give me the best law-and-economics translation you can: identify the entitlement at issue, who holds it, what kind of rule protects it, what would count as a violation, what externalities or agency problems are in play, and what would make the duty binding. Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in whether the duty to God reduces to an inalienability rule backed by a superior's property claim, whether the duty to society is an externality argument that Hume has already defeated, and whether the duty to self is either paternalism or a category mistake. Finally, tell me whether the residue is a defect of my framework, something law and economics cannot see, or a sign that Hume's original argument is less clear than it looks. I want the translation and the residue, not a reading list.
