I'm a doctoral student in philosophy working on the transcendental-argument tradition, and I keep encountering in conversation a comparison that I have never seen executed with any care, so I'd like you to carry it out properly. On one side stands the canonical Kantian form: an argument that isolates a condition without which experience — or self-consciousness, or the unity of a manifold — could not occur at all, and that treats the denial of that condition as covertly presupposing it. On the other side stands the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument as it functions in linguistics: the child's exposure to the data is too thin to determine the grammar she ends up acquiring, therefore she must be bringing something to the task innately and domain-specifically.

I want a detailed comparison of these two argument-forms, not a survey of either. Please do the following.

First, set out the abstract structure of each: the premises, the inferential move, the modality of the conclusion, and the kind of opponent each is designed to defeat. Then say where exactly they diverge — at a premise, at the inference, or at the strength of the modal operator.

Second, test the linguistic argument against a criterion I have been using to sort genuine transcendental arguments from pretenders. The criterion is this: the argument must establish that the denial of the alleged condition is not merely false but self-undermining — that one cannot so much as coherently entertain the denial without relying on the very thing denied. Does the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument satisfy that criterion? If it does not, say so plainly, and identify which of its steps would have to change for it to qualify.

Third, apply the reverse pressure. If the linguistic argument is not transcendental in the strict sense, is the Kantian form thereby exposed as an unusually strong instance of something more ordinary — an inference to the best explanation with a modal flourish? Or does the comparison show that the Kantian form performs a kind of work no explanatory inference can perform?

Fourth, I have a suspicion I would like you to assess rather than flatter: that the two arguments draw their apparent strength from opposite sources — the Kantian from the supposed unintelligibility of the denial, the linguistic one from the supposed poverty of the evidence — and that this difference matters far more than the shared condition-of-possibility framing suggests. Is the suspicion correct, and what follows for how each argument should be attacked?

Throughout, assume I know both literatures well; skip the exposition and go straight to the joints. What I am most interested in is what the comparison costs each side.
