I have been arguing with friends about a family of cases that all turn on weighing a single enormous harm against a great many vanishingly small ones, and we keep reaching for a verdict before we have agreed on what a verdict would even have to satisfy. Start from the crude case — one person undergoing prolonged agony against an enormous number of people each suffering a single momentary speck of grit in the eye, nothing lasting, nothing anyone would recall — and then bend it in ways that seem to us to matter. Make the many small harms fall on one person in sequence rather than on many; make each of them too brief to be noticed at all while still leaving a measurable stress response; make each of them individually compensable at a penny apiece where the agony is not compensable on any terms; make them foreseen and dreaded rather than unforeseen; make the person who suffers the agony someone already in constant severe pain, so that a speck adds almost nothing to her; and finally replace the certain small harms with a tiny independent chance of the agony for each person, so that the question becomes one about risk rather than about harm done. Before I take a side on any of these, I want the specification rather than the verdict.

So please write me the desiderata a theory of aggregating tiny harms against large ones would actually have to deliver. I want the cases it must get right, each with the consideration that makes it a case the theory is answerable to; the distinctions it must not collapse, such as that between how bad a harm is and how many people bear it, or between a harm's being imperceptible and its being negligible, or between a harm's being small and its being freely tradeable against more of the same; the formal constraints it has to respect, of which I would count the transitivity of the better-than relation and the requirement that adding one more speck to a pile never cause the pile as a whole to stop mattering; and the findings or platitudes it must stay consistent with, for instance that harms admit of arbitrarily fine gradations and that some harms are treated as lying beyond compensation altogether. For every requirement, say what motivates it. Then tell me which of these demands pull against one another and cannot be jointly met — I suspect the sorites-flavoured demands and the aggregation demands do, but I want your diagnosis of exactly where the conflict lies and whether any requirement has to be surrendered — and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the theories people actually hold.

Close with a short assessment, in prose, of how the main families of answer fare against the list: the thoroughgoing totalist, the lexical or threshold view that refuses to trade the agony against any quantity of specks, the person-affecting view that only counts harms to identifiable individuals, and the view that denies the comparison is meaningful at all. Please write this as continuous prose, the way one would set it out in a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — and in British English.
