I'm not a philosopher, I just read a lot of it and I manage a small team, and there's a pattern I keep running into that I dont have a word for.

Here it is. Everyone already knows the thing — the deadline is impossible, the plan is bad, the office kitchen is disgusting. Everyone also knows that everyone else knows; there's no secret and nobody's fooling themselves. And nothing happens. Then one person says it out loud, once, in a normal voice, in front of everybody, and within a week the whole situation flips, and afterward people talk as if it had always been obvious and there was never any gap at all. What bugs me isn't secrecy, because there wasn't any. The fact was fully known the whole time and it did zero work until it got said.

Second case, same shape but more personal. Two people in a long partnership, both privately sure it should end, both sure the other is sure. Nothing happens for a year. Then one of them says it, once, and nobody is surprised.

But here's the case that wrecks my theory. Everyone I know dislikes the restaurant we keep going to. We have all said so, out loud, to each other, many times, in groups. Nothing ever changes. So saying it in public can't be the whole mechanism. (Saying it in private definately isn't — someone told me months ago, just me, that the deadline was impossible, and I did nothing about it.)

My hunch is that what flips a situation isn't information, since nothing was unknown, but something about who has to go first, or what it costs to be the one who says it, or what becomes allowed once somebody has said it. I can't tell if that's one thing or three, and I can't tell whether the missing piece is really knowing the fact or knowing that other people will actually act on it — which feels like a different kind of thing to know.

So: is there an established name for the gap between everyone knowing a thing and it being out in the open? More to the point, does the best-fitting name actually cover cases like mine, or only the easy ones? And what separates the cases that flip from the restaurant that doesn't?

Don't just hand me a label and stop — the edges are the point. Write it in American English, skip the reading list, and give me these sections in this order:

1. "Short answer" — three to five sentences. Does it have a name, and what's the closest fit.
2. "Closest existing terms" — three named concepts, about 120 words each: what each one covers, and exactly where it stops covering my cases.
3. "What saying it out loud actually does" — about 250 words, and say plainly if the answer is that it isn't about knowledge at all.
4. "The case that doesn't fit" — about 150 words on my restaurant case and what it rules out.
5. "If nothing fits, coin it" — a name, a one-sentence definition, and three boundary cases: one clearly in, one clearly out, one you're undecided about.
