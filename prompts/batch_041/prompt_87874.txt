In the control room of a sleep laboratory, between the third and fourth awakenings of the night, two philosophers of mind are arguing about what the recordings could possibly establish. The first holds that dreams are experiences: a dream report given on waking is a fallible memory of a phenomenal episode that genuinely occurred during sleep, and the laboratory is in principle an instrument for dating and describing such episodes. The second holds that dreams are not experiences: nothing phenomenal need have happened during the sleep at all, dreaming is a waking achievement (the production of a narrative, the insertion of a memory), and the question of what a dream was like at 3:40 a.m. is ill-formed rather than merely hard to answer.

Write their dialogue. Let their argument run over the laboratory's own paradigms rather than over generalities about consciousness. Among the cases on the table:

- a subject who, having agreed on a distinctive pattern of eye movements in advance, produces that pattern during a REM period and afterwards reports having been lucid and having deliberately signalled;
- a subject woken by a stimulus delivered during REM — a name spoken over the intercom, a spray of water — who reports a long dream in which the stimulus figures as a natural element, as though the dream had already been running for minutes before the stimulus arrived;
- a subject woken at the same point in the REM cycle on two successive nights who reports two quite different dreams, and a subject woken from REM who reports nothing at all;
- a lucid subject asked to signal the beginning and the end of a pre-agreed task, whose two signals either do or do not bracket the task's real duration.

Each character should press that the other's reading of a case is question-begging or empirically empty. Have the defender of dream experiences say what the deliberate signalling adds that a sufficiently clever non-experiential process could not have supplied, and have the sceptic say what would count as evidence that a dream happened at 3:40 rather than being confabulated at 6:00. Neither should be a strawman: each should concede the other's strongest point and then try to absorb it. End with the disagreement sharper than it began rather than resolved.
