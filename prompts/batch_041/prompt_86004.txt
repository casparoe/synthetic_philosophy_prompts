Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the candidate implications before you commit to anything.

Here's the idea I keep circling around. Everyone accepts that natural selection produces things that look designed — a heart, a wing, an enzyme. Fine, that's the easy part. The sharper claim is that these things can carry a rationale: the arrangement encodes something like an inference, do X because Y, where Y is a fact about the world, without any mind ever having drawn that inference. The reason is real, but there was never a reasoner; it floats free. My favourite example for getting students going: a bacterium that cannot tell which way the food is getting better — it can only register that things are better or worse than they were a second ago — and the trick it has ended up with is to keep swimming when conditions improve and to reorient at random when they don't. That is a genuine solution to a genuine problem, namely following a gradient you cannot sense, and nobody solved it. Not the bacterium, not a designer.

So: what are the most important implications of taking that seriously? I want your top handful, ranked by importance, with the reasoning shown, not just a list. In particular I want to know what it does to two things.

(a) What am I entitled to say about purposes in nature? Is there still a real difference between the heart is for pumping and the heart pumps, or is the teleology now just a useful fiction that I should stop teaching as if it were a claim about the world?

(b) Does the idea have a principled stopping point? My colleague in the biology department keeps needling me: if selection can produce reasons without a reasoner, why not say the thermostat has a reason to fire, or the market has a reason to reprice, or a chatbot has reasons for what it tells me? I think he's wrong, but I've never been able to say propperly why, and I'm not confident the usual moves — no representation, no function, no learning — survive the case I just gave you.
