ok so I'm in my school's philosphy club and I have this view I can't shake, and I want someone to actually try to knock it down properly.

The view: it's not just allowed but often good for people and countries to tie their own hands ahead of time. Ulysses has his crew lash him to the mast so he can't jump in when the sirens start singing. A country writes something into its constitution that a later majority isn't allowed to undo, however big that majority is. Me during exam week, locking my phone in one of those boxes with a timer on it. In all three cases the point is that the future you — future me, a future majority — is exactly the thing you need protecting from. So when someone says but the future majority won't want that rule anymore, I don't think that's an objection, I think that's the whole design. A precommitment you can't be tempted to break isn't a precommitment, it's just a preference.

What I want from you is the honest case against it. Not the cheap version — what if you precommit to something evil, yeah fine, but that's true of literally every act. I mean the stuff that should make me either drop the view or shrink it into something less exciting. Rank them, most damaging first, and tell me which ones a smart defender would just shrug at versus which ones they'd have to actually give ground on. And be honest at the end about whether the view has quietly turned into something that can't lose — if every counterexample gets answered with then it wasn't a real precommitment, that seems like a problem, but maybe it isn't?

Two things I made up myself that I want you to take seriously, because the standard examples feel too easy:

1. The emergency button. My lockbox actually has a button that opens it any time, but pressing it charges me $200 automatically. Is that still tying my hands, or is it just a fine for changing my mind? And if it's just a fine, does that mean every real precommitment is either fake (you can always get out) or dangerous (you can't)? I feel like there's supposed to be no third option and I don't like it.

2. The crew. Ulysses only stays tied up because other people tied him and choose not to untie him. Same with a constitution — the courts and the police are the crew. But the crew has its own interests, and unlike Ulysses's crew, they get to keep the rope long after the guy who asked for it is gone. Does the whole analogy fall apart once the enforcer isn't the same person as the one being bound? Or is that just what a constitution is, and it's fine?

Other stuff I'm unsure about: does this view secretly assume my present self is more real or more rational than my future self, and is that even a coherent thing to believe? If changing your mind is usually right and only sometimes weak, doesn't that make hard precommitment a bad bet most of the time? And does it change anything that a constitution binds people who never agreed to it, while Ulysses only binds himself?

Don't give me a reading list or names to go look up, I can't go online — just the arguments, and tell me what would actually move me.

The thing that would move me most: a case where tying your hands makes you worse off in a way you couldn't have seen coming, and where staying flexible would've been better. If there's a clean example of that, I think I have to give up the strong version.
