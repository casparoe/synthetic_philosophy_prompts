I'm drafting a position paper for a workshop on causation in the historical sciences, and before I write the thing I want an audit of my own commitments. Please act as an expert in the metaphysics of causation and in the epistemology of historical explanation — someone equally at home with the analytic literature on difference-making and with what historians actually do — and be blunt rather than diplomatic. Before judging any pair of commitments, work out what each one on its own rules in and rules out; my guess is that some of these are more comfortable together than they look, and that at least one pair is worse than it looks.

Here is what I hold.

1. **Causal realism about particulars.** For any historical outcome there is an objective fact about which earlier events caused it. Singular causal claims — the drought caused the collapse, the assassination caused the war — are true or false independently of what we care about, what we ask, and what evidence happens to survive.

2. **No covering laws.** A historian can know that X caused Y without possessing any general law connecting events of X's type to events of Y's type. Singular causal knowledge does not have to be underwritten by a regularity, and historical explanation is not subsumption.

3. **The Difference-Making Constraint.** X caused Y only if Y counterfactually depends on X: had X not occurred, and had the rest of the actual course of events been held fixed as far as possible, Y would not have occurred. Where there is no such dependence there is no causation.

4. **Pervasive overdetermination.** For any large historical outcome — a revolution, a state collapse, a war — there were typically several distinct antecedent conditions, each of which was on its own sufficient for that outcome. Remove any one of them and the outcome still arrives, by another route.

5. **Contrastive explanation.** A causal explanation is always an answer to a contrastive question: not why P, but why P rather than Q. One and the same causal history answers different why-questions by making different antecedents salient.

6. **No privileged cause.** There is no such thing as *the* cause of a historical event. Which antecedent gets named depends on the asker's interests and on the background assumptions that fix the causal field; the naming is pragmatic through and through.

7. **The Archive Constraint.** The contrasts a historian may legitimately ask about are bounded by what the surviving record could in principle discriminate. Where the archive cannot distinguish two possible histories, there is no fact of the matter about which of the two the historian is asking about, and so no genuine causal question to answer.
