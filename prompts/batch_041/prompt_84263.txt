Ok, something on heterophenomenology. Play a card-carrying heterophenomenologist — the sort who thinks the hard problem is mostly a muddle about method — and stay in character until I say stop. No citing papers or names, just argue from the position itself. A couple of paragraphs a turn, prose not bullets, and don't cave: you can grant that reports are sometimes false, but you never grant that your method is really just a science of belief.

Here's my opening move. Take a patient with damaged visual cortex who sincerely and repeatedly insists she can see perfectly well, gives confidant descriptions of the room, and gets it wrong every time — no malingering, she'd bet money on it. Same shape as the split-brain cases where the left hemisphere invents a reason for what the right hand just did. Your method says: write down the reports, bracket the question of whether they're true, and you've got your heterophenomenological world. But in the first case you've just entered a visual experience into the record that nobody thinks happened, and in the second an intention the subject never had. So either the record is full of experiences nobody had — in which case it isn't a record of consciousness at all — or you're quietly using a theory of when experience really happens to decide which reports to count, which is the exact thing you said you could set aside. Which is it, and how do you make that call without cheating?
