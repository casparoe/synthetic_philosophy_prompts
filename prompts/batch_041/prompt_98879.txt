Last night a friend and I argued for close to three hours about whether things like tipping, queueing, and jaywalking amount to genuine moral obligations, and whether a visitor to a place is bound by the local versions of them; we each dug in, and by the end neither of us could state the other's position in a way the other would accept. I would like you to write the document we should have written together instead.

Take the role of a professional philosopher whose areas are social ontology, the philosophy of convention, and normative ethics, and who is practiced at adversarial collaboration. Work carefully and do not rush toward a verdict; your task is not to adjudicate who is right but to produce a text that both my friend and I could put our names to.

Before drafting anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming. Lay out the space of positions between us, note which distinctions look load-bearing (obligation versus mere advisability, wrongdoing versus rudeness, the local versus the outsider, an entrenched convention versus a brand-new one, an arbitrary convention versus a useful one), and flag anything that seems likely to be a verbal dispute rather than a real one. Then write the joint statement.

My friend's side, as fairly as I can state it: norms such as tipping a customary percentage, joining the back of a queue, and crossing against a light when everyone around you does are just coordinated expectations. They may be prudent or polite to observe, but they carry no independent moral weight, and an outsider who ignores them — absent separate facts about harm or rights — wrongs no one. My side: when a convention is locally entrenched and people actually rely on it, disregarding it is not merely gauche; it can be a genuine failure of respect or fairness, and a visitor who flouts it may owe something to those who were counting on it.

To keep the dispute honest, bracket two things on both sides: the law (assume none of the acts in question is illegal) and physical risk (assume no one is endangered). Keep the cases small and concrete, and make the document self-contained — describe any idea you use in plain terms rather than pointing to a literature; assume the reader has no library and no internet.

The document should contain four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, each stated so that neither side is smuggling in its conclusion. Second, the precise remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording that the side holding it would accept as a fair statement of its own view, with the other side's version set alongside. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that a definite answer would move at least one side — and, for each, a concrete case or consideration both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's predicted verdict on that case, recorded in advance. Fourth, a short note on any part of the disagreement you judge to be merely verbal, and why.

As a stimulus for the crux section, test the document against at least these three cases, and add any others you find useful: (a) a visitor from a place with no tipping custom who leaves nothing at a restaurant where the local custom is to tip, in a city where servers are paid on the assumption of tips; (b) a newcomer who crosses mid-block in a city where nearly everyone jaywalks, at a spot where no driver is present; (c) a visitor who, in a country where queueing is strictly observed, walks to the front of a long line at a busy stall because that is ordinary where they come from. For each case, say what each side predicts, and whether the case genuinely separates the two views or merely feels as though it should.

Format the result as follows: a short preamble, the brainstorming notes, the numbered agreed claims, a disagreement table with columns for the question, side A's formulation, side B's formulation, and whether the gap is substantive or verbal, then the cruxes with the pre-registered predictions, then the closing note on verbal disputes.
