In a seminar on religion and the state, a proposal to let the revenue authority collect a levy on behalf of religious bodies was disposed of with the remark that it violates the separation of church and state. I could not have said which of several quite different claims that remark was making, and I have come to suspect the slogan does different work in different mouths.

Please set out the precise readings the phrase could carry, ordered from the nearly trivial to the strongly contested: that there is no state church; that public money must not reach religion; that religious doctrine must not enter law or public reason; that faith is a private matter; that the state must be neutral among religions and between religion and non-religion; that religious symbols have no place in public institutions; that conscience must not be coerced. For each reading, say what it would and would not forbid, whether it is a platitude, a substantive political commitment, or a claim that no actual state satisfies, and which reading the seminar remark actually needed.

Then test the readings against four arrangements, which I understand to be real. In the first, a church is established by law — its bishops sit in the legislature by right and the head of state is its supreme governor — yet it levies no religious tax on its members and is largely self-financing. In the second, no church is established, but the tax authority collects a religious levy from members of recognized religious bodies, who escape it only by a formal declaration of leaving made to the state. In the third, a constitutionally secular republic pays the salaries of recognized clergy and provides confessional religious instruction in state schools in one region, under a treaty predating its secular settlement. In the fourth, school-sponsored prayer is unconstitutional, while the legislature's chaplains are paid from public funds and churches are exempt from property tax. Say for each arrangement which readings it satisfies and which it violates, and whether these four are really four positions or a smaller number of dimensions crossing. I am also told that in some of these places the phrase is not used at all, and that the local vocabulary of neutrality, laïcité, and establishment does not translate cleanly into the others; if that is right, say which reading each local vocabulary encodes.
