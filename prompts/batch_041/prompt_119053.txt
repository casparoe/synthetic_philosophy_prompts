My reading group has spent three meetings on whether it makes sense to hold degrees of belief about mathematical claims, and one of us, a statistician, closes every discussion with the same analogy. Suppose I flip a coin and cover it with a cup before anyone has looked. There is a fact of the matter about how it landed; my uncertainty is simply ignorance of a determinate fact; one half is the right credence; and nothing mysterious is happening. Undecided conjectures are the same, he says: the cup has not yet been lifted, and a proof would be the lifting. On his view that dissolves the puzzle. There is nothing special about so-called logical uncertainty; ordinary probability theory applies to arithmetic exactly as it applies to the coin; the only genuine labour is choosing a prior over mathematical propositions; and our inability to declare an undecided conjecture certainly true or certainly false is no more troubling than declining to say how a hidden coin landed. He also points to a construction he read about, in which a reasoner is modelled as a market of traders buying and selling shares in sentences, where a share in a sentence pays a dollar if the sentence is true and its price plays the role of a probability. The construction is computable, is built so that no computable trading strategy can make an unbounded profit, assigns prices to every sentence in a formal language and refines them over time, learns the patterns of truth and falsehood in a language long before it can actually evaluate the statements, becomes coherent in the limit, and even holds sensible beliefs about its own beliefs without tripping over the usual self-reference paradoxes. That, he says, is the cup being lifted, and it shows there was never a special problem here.

I am not persuaded, and what I want is an audit of the analogy rather than an explanation of it. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the coin, the flip, the cup, the lifting of the cup, the physical chance process that makes one half the correct answer, and the fact that the coin's landing had a causal history and might have gone otherwise — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in what the coin case quietly supplies and the mathematical case may lack: whether there is any counterpart to the symmetrisation argument that fixes the fair-coin prior, whether it matters that the coin's outcome is independent of my reasoning about it while my mathematical beliefs are themselves the output of reasoning that can go wrong, and whether the fact that a proof of one statement would immediately move my credences in everything it implies — including infinitely many statements I have never entertained — has any analogue in a coin I merely have not looked at. Then say whether his conclusion survives: is the puzzle really dissolved, or does the analogy presuppose precisely what is in dispute, namely that there is a determinate fact of the kind a credence can be graded against together with a natural measure on the space of such facts, so that the apparent dissolving of the puzzle is just the assumption that the puzzle is soluble? If the conclusion does not survive, sketch the analogy you would substitute and say what it captures that the coin does not. Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
