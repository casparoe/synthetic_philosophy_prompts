I am a high school student writing a seminar essay on the uses and limits of applying a thinker from the past to present-day controversies, and I would like you to act as a professor of political and social theory who has spent a career working on Max Weber. Please think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming rather than by staking out a position.

The controversy I have chosen is nuclear power. Weber died in 1920, before fission had been discovered, so he had no view of it; but the two countries whose politics he knew best have since taken opposite paths. Germany built up a nuclear industry and then dismantled it, with the last three reactors shut down in April 2023, the culmination of a movement that began with the protests of the 1970s at Wyhl and Gorleben and eventually produced the Greens. France, after the 1973 oil shock, built a fleet that still runs 56 reactors and supplies roughly 65 to 75 percent of its electricity under a state-owned utility, and is now planning to build more — though its ageing plants have proved fragile, with corrosion problems taking much of the fleet offline in 2022 and output falling to a decades-low, and its first new reactor in a generation, at Flamanville, arriving about seventeen years after construction began and at several times the original budget. The EU, meanwhile, has labelled nuclear power a transitional green investment, and Germany has at times been importing French nuclear electricity while closing its own plants.

What I want from you is a reconstruction, not a survey. Which of Weber's ideas do the real work here — the distinction between an ethic of conviction and an ethic of responsibility, rationalisation and disenchantment, his conception of the state, his writings on Germany's standing among the great powers — and what would he have concluded about the German exit and the French fleet? Is the ethic of responsibility determinate in this case, or does it genuinely leave the question open? Where would his temperament and his nationalism have pulled against his own theoretical commitments? Would he have been willing to treat a dread-inducing but statistically small risk (a reactor accident, the residual risk the German debate insists cannot be engineered away) and a diffuse but near-certain one (emissions, and the coal Germany burned instead) as commensurable at all? And what would he say is the real question the current debate avoids?

Please close by separating the claims that follow securely from his own arguments from the ones that are really my speculation dressed up as his. Do not invent quotations for him; I do not want fake Weber quotes in my essay.
