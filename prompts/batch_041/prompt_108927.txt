The old line at our plant shuts down at the end of the month, and my local's putting on a farewell dinner for the twenty-two night-shift lads and lasses who are getting let go. The company's paying for the hall, the plant manager speaks first (it'll be all "the future is now, exciting new chapter"), and then me, because I'm the shop steward. Awkward part: I'm not going anywhere, I'm staying on to run the new line, and the room knows it.

I've got six minutes, call it 900 words, and I want it to be true, which is turning out to be the hard bit. I can't stand up there and say the machines are just tools, because that's the manager's line and it's a lie — the new line runs to a cycle time, and sure, the operator can technically pause it, but pause it and your behind, the line stops, and forty people turn round and look at you. The pace isn't yours anymore. But I also can't call the machines the enemy: Terry, who's on days, helped build this one and he's a decent man, and the same machine run by us instead of for them would be a three-day week — I honestly believe that. I think the true thing is that the machine isn't taking their jobs, the decision to buy it is, but I can't say that at a dinner without it sounding like something off a badge.

Marx had the distinction, machinery as such vs machinery as capital, and the bit about the first Luddites going for the frames when the real target was the people who owned them. Use that. But don't quote him — I can't check a quote and half the room wouldn't know who he was. No citations, no reading list, alot of these people are burying a job, they don't want homework.

So: write the speech. It has to comfort twenty-two people who are losing their jobs without telling them it's fine, it has to keep the peace with the manager sitting two tables over, and it has to deal with the fact that I'm the one who'll be running the thing. Land it somewhere usable, not just sad.
