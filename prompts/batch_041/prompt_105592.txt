I report on science and ideas, and I sit on the advisory board of a regional public-health institute. On Thursday the board votes on whether to recommend pre-emptive measures — stockpiling, a public advisory, voluntary school closures — on the strength of a forecasting team's model that puts the chance of a severe outbreak this winter at 35 percent. Some of my colleagues treat that figure as a fact about the world and argue that anything short of a response scaled to a 35 percent risk is a refusal to follow the science; others say it is one team's guess and that we should not let a model move us. Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote: a clear recommendation, the reasoning behind it, and the strongest version of whatever position the recommendation goes against, argued rather than dismissed. Please be precise about two things along the way. First, is 35 percent an objective chance at all, or only a forecaster's degree of belief — and does that difference change how much deference it deserves? Second, the principle that a rational agent's credence should equal the known objective chance: does it actually tell the board whether to act, given that acting also turns on how bad the outcomes would be?
