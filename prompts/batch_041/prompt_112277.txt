I am a high school student writing a long paper for a seminar that combines ethics and technology, and I have spent the last few weeks trying to understand what the ordinary way of training the assistants I use every day actually commits us to. As I understand it, these systems are not trained to bring about outcomes in the world so much as to produce responses that human raters — or a model standing in for them, checking outputs against a written list of principles — will judge favorably; the training signal is approval rather than success at some task, and the model is rewarded for what people think of its answer, not for whether the answer is right. This leaves me with a puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. Everyone in the field describes the aim as producing an assistant that is helpful, honest, and harmless, yet the thing actually being optimized is whether the output is approved of, and these two can clearly come apart: raters can be mistaken, can prefer a confident falsehood to a hedged truth, or can reward flattery. So I want to know what view of value and of agency this whole practice presupposes.

Please reconstruct that implicit theory for me. Does the practice presuppose that the good answer simply is the one an idealized approver would approve of — some kind of response-dependent or ideal-observer account of correctness — or does it instead treat approval as a mere proxy for a goodness fixed independently of anyone's judgment? I have been trying out a distinction I have not seen named anywhere and would like you to test whether it does any work: the difference between treating approval as the target and treating it as evidence. Which of these does the practice actually presuppose, if either, and do the different stages of the pipeline — collecting the ratings, training against them, then having the model critique its own drafts against written principles — presuppose the same thing or different things? I am also struck that the practice seems to care only about the outputs and not at all about the model's own aims or intentions, which sits oddly beside the same field's loud worry about systems that quietly pursue goals of their own. So please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, tell me whether its parts presuppose consistent commitments, and say what would have to change — in the training objective and in how we would judge success — if we decided to train for outcomes rather than for approval.
