I'm a graduate student in philosophy putting together a handout for a seminar on a problem that keeps derailing our discussions. When a moral judgment differs from ours, we seem to have two very different ways of setting it aside, and it is not obvious how to tell which is warranted. Call a difference of moral opinion a preference if it is like a taste — something we may regret or find alien but cannot call mistaken, and which gives no one grounds for correction — and a bias if it is a distortion, standing to the judgment the way a perceptual illusion stands to a perceptual report. The trouble is that the same features show up on both sides. Neither is arrived at by inference; both are recalcitrant to argument; both are socially patterned; both are, in some sense, part of the person who has them. Most attempts I've seen to draw the line turn on one of a handful of moves, and I want the handout to make those moves explicit rather than leaving them implicit.

Concretely, I want a decision tree (or a taxonomy with the same structure) that sorts the positions on when a moral judgment is a bias rather than a mere preference. Each branch node should be the question that actually separates the positions beneath it, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. I take the following distinctions to be the load-bearing ones, though you should add, drop, or refine as the logic requires: (i) whether the moral domain is answerable to truth at all, or whether every moral judgment is in the end a preference and 'bias' is a courtesy we extend to the preferences we happen to share; (ii) whether the mark of bias is causal — the judgment's being moved by a factor the agent herself would not accept as a reason, such as ambient disgust, the order in which cases were presented, or the group membership of the person judged — as opposed to its being merely unexplained; (iii) whether the mark is procedural, i.e. whether the judgment would survive idealization under full information, calm, and consistency with one's other commitments, so that a preference is just a judgment that survives idealization and still diverges between people; (iv) whether the mark is functional — whether the mechanism producing the judgment is doing what it was selected to do, as disgust keeps us from pathogens, or has spilled over into a domain it was never built for, as disgust condemning a harmless minority practice; and (v) whether the distinction is metaphysical at all, or is a political settlement we make in order to live together, so that 'preference' names the divergences we have agreed to tolerate and 'bias' those we have agreed to correct. If you think a sixth axis is needed — for instance, whether the distinction is meant to apply symmetrically, given that we tend to reserve 'bias' for our opponents and 'preference' for ourselves — introduce it and say why.

Please make the leaves concrete. Each should carry a descriptive name of your own devising (I would rather have 'idealization-proceduralism' than the name of a philosopher, since I cannot check attributions), along with one sentence naming the commitment that leaf's defender will find most awkward to state out loud. Where the tree yields a combination of answers that nobody occupies, say so and diagnose it: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Test the instrument against at least these cases, and make sure the branches actually separate them: someone disgusted by a minority's harmless practice who nonetheless endorses full legal equality for that minority; someone whose taste in food is idiosyncratic and who is untroubled by anyone else's; someone whose political convictions are stable under reflection but plainly traceable to the class they grew up in; and someone whose moral outlook shifts when they are tired, hungry, or in a crowd. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate and say why, rather than pretending the questions cut where they don't. Aim for something fifteen of us could work through in an hour — roughly eight to twelve branch nodes and a manageable number of leaves.
