I'm a fourth-year PhD student in econ (game theory, foundations-ish side) and I've been drafted to run our theory reading group's session on the foundations of solution concepts next month. Last time we did this it turned into everyone announcing which equilibrium concept they "believe in," which taught us nothing, so I want to hand out a decision tree instead of a list of names — something that takes a person from a starting commitment to an actual position on what solution concepts are doing, with the mixed-strategy business as the main spine.

The starting commitment should be something nobody in the room would deny: if a strategy is never a best response to anything the other players might do, you can delete it, and then delete again, and so on. From there I want the branches to be questions a person can answer without already knowing which label sits at the end of the branch. So don't write "do you accept the epistemic reading of mixed strategies?" — write "when a player randomizes 50-50, is she doing something, or is 50-50 just a fact about what everyone else thinks she'll do?" Build it from the questions outward — work out the answer combinations first and hang the names on them afterward — rather than starting from my list below and hunting for questions that separate it. If a combination turns out to be empty, say so and diagnose it: nobody's there because it's incoherent, or because it's coherent but nobody wants it, or because it just hasn't been explored.

Branch points I definitely want, though rephrase them properly and add whatever I'm missing:
- When you delete, do you delete on weak dominance or only strict? I know weak elimination is order-dependent and can kill off Nash equilibria while strict isn't, but I want the respondent to have to commit before they see where it leads.
- Is "never a best response" the same thing as "strictly dominated"? It depends on whether the potential dominator is allowed to be mixed, and I want that fork explicit rather than buried.
- Does the procedure stop when you hit a fixed point of the deletion, or does a solution additionally have to be self-enforcing, i.e. everyone's strategy a best response to the rest? Roughly the rationalizability-vs-Nash fork.
- On the belief reading of mixed strategies: do the beliefs have to be independent across players, or can they be correlated? I'm told independent and correlated rationalizability only come apart at three or more players, and I'd like that used as a case that the respondent has to answer, not as a footnote.
- What has to be common knowledge — the payoffs, or also that everyone is rational? And do you think the players themselves can carry out the iteration, or is that something only the modeller does?

Leaves I want on the tree at minimum: deliberate randomization (she really rolls the dice); Harsanyi-style purification (the randomness lives in tiny private payoff shocks, so the mixed equilibrium is a limit of pure ones); the belief/epistemic reading with a mediator (correlated equilibrium); independent rationalizability; correlated rationalizability; Nash plus the trembling-hand / proper-equilibrium style refinements that read mixed strategies as limits of mistakes; the stable-set camp; and the skeptic who thinks common knowledge of rationality is either never true of real agents or self-undermining, so iterated elimination has no epistemic foundation at all. Add any leaf the spine actually generates that I've left off, and tell me which of my named ones collapse into the same node. Each leaf should carry the name plus one line on the commitment that would embarrass whoever holds it. Keep it to about eight branch nodes so it fits on one side of a page.

Two things I care about more than the tree being pretty:

1. If this is really a grid and not a tree — if the branches aren't independent, say the mixed-strategy-interpretation question cuts across the dominance question rather than ordering it — then tell me and give me the grid instead. Don't force a tree to make me happy.

2. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't separate by any question about cases. If two of them differ on a question about the world rather than about the solution concept, that's fine, just say so rather than papering over it.

I've probably misattributed half the labels in my head, so don't take my glosses as gospel, and if a node I asked for is doing two jobs, split it. No citations or reading lists, please — this is a handout, not a syllabus, and I dont want it turning into a bibliography.

One last thing, and take it seriously rather than humoring me: if the framing is off — if "the mixed-strategy question is the main spine" is just wrong, or a decision tree is the wrong instrument because these positions don't sort that way — say that in the first paragraph and then do whatever you think the right version of the task is, or tell me plainly that it can't be done well. I'd rather get a seperate short note telling me my instrument is wrong than a beautiful tree built on a bad premise.
