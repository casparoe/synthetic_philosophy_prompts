I am preparing a session for our graduate proseminar on the demarcation problem, and rather than run through the usual Popper-versus-the-critics material I would like to work backwards from my own verdicts and see what principle, if any, they add up to. Here is where I actually come out, case by case. The new chronology associated with Fomenko, which compresses most of recorded history into the last millennium or so and treats the standard timeline as a fabrication, I call pseudohistory, and not merely mistaken history. The phantom time hypothesis, on which some three centuries of the early Middle Ages were inserted into the record by tenth-century chronologers, I call pseudohistory. Atlantis treated as a real place whose site we might yet locate; the claim that the pyramids were built with extraterrestrial help; the claim that a Chinese fleet reached the Americas before Columbus; and the claim that Great Zimbabwe was raised by Phoenicians or Sabaeans — pseudohistory, all of them. On the other side of the line: the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, the proposal that a comet or airburst set off the Younger Dryas cooling, I do not call pseudoscience, even though I suspect it is false and its proponents have kept it alive through several rounds of failed predictions. The redating of the Thera eruption, which disturbs a great deal of received chronology and is pressed in part by people at the margins of the field, I do not call pseudohistory. Schliemann digging at Hisarlik on the strength of Homer I do not call pseudohistory, even though it looks like the same move as reading Plato's Timaeus as a treasure map, and even though he was a showman whose identifications were mostly wrong. And the cosmological multiverse I do not call pseudoscience, though I cannot name the observation that would refute it.

Three pairs in that list pull against each other and I cannot get them to sit still. First, I am tempted to say the pseudohistorical cases share a refusal to be refuted, but the Younger Dryas case has survived refutation attempts just as stubbornly, and the multiverse is arguably unfalsifiable outright, so falsifiability in practice seems not to be what is doing the work. Second, I want to locate the difference in method — Fomenko treats a similarity of names or of narrative motifs as evidence that two events are one event — but Schliemann's method was also, at bottom, a similarity of story to site, and I exempt him. Third, I notice that I am far more willing to say pseudohistory when the people making the claim lack standing in the relevant field; but Great Zimbabwe's non-African origin was endorsed by people who did have standing, and the Thera redating is being pressed by people with the right training, and I still hesitate over it. So I worry that my principle is really a sociological one about who is speaking, dressed up as one about the claim.

The principle I have been half-using is this: a claim is pseudohistory when the people advancing it present it as an ordinary empirical claim about the past while operating a self-sealing programme — when disconfirming evidence gets absorbed as further proof of suppression or of a deeper pattern, and when the standards of argument the community accepts would not be accepted by that community anywhere else. I can see that this is close to vacuous, because self-sealing is exactly the property I am trying to characterise, and I cannot say what distinguishes a genuinely self-sealing programme from a hard, evidence-poor question on which the evidence really is fragmentary. What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case; for each clause of it, the case above that forces the clause; and then the principle's verdicts on two cases I have deliberately left without a verdict — the Kensington Runestone, the Minnesota inscription that purports to record a fourteenth-century Norse expedition, and the Vinland Map that Yale presented to the public in 1965 as a genuine pre-Columbian map. Tell me whether your principle sorts each of those with Fomenko or with the Thera redating, and why. Finally, tell me which of the verdicts I have already given it cannot accommodate, and whether you think that verdict is the one that should go. I would rather have a diagnosis of the mess than a tidy four-condition definition that ignores half of what I have said; if the honest answer is that there is no single principle here and that pseudohistory is a family-resemblance label, or a term of abuse with no descriptive content, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a criterion to cover the cases. And if I have misdescribed any of these episodes, or loaded the list in a way that makes my question easier than the real one, I would like to be told that too.
