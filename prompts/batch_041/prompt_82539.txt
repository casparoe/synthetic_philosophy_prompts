I'm in a reading group working through al-Ghazālī's critique of the philosophers, and I've hit a wall on the seventeenth discussion, the one about causation and miracles. Since I don't trust my own rendering of the Arabic and don't want to rely on someone else's translation wording, I've written out below my own reconstruction of the argument as I currently understand it; please treat this paraphrase as the passage to be explained, and correct it where it has gone wrong.

(1) The philosophers hold that the connection between what we take to be a cause and what we take to be its effect is necessary: given the one, the other must follow, and this necessity is a real feature of the world rather than a matter of convention or stipulation. So contact of fire with cotton must produce burning, drinking must quench thirst, severing the windpipe must kill, and so on for everything else we observe. (2) Against this, the connection is not necessary in itself. Things follow one another because God has established them in that order; the fire does not act, since fire is an inanimate body and has no action. The agent is God, either directly or through an intermediary. (3) What we observe, then, is a habitual ordering: God's customary way of creating things in a fixed sequence, so that burning is created when fire is present, though not because of the fire. This order is stable and can be relied upon in practice, yet it can also be interrupted, and we call an interruption a miracle. (4) The philosophers cannot demonstrate that the fire is the agent; their inability to conceive the contrary is a fact about their minds, not a proof about the world. (5) As for Abraham in the fire, I understand Ghazālī to say that he need not commit himself between two descriptions of what happened, one in which the fire kept its heat but the burning was not produced in the body (or a barrier was created), and another in which a power in the fire was prevented from taking effect — and that for his immediate purpose it is enough to show that the philosophers' claim to demonstration fails.

My friend, who works on Hume, reads all of this as an anticipation of the point that our expectation of the effect is only a psychological habit, and she thinks that is what Ghazālī means by calling the order habitual. I suspect she is wrong but I can't say precisely why, so I'd like help with three things. First, what work does the notion of a stable divine custom actually do in the argument, and how does it differ from a fact about the psychology of observers — in particular, does the stability of the custom carry any epistemic weight for how we should reason about the future, or is it doing something else entirely? Second, is Ghazālī asserting occasionalism as his own settled metaphysics here, or is he doing something more dialectical, namely showing that the philosophers cannot claim demonstrative knowledge of necessary connections while leaving open more than one positive account of what happens when fire meets cotton; and if the latter, what exactly does the argument establish and what does it leave undecided? Third, how do the two descriptions of the Abraham case in (5) differ in what they say about natural powers, and why would someone who denies that fire acts at all still be willing to grant the second description as a possible alternative rather than treating it as a rival to be refuted? I'd also like to know whether my step (3) conflates two separate claims — that the connection is not necessary in itself, and that we cannot know it to be necessary — since I have a nagging feeling that running them together is what makes my friend's reading sound plausible.
