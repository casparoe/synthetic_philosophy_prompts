I chair the historical marker committee in a small Midwestern town (I'd rather not name it). In 1873 a mob from the town burned the church of the Black families who farmed the east side and drove them out, and their land was divided among neighbors; some of those parcels are still in the same families. We are finally putting up a plaque, and I am stuck on the word "we." The draft I circulated begins, "In 1873 the people of this town..." Two committee members are dug in against each other. One is a fourth-generation descendant of a man who rode with the mob; he refuses to stand at the unveiling and say "we did this," because he didn't do it, and he thinks the phrase lets everyone off lightly by turning a particular crime into a mood. The other moved here in 2019 for a teaching job and has no family here at all; he has no trouble with "we," since this is his town now, and he would rather carry something he didn't do than pretend it isn't his. My own grandparents arrived in the 1950s, so I am neither. The descendants of the families who were driven out have declined our invitation; their one request is that the plaque name the men who did it rather than speak in the first person plural. Whose instinct should govern the wording, and why? And the narrower question I think is the real one: when I stand up and say "we did this," am I making a claim about the past that is true or false, or am I doing something else — incurring a debt, claiming a membership — in which case arguing over whether it is literally true is the wrong way to settle it?
