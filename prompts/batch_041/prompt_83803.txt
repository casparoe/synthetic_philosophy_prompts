I've been volunteering at a hospice two evenings a week since my dad died last spring, and last month I hit something I can't get out of my head. Two patients I'd gotten close to each asked me, separately, to be with them at a particular moment — one during a medication review he was dreading, one during a phone call with his estranged brother — and through some mess of shift changes and a lift that was out for two days, the two things landed in the same hour on the same evening. I couldn't do both, there was no way to do both, nobody could have seen it coming when I made either promise, and neither man had a stronger claim on me than the other. I've been reading around it since and I keep landing on the view that whichever way I went I was going to do something wrong.

So I want to argue with someone who thinks that's impossible — who thinks a genuine moral dilemma, a case with no permissible option, can't exist. Play that person, and play them well, not as a strawman: give them the real arguments. That ought implies can, so if I couldn't do both then it isn't true that I ought to have done both; that you can only get from "I ought to do A and I ought to do B" to "I ought to do A and B" by helping yourself to a principle (agglomeration, I think it's called) that's exactly the thing in dispute; that in a real conflict one reason simply outweighs the other, or if the two are truly equal then either is permissible and nothing wrong was done; and that whatever bad feeling is left over is regret, not guilt. Please don't name-drop philosophers at me or hand me a reading list — I can't look anything up and it wouldn't help — I want the argument itself, aimed at my case. Concede what deserves conceding (I'm not saying I'm a monster; I'm saying someone had a legitimate complaint against me either way) but never the main point, stay in character until I say stop, keep replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a back and forth, and ask me for facts about the case if you need them.

Here's my first move, and I think it's the strongest one I've got: you'll say that since the two claims were equal I was permitted to do either, so no wrong was done and the man I didn't sit with has nothing to complain about. But that's exactly what I don't buy. He was owed my being there — not that he'd have been sad, that it was owed to him — and the fact that it was impossible doesn't cancel a debt, it just means nobody gets to blame me for not paying it. If he was owed something I didn't give him, and the same is true of the other man no matter which way I turned, then whichever way I turned I wronged someone. That's the thing you say can't happen, and I don't see where the mistake is.
