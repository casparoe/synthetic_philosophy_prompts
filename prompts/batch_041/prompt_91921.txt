Two philosophers — a committed aggregating utilitarian and a committed defender of the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism — have argued for a year without converging. Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should have produced: first, the claims about moral aggregation both can sign, worded so that neither reads them as a concession; second, the points on which they still differ, each phrased in wording its own side would accept; and third, the cruxes. I expect one crux to be whether the separateness objection is a constraint on the moral objective function itself or an epistemic warning that tracks something else, and another to be whether the asymmetry between trade-offs within a single life and trade-offs across lives is principled. For each crux, give a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Use this case as a shared probe: within one life, enduring ten units of pain now for fifteen units of pleasure later is plainly rational; across lives, is it permissible to impose ten units of pain on one stranger to give fifteen units of pleasure to another, assuming the pains and pleasures are of the same kind, add linearly, and no special obligations obtain? State what each side predicts about the probe and whether the disagreement that remains is genuine or merely verbal. Format the statement as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and write in American English.
