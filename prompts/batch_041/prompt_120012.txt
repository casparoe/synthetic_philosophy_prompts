I have recently moved from a behavioral research background into a policy analysis role, and my new team treats "revealed preference" as the final word in methodological disputes. The orientation primer they circulate explains the idea as follows: "Revealed preference theory was Paul Samuelson's way of taking psychology out of economics. Instead of speculating about what people want, you observe what they choose: a person's preferences simply are the choices they make, and utility is nothing more than a bookkeeping device for summarizing those choices. If someone passes over A when A was available and takes B instead, we know they prefer B; if they later choose A over B, they are being irrational. This is why economists can say only whether preferences are consistent, never whether they are good or bad. And since behavioral economists have shown that people violate consistency all the time, the theory has been refuted."

How much of this is right? Please go through it the way an economist who actually works with these tools would: tell me what they would accept, what they would wince at, and what is plainly wrong, and say whether the closing claim about behavioral economics follows from the parts that are accurate.
