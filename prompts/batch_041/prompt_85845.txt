I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of psychiatry, working through the literature on Hacking's looping effects, and one of the regulars has been arguing that the whole worry is overblown. His line is that classifying is carving a carcass at its natural joints: the butcher's skill lies in finding the seams that are already there, between shank and loin, say, rather than sawing through bone wherever it happens to suit him, and once the cuts are made they are simply names for portions of an animal whose anatomy never depended on the shop, the customers or the recipe book. Transposed to psychiatry, he says, this shows that the fact that patients' symptoms, self-descriptions and social roles shift under a diagnostic label, and that the label itself is periodically revised, is no evidence at all that diagnostic categories fail to track real distinctions: the customers may rename the cuts and cook them differently from one decade to the next, but the animal is what it is. He uses this to conclude that Hacking's stock cases — the multiple-personality episode of the 1980s and 1990s, or the shifting fortunes of the trauma diagnoses — are episodes in the history of labelling rather than evidence about the metaphysics of psychiatric kinds. I suspect the image is doing far more work than he admits, and that it is carrying his conclusion rather than merely illustrating it, but I can't yet say precisely where it fails. Could you audit it for me?

Start with the correspondence table — what plays the role of the butcher, the knife, the joints, the carcass, the shop, the customers, the recipe book, and the successive generations of animals — and then lay out the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the conclusion he is actually drawing and those that are merely decorative. Three things interest me in particular. First, whether the object in the psychiatric case is dead and inert or alive and responsive, and whether replacing the carcass with a breeding herd — an animal whose anatomy is partly a response to earlier generations of cuts — counts as a repair to his analogy or as a change of subject. Second, whether Hacking's contrast between kinds that are indifferent to how they are described and kinds that are interactive with their descriptions has any counterpart inside the butcher's shop, and whether the analogy can even be stated without it. Third, what the image quietly imports: a single, interest-independent, timeless anatomy; a one-off act of cutting rather than an iterated practice; a clean separation between cutting and naming; and a purpose (the recipe) that is fixed and external to the object being cut. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives the audit. If the analogy is repairable — perhaps by making the butcher also a breeder, and the shop a niche of institutions and practices that a kind needs in order to exist at all — does the repaired version still defuse the looping-effects worry, or does it end up supporting Hacking instead? If there is a better analogy for the same job, give me that, and say what it would cost him to adopt it. Please write your answer in British English.
