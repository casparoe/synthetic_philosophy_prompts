Popper's handling of Darwinism is usually told as a small embarrassment: he wrote in his intellectual autobiography that Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme, the line was picked up by anti-evolution polemicists, and he later recanted, insisting that natural selection, properly formulated, is testable. I don't want that story retold. I want the episode worked as a case study in Popper's own demarcation criterion, running in both directions: the criterion applied to Darwinism, and the case used to test the criterion. Take the following as given, and say so if any of it misstates the record: the autobiography appeared in the mid-1970s; the recantation came in a 1978 paper on natural selection and the emergence of mind, where Popper maintained he had always been a Darwinian and that natural selection can be formulated so as to be far from tautological and, in that form, testable, though perhaps not strictly refutable.

First, reconstruct the earlier position in Popper's own vocabulary. Was he demoting Darwinism, or relocating it within a taxonomy in which metaphysical research programmes are not thereby worthless, serving as sources of problems and heuristic guidance for a science that can never dispense with untestable presuppositions? Which reading fits his other commitments better, and what turns on the choice?

Second, do the mapping carefully for at least four distinct targets: the bare principle that better-adapted variants tend to survive; that principle with fitness characterized independently of survival; the conjunction of selection with heredity and variation; the historical claim of common descent; and the full synthesis. For each, state what the criterion implies about testability, what evidence actually bears on it, and whether the criterion's verdict matches the scientific community's reasonable confidence. Treat the tautology worry — that natural selection amounts to the survival of the survivors — on its merits rather than waving it away.

Third, the recantation. Which diagnosis is more defensible: that Popper changed his mind about the evidence, that he changed the formulation of Darwinism under assessment, or that he quietly relaxed the criterion? Say what each diagnosis costs his other views.

Fourth, the general lesson. If a criterion of demarcation consigns the central theory of biology to the metaphysical, should one revise the criterion, revise the theory's formulation, or accept the implication? Does the Duhem–Quine point about auxiliary hypotheses make the original verdict nearly inevitable for any theory, and if so what work remains for falsifiability? Note briefly how Popper's phrase sits relative to Lakatos's research programmes, if it matters.

Fifth, the misuse. Popper's sentence became an anti-evolution talking point. Does that reveal something about the criterion itself — that an unfalsifiability verdict is a rhetorical weapon whatever the user intends — or only about its readers?

Sixth, the reverse direction. Popper's evolutionary epistemology treats the growth of knowledge as continuous with biological evolution, conjecture and refutation as trial and error elimination. Does that analogy presuppose that natural selection is a genuine, testable, truth-tracking process? If so, does the earlier demotion threaten the epistemology, or does the epistemology supply resources to correct the demotion?

End with a verdict: confirmation of falsificationism, anomaly for it, or evidence that the criterion was never doing the work it was advertised to do. No citations or reading lists; where the historical record is uncertain, say so and proceed on the facts supplied.
