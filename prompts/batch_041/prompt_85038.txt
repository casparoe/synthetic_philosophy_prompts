I work on the long-term impacts desk at a public infrastructure fund -- there are two of us, we get about six loan applications a month, and each one needs a one-page screen from us before it goes to the investment commitee, which signs off on whatever we recommend. Our whole screen is basically one question: who ends up worse off if we fund this? Which works fine for road widenings and flood defences. The problem is the projects that keep me up at night are exactly the ones where that question comes back empty. Last month it was a lithium mine: thirty years of jobs and royalties for a district of twelve thousand people, a tailings dam that will leach into the aquifer for a century after the mine closes, and the whole thing only happens because a road gets built and a processing town grows up around it -- so the people drinking that water in 2090 are, in a lot of cases, people who wouldnt exist if we'd said no. My colleague says then they can't complain, nobody is worse off, fund it. I don't buy it but I can't say why in a way that fits on a screen. This is the nonidentity problem, right? I've read enough Parfit to recognise the shape of it but not what to do on a Tuesday.

So: turn whatever you actually know about this area into a screening procedure I can run in a morning and hand to a smart non-philosopher. I want the questions in order, what counts as an answer good enough to move on, where the procedure stops and says there is no basis to object, and the points where it has to stop and say this is a values call and a human decides -- and I want those marked clearly, not smuggled in as technicalities. It has to work with the person-affecting idea (something is wrong only if it makes someone worse off), the impersonal idea (an outcome can be worse even if no one is worse off), the no-difference view (the identity of the people involved makes no difference to how bad an outcome is), and threshold or decent-minimum views -- and it has to say which one it is using at each step, because I suspect the committee will hate all of them for different reasons and I need to be able to tell them which one I used. Don't give me reading lists or citations, I can't check them and neither can you, just use what you know. Then run it on two or three cases: the lithium mine above; a dam that would resettle four thousand people who exist either way but would also create a valley economy of maybe fifty thousand people who otherwise wouldn't exist at all; and one where it bites -- a project that leaves a small group badly off but is the only route by which a much larger future population exists.

Be honest about where the procedure will misfire. In particular I want to know how it is supposed to tell the same-people cases from the different-people cases when the answer turns on guesses about migration and fertility that my desk is not remotely competent to make, and whether the procedure can ever tell me to reject a project on nonidentity grounds at all -- or whether it only ever tells me that my fund's no-harm-to-future-generations mandate is either empty or secretly requires a value our charter doesn't let me use. I'd rather find that out now than in front of the committee.
