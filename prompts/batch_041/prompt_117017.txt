I've been reading rationality blogs and lurking on LessWrong-adjacent forums for the better part of a decade, and for the last two years I've run a small fortnightly discussion group that started out on AI-risk papers and has drifted into value theory. Last week's session was hijacked by an argument between two regulars about a decision a friend of ours is actually living through. The friend, Anya, has to choose between a stable, reasonably paid job in her hometown — near her parents, inside a community she has spent fifteen years building, doing work she finds dull — and a three-year research post abroad that is badly paid, insecure, and the thing she has wanted since she was nineteen. She has been circling it for months and says, apparently sincerely, that she cannot say either life is better; she only knows she cannot stop thinking about it. The two regulars are Marcus, a former physicist now doing data science, who thinks about decisions the way an expected-value calculator does, and Nadia, a philosophy graduate student working on ethics. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it. I didn't take a side, and I'm not asking you to vindicate the person I agree with, because I genuinely can't tell which of them that is — both made arguments I couldn't answer.

1. Marcus: Anya's inability to rank the two lives isn't evidence about the lives; it's evidence about Anya. Two magnitudes are close, the stakes are large, and the noise in her estimates swamps the gap. If she knew everything there was to know about both futures, there would be a fact about which was better for her — that is just what it is for them to be options for her at all. Hardness of choice is a fact about the chooser's information, not about the values.

2. Nadia: That's the assumption, not an argument. Some things are valued in different registers — loyalty, integrity, love, being good at something, being needed — and there is no exchange rate between them. Asking for one is like asking how many miles make a kilogram. And the case is underdetermined in a way information cannot fix: no conceivable fact about either life would settle it, which is precisely unlike a case where she would want more data.

3. Marcus: Then here's a test that separates the two stories. If A and B are exactly as good as each other — a genuine tie — then a hair's improvement to A makes A strictly better than B. If your "incomparability" survives the hair's improvement — A+ still neither better nor worse nor equal to B — you have invented a fourth relation to do work that "I don't know" and "the value is vague" already do. Indeterminacy of a ranking is not a new kind of ranking.

4. Nadia: Two replies. First, if you call it vagueness, notice what you've conceded: a vague fact is still a fact, and the fact you're pointing at is a fact about a single scale on which careers and communities and one's parents' needs can all be weighed — which is the thing I'm denying. Second, the hair test cuts both ways: add a hair to one option and I can add a hair to the other, and we are where we started. And more importantly, "which is better" may be the wrong question. She doesn't have to rank the lives; she has to live one. What she is doing when she decides is not discovering which option is higher but deciding what to be — and once you see that, the demand for a ranking starts to look like a demand that the decision be made by something other than her.

5. Marcus: That's either a description or a rescue. As a description — "she constitutes herself by choosing" — fine, no quarrel; people constitute themselves by choosing even when the ranking is obvious. As a rescue — that the choice is justified by being self-constituting — it fails, because she can constitute herself badly. The person who picks the worse life constitutes a worse self. And here's the practical version: if there's no ranking, then a coin flip, a gut feeling, and who happened to ask first are all equally defensible, and "deliberation" is just the name of the delay before one of them wins.

6. Nadia: The coin flip is instructive, actually, because it's not what she wants — she would rather choose either life than have one assigned to her. So something is guiding her even when no ranking is. But I don't think "justified" has to mean "ranked." Deliberation can issue a verdict of another form: this is what the situation calls for, this is what I can answer for. And regret belongs here in a way your picture can't explain. Whichever way she goes, she will have lost something she had reason to value, and it will be fitting for her to feel the loss — not as a sign that she chose wrongly, but as a sign of what the choice cost. On your view, if she chooses rightly, the regret is just a miscalibration.

7. Marcus: A ranking explains all of that. She lost something genuinely valuable, and the value of the road not taken doesn't evaporate because the other road was slightly better; nobody thinks regret shows up only when you chose the worse option. So regret is not a fingerprint of incomparability. And your "what the situation calls for": if it's more than a feeling, then the situation called for it for reasons, and reasons can be stated — and once stated they either favour one option or they don't. If they do, you have a ranking in another vocabulary; if they don't, I can ignore them. As for the hair test, consider a thousand hairs, or ten thousand: at some point you will agree that the improved option is better. So the unranked zone isn't a stable fourth relation; it's a region where comparison is indeterminate at the edges and determinate in the middle, which is exactly the shape of vagueness.

8. Nadia: I think we may be trading vocabularies. I'm not claiming the reasons have no content; I'm claiming they have the wrong shape to be added up. "She is my father" and "this is the work I was made for" don't come with weights. And the fact that a reason favours an option doesn't make the option better than its alternative — it makes it the thing to do. If you insist that every practical reason must be commensurable, you have built your conclusion into what you are willing to count as a reason.

9. Marcus: Let me put the worry as a challenge rather than a refutation. If her reasons can't be added up, then when she finally picks, whatever she picks, there is nothing more to be said about whether she picked well — and I don't believe that, and I don't think she does either. She isn't asking which life is more convenient. She is asking which is better, and that question has an answer even if she never gets to read it.

I'd like you to take on the role of a philosopher working at the intersection of value theory and the theory of rational choice — someone who knows the decision-theoretic tradition and its critics from the inside, and who is willing to grant that a person can be right about a particular move while wrong about the larger claim. Please begin by mapping the positions before judging them: state each one in its strongest form and identify the single premise that carries the most weight in each. Then go through the nine moves one at a time and rate each on a scale running from clearly mistaken through questionable, defensible, right, to clearly right, with a short justification for every rating; where a move is really two moves, split it and rate the halves separately. Be careful here — the temptation will be to resolve the dispute by declaring one side's vocabulary the correct one, and that is exactly the move under dispute, so if you do it, do it knowingly and say why it isn't question-begging. After the ratings, tell me where the load-bearing disagreement actually lies (my guess is that it sits somewhere near the question whether "no information would settle it" is a claim about the world or about our concepts, but I may have that wrong), and say whether any part of the exchange is merely verbal — if so, which part, and what would make it substantive; if not, why not. Then say what each of them is failing to see, including anything neither of them raised, and what each would have to concede for the other's position to become live. Finally, say who is more right overall, with an explicit confidence level, and don't split the difference out of politeness: if one of them is defending something untenable, say so plainly. I don't need citations or reading recommendations — I want the arguments themselves, and I'd rather you flag your own uncertainty than paper over it.
