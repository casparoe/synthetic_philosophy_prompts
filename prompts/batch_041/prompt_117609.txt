I clerk for the amnesty panel of the Solano Commission, the body our country established after the ten-year internal conflict to trade immunity from prosecution and civil suit for full disclosure. Our terms of reference permit amnesty only for acts committed in pursuit of a political objective, and in practice the panel treats that phrase as turning on four things: the applicant's motive, whether the act was authorized within the applicant's own organization, the status of whatever the act was directed against, and whether the applicant has disclosed everything material. Every applicant below made full disclosure, so the fourth condition does no work in separating them. I have been asked to draft the memo predicting the panel's decision in next month's case, and since the panel has never published a general test, the only route is to mine its track record. What it has done so far:

- 1987: a captain of the state security police led a night raid on a house where an armed insurgent was sheltering; his divisional commander had authorized the raid in writing; the insurgent was shot dead; amnesty granted.
- 1988: a cadre of the liberation movement, acting on no one's orders, shot a shopkeeper he suspected of informing on the movement; the shopkeeper was unarmed and belonged to no armed group; denied.
- 1989: a movement commander ordered a bomb planted at an air force supply depot; the movement's national command had authorized the operation; two soldiers on guard and a civilian passer-by were killed; granted, the panel noting the civilian death but treating it as no bar.
- 1990: a police sergeant beat a detainee to death in an interrogation he had arranged on his own initiative, believing the man had information; denied.
- 1991: a movement operative, authorized by his regional command, assassinated a police major who ran a torture unit; granted.
- 1992: a state security officer, in the course of an authorized operation against an armed cell, killed a member of that cell who had also killed the officer's brother three years earlier; denied, the panel finding that his dominant motive was a private vendetta rather than the political objective.

The written reasons in the 1988 and 1990 denials say only that the acts were not associated with a political objective, without saying which element failed.

Next month's case: a senior commander of the movement's northern front. In 1989 his unit took an army lieutenant prisoner in a firefight; the lieutenant was disarmed and held for two days, then executed on the applicant's written order, which the movement's national command countersigned and which followed that command's standing directive that officers of the army's intelligence corps were not to be taken prisoner alive. The applicant says he understood that the prisoner would otherwise have been exchanged and returned to duty against his unit. He has never denied signing the order, he took no personal gain from it, and the act falls within the mandate's period, so neither disclosure nor jurisdiction is at issue.

I would like the following: your prediction of the panel's decision; the candidate tests the record is compatible with — at least two that fit all six outcomes, each stated precisely enough that I can see how it would have handled each of the six, together with any tempting test the record rules out; what each surviving test predicts about the commander; your own best guess, with the reasons for preferring it; and the case that is not on the record and would most sharply separate the surviving tests, so that we could ask the panel to take it up. I would also like your view of what the panel commits itself to either way: whether it grants or denies, what principle does it thereby adopt about prisoners, about orders, and about the difference between what an act was aimed at and what it was authorized by — and a word on a colleague's suspicion that there is no test here at all, only a body keeping the commanders of both sides out of prison. Finally, please say which precedent on the list is the closest analog and where the analogy breaks down.
