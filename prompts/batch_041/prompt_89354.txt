I've been reading up on the interpreations of probability for a seminar — frequentism vs subjectivism vs propensity — and I found a blog post that laid it all out in a way that felt really satisfying at the time. Here's the gist, as close as I can remember:

'Probability is one of those words that hides three different theories behind it. The frequentist says the probability of an event is its limiting relative frequency in a long run of trials — that's why you can only assign probabilities to repeatable events, and why a probability statement is really a prediction about what happens in the long run. The subjectivist (or Bayesian) says probability is just your degree of belief, so it isn't a fact about the world at all, and any set of degrees of belief that's internally consistent is as legitimate as any other — that's why Bayesians are happy to put a prior on anything, like the existence of God. The propensity view is the middle way: probability is a real tendency in the thing itself, a disposition to produce outcomes with certain frequencies over the long run, wich is why it's the natural reading for quantum mechanics — it lets you talk about the probability that a particular radioactive atom decays in the next second, even though there's only one atom and it only decays once.

The moral is that these aren't really rival answers to one question. They're three tools. Use frequencies when you have data, credences when you don't, and propensities when you're doing physics.'
