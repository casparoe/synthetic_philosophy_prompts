I have no background in statistics or philosophy — I compile waiting-list figures for a regional health board — but an argument I had at work has turned into something I cannot stop chewing over, and I would rather have the general rule than another set of worked examples. The issue is what to believe when a comparison reverses depending on how finely the data are broken down (I gather this is what people call Simpson's paradox). My own verdicts are as follows. Two treatments for the same condition: pooled, the older one cures a higher proportion of patients; split by whether the case was mild or severe, the newer one wins in both groups, because the older treatment went mostly to mild cases. I believe the newer treatment is the better one — the severity split is the comparison that matters, since severity is what decided who got which. Admissions: boys are admitted at a higher rate overall, yet in every faculty the rate for girls is the same or higher; on the question of whether girls were disadvantaged, I believe the pooled figure, on the grounds that where they applied in greater numbers — the crowded faculties — is part of how they fared rather than something to be divided out, though I am uneasy here. Batting averages: a batsman has the higher average in each of two seasons, yet the other has the higher average across both, because one of them had far more innings in his good season; I take the two-season figure as the fair summary of who was the better batsman over the period, and I do not think the season split shows anything except that the seasons carried different weights. And one that unsettles me: staff who took a training course are promoted less often overall, but within each department those who took it are promoted more often, the course having been offered mainly in departments with few posts to fill. I say the trained staff are doing better and the department split is the right one — which looks to me the same shape as the admissions case, where I went the other way, and I cannot say what distinguishes them.

Two further cases make me doubt my instinct altogether. Two diets are compared for heart health: the first looks better overall, but among those who later needed a bypass the second looks better; since needing an operation is a consequence of heart trouble rather than a cause of it, I take the overall comparison and ignore the split. Patients on one drug relapse more often overall, yet do better among those who completed the full course — but you cannot complete the course if you relapsed early, so I think that split is meaningless. My working rule has been: trust the finer breakdown, because it compares like with like, except where the groups are unequal in size for reasons unconnected with the outcome. I suspect it is too crude, partly because of the admissions case, and I would like it tested and repaired. In particular, can any honest rule here avoid knowing the causal story behind the numbers — which factors caused the treatment and which the treatment caused — or must every defensible rule use it? What does your repaired rule say about at least two cases I have not listed, one where it overrules my instinct and one where it confirms it, and which of the verdicts above does it force me to abandon? Please lead with the rule itself in one or two sentences, then work through my cases, then the new ones; no more than 700 words, in British English.
