I facilitate a monthly discussion group for adults with no formal training in philosophy or mathematics — a few have science or engineering degrees, most do not — and next month I am responsible for a ninety-minute session on supertasks. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a plan for delivering one. My worry is that the group will leave thinking they have met a charming paradox about infinity — amusing, unfalsifiable, and inert — rather than grasping the specific question the topic actually raises, so the plan has to be built to make that question unavoidable rather than merely entertaining.

The material I intend to work from is the following. First, the standard lamp case: a lamp is off at time zero; it is switched on at half a minute, off at three-quarters of a minute, on at seven-eighths, and so on, each interval half the previous, so that the switchings occur at the times 1 − 2⁻ⁿ for n = 1, 2, 3, …; at one minute, is the lamp on or off? Second, a variant I would like to use alongside it: a dial whose pointer begins at angle zero and whose angle after the nth step is π(1 − 2⁻ⁿ) radians, so that its positions run π/2, 3π/4, 7π/8, … and converge to π. The dial, unlike the lamp, has a final position fixed by the values at finite times together with a requirement of continuity; the lamp, whose state flips rather than converges, does not. I want the session to turn on exactly that contrast — that a task can be completed without its outcome being determined, and that whether the outcome is determined depends on what we require of the quantity in question across the limit.

Please give me the teaching plan. Specifically: the order in which the components must be introduced, and what depends on what — what has to be in place before the lamp question can even be posed sharply; which of my two examples should carry the session and which should be demoted, and why, including whether I have the contrast backwards; the misconceptions to expect and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. My guesses at the misconceptions are that the group will take the puzzle to be about whether an infinite sequence of actions can be completed at all; that there must be a fact of the matter and the puzzle is to find it; that the correct answer is that the lamp is neither on nor off, as though it occupied a third state; that the case shows mathematics is inconsistent; and that the dial, if it works, settles the lamp. Tell me which of these are real, which are my inventions, and what I have missed.

They will also ask whether any of this is physically possible. I want that addressed, but I do not want it to swallow the session; tell me where it belongs and how to bound it. Close with the single question I can put to the group at the end that would distinguish someone who has understood the issue from someone who has merely been entertained, and with an explicit list of what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out. I do not want readings or references; this is a discussion, not a course.
