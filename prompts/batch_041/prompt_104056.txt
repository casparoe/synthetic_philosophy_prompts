I sit on the research risk review committee at a mid-sized university. We're the people who have to weigh in before a lab publishes something with obvious dual-use potential, releases a dataset or a tool, or starts a line of work a funder has flagged. We meet once a month, 45 minutes, usually four or five items, and most of the people in the room are not specialists in whatever the item is about — there's a chemist, a lawyer, a comms person, me. Right now we basically improvise, and the same fight eats the meeting every time. Someone says we shouldn't let X happen because X is bad if it happens at all. Someone else says fine, but if we say no, a lab in another country, or a company, or some hobbyist will just do it, so what does our no even accomplish. Then a third person says well if everyone reasons like that then of course it gets done. We never resolve it, we take a vote, and the vote tracks whoever spoke last.

I recently ran into an idea that I think is about exactly this, the unilateralist's curse. As I understand it: when lots of different agents can each independently do some thing, and the thing is bad whenever anyone does it, then the sheer number of possible doers makes it likely at least one of them will do it — even though most of them, individually, think it shouldn't be done. And I half-remember there being a proposed remedy, something like a principle of conformity — act the way you think the consensus of people in your position would act, instead of on your own read of the situation. I don't remember it well and I might be mangling it, so please don't lean on the name or on anyone's version of it. Work from the shape of the idea, and if the idea as I've stated it is confused, say so and fix it.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my committee can actually run. The questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a good enough answer to each one; where the procedure stops and the answer is no; and the places where it has to stop and hand the call to a person because it's a values question, not a facts question. It has to tell us when the curse actually applies and when it doesn't — in particular I think there must be a mirror-image case where the thing is good if at least one agent does it, and then everyone hanging back is the failure, so the whole logic flips and someone should just go. It has to handle the fact that we usually have no idea how many other actors could do the thing, or how likely they are to. And it has to say something about private information: if I know something the others don't, does that license me to ignore the conformity step, or is the whole point that it doesn't, and I have to put the information in the room first. Keep it to something we can run in a few minutes per item, with no numbers, for an audience that hasn't read anything about this. The output should be a recommendation plus a short written record we can defend, and the VP for research signs off at the end.

Then run it on three items, including one where it gives an answer I won't like:

1. A lab wants to publish a paper describing a method for making a common organism considerably more dangerous. Nothing is actually released into the world but the method becomes public.
2. A group wants to openly release a piece of software that several other teams are racing to release anyway, and that has an obvious benign use and an obvious ugly one.
3. Our own group's project — a study whose result I have private, unpublished reason to think is both safe and genuinely valuable, but which the procedure, followed honestly, would probably tell me to shelve.

Finish with what the procedure can't do and where judgment can't be squeezed out of it, and give me the one-page version we can paste into the minutes.
