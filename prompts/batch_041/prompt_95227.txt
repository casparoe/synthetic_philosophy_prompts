I sit on the grant panel at our community foundation. Five of us, we meet Monday, and I'm the one who has to circulate the recommendation, so I'm coming to you a little late.

The case: one pot, $12M, two finalists, and we cannot fund either one in full.

Wrenfield (the big urban outfit, they've been with us twenty years) asked for $9M. Their own projections say the money gets less and less effective as it grows: about 15 impact points per $1M for the first $3M, about 10 per $1M for the next $3M, and only about 5 per $1M for the last $3M. So $9M is their ceiling and it buys 90 points. The valley clinic coalition asked for $5M, flat 8 points per $1M the whole way, and its a hard ceiling -- they've told us they physically cannot spend more than that this year. So 40 points. Our program officer put both proposals on that one scale so we could compare them.

If we don't spend the money it goes back in the endowment and neither of them will recieve a dime.

One of my fellow panelists has gotten very attached to a rule she found. In plain terms: don't fund them to the same fraction of what they asked for, and don't fund them to the same number of points; fund each one to the same fraction of the best it could possibly have done with our money. If that lands at 91% for one and 91% for the other, that's the rule. She calls it the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution and says it comes out of bargaining theory, which is more than I can say for it.

I like the sound of this and can't say why, which is a problem, because Marcus is going to argue that (a) it gives us less total impact than just buying the cheapest points first, and (b) that we can't explain it to either applicant without sounding like we invented it in the parking lot.

One more thing. A trustee has all but promised the valley coalition a $1.5M restricted gift for the same clinic work -- already committed, theirs whether or not we fund them, outside our pot entirely, and it does the same kind of good, so it's 12 more points on the same scale. Marcus says that's irrelevant to our decision. I'm not sure he's wrong, but I want the memo to settle it, because if our split shifts just because somebody else gave one of them money, I need to be able to say why.

What I need, by Sunday night: a straight recommendation with the actual dollar split and the percentage of each one's max it comes to (I can't do the arithmetic, that's partly why I'm asking); the principle in one sentence I can say out loud at the meeting; Marcus's case at its strongest and an honest answer to it, including whether we're actually giving something up; and two lines on what we do if the pot or the gift moves before Monday. If your instinct says the rule is a bad idea, say so and tell me what to do instead -- I'd rather hear it now. No footnotes, no citations, nobody reads them.
