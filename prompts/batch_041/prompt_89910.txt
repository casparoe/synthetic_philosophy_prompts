Ok so quick context: I'm a journalist, I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest mag, and I'm working up a long feature on triage — the whole who-gets-the-ventilator, who-do-you-pull-from-the-water thing that got so heated a few years back. The funny part is that everyone I interview has a strong opinion about whether the numbers count, and they all think there view is just common sense, even though they flatly contradict each other. So I want to close the piece with an interactive thing readers can do: a decision tree (or a quiz with a scoring key, whichever you think works better) that sorts a reader into the view they actually hold.

Here's what I'm after and what I don't want. I don't want a rota of famous names and their famous thought experiments — my readers can google that themselves and anyway it makes for a boring quiz. I want the tree grown out of the real fault lines, so every fork is a question a normal person can answer about a concrete case without realizing it's the fork between two camps that have been fighting about this for fifty years. The kinds of things that seem to actually split people: does a coin flip feel right to you, or does it feel like a cop-out when you could've saved five? Does your answer change if the one person is standing right in front of you? If saving the five means carving up the one rather than just letting him drown? If the one is going to die next week anyway? If the one is you?

I want the leaves to be positions that real people actually hold — the numbers-never-count-flip-a-coin view; the plain save-the-five-each-counts-for-one view; the numbers-count-but-only-as-a-tiebreaker view; the weighted-lottery view where five people get a better chance but not five times better; the numbers-count-unless-the-competing-claim-is-trivial view; the it's-partly-about-what-you-personally-can-do-and-who-you're-connected-to view; the you-can't-use-the-one-as-a-means view; and the each-additional-life-is-worth-a-bit-less view. If a couple of those aren't actually distinct, say so and merge them. If the tree coughs up a branch nobody defends, don't just leave it blank — tell me whether it's incoherent, repellant, or just unexplored. And flag any two leaves my questions can't really pull apart, I'd much rather know than have it fudged.

Two last things. My readers aren't philosophers, so keep the branch questions in plain concrete language — a case, not a label — and have the scoring key map answer patterns onto positions rather than just asking people which one they'd like to be. And no reading list, no citations, that's not what this is. Maybe eight to twelve branch questions, or however many leaves it takes.
