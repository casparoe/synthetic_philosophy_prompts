Alright, here's the deal. I run a grad seminar on the ethics of reproduction, and every year we get to procreative beneficence and the whole thing collapses into the same pantomime — someone trots out the "select the child with the best life" line, someone else says "expressivist objection," everyone nods, nobody changes their mind. I've got two colleagues who've been circling each other on this for years. One of them is a committed procreative-beneficence person: there's a moral reason to bring into existence the child, from among the possible children you could have, who'd be expected to have the best life, and that reason bears on selecting for or against traits, including disabilities. The other comes out of disability ethics and holds the expressivist line: choosing against disability in your future child expresses the judgment that disabled lives are worth less, and that expression is a wrong in itself, independent of any effect on the child you end up with. They agree on almost nothing except that the other side is confused.

I want to see the document they should have written by now. So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps. What I want out of it:

- a numbered list of claims both can actually sign — about what selection is, about what the disagreement is not about (I suspect a chunk of it is a red herring), about the status of the "best life" measure, about the difference between the child you'll have and the people who already exist, and about which harms are even in scope;
- the residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording (i.e. write my beneficence colleague's position the way he'd state it, and hers the way she'd state it — no strawmen, no pre-loaded "so-called" hedges);
- the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody;
- for each crux, a concrete case or argument that both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two things I want them to have to chew on, because they never do. First: I keep pushing a case where the trait in question is only a disadvantage relative to how we've arranged society — an embryo who'll be exquisitely sound-sensitive, say, miserable in a noisy city and fine (even advantaged) in a quiet one — and I want to know whether "the best life" is even well-defined independently of the world the kid will live in, and whether both sides will bite that bullet the same way. Second: make them separate the question of what an act of selection is *about* (the child you could have) from the question of what it *says* (about people who exist), because I think the expressivist objection lives or dies on whether those can be pulled apart, and I want that on the page as a shared test rather than a talking point.

Don't give me a survey of the literature and don't cite anyone — just the statement. And if you think part of their fight is really just a verbal dispute, say which part and why.
