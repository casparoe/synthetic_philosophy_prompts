I teach the final-year option on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I am setting the end-of-module exam. The second half of the module is on takeoff speeds, and I have grown tired of scripts that rehearse the standard exchange — the positions usually labelled explosive takeoff, continuous takeoff, and the hardware-overhang scenario (a large stock of idle or underused compute becoming suddenly useful) — and then sit on the fence with a paragraph of hedging. My students can do that recitation in their sleep. What I want to test is whether they can do the analytical work underneath it.

Two moves from the module need to be forced into the open, because as far as I can tell they are not standard in the published debate.

The first is the rate/ceiling distinction. Self-improvement that raises the *rate* at which a system searches a fixed space of designs is a different claim from self-improvement that raises the *ceiling* of that space, i.e. changes which designs are reachable at all. Most arguments that a system will improve itself very quickly establish, when pressed, only the first. I want students to say what the second would require, and why it does not follow from the first.

The second is the generation/validation distinction and the toy model we built on it. Let C(t) be the system's capability, and let the improvement law be dC/dt = k · C^α · V, where V is validation throughput — the rate at which candidate improvements can be checked against the world — bounded by physical experiment time and by the fidelity of the system's own simulations. Self-improvement arguments inflate the generation term (the search over designs: k and α) and treat V as though it were free. Students should be able to say what must be true of α and of V for this law to yield a finite-time blow-up rather than merely fast exponential growth, and then to ask whether the fast-takeoff arguments are really arguments about α, about V, or about the growth law changing form. Note that V may itself be a function of C — a system better at designing or simulating experiments extracts more validation per unit time — and part of what I want students to see is that the question of explosive takeoff then collapses into whether V grows fast enough, which is a question about the world rather than about intelligence.

Please write, for a 90-minute hand-written exam with no notes, an assessment on this topic. The essay question itself must be phrased so that no student can pass by summarising the debate, and it must make explicit what the essay has to *do*; a list of required tasks is fine. At a minimum it should compel candidates to:

(a) draw the rate/ceiling distinction and argue why rate-raising does not imply ceiling-raising;
(b) state the condition on α and on V under which the toy model gives a finite-time singularity, and give at least one qualitative reason to doubt that V is unbounded;
(c) classify the standard fast-takeoff arguments as arguments about α, about V, or about a change in the form of the law, and say what the last of these owes us by way of evidence;
(d) defend a verdict on whether the available arguments support a discontinuity as against merely a fast takeoff;
(e) say what, if anything, turns on that difference for the practical conclusions people draw — in particular whether a governance or coordination response has time to operate.

Alongside the question, supply a model answer of about 600 words that would earn full marks, and a marking scheme out of 20 built from five must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. Add a short note on what separates a top-band answer from a solid second — the specific moves, not the general impression.

In the model answer, be explicit about how confident you are in each substantive claim, and single out the one point you are least sure of. I would rather the model answer flag its own soft spots than assert uniformly.

End with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...} enclosing, in under fifteen words, the single move that most reliably separates the top band from the rest.
