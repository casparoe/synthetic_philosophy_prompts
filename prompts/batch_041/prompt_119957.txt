Alright, I want to be argued out of a view I've talked myself into. Not the textbook version of it -- my version, which I think is harder to kill.

Call it the availability thesis: every fact about the world is at bottom a fact about what experience is available and under what conditions. Not what someone actually experiences -- I'm not saying the room goes dark when I leave -- but what would be experienced if such-and-such were done. 'There's a tree in the yard' unpacks into a family of conditionals about what you'd see, touch, bump into, and so on.

Two bits I want to insist on. First, the master argument isn't the load-bearing part for me. I know the standard hit on it: you can't conceive an unconceived tree, sure, but that's because conceiving it makes it conceived, so the argument only gets you that nothing can be both unconceived and conceived, which nobody every denied. Fine. What actually moves me is closer to a no-sample argument -- every sample of 'existence' any of us ever has is an experiential one, and mind-independent existence is supposed to be a kind of existence we have, by construction, no sample of at all. Second, parsimony: whatever the physical story does, the explanatory work seems to be done by the regularities, the stable lawlike intersubjectively shared patterns. Bolting on 'and there's also stuff in itself behind the patterns' doesn't seem to predict anything extra. So to me the mind-independent world looks like an idle wheel people keep because it's grammatical.

Yes, this is roughly Berkeley with the God part doing alot of quiet work, and I'm suspicious of exactly that.

So what counts against it? Rank the defeaters, fatal first, and be honest about which ones I'm entitled to shrug off. In particular:

- In-principle unobservable facts. Black hole interiors. Whatever's going on beyond the cosmic horizon. Past events that left no trace at all, not one photon. If some of those are facts, and no possible experience could bear on them, then my unpack-into-conditionals move has nothing to unpack -- unless I say those aren't facts but parts of theories, which is where I get nervous.
- The unperceived present. Is the camera in the empty room handled by the counterfactual, or is that just the fridge-light joke with extra steps?
- Other perceivers. If everything is experience, whose? And is 'coordinated' an explanation or a name for the thing to be explained?
- Causation. What makes my experiences arrive in this order? If the honest answer is 'the regularities just are like that', is that a respectable stopping point or a promissory note?
- Insulation. This is my real worry. If every apparent counter-example gets absorbed as 'well, that's a fact about available experience', say so, and then say whether that's fatal or just the price of a view this general. Compare it to other hard-to-kill views and tell me if mine is worse.

And tell me what the smartest defender of this kind of view would concede counts against them -- not what critics say, what they'd admit. If that list comes back empty, that tells me something too.

What would move me most is a fact we have good reason to believe that no possible experience could in principle bear on, and where dropping it costs real predictive or explanatory power, not just tidiness. If nothing like that exists, say so, and tell me what the view is buying me that's worth the awkwardness.
