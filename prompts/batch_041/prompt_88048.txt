I've been volun-told into giving the short welcome at our court's naturalization ceremony next month -- the three-minute thing before the oath, in the old courtroom, the one with the Jefferson portrait on the wall behind the flag. And I keep circling one problem I can't get past. These people are about to swear allegiance to a thing that was built by men who owned other human beings, and there are only two speeches anyone ever gives in this room. One is "the founders were flawed but their ideals were great." The other is "this country was founded in crime, join it anyway." Both are insults to somebody in the room, just different somebodies. It's the new citizens and their families on one side (some of them from places where saying the thing they're about to say would get them arrested), and on the other side the local bar association, a veterans honor guard, and two retired history teachers who will audibly wince at anything glib. I've thought about this alot.

Here's the part I actually need help with. I want the speech to take the "he was a man of his time" defense seriously before it does anything else -- give it its best form, the version a smart defender would read and say yeah, thats the argument, not a strawman. And then I want the speech to have to live with the thing my friend Priya keeps saying about that defense (she teaches early American history). She says it always assumes there was one opinion back then, one single "time," when the fight was live and going on right then, and he was on one side of it and knew there was another side. She calls it the "which time?" problem. I don't want the speech to just repeat her point, I want it to be stuck with it.

Two things I'm not allowed to say, by my own rule. I can't say "flawed," because it's the word everybody uses to get out of this room and it does all its work invisibly. And I can't say "we shouldn't judge the past by the standards of the present," which is the exact sentence I'm trying to avoid needing. Also no footnotes, no reading list, nothing that sounds like a seminar -- this has to be sayable out loud by me in about three or four minutes, and it can't end with a verdict, because I don't think there is one. It should leave the room with the weight of the thing, not a resolution.

After the speech, give me a table with these columns: Move (the actual phrase or beat in the speech), Which way it cuts (does that move license the "man of his time" defense, undermine it, or split it), and What it costs (what the speech has to give up or concede in order to make that move). Four or five rows.
