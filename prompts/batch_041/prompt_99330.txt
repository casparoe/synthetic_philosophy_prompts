I'm a software engineer by trade — I've spent my career on instruction sets, memory hierarchies, concurrency, and fault-tolerant distributed systems — and I have no formal background in philosophy or neuroscience. I've been reading about John von Neumann's late thinking on the brain, especially the comparison he develops in his unfinished book The Computer and the Brain, and I'd like you to do something specific with it.

Take von Neumann's main claims about the brain: that neurons fire in an all-or-none way and so look digital; that the brain and the computers of his day differ sharply in component speed and in degree of parallelism; that a system built from components far less reliable than a computer's nonetheless computes reliably, so something like redundancy or error correction must be doing the work; and his suspicion that the brain's signalling is not a straightforward pulse code of the sort a machine uses, but is carried in some statistical or frequency-based property of the signals, so that the brain's "language" may be a different kind of code altogether. Restate each of these claims as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use.

By that vocabulary I mean: analog versus digital signals, sampling and quantization, clock rates, serial versus parallel execution, instruction sets, shared-memory bus architectures and the fetch-decode-execute cycle, registers and the memory hierarchy, stateful versus stateless components, encoding and line codes, checksums and error-correcting codes, redundancy and replication, graceful degradation, and the difference between a signal's instantaneous value and a statistic computed over a window of it.

For each von Neumann claim, tell me what survives the translation intact, what survives only if I distort it (and how), and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I'm most interested in that last category. Where something fails to translate, tell me whether the failure is a gap in the engineering vocabulary — something it genuinely cannot express — or evidence that von Neumann's own claim was vaguer than it sounds, a promissory note rather than a thesis. And at the end, tell me whether the residue, taken together, amounts to a single substantive claim about the brain that engineering language cannot capture, or whether it dissolves into several loosely related observations once you force them into a precise form.
