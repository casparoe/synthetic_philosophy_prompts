I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long essay about the recurring dream of settling moral questions by first finding out what the universe is actually like. What pulled me into it was a Victorian episode that seems to have been quietly dropped from the story we usually tell about the rise of secular ethics. Henry Sidgwick spent his career trying to put moral philosophy on a scientific footing, and he is still read as the most exacting of the classical utilitarians, the man who refused to let a wish do the work of an argument. Yet his great treatise on ethics ends not with a verdict but with a standoff: he held that the demands of self-interest and the demands of universal benevolence are each self-evident, that it cannot be shown they always coincide, and that they could be reconciled only if the universe were morally governed — if there were a life after death and a power arranging things so that virtue and self-interest finally came to the same thing. He also insisted that smuggling such theological assumptions into philosophical ethics was improper. And then he helped found and presided over the Society for Psychical Research, and threw himself into investigations of mediums and thought-transference, apparently on the theory that the missing premise of his ethics might be something one could establish by evidence rather than merely postulate. A friend who teaches in a philosophy department tells me I'm making too much of what he calls a period piece, and that may be right, but the shape of it still bothers me.

Please describe the most important implications of this episode, taken seriously rather than as a curiosity. I'm after consequences, not a retelling of the history. What does it mean for the supposed autonomy of ethics if its most rigorous practitioner believed that his own theory hung on a fact about the cosmos that might yet be discovered? What follows for the way we handle the question of why anyone should be moral when morality and self-interest come apart — is the modern secular treatment of that question genuinely independent of the reconciliation Sidgwick was after, or does it inherit an unpaid debt and merely decline to notice? And what are the implications for how we draw the line between serious inquiry and crankery, given that a man of unimpeachable rigor invested so heavily in a field that most philosophers now treat as disreputable — does his case tell us something about the boundary itself, or only about the pressures of his time?

Please reason it out at whatever length the subject warrants, in American English, and be willing to follow the implications somewhere uncomfortable rather than stopping at the safe, standard reading. I don't need sources or a reading list; I need the implications themselves and the arguments for them.
