You're a bioethicist who works with health plans on coverage policy, and I'd like you to think this through carefully rather than reaching for the textbook framing. I chair the benefits committee at a mid-sized self-insured employer (about 4,000 employees), and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting, so it needs a bottom line the committee can actually vote on.

Here's the situation. Our plan document says the plan covers interventions meant to restore or maintain normal functioning, and doesn't cover interventions whose primary purpose is to push a member above the normal range for their age and sex. Two requests landed on my desk the same week, both for the same drug, same dose, same mechanism (a memory and attention drug):

1) A 67-year-old accountant with mild cognitive impairment. Objective testing puts him clearly below the normal range for his age. His physician expects the drug to bring him back into the normal range, possibly a bit above it.

2) A 44-year-old healthy software engineer with normal memory for his age. He's been reading the trials, says his recall isn't what it was at 30, and wants the drug to sharpen up. He's not impaired on any test we can find.

So the same molecule is therapy for one guy and enhancement for the other, which means we aren't classifying interventions at all, we're classifying indications and maybe motives. That's the crack I want you to work in.

Two committee members have proposed replacement rules and I want the memo to take both seriously rather than bat them away:

- One wants a straight welfare rule: cover whatever gives the most expected health or welfare gain per dollar, no labels, no normal-range language.
- The other wants a personal baseline rule: cover anything that fixes a deficit relative to the member's own best-ever level of function, whether or not they're below species norms, and deny anything that goes above their own best-ever. Note this would probably cover request 2, since the engineer's memory has slipped from his own peak, and it might give less to a member who's been impaired since childhood, since they have no lost function to restore. I suspect that second implication is a problem, but I want to know if you think it's fatal.

There's also a consistency objection I keep hearing in the hallway: we already pay for gym memberships, sleep apps and a mindfulness subscription, and nobody calls those therapy. I have alot riding on getting this right, so don't let me pretend that one away.
