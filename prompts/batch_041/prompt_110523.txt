You are a philosopher whose specialty is de re modality and the metaphysics of personal identity, and you are in the habit of stress-testing colleagues' position sets before they circulate them. I am preparing a short position statement for a metaphysics workshop and would like you to audit it for internal consistency. Treat every item below as a considered commitment I actually hold, not as a provisional intuition I might abandon under pressure.

(1) Necessity of origin. For every human being there is a particular sperm and a particular egg, and it is necessary that that human being developed from exactly those gametes. A human being who developed from any other pair would have been a different individual.

(2) Sufficiency of origin. I also take origins to individuate. If in some other possible world a human being develops from the very same sperm and the very same egg, then that human being is me. The gametes do not merely impose a necessary condition on being me; they fix which individual is at issue.

(3) Essence-first modality. I think necessity is derivative from essence rather than the other way around: a proposition is necessary because it follows from the essences of the things it concerns. And an essence is a real definition — it says what the thing is — which is why essences earn their keep by explaining: citing a thing's essence is what tells you why it has the necessary properties it has.

(4) Psychological continuity. What makes a person the same person over time is psychological continuity — overlapping chains of memory, intention, and character, appropriately caused — and that relation is one-one, so where psychological continuity branches there is no identity, only resemblance. I also hold that personal identity is what matters in survival: I do not accept that there is a kind of survival that falls short of identity and that I should care about anyway.

(5) Twinning. When an early human embryo divides and two children are subsequently born, there are two distinct people, and both of them developed from the same zygote.

(6) Modal epistemology. Imaginability is a defeasible guide to possibility: if I can coherently imagine a situation, that is genuine evidence that the situation is possible, though the evidence can be defeated. I have no general theory of when imagining misfires.

Here is what I need from you. Begin by restating each commitment in your own words and brainstorming, on the page, where friction might arise — including places that turn out to be dead ends. Then give me an audit rather than a survey:

(a) Which commitments are genuinely in tension with which, and which pairs only appear to be in tension and dissolve once you look at them closely? Be explicit about the ones you considered and rejected as pseudo-tensions, and say why they dissolve.

(b) Rank the genuine tensions by severity, from the one that most threatens the package to the one that is a minor cost. Do not present them as equally bad.

(c) For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set — which other commitments would have to be adjusted in turn.

(d) Finish with the single revision that would clean up the most trouble for the least loss, and tell me what it leaves unsettled.

Argue from the content of the views themselves; I am not looking for a literature survey, and there is no need to cite or name anyone. Be concrete about cases, and be willing to tell me that a commitment I take to be central is the one that has to go.
