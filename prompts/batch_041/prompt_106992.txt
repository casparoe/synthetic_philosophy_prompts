I'm taking a course on distributive justice this term, and for our unit on the priority view and the leveling-down objection we were told to write out our own considered judgments on a family of allocation problems before reading any of the theory, so that we'd notice where our pre-theoretical verdicts pull in different directions. I've done the exercise and I'm now stuck, because my verdicts seem to support two incompatible pictures of what makes an outcome good. The cases all involve a fixed resource that will raise or lower the welfare of two groups, one of which starts out considerably worse off than the other. (1) The resource can either give the worse-off group a modest gain and the better-off group nothing, or give the better-off group a substantially larger gain and the worse-off group nothing; I choose the first, and I would still choose it when the second option produces a somewhat larger total gain. (2) A third option makes the better-off group worse off and leaves the worse-off group exactly where it was; I say this is simply worse than the status quo — not better, not neutral — and the fact that it narrows the gap does not begin to redeem it. (3) A fourth option raises both groups, but raises the better-off group far more, so the gap widens; I am untroubled by it and would take it over the status quo. (4) Here is the one that bothers me. When two options yield the same total and the same benefit for the worse-off group, differing only in how the gains are spread — one leaving the groups closer together, the other further apart — I reliably prefer the more equal option, and I can hear myself justifying it by saying that the more equal outcome is simply better. But verdict (2) commits me to denying that equality is ever good in itself, since there it is bought at a cost to someone and I call the result worse. I suspect (4) is a proxy for something else — perhaps the worse-off group's claim, or a kind of insurance against being the loser — but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine rescue or just relabeling.

My tentative principle is that a benefit matters more the worse off its recipient is, and that a loss never counts as a gain merely by making the distribution flatter; that principle seems to handle (1) through (3) and to leave (4) entirely unexplained. What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible. Please state each candidate precisely, tell me which verdict motivates each clause, say what it predicts for cases I have not considered — in particular one in which the worse-off group is already above any plausible sufficiency threshold, and one in which a large number of better-off people each gain a little while a single worse-off person loses a great deal — and be honest about which of my four verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the simplest principle that fits (1) through (3) forces me to give up (4), I would rather be told that plainly than be handed a patch designed to save every verdict at once; and if you think (4) can be kept without treating equality as intrinsically good, I would like to see exactly what does the work instead.
