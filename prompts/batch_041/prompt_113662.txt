I am putting together the closing handout for a graduate reading group on tense, modality, and the open future, and I want it to be a single decision tree that carries a reader from the raw sea-battle puzzle to a named position on it. The recurring failure in our sessions is that people announce a label — Ockhamist, Peircean, or one of the supervaluationist options — without anyone being able to see which of the several independent questions they have actually answered, and I suspect that some of the labels we keep opposing to one another are not even distinguished by the cases we have been using. What I want, then, is an instrument built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: every internal node should be a question answerable by appeal to a concrete case or a scrap of ordinary linguistic data, phrased so that a reader can answer it without knowing which position lies downstream. Start from a commitment a reader might already have — say, accepting that if a claim about tomorrow is already true today then nothing anyone does tomorrow can make it false, or alternatively that this conditional is precisely where the fatalist cheats — and let the tree branch from there.

Drive the branching with four or five binary distinctions, which you should state up front and keep to no more than five. The ones I care about are: whether the future is already fixed by the past together with the laws; whether a contingent future-tensed claim has a determinate truth value now; whether excluded middle holds for the disjunctive claim that either it will be that p or it will not be that p; whether there is exactly one actual future or genuinely many equally real ones; and whether the truth of a future-tensed claim is absolute or relative to the context of assessment. For the material of the questions, use tomorrow's sea battle, a fair coin to be flipped tomorrow, an atom with a half-life that will or will not decay within the hour, whether a particular friend will accept a job offer tomorrow, a forecaster who says it will rain and is then asked, once the day is out, whether she was wrong, and a forecaster who turns out to have been right and insists that she was right all along — that last pair is the only thing that will separate two positions which otherwise look identical at the moment of utterance.

Each leaf should be a position, named if it has a standard name and marked as unnamed if it does not, with one sentence stating the pattern of answers it embodies and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the case it handles worst, or the thing it has to swallow. I need the leaves to include, at minimum: the reading on which bivalence together with the fixity of the past already settles what will happen, so that deliberation is idle; the reading on which future contingents do have truth values now even though the future is not determined by anything prior; the reading on which they have no truth value now, excluded middle fails for the relevant disjunction, and the future is genuinely branching; the reading on which they have no truth value now but excluded middle survives because the disjunction is true on every way of resolving the indeterminacy; the reading on which there is no unique actual future yet a future-tensed claim can be true or false relative to a later assessor, so that she can disagree with the earlier speaker without either being mistaken; and the reading on which future contingents are not gappy but uniformly false. Add the theological variants — truth grounded in atemporal knowledge, or in knowledge of what each free creature would freely do — only if your tree can genuinely distinguish them from their secular cousins by a question about one of the cases; if it cannot, put them in a note rather than a leaf. Finish with a compact table of the answer-combinations your branching generates, marking each cell as occupied, empty because incoherent, empty because unattractive, or empty because merely unexplored, with a clause of diagnosis for the empty ones, and then flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate instead of letting a final question pretend to do the work. I do not need citations or a reading list — only the instrument.
