I'm in a political theory course and we just did Nuremberg, and now I keep seeing the same pattern everywhere and I can't find a word for it. Here's the thing. Somebody does X. At the time, X isn't against the rules -- or the rules are so vague or optional that nobody would've thought to object, and often basically everyone was doing it. Later, a group with power declares that X was always forbidden, and the reason they give isn't "we've now decided X is bad," it's "X is so obviously wrong that the only reason you'd insist it wasn't prohibited is to hide behind a technicality." And the kicker: the people doing the declaring were usually fine with X, or doing it themselves, while it was happening. So the retroactivity and the selective enforcement come as a package, and the "obviously wrong" move is what lets them skip the reply "you were ok with it too." Is there a name for this exact combo? I know ex post facto is nearby, and the natural-law idea that a monstrously unjust law isn't really law is nearby, but neither one is about the case where the rule just didn't exist yet and the enforcers were complicit. If it has no name, can you carve one out for me -- what the move actually is, how it differs from plain retroactive law and from scapegoating, and where the line falls between "applying a norm we all genuinely already shared" and "inventing a norm and backdating it." Also, please be upfront about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely disputed among people who study it -- I don't want a confident answer that turns out to be one side of a fight. And dont just hand me a label, I care way more about the carving.
