ok so im putting together a reading for a session on Jain anekantavada and i want to close with a poem instead of yet another prose summary. the thing i actually want the poem to do: kill the reading where anekantavada is just "everything is kind of true i guess" / syadvada as wishy-washy hedging. every time i teach this someone says "so it's just saying maybe to everything" and i want the poem to make that reading unavailable without me standing there lecturing about it.

constraints, since i think the form should carry the philosophy:

- a villanelle, 19 lines, but the two refrains arent fixed strings. theyre the same clause coming back under a different respect each time, so the reader watches one sentence get relocated rather than repeated. the closing two lines have to set the two refrains side by side so they read as compatible, not as a contradiction that the poet gave up on.

- not one line may mean "perhaps" or "im not sure". the qualification is about the angle the claim is made from, not about the speakers confidence. if a reader could paraphrase any line as "who knows, maybe," the poem has failed.

- one line has to be the inexpressible one, the avaktavya moment, where from the standpoint available the thing is neither simply affirmed nor simply denied. make that line the most concrete line in the poem, not the vaguest. i want it to feel like a location, not an excuse. if that line is the airiest line in the piece ive lost the argument.

- somewhere the poem needs the naya / durnaya distinction: the standpoint itself isnt the problem, treating it as the whole is. the poem should make that turn audible, maybe by letting one partial standpoint get absolutized and visibly go wrong. you can name standpoints if it helps (the class one, the momentary one, the word one) but dont just list them like a syllabus.

- use the words perspective / viewpoint / angle at most once total. the qualification has to be enacted, not named. im so tired of the word perspective.

- voice: someone who holds the view and is fed up at being called a relativist, so a bit prickly, not serene. no lotus flowers, no blind men and the elephant, ive read that a hundred times and so has everyone in the room.

accuracy bar: if someone in the session knows the doctrine, they should be able to point at the refrains and say yes, thats the syat doing its work, and at the absolutizing stanza and say yes, thats a naya gone durnaya. someone who doesnt know it should just get a good poem.
