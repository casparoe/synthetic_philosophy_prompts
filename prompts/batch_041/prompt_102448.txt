I am a high school student, and I run the philosophy club at my school. We meet once a week for fifty minutes; there are usually between fifteen and twenty of us, aged fifteen to eighteen, a mix of students who enjoy mathematics and students who are there because they enjoy arguing. Next month the session is mine to run, and I would rather build something the group does than deliver a talk. I am writing to ask for a fully worked-out activity design.

Here is what I want people to leave having felt. I have been reading about Condorcet's jury theorem, and I worked the numbers out on a calculator to convince myself. If every voter is right more often than not, and their errors are independent of one another, then a majority is more likely to be right than any single voter, and the majority's accuracy climbs toward certainty as the group grows. At a competence of 0.6 per person: one person is right 60 per cent of the time, a majority of five about 68 per cent, a majority of nine about 73 per cent, a majority of twenty-one about 83 per cent. The same arithmetic runs backwards below chance: if each person is right only 40 per cent of the time, a majority of twenty-one is right about 17 per cent of the time, which is worse than one person guessing. That inversion is the first thing I want them to feel, because it shows the theorem is not flattery about crowds; it is a conditional statement, and the condition matters.

The condition is the second thing. The errors have to be independent. If everyone's mistake is the same mistake — a shared misleading clue, a single confident voice everyone defers to, a room that has already discussed the matter and converged — then the majority simply reproduces one error at high volume, and a large group can be wrong with more confidence than a small one. I find it striking that the ordinary classroom move of letting everyone discuss the question before voting is precisely the move that destroys the theorem's assumption, and I would like the activity to make that visible rather than to mention it.

The third thing, if there is time, is the contrast with Condorcet's other famous result: with three or more options and sincere voters, pairwise majorities can run in a cycle, so the group's preferences are incoherent even though every individual's are perfectly consistent. I would like participants to see that this does not refute the first result but does limit what a majority verdict can be taken to mean. If fifty minutes will not hold all three, tell me which to cut and why.

What I need from the activity: the discovery must come before the naming. They should run into the miracle of aggregation, then run into whatever destroys it, and only then hear what they have been doing. Nothing should be explained from the front until the debrief.

Please act as an expert in social choice theory who also designs simulations for classrooms, and think carefully before you commit to a design. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanics and saying which you are choosing and why; then give me the full design: setting and group size, materials, roles or rules, the exact sequence with timings, how the group's answers are scored so that no real money is needed, and the debrief, which should map particular moments in the activity onto the parts of the theorem they illustrate. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure, and be honest about which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will have to state myself.

Two practical constraints. There are no computers or internet in the room, and materials must be cheap — index cards, dice, coins, beads, a bag. And a warning from my own attempt last term: I had everyone guess coin flips, and of course every individual was at chance, so nothing happened and the session fell flat. I need a way of giving each participant a genuinely better-than-chance chance of being right, with the level of competence known in advance rather than hoped for, and a ground truth that I can check afterwards.
