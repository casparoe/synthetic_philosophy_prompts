I serve on the emergency classification panel of a consortium of eleven humanitarian agencies, and on Thursday morning the panel votes on the Vessari declaration. I have to circulate my written opinion the night before, and I would like you to draft it. For register: this is an internal document, read by nine people who know the file, and it will be minuted.

The situation. In the Andar and Kemal provinces of the Republic of Vessari, eleven months of conflict have produced what our field office calls a starvation crisis and what the government calls a security operation. The provincial capital's markets have grain on the shelves and prices that are high but not extraordinary. What has collapsed is purchasing power: the field office estimates that a day of casual labour in the provincial capital bought roughly four days of staple calories a year ago, and buys roughly one and a quarter days now. Convoys into the two provinces are turned back at checkpoints, a state-aligned militia has seized two warehouses, and three of our national staff have been detained and released without charge. Our mortality and malnutrition surveys cover perhaps a third of the affected districts, because the rest are unreachable, and the panel's protocol — mortality plus acute malnutrition prevalence — is written so that only survey evidence can trigger a declaration. On the evidence we can collect, we are below threshold. On the evidence we cannot collect, nobody on the panel believes we are below threshold.

Three questions are actually on the table, and I suspect they are being run together. First, do we declare a famine? The word triggers the donors' top funding tier and commits us to a set of operational obligations. Second, do we attribute the crisis to the government's conduct in the declaration itself? The Ministry has indicated unofficially that our visas will not be renewed if the public statement describes the crisis as man-made. Third, do we adopt the field office's proposed amendment to the protocol: a third category, 'response failure', defined as the share of the affected population within reach of a functioning relief corridor, capable on its own of triggering a declaration when it falls below a threshold the field office has not yet specified. Our legal adviser says this is inventing a kind of famine in order to reach a political conclusion, and that a category we cannot measure is one we will eventually use to declare a famine that isn't one. The chair says the alternative is a classification system that can see only what it can survey, which is how Bengal, Ireland and the Chinese famine of the late 1950s came to be filed as natural events, and that a protocol blind to a government's hand is not neutral but complicit. Both are making a serious point, and I do not think the panel has noticed that they are not contradicting each other.

I am genuinely undecided, and I would rather the memo reach the conclusion the reasoning supports than the one I would find comfortable. I have a mild preference for declaring, because the funding tier is real and the next harvest is four months away, and I am aware that this preference may be doing work I have not examined.

Please write the memo, roughly 1,600 to 1,800 words, in these sections, in this order, at these rough lengths:

- Recommendation (100–150 words). What I should vote for, in one sentence, then the two or three things that follow from it.
- What is actually before the panel (200–250 words). The three questions separated, and why they can be decided separately even though everyone is treating them as one.
- The case for declaring and naming the cause (300 words). At its strongest.
- The strongest case against (300 words). At its strongest, not as a formality: the credibility of the threshold, the access we would lose, and the position of our national staff all belong here.
- What the historical comparisons do and do not support (250 words). The chair will cite Bengal, Ireland and the late-1950s Chinese famine whether or not I do; say what those cases actually establish about famines that occur while food is available in markets, and where the analogy to a physical blockade of two provinces breaks down. I do not need citations, and I will not use any I cannot check, so argue from the substance.
- Should we adopt 'response failure' (250 words). Whether it is a genuine third category or the old access-failure category relabelled; if we adopt it, the form of words and the threshold; and what adopting it commits us to the next time a government blocks access to a population that is not yet starving.
- What I concede, and what the panel cannot settle (150 words). The part of the opposing case I cannot answer, and the questions that are value judgments rather than measurement problems, which the panel should decide as such instead of disguising them as protocol.
- Motion and fallback (150 words). The exact wording of the motion I should move, the fallback if it fails, and the specific actions with owners and dates: public statement, confidential annex, referral of the protocol amendment, and a security review for national staff.

One constraint worth honouring: the declaration text and the confidential annex are different documents with different audiences, and if that distinction lets us do something we could not do with a single document, I want you to say so and to say what it costs us to rely on it.
