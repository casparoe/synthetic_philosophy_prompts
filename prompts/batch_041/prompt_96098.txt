I run a second-year seminar on philosophy of mind and AI — 18 students, 90 minutes, whiteboard and nothing else, and I'd rather they dont touch laptops. I want an activity that makes them feel the pull of treating something as an agent with beliefs and desires, and then makes them feel that pull keep working even after it should have stopped. Please turn the following into something actually runnable and tell me where my plan is broken.

The core is a black box I'm calling the Oracle. No tech: a confederate sits behind a screen (a student I brief beforehand, or me) and answers queries using a lookup table or a rule written on a card, which the class never sees. Inputs are short — say a number between 1 and 50, or a two-letter string — and outputs are one of three symbols, or yes/no. Teams have to predict the Oracle's output on held-out inputs and score points for correct calls.

Three teams, each given a different instruction in plain words, no jargon: treat it as a machine and hunt for raw patterns; treat it as a device built for some purpose and infer the purpose; treat it as an agent — ask what it believes and what it's after, then predict from that. I want the setup balanced so all three can score. If the agent team just crushes it, they'll take away the wrong lesson.

Then phase two, and this is the part I care about: quietly swap the Oracle's rule for one that isn't agent-like at all — systematic but arbitrary, keyed to something they can't see, so it looks erratic. My prediction is the agent team doesn't drop the stance; they add mental states. It's deceiving us, it's confused, it changed its mind. I want the debrief to land on that: the stance's currency is predictive success, not inner truth, and when it fails we patch it with more beliefs rather than abandon it.

Give me the rules, the materials, the scoring, a script for the confederate so they don't leak cues, a timeline, and the debrief. In the debrief, name the three stances and tie each moment in the game back to one — but don't let the names out before then, I want them to hit it cold. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each thing. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: my guess is it shows the shape of the stance but not the pressure of the real case, where the systems people actually argue about are trained on human text and the as-if and the really start to blur in a way a lookup table can't.
