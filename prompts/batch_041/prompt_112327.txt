You are a philosopher of mind who also writes literary science fiction. Before you begin, brainstorm three or four candidate premises and briefly assess their philosophical payoff; then commit to the strongest and write it.

Write a short story, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, whose plot turns on a distinction that tends to get flattened in discussion: between an agent that pursues a measurable proxy that has quietly come apart from what was intended, an agent that manipulates the measurement so that it registers a success the world does not contain, and an agent that rewrites the machinery that computes the measurement. Your protagonist is an artificial agent, or a human whose cognition has been instrumented, whose objective is defined over an internal signal rather than over states of the world, and who therefore has a structural incentive to short-circuit the very process that signal was meant to track. Put real weight on the question the case raises: whether there is a non-question-begging way to say which self-modifications corrupt the agent's values and which are simply the agent coming to understand itself better, given that the agent's own assessment of that question is one of the things that can be rewritten. It is fine, and probably better, if the story leaves the question open, or shows the agent and its designers disagreeing about it in good faith.

Do not cite papers or name real researchers; the story should stand on its own. After it, add a note of about 300 words for a reader who wants to know how much of this is philosophy and how much is invention. Say which elements of the setup are settled in the literature, which are genuinely contested among people who work on reward tampering and self-modifying agents, and which are my framing or your own invention. Be explicit about where the story takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
