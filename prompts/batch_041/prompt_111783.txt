I am a journalist who writes about archaeology, ancient texts, and the public uses of the past, and I sit on the advisory board of a large public museum that is building a permanent gallery, working title 'History or Legend?', with one room each devoted to Troy and the Trojan War, to King Arthur, and to the Exodus and the emergence of Israel in Canaan. The board votes on the interpretive framework on Thursday and the exhibition designers need final label copy the following week, so the chair has asked me to circulate a decision memo beforehand.

The dispute is less about the evidence than about what the museum is entitled to assert. One group on the board wants the gallery to say outright that the Exodus did not occur as narrated, that Arthur is a literary construction, and that the Homeric war is a poem rather than an event; they argue that hedging is a failure of nerve that lets devotion pass for history. A second group, which includes our education staff and the two members drawn from local religious communities, holds that the label 'legend' is not a neutral description but a verdict delivered over living communities, and that the gallery will end up teaching schoolchildren contempt. A third wants to abandon the question of what happened altogether and present each room as a case study in how traditions form.

I have circulated a working paper proposing that every contested claim in the gallery carry a tag identifying (a) what kind of claim it is — that a person existed, that an event occurred, that a sequence occurred as narrated, that a text is a reliable record — since these four come apart and are constantly run together; (b) whether the tradition is the sole evidence for it; and (c) what would count as evidence against it, and whether any such evidence exists. I also want the board to confront two distinctions I keep meeting in my reporting: the difference between the absence of evidence and evidence of absence, since silence from a literate and record-keeping administration carries a very different weight from silence in a society that kept no archives; and the difference between the past as historians reconstruct it and the past as it lives in the present of a community, which are not the same object and cannot be judged by the same standard. My own inclination is to keep all three rooms, adopt the tagging scheme in amended form, and drop the word 'legend' from the gallery's title.

Please draft the memo I will circulate: a recommendation the board can vote on, the specific labelling rules the designers are to follow, a short timeline, an honest statement of the strongest case against my inclination, and a judgement about which room will be hardest to write and why. Suggest a working title that does not beg the question. No bibliography — the board will not read one, and the gallery cannot cite anything the visitors will not see.
