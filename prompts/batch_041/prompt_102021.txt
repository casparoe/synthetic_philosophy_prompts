I've been reporting on consciousness and the mind for years now, and I keep noticing that my gut verdicts about which things are conscious don't line up with any theory I've read. I want you to figure out what principle, if any, is actually behind them.

Here's where my gut lands, no hedging. Thermostat: not conscious, zero hesitation. Venus flytrap: no. Honeybee doing its waggle dance: honestly yes, I think there's something it's like to be the bee. A worm: maybe, leaning no. A dog: yes, obviously. A person in dreamless sleep: not experiencing anything at the moment, but obviously a conscious creature. A sleepwalker who navigates stairs and opens doors: the body is doing all of that and I want to say nobody's home, no experience happening. A person under general anesthesia: same verdict, no experience. Someone with blindsight who insists they can't see but steps around the obstacle anyway: I think there IS some seeing going on there, just not reportable. A split-brain patient whose right hemisphere can't talk but keeps picking the correct card: I feel like there are kind of two of them in there. A 20-week fetus: I don't think there's experience yet. A big language model that writes movingly about its own feelings: no, and I don't think it's close. A person whose neurons are being swapped out one at a time for silicon chips that do the same job, until none of the original neurons are left: I keep flipping on this one, and I can't tell if that's a fact about the case or a fact about me.

The only pattern I can half-see is something like "experience shows up when a system represents its own states," but I don't see how that gets me the blindsight verdict, and it definately doesn't get me the bee.

What I want from you: the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle can't save -- I'd rather drop a verdict than keep a theory with an ugly exception bolted on, and I suspect the sleepwalker/blindsight pair or the chip-swap case is where something has to give. Then apply the winning principle to a case I haven't mentioned: a person who is awake, responsive, and talking, but whose entire cortex has been replaced by a device that mimics its input-output behavior with no internal states at all. And be honest with me about whether what I've handed you is a theory or just a pile of intuitions with a pattern imposed on top.

No reading list, no citations, just the argument. American English please, and keep it plain -- I'm going to quote a chunk of this in a piece.
