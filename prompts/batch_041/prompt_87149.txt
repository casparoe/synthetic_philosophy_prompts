I spent most of Saturday evening arguing with a friend about Maxwell's demon, and I came away less sure of myself than when I started. His line is that the demon was never really exorcised: once you take seriously the idea that information is as physical as energy, there is nothing paradoxical about a small agent extracting work from a gas it has measured, and the erasure-cost arguments are a way of protecting a law we already believe rather than an account of why the demon fails. I think he is smuggling something past me, but I could not name it, and that has become a practical problem, because this stopped being abstract about a year ago. I do technical due diligence for a small hardware fund, and every few months we get a pitch for an information engine or a feedback-driven heat harvester, usually with a single-particle experiment attached and a claim to beat some Carnot-type bound. I have two days per pitch and no statistical mechanics beyond a first course; my colleague co-signs with me, the investment committee signs off after us, so a careless yes is expensive and a reflexive no is embarrassing.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run on one of these pitches. I want the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure terminates in a rejection, and the point at which it has to hand the judgment to a person — my colleague, a real physicist, or the committee — because what remains is interpretation rather than bookkeeping. It should force the pitch to say where the entropy ends up, whether the agent's own memory and its resetting are inside the accounting, whether the measurement is claimed to be reversible and what that is supposed to buy, whether the information being exploited is already available to the system in another form, and whether the demonstrated effect depends on an initial nonequilibrium state or an external reservoir that the demonstration quietly supplies. Tell me which distinctions I must not let a vendor flatten and which are safe to drop when I explain the procedure to people who are not physicists. Then run it on three cases: a device claiming to turn ambient thermal noise into usable work through a feedback controller; a genuine single-molecule feedback-trap result of the kind my friend would cite as vindication; and a scheme I am attached to, in which the demon's memory is never erased because the measurement itself is made reversible — I want to know whether the procedure shuts that door or merely relabels what is behind it, and I will not enjoy the answer either way. Say also what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire on a device that really is novel. Please write it as continuous prose, with the ordering carried by the sentences themselves: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables, the way you would explain it to me in one long email, since I will be reading it on a train.
