I have spent my working life among words, and I have never had much patience for puzzles about names; they always struck me as the kind of thing that matters only to logicians. Last month that changed, and I have not been able to set the question down since. Quite by accident I learned that two people I had long known about were one and the same. My mother had spoken for years of an old friend under a familiar nickname, and I had separately come to know a woman under her formal name, and it turned out that these were the same person. I had two sets of memories, two sets of expectations, two distinct files in my head, and they collapsed into one. Nothing in the world changed. I learned only that two names name one woman. And yet it felt like a genuine discovery, a fact I had come to know about the world, and I cannot get the two impressions, the trivial and the substantial, to sit quietly together. I am told this is an old puzzle, usually illustrated with the morning star and the evening star, and I would rather not have it rehearsed on that example.

What I want instead is a careful working-through of a different episode: Edmond Halley's recognition that comets observed decades apart were one and the same body returning on a long, regular orbit, and his willingness to stake his reputation on predicting when it would come back. Analyze what Halley discovered through the distinction between a term's sense and its reference. Say plainly what the reference was and what the competing senses were, and then locate exactly where the information in his identity claim lived, whether in the senses, in the object, or somewhere the distinction does not reach. Then turn the lens around and tell me what the episode shows about the distinction itself. Does it confirm the account, or does it strain it? Consider, for instance, whether a sense can be time-bound: the descriptions under which the comet was recorded each carry a date in a way that the morning star's descriptions do not, and I want to know whether that is a genuine problem for the account or merely a wrinkle. Consider too whether the case suggests that the informativeness of an identity belongs to the world rather than to our ways of presenting it. And finally, be candid about where the distinction is simply silent. I suspect it has nothing to say about how Halley came to suspect the identity in the first place, or about why the discovery mattered to anyone, and I would rather you say so than make the theory speak where it cannot.

Please give me your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter. No headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold text, no tables, just an argument that unfolds across paragraphs.
