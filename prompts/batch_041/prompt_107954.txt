I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy preparing a two-page proposal for a small internal grant that would buy me a single semester of full-time work, and I would like you to draft the proposal for me. The subject is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. I do not want a proposal that merely restates the theorem, or that rehashes the familiar framing of it as the derivation of utilitarianism from individual rationality, a Pareto condition, and an equiprobability or veil-of-ignorance premise. My half-formed idea — I have been arguing about it for weeks with a friend in the economics department, who is convinced it is confused — is this. The theorem is usually defended against the charge that it is rigged by insisting that the axioms it places on the impartial observer's preferences are purely formal: an expected-utility representation, on that defence, is just a convenient encoding of coherent comparative judgements about lotteries and carries no ethical content, so whatever ethical weight the conclusion has must come from impartiality rather than from decision theory. I suspect the reverse. I suspect the ethical work is done by the specific uncertainty axioms, and above all by the independence axiom, and that if those axioms are weakened while impartiality and Pareto are held fixed, the conclusion is not utilitarianism but a family of aggregation rules that differ in how they treat the worst off. What I want the proposal to do is convert that suspicion from a hunch into a well-posed question.

The question I want posed is a localization question. Fix a Pareto condition and an impartiality (equiprobability) premise. Vary the decision-theoretic axioms imposed on the impartial observer's preferences over lotteries of individual positions: full expected utility with independence; then successively weaker packages that retain continuity and some monotonicity with respect to mixtures but drop or dilute independence; and, if the mathematics permits, at least one representation that is not a probability-weighted average at all. For each package, characterize the class of social welfare orderings that satisfy it together with the fixed ethical premises. The hypothesis to be tested is that only weighted utilitarianism survives the strongest package, and that the class of surviving orderings widens in a describable way as the uncertainty axioms are weakened — so that the theorem's normative bite is located in the axioms about risk rather than in impartiality. I would also like the proposal to say what a characterization actually amounts to in this setting (a representation result, a uniqueness-up-to-positive-affine-transformation result, or merely a containment result), and to flag, without overreaching, how the surviving rules bear on the distinction between ex ante and ex post evaluation of a lottery of lives.

Please write the two pages. The proposal has to state the question so that it has a determinate answer, including exactly what is held fixed and what is varied and what would settle the matter; give a brief account of why the existing discussion leaves it open, framed in terms of positions and arguments rather than citations, since I will supply the references myself and do not want a bibliography or a reading list; set out the method month by month for a single semester, being honest about where the risk lies — I am comfortable with the formalism of expected utility but I am not a decision theorist, so say what I would have to learn and what I would do if I could not learn it in time; enumerate the possible outcomes (the localization claim confirmed; the claim refuted because the class of admissible rules turns out to be invariant across the packages; or the characterization collapsing because the weaker axioms admit so much that nothing interesting can be said) and say which consideration would decide between them; say what would count as the project failing outright; and give a realistic account of scope, naming what I would leave out and why leaving it out is acceptable for a semester. The panel is interdisciplinary and not all of them are economists, so the opening framing has to be intelligible to a non-specialist without being watered down. End with a single sentence stating the proposal's central question, on a final line beginning 'Question:'.
