I'm a retired physiologist — 38 years in a lab, mostly cardiovascular, so this philosophy stuff is new to me and I'm still tripping over the terminology. I'm moderating a Q&A next month with a philosopher who works on pain. Her line is that the pain asymbolia cases — patients who say the pain is there but it just doesn't bother them at all, no distress, no pulling away — show that the badness of pain is not intrisic to the sensation itself. On her view, the sensation is one thing, the awfulness is another, and the awfulness is really carried by the affective/motivational bit. So a creature could be in pain, in the sensory sense, without being harmed by it.

I want to press her on this without just rehashing whether pain is essentially unpleasant. Could you draft 8-10 interview questions for me, in a sequence that builds? I want at least one question each on:

- the reverse dissociation: cases where someone seems to have the awfulness without any localized pain sensation (the opposite of asymbolia). Is that even coherent, and what would it show about her view?
- what badness is supposed to mean here: is it intrinsic to the feeling, or is it a matter of what the experience represents (damage, threat) or what it makes you do (avoid, escape)? In the asymbolic patient, which of those is missing?
- the welfare question: is the asymbolic patient worse off for having pain? If not, does that mean pain isn't bad for you as long as you don't mind it? And if she says yes, where's the badness located if not in the unpleasantness?
- the function of pain: if pain's job is to protect and to get you to rest and heal, and asymbolics lose the motivation but keep the sensation, does that show the badness is really the motivational role rather than the sensation? Or does it show the sensation is doing something else?
- that patient who felt burning pain in his hand just from seeing something move toward it, and yanked his hand back — what does that do to the idea that pain is about tissue damage?
- how much weight philosophers should put on these clinical cases at all. Are they evidence about the nature of pain, or just about how the brain can wire things up?

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — I don't want to be caught flat-footed when she gives the standard reply. And don't hedge: I'd rather you pick the questions you think are actually the sharpest and commit to what you think her answer will be and why, rather than giving me a menu of options. If you think one of my framings is confused, say so and give me the better framing.

Also, I've been kicking around a couple of my own cases and I'd like you to work them in if they're any good. First: imagine a dial that turns down the unpleasantness of pain while leaving the sensation completely intact. At what point does it stop being pain? Is there a point where it's still pain but no longer bad? Second: imagine a creature that has the full aversive response — it recoils, it struggles, it would do anything to make it stop — but there's no pain sensation at all, just a blind urge to escape. Is that creature in pain? Is it suffering? I'm not sure if these are the same question or two different ones, so tell me what you think they're actually probing.

Finally, one thing I keep getting stuck on: if the awfulness is seperable from the sensation, does that make the mind-body problem easier or does it just move the mystery to the affective bit? I'd like a straight answer on that, not a both-sides thing.
