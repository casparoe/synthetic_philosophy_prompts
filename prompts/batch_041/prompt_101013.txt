I am taking a seminar on causation and responsibility, and for several weeks we have been working through a run of cases in which a harm follows someone's failure to act. Each week the instructor asks whether the failure caused the harm, and she has given her own verdicts on the following.

A lifeguard on duty, well able to reach a struggling swimmer, who stayed in his chair: caused the drowning. A parent who for weeks did not feed a young child, who then died: caused the death. A nurse assigned to watch a patient who skipped the required checks, the patient dying of a complication that a check would have caught: caused the death. A doctor who began treating a patient's collapse and then stopped and walked away: caused the death. A bystander on the same beach who could not swim, stood far from the water, and did nothing: did not cause the drowning. A neighbour who smelled gas but had no key, no telephone, and no way to reach anyone in time, and did nothing: did not cause the explosion.

The case we turn to next week is this. A warehouse has two loading doors, one assigned to each of two night guards, who are each required to lock their own door at the end of the shift. On the night in question both guards forget. A thief enters and steals a consignment of goods. It is established that the thief had cased both doors and would have entered through whichever door remained unlocked, so that had either guard locked his door, the thief would simply have used the other and taken the same goods.

I would like you to predict the instructor's verdict, both on each guard individually and on the theft as a whole, but I am less interested in the verdicts than in the rule she appears to be running, the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, and which case — this one, or some further case you devise — would best tell those rules apart. If her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so, and tell me what principle she would be adopting under each of the competing verdicts.
