A friend from my reading group is a Burke guy and he dropped this on me last week. He calls it the architect problem. The seed is the fence thing — Chesterton's line that you shouldn't pull a fence down till you know why it got put up. His version goes like this. (1) An institution is a fence. (2) You're justified in removing one only if you know why it's there. (3) And 'why it's there' can only mean one of two things: the intention of whoever built it, or the function it currently performs. (4) For anything older than a couple of generations the builder's intention is gone — the architect is dead, there are no drawings, we're guessing. (5) Current function isn't fully knowable either, because an institution can be load-bearing in ways that only show up once you take it out. He has a name for that, the demolition externality — the reason you find out too late. (6) So for any old institution you can never satisfy (2). (7) So you're never justified in removing one. And he says that's not a reductio, it's just Burke taken seriously, and the burden is on me. I said (7) would've kept slavery and coverture and the rotten boroughs, and a principle that licenses those is dead on arrival. He said that's an appeal to consequences and anyway the abolitionists knew exactly why those institutions existed. There's definately something wrong before (7) but I keep sliding off it somewhere between (4) and (6). Which premise or step do I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I'd like to keep (1), and honestly (2) as well — I think there's something real in the fence idea and I don't want to just toss it. So tell me whether I can keep those and drop the rest, or whether the thing is all one piece and has to go.
