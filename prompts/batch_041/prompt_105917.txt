Retired atmospheric chemist here — thirty-odd years measuring aerosols, and since I packed it in I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group. Bright people, no formal training, no looking things up. Next month it's my turn to lead the session, and the topic is the inefficacy objection: my one flight, my one vote, my one burger makes no difference to the harm, so where's the argument that I still shouldn't do it? Don't give me the argument — give me a plan for teaching it. What has to come first and what depends on what: do they need to see the gap between an act that causes harm and an act that's merely part of a set that does before anything else lands, or can I start with the objection itself? What's the one example that should carry the whole session, and what does it need to have and not have — I was going to use one vote, but I suspect that smuggles in thresholds and electoral quirks that don't generalise, and anyway in my old line of work nobody ever measured my molecule, only the concentration, and I can't tell if that's the crux or a red herring. Where will they go wrong — my guess is they'll think the objection either kills every individual duty or is silly because what if everyone did it — and at what point in the sequence do I head each of those off? Give me one question to ask at the end that tells me whether they got the structure of the problem rather than just picked a side. And tell me what to leave out, and to say so.
