I'm a science journalist — I write a weekly ideas column and I've been trying to put together a piece on group decision-making and voting, and I keep circling back to Twelve Angry Men. I rewatched it last week with my brother-in-law, who's a public defender, and we got into a whole argument about it. What struck me this time is that the movie is basically a dramatization of the Condorcet jury theorem — or maybe a refutation of it, and I honestly can't decide which. You know the theorem: if every juror is more likely than not to get the verdict right, and they vote independantly of one another, then the bigger the jury the more likely the majority is to land on the right answer — it converges on certainty as the numbers go up, and it does the opposite if the individual competence drops below even odds. Twelve Angry Men starts at 11-1 for conviction and ends at 12-0 for acquittal, and on the surface that looks like the theorem working, the group correcting its own error. But the whole engine of the film is everything the theorem stipulates away. The jurors aren't independent — they're crammed in a hot room, taking cues from each other, one guy flips just because he wants to get to the ballgame, another wears down, and Juror 8 barely introduces new evidence so much as he makes each man actually face his own reasons. So is the film an illustration of the theorem, a reductio of it, or a demonstration that its assumptions are so false that any real-world convergence on the truth is basically a coincidence?

What I want is a proper reading of the film through the theorem, taking both seriously — don't just use the movie as a cute hook, and don't dumb down the machinery. Where does the drama actually map onto the theorem's parts (competence above one half, independence, sincere voting, the majority threshold), and where does it lean on something the theorem can't model, like the fact that deliberation changes how competent people are rather than just aggregating fixed opinions? I'm especially interested in the ballot scenes — the first show of hands, the secret ballot where the old man switches, the stretch where it goes 6-6 — and in whether the film thinks a jury can be 'right' in a way the theorem would recognize, or whether it's quietly making the anti-Condorcet point that a crowd can drift to the correct verdict for all the wrong reasons and still get vindicated by the plot. And be honest about where the film resists the reading; there's a moment I keep chewing on where the whole turn rests on one man's hunch plus a lot of social pressure, and I don't think the theorem has anything to say about that at all.

Practical note: this is for a general readership, so I need a reading that's faithful in both directions — to the film and to the actual result — and I'd love a closing thought on what the film adds to the debate that the formal theorem can't, like whether it dramatizes a failure mode of majority rule that political theorists should take more seriously. Don't worry about citations or who first proved what, I just want the argument and the reading. Oh, and if it matters, I'm the kind of viewer who thinks Juror 8 is a hero and my brother-in-law thinks he's a menace, so feel free to take a side if the analysis pushes you there.
