I host a small interview podcast on political philosophy, and I'm preparing the episode with next month's guest: a philosopher who has spent her career defending a claim that many of her colleagues regard as barely coherent — that a nation can be guilty of a historical wrong in a way that outlives everyone who committed it, so that members of the present generation can be guilty, and not merely responsible, not merely beneficiaries, and not merely under an obligation to repair, for something none of them did. Her signature move is to insist that this guilt is non-distributive: it is predicated of the nation as such and does not divide into shares that individual members hold, and yet it is meant to have real practical bite, grounding demands that living members must answer. She situates herself in the tradition of the fourfold distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt that Jaspers drew after the Second World War, but she argues that Jaspers' own scheme is unstable — that two of his four categories cannot, on his own description, be transmitted to people who did nothing, while the other two are not, on his own description, genuinely collective. I know her book's argument well enough to follow it, but I keep finding that my questions let her restate the thesis instead of testing it, and I'd rather the interview expose the joints.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do that work, and for each one add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want the sequence to include, at minimum: a question about what is supposed to make a nation the same agent across generations, given that its members, institutions, and self-understanding all change, and whether the persistence required is empirical or merely stipulated; a question pressing the no-control objection — that present members could not have done otherwise and so cannot be guilty — with the follow-up aimed at her likely reply that guilt here was never about what they could have done; a question that takes the non-distributive claim seriously and asks what exactly bears the predicate 'guilty' if nothing distributes to individuals, and whether a non-agential collective can carry a predicate that seems to presuppose agency; a question about the asymmetry between her view and Jaspers' categories, i.e. why the transmissibility she wants should attach to the political and moral registers rather than to the criminal and metaphysical ones, or the reverse; and a question about repudiation — whether a present member can exit the guilt by disavowing the nation or the wrong, and what that does to the claim that the guilt is non-distributive.

Keep the questions concrete and argumentative, and skip anything biographical; no question should be answerable with a summary of the view. I'd like the whole set to build, so that the later questions can presuppose what the earlier ones have forced her to concede, and so that at least one question makes her choose between the transmissibility of guilt and the non-distributive character she also wants.
