A friend of mine who's doing a PhD in philosophy told me about a thought experiment I'd never heard of, and I want to know whether the lesson he drew from it holds up. Here's his version.

Imagine a giant pinball machine where none of the balls are solid: each ball is itself made of thousands of much smaller balls, and each of those is made of smaller ones still, all the way down. You can describe the whole machine at the level of the big balls -- "ball 7 knocked ball 12 into the bumper, which sent it down the left chute" -- and that description lets you predict everything that happens, and it's how anyone would actually play the thing. My friend says the moral is this: since every bounce is fixed by what the tiniest components are doing, the big-ball story adds nothing on top. It's a shorthand we use because we can't compute the micro-story, and the balls, the knocks, the chutes are fictions. Then he says that's exactly the situation with beliefs and desires: the physical stuff does all the real pushing, so strictly speaking there are no beliefs doing any causing, and psychology is a useful fiction we'll drop once neuroscience matures.

Is that fair? I'm inclined to think he's basically right, since it matches the deflationary stuff I've read about mental causation, but something feels off about it. Go through it and tell me what's right, what's an overstatement or a conflation, and whether his conclusion about beliefs actually follows even if the earlier part is fine. And if this thought experiment doesn't really bear on mental causation at all and I'm asking the wrong question, say so plainly rather than humoring me. Also, flag which bits are just his gloss and which bits anyone working on this would agree on.
