Im setting the final for a senior seminar on teleosemantics — we've done the usual, Millikan's selectional story, Dretske's indicator story, Fodor's disjunction complaints, swampman, all that. My problem is that every essay question I draft can be answered by someone who's just memorised the standard menu: content = function, misrepresentation = failure to perform the function, swampman has no content, disjunction problem, done. I want one question that makes them actually do teleosemantics on a case they havn't seen instead of reciting it.

So here's what I've been toying with. I've made up a position for them to attack — I've been calling it present-tense teleosemantics (PTT). The idea: a state's content is fixed by the tracking function the state has right now, where right now means the role it currently plays in keeping the system viable, what it does in the system's ongoing regulation of its behaviour that the system currently depends on. No appeal to ancestry, selection history, learning history, or anything behind the state. If it currently does that job and the system currently needs that job done, it represents the condition it tracks while doing it.

And I've made up a creature to run it on: the cinder moth. Ancestrally the moth had a magnetic organ whose output drove a northward flight during migration; the migration destination is gone (built over), and now the same organ's output drives the moth to settle on warm masonry at night, which is what actually keeps it alive through the cold. The magnetic field is still what the organ responds to, the ancestral destination is not where the moth ends up, and the behaviour keeping it alive is the new one. I want them to work out what the organ's state represents under (a) selectional teleosemantics, (b) Dretske-style indicator semantics, (c) PTT, then argue which verdict is right, and say whether the case shows teleosemantics needs a notion of function that's neither purely historical nor purely present-tense.

Give me the question as I'd hand it to them — one essay, about 40 minutes of writing, they pick 1 of 3 on the paper so it can't be a dissertation — then a model answer of roughly 700-800 words written the way a strong student would write it, then a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific moves that should cost marks, so the A/B line is something I can point at in the script rather than a vibe.

Things I'd want riding on marks: whether they see that PTT makes misrepresentation either impossible or merely malfunction; whether they see the normal-conditions trap (you can't say tracks in normal conditions without smuggling in content, since normal is just whatever conditions the function was selected for, which is exactly what PTT gave up); whether they notice the cinder moth is a case where the historical and present-tense verdicts come apart and say something principled about which to trust rather than splitting the difference; and whether they can say what a recruitment-style function — the function it has now because of what it's now doing for the system — would have to look like so as not to collapse into either disjunct.

No citations, I care about the argument; if they name Millikan or Dretske fine, but no marks should ride on who said what. One more thing: in the marking scheme, flag which parts have a defensible answer that differs from my model one, so I know where to give credit for a good argument I didn't anticipate, and give me a one-liner per mark-bearing point on how students typically get that point wrong.
