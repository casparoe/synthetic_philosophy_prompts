I'm a graduate student in metaphysics putting together a session for an advanced seminar on laws of nature, and what I want from you is a decision tree — a flowchart, really — that carries a reader from a minimal starting commitment to a named position about what laws are. The pedagogical point is that students should find out which view they actually hold by answering questions about how the world is, not by picking a label they already like, so please build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a roster of famous names: first isolate the questions that genuinely split the positions, then locate the positions within the space those questions generate. The starting point should be something almost everyone in the room already accepts — that our best scientific theories state laws, and that the world contains some vast arrangement of particular, non-modal facts (which things are where, which quantities take which values, and so on) — and the first branches should ask what that starting point leaves open: whether the whole non-modal arrangement fixes what the laws are or whether the laws outrun it; whether a law-statement is a true description of a pattern in that arrangement or carries some further modal ingredient; whether the laws govern or produce the regularities or merely systematize them; whether they are metaphysically necessary or contingent; and whether they are grounded in something else or simply taken as primitive. Every node has to be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing where it leads — no node may be "do you accept Humean supervenience?" in disguise.

I want the leaves to include at least the following: a naive regularity theory; a best-system analysis of the Ramsey–Lewis kind; a governing or necessitation view on which laws are external relations that constrain what happens; a dispositional-essentialist view on which the laws flow from the natures of the properties themselves; a primitivist view on which the laws are an additional, ungrounded feature of reality; and an eliminativist view on which there are no laws over and above the regularities and the word "law" is doing no explanatory work. For each leaf, give the position a name, one sentence stating its most awkward or costly commitment, and a short note on where it stands on the necessity/contingency question and on whether it is Humean or anti-Humean in spirit. Where the tree throws up a combination of answers that nobody defends — say, a view on which the laws are contingent and non-governing yet still outrun the non-modal facts — mark it as unoccupied and say briefly whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate with the questions I have given it (my suspicion is that the two anti-Humean views that locate necessity in the properties themselves will be hard to tell apart), and tell me what further question, if any, would pull them apart. Keep the whole instrument to something a seminar could work through in an hour, so no more than roughly a dozen nodes.
