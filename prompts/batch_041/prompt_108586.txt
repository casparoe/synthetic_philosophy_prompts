I teach the mixed-strategy recitation in a first-year graduate game theory course: twenty students, eighty minutes, whiteboard only, no handouts. Roughly half are economists and half come from computer science theory. They have had the lectures on mixed strategies and on Nash equilibrium; they can solve a two-by-two mixed equilibrium by hand, and they know what a strictly dominated pure strategy is. What they have not seen is dominance by a mixed strategy. In one session I have to teach four things: that a strategy can be strictly dominated by a lottery over its rivals even when no single rival strategy dominates it; the picture that makes that fact feel necessary rather than lucky; what happens when the elimination is iterated; and what survives at the end, and what that result is and is not good for as a solution concept.

I would like one game to carry the whole session, and I have built this one. Player 1 has Top, Middle, Bottom; player 2 has Left, Right. Payoffs are written with player 1's first.

| | Left | Right |
|---|---|---|
| Top | 3, 2 | 0, 0 |
| Middle | 0, 2 | 3, 0 |
| Bottom | 1, 0 | 1, 5 |

My instinct is that this single matrix can do all the work. I would rather be told now that it cannot than find out in front of them.

Plan the session for me. I need:

- the order in which the ideas have to come, with the dependencies made explicit: what must already be in place before each step, and what specifically goes wrong for a student who is handed a later step without its predecessor;
- a verdict on the game above as the carrying example, with the features of it that are doing work at each point where it reappears, and the point at which the students rather than I should be the ones to produce the dominating mixture; if it should be replaced or supplemented, what the replacement must have that mine lacks (or the reverse), and where in the sequence it belongs;
- the misconceptions they will arrive with or form. My guess is that the big one is that a dominated strategy is one the opponent can punish, followed by treating the dominating mixture as something the player actually plays, and by reading survival of the elimination as a licence to play the survivor; for each, the precise point in the sequence at which it should be headed off, and what I should say there;
- one question or short task for the last ten minutes that separates a student who can run the arithmetic from one who sees why the mixture test is the right test and the pure-strategy test only a special case of it;
- what I should deliberately leave out and announce that I am leaving out. I am already inclined to skip the separating-hyperplane proof and the common-knowledge-of-rationality foundations; tell me whether that is right, and rule on whether to say anything at all about weak dominance and its order-sensitivity, and about how much of this picture survives with three or more players, where the clean two-player equivalence between strict dominance and never being a best response stops being clean.
