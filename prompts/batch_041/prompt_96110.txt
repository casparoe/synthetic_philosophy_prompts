I am a novelist, and four weeks from now I am to give the toast at the wedding dinner of two friends — a couple whose history includes a year I am largely responsible for, and who have asked me, more than once and never quite as a condition, not to bring that year up in company. The bride's family, who will fill most of the room, know nothing of it. The couple's friends know all of it. The groom's mother suspects something. I want a toast in which the most important thing I have to say — that I know what I cost them, that I have not forgotten it, and that I am glad, past argument, that they are standing where they are standing — is available to those two and to no one else, and is never once stated. It must also be a good toast for the room: funny where it can afford to be, warm throughout, and complete as a toast for a guest who hears in it nothing but affection.

Write the draft, and build it the way Grice would have built it rather than the way a greeting card would. Four constraints, which I have been thinking about more than is good for me.

First: it is not enough that I intend them to understand. I mean the thing only if I intend them to understand it because they recognize that I meant it; an implication that arrives without their knowing it was sent is not a message. So the meaning has to be recoverable by inference from what is actually there in the speech, on the assumption that I am being cooperative, by people who hold the relevant history.

Second: it must not depend on any particular word. If I paraphrase the passage and the point survives the paraphrase, then I have carried it in what I said; if the point evaporates the moment I choose different words, I have written a hint rather than a meaning, and it should come out. Grice, if I have him right, took this non-detachability to be the mark of the conversational kind.

Third: it must be withdrawable. If the groom's mother corners me by the coats and asks whether I was apologizing up there, I need to be able to say, without contradicting a single sentence I uttered, that I was toasting a marriage. I am aware that this is the constraint that looks like cowardice, and I want you to tell me plainly, in a few lines at the end, whether it is — whether an apology that can be called off this way is an apology at all, or whether the gap between what I say and what I only mean is simply the gap I am hiding in. I would rather be told that I have written a bad-faith machine than be flattered.

Fourth: the room must hold. The implication has to be particular to those two and to what we three know; everyone else must be able to hear the whole speech as nothing more than a good toast, without strain, and without the sense that something is being kept from them — because something will be, and if the toast advertises the fact it fails as a toast.

Give me the speech itself, five minutes and no more, and then, briefly, an account of where the meaning actually lives in it, which sentence is doing the load-bearing work, and what it would take to cancel it.
