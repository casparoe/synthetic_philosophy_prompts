I teach a graduate seminar on Wittgenstein's later epistemology, and I want to stage the live disagreement over what the certainties in On Certainty actually are. Write a dialogue, as exactly five numbered exchanges, between two equally competent philosophers: one who holds that these certainties are not propositions at all but ungrounded ways of acting — animal certainties that can be neither doubted nor justified, and at which any request for a reason simply runs out — and one who holds that they are ordinary propositions with a special status, exempt from doubt for now and open to revision when the evidence shifts. To get them off the textbook examples, give them a case of my own devising: a scientist's certainty that her field's standard instrument is not systematically miscalibrated, together with the fact that such certainties have occasionally proved false and been abandoned without the practice around them collapsing. Have each of them press the other where it hurts — whether the first view can account for a certainty being reversed at all, and whether the second, by making the certainty answerable to evidence, has quietly turned it into an ordinary empirical belief and lost what was distinctive about it. Each exchange should be one short speech from each philosopher, responsive to what the other has just said rather than talking past it, and the dialogue should open and close mid-argument, with no scene-setting, framing narration, or summary at the end.
