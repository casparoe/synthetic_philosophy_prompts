I'm a second-year PhD student in a group that works on alignment and evaluation, and next week I have to circulate a one-page position statement on methodology for our reading group. The group is split between people who treat alignment as an empirical engineering problem and people who treat it as a decision-theoretic one, and I'd like the statement to survive both audiences. Before I send it round I want a consistency audit, because I have a nagging sense that the position can't all be held at once, though I can't yet see where the seams are. Here it is, roughly as I'd write it.

(1) A safety claim is only as strong as its worst case. When I say a training procedure is safe, I mean: for every hypothesis about the system's objective that is consistent with the evidence we'll have at deployment, the system's behaviour stays within acceptable bounds. Average behaviour across hypotheses is not a safety property, it's a performance number. (2) Uncertainty about objectives should be handled at design time: build the system so that it's safe across a wide range of objectives rather than safe because we happened to write the right one down, and where it's uncertain, have it preserve its ability to be corrected rather than commit to irreversible action — in a case where asking me is cheap, I'd rather it stopped and asked than finished the task. (3) Systems that won't act under uncertainty are worthless. If I build something that does nothing whenever it can't establish that its action is optimal, I've built a paperweight; and a system whose behaviour can be pinned down in advance is one that can't do anything I couldn't do myself. I want systems that learn on the job and act. (4) The failure I most want to avoid is a low-probability, very-high-stakes one: a system that behaves well in the overwhelming majority of training runs and deployments, and catastrophically in a rare one. So the quantity I actually care about is expected loss concentrated in the tail, not the worst case. (5) Statistical testing is legitimate evidence: run the system across a broad sample of environments and see how it does, and for practical purposes that can be enough. (6) None of this should block deploying systems whose expected benefits are large, provided the risk is bounded in the sense of (1).

Go through this and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each real tension is. For each one, say what the cheapest way to restore consistency would be and what that repair costs me — I care more about the price than about being told the package is inconsistent. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I'd be giving up to make it.
