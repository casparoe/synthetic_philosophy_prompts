I'm a second-year PhD student and my partner is a cosmologist, which is how I ended up losing most of last month to the question of whether anything we do could matter if the universe is spatially infinite and more or less uniform. The position I've talked myself into goes like this. To compare two possible worlds that each contain infinitely many lives, you line the lives up in some order (the natural numbers will do), compute the running difference between the two worlds' cumulative welfare as you go out along that order, and then look at where the running difference ends up: if it settles on a positive number the first world is better, a negative number and it's worse, zero and they're equally good, and if it never settles then the two worlds simply aren't comparable in welfare terms. I think this is the right axiology and not just a bookkeeping trick, and I like that it keeps finite differences finite — on this view adding one extra happy life to a world that already contains infinitely many happy lives makes that world better, and adding one extra miserable life to an infinite paradise makes it worse. That seems to me exactly what you want, since the alternative is an infinity of bliss swallowing any finite amount of torture. I'm fairly sure this is what people call the limit-based approach, though I can never remember whose it actually is.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against it. Three worries I already have and can't tell how seriously to take. First, the verdict seems to depend on how I line the lives up: reorder them and the running difference can settle somewhere else, or stop settling at all, and I can't see why my arbitrary bookkeeping should get to decide which world is better. Second, whenever the running difference doesn't converge I get incomparability, and I'm suspicious of how much work "not comparable" is doing here — a defender can apparently reach for it the moment a case gets embarassing. Third, I have a vague memory that adding the same extra population to both worlds can flip the comparison, which if it's true feels like it ought to be fatal, but I can't reconstruct the example. Past those three: are there cases where the view gives a determinate answer that's just clearly wrong, rather than merely odd? What does it predict that a cardinality-based or an averaging-based view doesn't, and do those predictions tell for it or against it? What would the view's own defenders have to concede actually counts against them, as opposed to dismissing as a cost worth paying? And be honest about whether the whole thing has been insulated into unfalsifiability — if "the limit doesn't exist" can be pulled out whenever the view is threatened, is that a bug, or is it just the shape any view of this kind has to have? Relatedly, if the cosmologists eventually settle that the universe is finite, does that make the view idle rather than false, and does that distinction matter? Rank whatever you come up with from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd much rather have the arguments than a reading list — I know the literature well enough and don't need pointers. For what it's worth, the thing I'd personally find most persuasive is one clean case where the view says "better" and I'd confidently say no it isn't, so if you've got one, lead with it.
