I teach a 200-level comparative politics seminar -- 18 students, one 80-minute block -- and I want to run a session on democratic backsliding that gets them past the checklist. They can all list courts, media, elections, term limits, but they talk about backsliding as if it were a thermometer reading, and I want them to feel the thing that makes the concept both indispensable and slippery: that it names a real pattern of erosion, and that the label is also a move in a fight, applied by people with interests, at a threshold someone has to choose.

Here's the seed I've got. I invented a fictional country, Ravenmark, with a ceremonial president chosen by parliament, a constitutional court whose judges are appointed by a two-thirds vote, a public broadcaster funded by a license fee that the finance ministry can temporarily suspend, and a election commission whose members are nominated by parties in proportion to their seats. I've sketched ten events over eight years, each one individually defensible as normal politics -- a tax audit of an opposition newspaper, a court-packing proposal that fails, a redistricting commission that changes its rules, a broadcaster whose license fee is frozen during a budget standoff, an election commissioner who resigns and is replaced by a former party lawyer, etc. -- but cumulatively they shift the terrain. I want the students in role as government minister, opposition leader, constitutional court judge, public broadcaster editor, election commissioner, foreign donor, ordinary voter, and investigative journalist, each with a secret objective: some want to protect stability, some want to name backsliding to trigger international pressure, some want to avoid the label because it scares off investment, some just want to keep their job. They get the event cards and have to place each on a spectrum from normal politics to authoritarian consolidation, then place a single threshold token on the spectrum; Ravenmark counts as backsliding only if the events cross the token. Then they have to negotiate a joint public statement about the country's democratic health. For round one, don't even call it backsliding -- frame it as a democratic health audit. Round two: they find out their statement will be cited by a foreign parliament considering sanctions, and by the government as proof of foreign interference.

Can you design the whole thing? Rules, materials, role sheets, the ten event cards with enough specific legal and institutional detail that each one is defensible on its own, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the concept: minimalist versus substantive definitions, legal erosion versus illegal rupture, incumbent advantage, the asymmetry between labeling your opponents and labeling yourself, and the question of who gets to decide. I also want to know what will predictably go wrong the first time -- someone will try to win, someone will refuse to place the token, someone will say the whole exercise is rigged -- and how to handle it, and I want you to be honest about which feature of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in the debrief. No citations or reading lists, obviously; I want it runnable from the materials you invent.
