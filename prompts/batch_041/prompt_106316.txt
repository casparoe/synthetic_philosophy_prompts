I'm a third-year student in a joint philosophy and computer science program, and for a graduate seminar on induction and computational learning I've drafted an essay arguing that Solomonoff induction, far from vindicating Occam's razor, quietly dissolves the realist reading of it — and I'd like your comments on the draft. Please take the role of someone who works at the intersection of algorithmic information theory and the philosophy of science, and think the problem through carefully before you answer; it's fine to work it out at length in your head first. I'm not asking for references, a reading list, or a literature review — I'll supply citations myself, so please don't spend any of your reply on them.

The draft's thesis is that identifying the simplest hypothesis with the shortest program that generates the data does not tell us anything about nature's economy; it tells us something about the description language we happen to be using. I run three arguments. First, I grant that the invariance theorem gives us something — changing the reference machine can shift every description length by at most a fixed additive constant, and that constant is independent of the data — but I argue this is far weaker than the usual gloss suggests, because the constant is machine-dependent, is not something we can compute, and in principle can be made arbitrarily large; so, I claim, for any finite set of hypotheses and essentially any ranking I want among them, I can choose a reference machine that realizes that ranking. If that's right, the theorem buys convergence of predictions only in a limit no finite agent reaches, and gives no guidance about which machine is the right one to begin with. Second, I distinguish two things people mean by simplicity — compressibility in the algorithmic sense, and parametric simplicity in the sense of a model with few free parameters — and offer a case where they pull apart, a one-parameter model with a wildly nonlinear form versus a fifty-parameter model with a rigidly linear structure, arguing that defenses of the razor slide between the two senses without noticing. Third, I conclude that the razor is best read not as a claim about the economy of nature but as a norm governing our own representational commitments, a view I'm calling simplicity-relativism, and I admit near the end that I can't tell whether this is a clarification of the razor or a reductio of it.

What I want from you is four things: (1) accuracy — is my statement of the invariance theorem correct, and is the "any ranking for a suitable machine" claim actually true or an overreach, since I worry the constant might not be as free as I'm treating it; (2) whether the two senses of simplicity genuinely come apart or whether the parametric notion just reduces to the algorithmic one once you fix an encoding, which would undercut my second argument; (3) whether the conclusion is too strong — specifically, whether I'm conflating the impossibility of a machine-independent ranking with the impossibility of a justified one; and (4) whether the essay is genuinely interesting or a familiar point dressed up in fresh notation, plus the single change that would most improve it. Please begin your reply with your overall verdict on the draft in the very first sentence, and then give your reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate what I've asked for.
