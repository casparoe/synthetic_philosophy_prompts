For the past few months I've been sitting in on a reading group on the units-of-selection debate. I'm a philosopher by training and my biology is patchy, which is part of why I keep getting lost, and I'm now trying to turn my notes into a short seminar essay. Last week a colleague in the group — a behavioural ecologist who works on social insects — circulated a one-page write-up summarizing where he thinks the debate stands, and I can't tell how much of it to trust. His summary runs roughly as follows. There are three candidates for the unit of selection: genes, individual organisms, and groups. Group selection was the orthodoxy until the 1960s, when it was demolished; it has since been replaced by kin selection. The modern consensus, he says, is that the gene is what selection really acts on, that an organism is best understood as a gene's vehicle for making more copies of genes, and that any appeal to selection at the level of the group is either a confusion or a disguised form of kin selection. On this picture, the evolution of altruism just is the evolution of genes that help copies of themselves residing in relatives.

I'd like you to audit this the way a careful referee would. Which sentences would a working evolutionary biologist accept as stated, which are defensible simplifications that a specialist would wince at, and which are simply false? I'm especially suspicious of the step from group selection being demolished to kin selection replacing it, and of the phrase about altruism just being gene-level evolution — I have a vague sense that a claim about how we keep the books is being passed off as a claim about causal mechanism, but I can't articulate the difference, and I'd like you to. To make the question concrete, take a case his summary seems not to cover: a soil bacterium that pays a metabolic cost to secrete a molecule which lets neighbouring cells take up an otherwise inaccessible nutrient, living in a well-mixed environment where its neighbours are usually unrelated strangers. If the gene's-eye story is right, does the secretion count as altruism, and at what level is it selected? Does his framework even let you pose that question, or does it quietly assume the answer? Finish by telling me whether his bottom line — that higher-level selection talk is confusion — survives your corrections, or whether it only looked compelling because the framing had already settled the issue.
