You are an epistemologist and philosopher of science who knows Hume's argument about testimony to miracles down to its joints, and who is unusually good at turning an abstract principle into a procedure a busy non-philosopher can actually run. I'm a senior editor at a long-form magazine covering religion and science; several times a month I have to decide how much weight to give a dossier of testimony about an extraordinary event — a healing, an apparition, an incorrupt body, occasionally just a wildly improbable survival or recovery — and I have to decide without interviewing the witnesses myself, usually inside a day, with a colleague who has no philosophy or statistics. I've read Hume's essay on miracles and I broadly accept his rule that testimony should only be credited when it would be a greater marvel for the testimony to be false than for the event to have happened, but I've never been able to make that rule actually settle anything at my desk. Worse, I suspect the simple reading of it may not even apply to my cases: most of them involve events that are merely statistically extraordinary rather than violations of any law of nature, and I don't know whether Hume's maxim still bites when there's no law to violate — only a base rate.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure my desk can run on any dossier. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or of taste rather than of evidence. Build in the distinctions that matter: the prior improbability of the event versus the reliability of the testimony, many witnesses who turn out to share one source versus genuinely independent ones, and the difference between a claim that is merely unlikely and one that would reorganize everything else I believe if true. Be explicit that I'm not a statistician and can't always get a number, say what to do when I can't, and mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I read rationalist blogs and I'm comfortable with talk of priors, base rates, likelihood ratios, and the outside view, but the checklist itself has to work for my colleague, so don't make it depend on that vocabulary.

Then run it on three cases, at least one of which is a non-religious extraordinary claim so I can see it treat the religious and the secular consistently: a healing attested by a dozen people at a shrine, an incorrupt body whose devotees all trace back to one enthusiastic account, and a survival that is statistically freakish but perfectly physical. I expect one of these to come out with more credence than my secular instincts are happy with — include that case and don't soften it. Finish by telling me where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot do. Start by brainstorming the distinctions that must survive the simplification and the ones I can safely drop, then give the procedure, then the cases. Throughout, say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
