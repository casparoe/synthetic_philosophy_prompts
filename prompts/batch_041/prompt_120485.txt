I've been reading around supertasks for a seminar paper and have run into a summary I don't trust enough to lean on. The philosophy encyclopedia entry I've been working from introduces the topic roughly like this: 'Supertasks were introduced by J. F. Thomson in 1954 with his lamp puzzle. A lamp starts off; it is switched on, then off a minute later, then on again half a minute after that, then off after a quarter of a minute, and so on, each interval half the previous one, so that infinitely many flips have been made by the two-minute mark. Thomson's argument is that the lamp must be both on and off at the end: it cannot be on, since every time it was switched on it was switched off again before the end, and it cannot be off, since every time it was switched off it was switched on again; but a lamp must be either on or off. So the description is contradictory, and since the only thing wrong with it is that it demands infinitely many operations in a finite time, Thomson concluded that no supertask can be performed — an infinite sequence of tasks can never be finished, because there is no last one. Benacerraf's 1962 reply is that the description simply fails to determine the lamp's state at the two-minute mark: the flips fix the lamp's condition at each switching time, but there is no switching at the end, so nothing settles the matter. The lamp's final state is therefore genuinely indeterminate — not unknown, but unsettled — and since no contradiction follows, the puzzle is best read as a trick about the limits of our descriptions rather than a discovery about the limits of the possible.'

A friend of mine who is doing a maths degree insists the whole thing is a non-issue, for two reasons. First, he says the paradox is an artifact of the switch being a jump rather than a continuous quantity: if the device instead dimmed the lamp, setting its brightness to 1, then a half, then a quarter, and so on, the brightness at the two-minute mark would be 0, perfectly well-defined; so any supertask whose state variable varies continuously has a determinate outcome, and Thomson's lamp is just a badly designed example. Second, he says no real lamp could be flipped infinitely fast anyway — the switch would have to move faster and faster, so the setup is physically vacuous and tells us nothing about what is conceptually possible. I'm inclined to think Benacerraf has the better of it, but I can't tell whether the entry is giving me his actual reply or a tidied-up version of it, and I don't know what to make of my friend's objections.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Go through the entry's claims one at a time and mark each as accurate, as a harmless simplification, or as a genuine misrepresentation, saying in each case what someone working on this literature would put in its place; be explicit about what the entry gets wrong about Benacerraf's position, since I suspect it has been flattened into something more convenient; and tell me whether the moral it draws — that the puzzle concerns our descriptions rather than what is possible — survives once those corrections are made, and what a defender of Thomson would say in response. Finally, please assess my friend's two points: does the dimmer variant actually show that the indeterminacy is an artifact of the on/off switch, and does the physical impossibility of an infinitely fast switch settle anything about whether the supertask is conceptually coherent?
