Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words, in the third person and past tense. Set it in a fictional river port, in a period left deliberately unspecified but pre-industrial in its technology.

The port's customs house is required by law to record every cargo that passes through it, and for two generations it has done so with a device called the Concordance, which produces a written name for each crate, bale, and barrel set before it. The Concordance has never been observed to err. Its names are drawn from a closed dictionary: every entry is defined only by other entries, and the definitions have been revised so many times that no one now living can point to the occasion on which a term was first fastened to something outside the book. Certifying that the Concordance's names are anchored — that they are about the cargo rather than merely in step with it — falls to the guild of inspectors, whose seals are themselves entries in the dictionary. Your protagonist is a newly appointed inspector, taking up the post after the death of the man who trained him.

The story's central pressure is this: the Concordance's names track something with perfect reliability, but nothing in the archive can establish what it is they track. The same names would be produced if the device were answering to the cargo itself, or to the light falling on the cargo, or to some narrower or wider feature of the scene that has never yet come apart from the cargo in this harbor. The inspector's professional duty is to devise a test that would separate these possibilities. Make that attempt the spine of the story, and make its difficulty concrete rather than abstract: any such test must be described, recorded, and adjudicated in the dictionary's own terms, and the archive's only physical witnesses — sealed sample cases left by the Concordance's founders — have aged into objects that no longer resemble what they were meant to witness.

Additional requirements:

- Invent the guild's own word for the relation the inspectors are supposed to certify, and let its sense shift, without comment, over the course of the story.
- The story must contain one scene in which the question becomes physically urgent — in which the inspector has to act on an answer before he can justify one — and that scene must turn on something specific to the port's setting rather than on a generic thought experiment.
- Do not let the story turn on the device becoming conscious, wanting, deceiving, or waking up; and do not let it conclude that the names mean nothing.
- No character may name or explain a philosophical position. The ideas should surface through procedure, paperwork, seals, sample cases, and the inspector's work.
- Avoid the familiar furniture of thought experiments about artificial minds: sealed rooms, rule books, homunculi, blinking lamps, and tests named after their inventors.
- The ending must commit to something definite about what the inspector does next, even if the underlying question is left open. A story that dissolves into ambiguity will be judged a failure.
- Invent everything. Do not quote, cite, or allude to real texts, people, or scholarship.
