Six months ago I began sitting with a small Buddhist study group that reads early discourses in translation a few pages at a time and argues about them afterward, and last week two of the regulars had an exchange I have not been able to settle in my own mind. The topic has become personally pressing: I am trying to work out what I would be committing myself to if I began taking the precepts seriously, and I would like a careful adjudication rather than a survey of the literature.

Ruth opened. Buddhist ethics, she said, is at bottom a consequentialist theory, and the precepts are instrumental rules of thumb. Everything in the framework runs through consequences: acts are assessed by whether they lead to suffering or to its cessation, for oneself and for others; the precepts bind in general only because breaking them reliably produces bad results; and the tradition's readiness to set them aside in extreme cases, such as the familiar thought experiment in which killing one person would prevent a massacre, shows that they are subordinate to outcomes rather than categorical constraints. The precepts are a technology, not a code.

Miguel answered that this gets the structure of the doctrine backwards. In the tradition's own analysis of karma, what fixes the moral quality of an act is the agent's intention, not the state of the world the act produces. An act done from greed, hatred, or delusion is unwholesome however it turns out; an act done from generosity or compassion is wholesome even if it misfires. A theory that grades acts by the agent's motive rather than by their results is not a consequentialism at all, he said; it is closer to a virtue or character ethic, or at most a motive consequentialism, which is a different animal and needs a separate defence.

Ruth replied that motive consequentialism is still consequentialism, since it merely locates the good-making consequence in the formation of good character. And intention matters here, she said, because of what intentions do: intention is the proximate cause of action, action of habit, and habit of the suffering or peace that follows. Karma is a causal doctrine about long-run effects, and intention earns its place in it by being causally potent, not by being intrinsically valuable.

Then the doctrine cannot be a maximizing consequentialism, Miguel said, and that is what the term is usually taken to mean. In the cosmology the effects of an act ripple out over beginningless time and across rebirths, so no act's consequences ever terminate. But a maximizing theory needs to rank outcomes by total value, and where consequences are endless and the population of future beings is not fixed, those comparisons are undefined; every option threatens to come out with infinite value, and so all options tie. A theory whose comparisons all tie gives no guidance at all. This is not the ordinary complaint about uncertainty, he added; it is a failure of the value ordering itself.

Ruth answered that the infinite-horizon objection proves too much, because it applies to any consequentialism that takes the far future seriously, including perfectly respectable secular ones. Consequentialists routinely choose among options whose long-run effects they cannot rank, and they do it by giving priority to the near-term effects they can estimate with some confidence. The tradition does the same: its operative instruction is to abandon what is unwholesome now and cultivate what is wholesome, and it treats distant karmic consequences as background motivation rather than as data to be summed. And the no-self doctrine, far from being an obstacle, is an argument for the impartiality consequentialism requires: if the boundary between my good and yours is as conventional as the boundary between my good now and my good next year, then suffering counts equally wherever it occurs. That is agent-neutral concern, not an alternative to it.

Miguel's last word was that if the guidance reduces to cultivating the wholesome, the consequences are doing no work in it and the label is doing all the labour. There is also, he said, a deeper mismatch. Consequentialism evaluates acts by states of affairs, and it needs the good to be something like a total quantity of well-being on which different outcomes can be compared. In this tradition the goal is not a state of the world at all; it is the cessation of suffering, described as something attained by each being rather than as a global condition whose value could be computed and maximized. Meanwhile the theory's own operational test, the quality of the intention, can be applied without ever ranking worlds. When a doctrine's working criterion and its official value theory come apart like that, he said, we should follow the working criterion.

I have read the exchange over several times and I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it; each of them seems to land a real blow and then to overreach somewhere. What I want is a point-by-point adjudication. For each of the six moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why, and be willing to split a move, since several of them contain more than one claim. Then say who is closer to right overall, and what both of them are missing: the distinction or consideration that would recast the dispute rather than resolve it in either one's favour. I do not need reading recommendations; I need the arguments assessed.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — who has the better of the argument, and on which points — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
