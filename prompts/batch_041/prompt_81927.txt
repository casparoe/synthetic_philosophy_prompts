I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to make sense of a pattern that keeps surfacing both in my work and in the things I read for pleasure: structures that fold back on themselves, where one level operates on the level above it and the level above operates back on it. I have seen the phrase the inviolate level used for the level that such a structure cannot reach, and I would like a principle that tells me when a self-referential structure has one and when it does not.

Here are my verdicts on a set of cases, going by gut. Two hands, each drawing the other, as in Escher's Drawing Hands: coherent, and it has an inviolate level, namely the artist, who is not in the picture and without whom neither hand would be drawn. A program that prints its own source code: coherent, and it has an inviolate level too, namely the interpreter that gives its symbols their meaning; the program can mention itself but cannot change what its own operations mean. The liar sentence, the one that says of itself that it is false: not coherent, it has no truth value, and I would say it has no inviolate level, because there is nothing for its truth to rest on. Gödel's sentence, which arithmetic is made to utter about itself, saying of itself that it is unprovable: coherent, and it has an inviolate level, namely the rules of the formal system, which the sentence can describe but cannot alter. A compiler whose source is written in the language it compiles, bootstrapped from an earlier version of itself: coherent, with the seed binary or the machine beneath it as the inviolate level. A constitution that contains the procedure for its own amendment: coherent, with the amendment rule as the inviolate level; but a constitution that allowed any of its clauses, including that one, to be repealed by ordinary legislation would have no inviolate level, and I do not think it would really be a constitution. A game whose players may rewrite the rules as they go: no inviolate level, and I do not think it is a game, because there is nothing for the moves to be moves of.

My provisional principle is that a tangled hierarchy is well-behaved exactly when it has a base case, a level whose behaviour does not depend on the levels above it, the way a recursive function needs a base case if it is to terminate at all, and that the liar sentence is the case with no base case. But I cannot tell whether the base case has to lie outside the system, as the artist and the interpreter and the hardware do, or whether it may lie inside it, as the rules of arithmetic appear to, and my verdicts pull me in both directions. The Gödel sentence and the liar sentence look to me almost identical in structure, yet I call one a discovery and the other a dead end, so whatever principle I am reaching for has to say what actually separates them, and not merely that one has an outside and the other does not.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of rival principles, captures these verdicts. State it precisely, give the case that motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed: a programming language in which every symbol's meaning, including the meaning of the constructs used to define meaning, is itself a redefinable value, so that a program can redefine the very operations it runs on. Would such a language have an inviolate level, and could a convention that is never written down in the language itself serve as one? Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and tell me which one I ought to give up. Please write your answer in British English.
