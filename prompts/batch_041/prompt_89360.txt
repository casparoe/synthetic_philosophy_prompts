A colleague in my department has spent the past year rating conspiracy theories one at a time, always on the same single question — whether it is reasonable for an ordinary person to believe the theory — and I am trying to reconstruct her standard before she goes to press. Her verdicts so far are these.

Reasonable to believe: that a single pharmaceutical firm buried trial results showing that one of its drugs caused serious harm, the buried results surfacing years later in litigation. That a small unit inside a national security service ran an unlawful domestic surveillance programme, later confirmed by declassified files and a whistleblower. That a handful of executives at an industry trade association colluded to suppress an unfavourable internal study, as shown in leaked minutes.

Not reasonable to believe: that the Apollo landings were staged in a studio, which would have required thousands of engineers, astronauts and their families to keep the secret. That a mass-casualty school shooting was staged by a large cast of government-funded crisis actors. That a national health service invented a disease in order to justify mass vaccination, a claim resting on a few anomalous statistics and requiring tens of thousands of clinicians to stay silent.

The new case she is about to be shown: a theory that a single mid-level regulator, working with two or three colleagues, quietly altered a safety guideline in order to spare a firm in which they later took employment. There is no leaked document and no whistleblower, only a suggestive timeline and some unexplained omissions in the public record, and none of the individuals concerned has a documented history of anything of the kind.

Tell me how she will rate it. I am less interested in the rating than in the rule she is running. Set out the candidate rules that fit her existing verdicts equally well — for instance a rule keyed to how many people would have to keep the secret, one keyed to whether some documentary trace has already surfaced, one keyed to the accused party's record — say what each of them predicts about the new case, tell me which you would bet on and why, and name the further theory on which the surviving rules would make opposite predictions. If you think her single question is quietly conflating two different standards — whether a theory deserves investigation and whether the evidence on offer licenses belief — say so, and say what difference that makes to your prediction.

Finish with a single line of the form Verdict: <reasonable | not reasonable>; <short name for the rule she is running>; <the discriminating case, named in a few words>.
