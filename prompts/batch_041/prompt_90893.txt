I run a weekly 75-minute session for about 18 second- and third-year philosophy students, nominally a value theory seminar but honestly it turns into whatever the room wants to argue about, and the projector in that room has been broken since October so anything visual has to go on paper or the whiteboard. Next week I want to do the question of whether better-than can fail to be transitive. I know there's a debate about counterexamples to transitivity, spectrum-style arguments, and I've seen money-pump arguments aimed at people whose preferences go in a circle, but I don't want a lecture on any of that and I don't need citations or a reading list — the students should walk into the problem themselves and only hear the vocabulary at the end. So please design me a runnable in-class activity rather than an explanation. What I want them to come away having felt is the specific squeeze: that every comparison they made themselves was obviously right, that the whole set of them can't be true at once if better-than is transitive, and that getting out costs something whichever way they go.

Here's the shape I have in mind, though change it if you can make the trap bite harder. Before class I make a deck of about ten cards, each describing a life with one chronic condition, given by two numbers: how long it lasts and how bad it is on a 0-10 scale (anchored so 1 is something you'd only notice if you thought about it, 10 is the worst you can imagine). The first card is the mildest and longest — a whole life of the faintest version — and each next card bumps the intensity up one notch while cutting the duration by a big chunk, so that any two neighbours differ by a barely-tolerable bit more pain but years and years less of it. In the session I split them into pairs, give each pair exactly one neighbouring pair of cards, and have them write a verdict slip: which of these two lives would you rather live. Nobody sees more than their own two cards. Then we pin the slips on the board in card order and read the chain out loud, and I take a show of hands on the two end cards against each other — and if the room flips, we've got a circle made entirely of their own judgements. After that I want a live demo with three volunteers and a bowl of chocolate coins or the raffle tickets left over from the department open day: a broker offers swaps along the chain, each swap costing one token, and since every swap is an improvement by the group's own verdicts, the volunteer trades their way back to the card they started with, minus the tokens. Then the last chunk of the session is repairs: split the room into four or five groups and give each one a way out to defend in three minutes — deny transitivity, take back one particular step and say which and why, say the two lives are incomparable or on a par, or bite the bullet and accept the chain's conclusion — then a quick vote on which repair cost the least, and only then do I name the ideas.

So what I need back is: the exact construction of the cards (with numbers, and tell me how to sanity-check that the neighbour verdicts really will be near-unanimous and the two ends really will come out the other way), the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and the debrief that maps each moment in the room onto the parts of the idea — transitivity, spectrum arguments, incomparability, lexically prioritising one dimension, and the money pump. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it; last time I tried something like this, half the room refused to choose at all on the grounds that both options were miserable, so I need a rule for that and a way to use those refusals rather than lose the room to them. And be honest about the part the activity can't do — where the staging shows a cost rather than proving anything, or where it quietly assumes a contested claim about rationality or about whether a forced choice is legitimate, so I know what I have to supply myself. Finally, say how confident you are that the ladder will actually produce the verdict pattern you're predicting, and flag the single design choice you're least sure about.
