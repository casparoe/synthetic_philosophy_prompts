In nearly every discussion of truth commissions I have taken part in, someone eventually reaches for a therapeutic analogy: the society is the patient, the atrocity is the wound, the commission is the therapist, testimony is the talking cure, and reconciliation is the recovery. The analogy is seldom decorative. It is usually doing argumentative work, and the conclusion it is made to support is that after mass atrocity the priority is to heal, that public testimony is itself medicine, and that whatever keeps the wound open — prosecutions, reparations disputes, continued political contestation — is a failure of care.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on truth commissions. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly, as a table if that helps: what plays the role of the patient, the therapist, the wound, the symptom, the session, the resistance, the cure, the confidentiality of the consulting room, the consent to treatment, and the fee — and note who pays it. Then say where the mapping breaks and, more importantly, which of the breaks bear on the conclusion the analogy is used to license, as opposed to being merely the analogy read uncharitably. Please consider at least the following candidates and rank them rather than list them: that a patient consents to treatment while a collectivity never does and does not have a single mind; that therapy depends on privacy whereas a commission's purpose is publicity; that a therapist serves one interest whereas a commission must adjudicate among victims, perpetrators, and a state that is itself implicated; that the analogy locates the injury in the psyche and thereby recasts a dispute about institutions, land, and power as a dispute about feelings; and that it treats truth as instrumental to well-being, whereas the demand for truth is often pressed as something owed to victims whether or not it heals them. Say also what the analogy quietly assumes about the relation between a collective and its members — whether individuals figure as ends in themselves or as organs of a larger patient — and whether any of the disanalogies can be repaired by a more careful version of the analogy, say family therapy instead of individual therapy, or a public-health campaign instead of a cure.

Finally: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it all along? If the analogy cannot carry it, what would be a better one for what a truth commission actually does? And say in passing whether the analogy fares differently where a commission had no amnesty to bargain with, as in Argentina, or where truth-telling was bound up with community courts and reduced sentences, as in Rwanda.
