I'm a third-year PhD student in epistemology, and next month I have to circulate a one-page statement of my own position for a seminar on higher-order evidence, so that the rest of the group can take shots at it before the term ends. I would rather find the cracks myself first, and I would rather have an audit than a sympathetic reading. So here is where I have landed, stated as flatly as I can manage.

(1) Evidentialism about the first-order question: the credence I am justified in having in a proposition is the one supported by my evidence bearing on that proposition. Facts about how reliable I am — that people with my background tend to be overconfident about this kind of question, that I was rushed, that I had not slept — are facts about me, not evidence about the proposition. (2) Higher-order defeat: and yet, if I learn that a belief of mine was produced by a process that is unreliable on that kind of question, it is no longer rational for me to go on holding the belief, even when I cannot point to any particular step that went wrong. (3) No rational akrasia: it is never rational to believe something while believing that it is irrational to believe it; people are akratic constantly, but never rationally so. (4) Conciliationism: when I learn that an epistemic peer — same evidence, comparable competence, no reason to think either of us is better placed — has reached a different conclusion on a question that turns on judgment rather than calculation, I should move a substantial way toward her credence. (5) An exemption I have not been able to talk myself out of: I do not think (4) applies to (4) itself. A good many epistemologists think conciliationism is false, several of them are at least as careful as I am, and I do not think that should move my confidence in conciliationism much at all; I keep it at around 0.85. (6) A verdict on a case from last term: a colleague pulled up a log of my own reading history and showed me that my conviction that a certain contested argument is sound took hold within ten minutes of my reading a very persuasive blog post defending it, and that I have never gone back to check whether it was the argument or the prose that moved me. I still find the argument compelling, and my evidence bearing on the argument itself is exactly what it was; my verdict is that I should leave my credence roughly where it is, since nothing has happened that bears on whether the argument is any good.

What I want is a consistency check, ordered by severity rather than offered as a list of equally bad problems. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with each other, and which only look tense? For each genuine tension, tell me which member would have to go and what giving it up would cost me — I care more about the price of the repair than about the repair itself. I would also like to know whether any of the trouble only appears when three or more of these are combined rather than when they are taken two at a time, and to finish with the single revision that would resolve the most while costing the least. Please do not tell me the position is rich or subtle; tell me where it breaks.
