I write about science and ideas for a living — mostly the messier end of medicine and psychology — and I've got a pattern sitting in my notes that I've never found a name for. Here's the shape of it. Experts build a category to pick out a real problem: a checklist a school uses to decide which kids get extra support, a symptom scale for a chronic condition where the diagnosis is also the thing that unlocks treatment or a benefit. It works for a while. Then the people being sorted learn the checklist — not by reading it, but by trial and error, by comparing notes, by noticing which questions the intake appointment asks and which it doesn't — and they start answering in whatever way gets them through the door. Clinicians notice the checklist is now catching people it shouldn't, so the experts do the obvious thing: they tighten the definition, add items, add exclusions, carve it finer. Which hands everyone a sharper target to aim at. Meanwhile the only thing keeping the numbers honest is the clinician's gut, which is exactly what the checklist was brought in to replace. So the category gets more and more carefully defined and means less and less, and every round of fixing it makes the next round necessary.

A friend who teaches philosophy told me this is just Hacking's looping effect — classifying people changes them, so you have to go back and revise the classification — and I see why, but it doesn't feel like the same animal. As I understand Hacking the interesting part is that people take the label up into who they are, and the label then has to answer to the people wearing it; what I'm describing is colder than that, nobody in my cases cares about the category itself, they just want the support, and the feedback runs through the instrument rather than through anybody's sense of self. Other people have offered Goodhart's law, or the social-science version with Campbell's name on it, and that's nearer, but it's too general: here the thing being gamed isn't only a measure of something, its also supposed to be a kind of person, and it's doing both jobs at once. Overdiagnosis and concept creep seem to point at the result rather than the mechanism, and it's definately not malingering — most of the people doing it aren't lying, they're being shaped by the questions. Someone said it's the medical version of teaching to the test, which I like, except a test isn't also an entitlement.

So, does this have a name? I've poked around and haven't found one that fits, and honestly I'd rather have the thing carved properly than have a label — I'm not after a reading list. What makes it seperate from the looping effect, from Goodhart, from plain faking? Does it only happen when the category is the gateway to something scarce — money, a drug, an accommodation — or would it happen to any checklist left in place long enough? Is the drift one-way, or does it swing back when the criteria get tight enough that the label stops being worth having? And if nothing established fits, coin something, define it so I can tell it apart from its neighbours, and tell me the cases at the edge where you're honestly not sure it applies.
