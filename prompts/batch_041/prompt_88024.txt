Something thats been bugging me. Think about how we explain big disasters after the fact. A train comes off the rails, people die, and the official safety investigation comes out and basically refuses to name a cause. It says theres no single root cause, it was a combination: the track was worn, the inspection regime had been cut back, the driver was on hour eleven of a shift, the alarm in the cab was confusing. And these reports usually say up front that their job is to find out what happened so it doesnt happen again, not to assign blame. Then a couple of years later a prosecutor charges the maintenance manager with manslaughter, and that case turns entirely on whether what he did or didn't do was a but-for cause of the deaths. Meanwhile the same report that refused to name a cause will definately recommend that inspections be doubled, which only makes sense if you think that with more inspections the crash wouldn't have happened, i.e. that the worn track was a cause all on its own. So what theory of causation is each of these practices presupposing? Is the no-single-cause line actually consistent with the report's own recommendations, or is it just a polite way of saying lots of things contributed that doesn't commit you to anything? And if the crash was genuinely overdetermined -- two seperate failures, either one of which would have been enough on its own to put the train on the ground -- does that mean the criminal case collapses for lack of a but-for cause, and does it also break the recommendation logic, since fixing one of the two things wouldn't have prevented anything? Same question for history, which is where I actually care about this: when a historian says a revolution or a war had many causes, is that the same claim as overdetermination or something much weaker (a list of conditions, most of which aren't sufficient on their own)? And when historians do the counterfactual move -- no bad harvest, no uprising -- are they quietly borrowing the prosecutor's but-for test, and does that mean overdetermined events are simply beyond historical explanation? Tell me what each practice is really committed to, which parts merely fit well rather than being required, where the whole thing is underdetermined between rival views, and what would actually change in the courtroom if we swapped the but-for test for one that tolerates overdetermination.
