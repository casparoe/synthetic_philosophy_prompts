My friend Mara has a guitar, a kayak and a bike, and her preferences over them run in a loop: she prefers the kayak to the guitar, the bike to the kayak, and the guitar to the bike. The flea market has a stall that will swap any one of the three for any other for a dollar. Last month she walked in with the guitar, swapped it for the kayak, then the kayak for the bike, then the bike for the guitar, and walked out with a guitar and three dollars less than she came with. She could have kept the guitar for free, or gotten the bike for a single dollar. Suppose instead the three swaps had happened on three consecutive mornings, each one offered fresh.

Ravi and Lena have been going at each other about this for a week. Ravi thinks the loop shows her preferences themselves are incoherent, and he thinks the pump is the proof: whatever she's holding, you can offer her one more swap she'll take, and each time she takes it she's worse off than if she'd stopped. He adds that even a Mara who sees it coming and refuses the third swap isn't in the clear — she turns down the guitar for the bike while preferring the guitar to the bike — and that if she does any trading at all she pays two dollars for a bike the stall would have sold her for one.

Lena thinks thats too quick. She says the pump only runs on someone who keeps saying yes, and a foresighted Mara just declines; declining isn't irrational, since rationality doesn't require you to maximize at every single instant, and people bind themselves to plans all the time — gym contracts, being tied to a mast, deadlines. She also says that when the swaps are spread over three days, it's not obvious Thursday-Mara owes Tuesday-Mara anything; if each morning she's simply taking the option she then prefers, where exactly is the mistake? Ravi says that if no version of her on any given day is ever worse off, then the pump has nothing to complain about and neither does Lena — but the whole point was that Mara-as-one-person ends up behind. Lena says that's just smuggling in a whole-life chooser whose preferences might not loop at all; Ravi says nothing stops the loop from being rebuilt one level up, over whole histories.

Write the dialogue these two would actually have, a few rounds each, both of them at their sharpest — no strawmen, and they should be answering each other's actual points rather than talking past each other. At some stage have one of them bring up the option of Mara paying a friend five dollars to stand beside her in the stall and veto the swap she'd regret, and the other say what that does and dosen't show.
