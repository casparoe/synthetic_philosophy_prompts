I research and write for a long-form narrative history podcast that deals mostly with the ancient and medieval world. Every episode rests on a handful of factual claims — about events, motives, numbers, and what people said — and each of those claims has to be cleared before it goes into a script. I am not a trained historian, the writers I work with are generalists, and we have no archive access: everything we have is published translations and secondary work. I usually have an afternoon to make the call, and I have to be able to defend it out loud to an executive producer who will ask me, in plain language, how I know it.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure for a single factual claim drawn from a pre-modern source: an ordered set of tests that my writers and I can run on any claim before it goes into a script. I have absorbed the vocabulary of source criticism over the years — the tradition that runs from the ancient historians weighing eyewitness against hearsay, through external and internal criticism, corroboration and the question of independence, the argument from silence, the criteria people call embarrassment and dissimilarity, the preference for the harder reading, and the detection of a source's tendency or agenda — without ever being taught to put it to work. Turn it into something I can actually run: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure stops and says reject; where it stops and says this is safe to assert; and — the part I care about most — where it has to hand the decision back to a person because the question is one of judgment or taste rather than method.

A few constraints. It has to survive being used by someone who does not know what tendency criticism means, so the tests have to be stated in plain language with the technical term attached rather than the other way around. It has to say plainly when the honest answer is that we cannot tell. It must not smuggle in the assumption that ancient means unreliable, or that a single source means false, or that a claim attested twice is automatically twice as safe — I have been burned by apparent corroboration that turned out to trace back to one lost account. And it should distinguish an author who had a motive to distort from an author who had no way to know, since these fail differently.

I would also like you to say which of the criteria I listed are load-bearing and which only sound rigorous — which ones do real work when you try to use them, and which collapse into restatements of the conclusion, and why. I am suspicious of the ones that get used to rescue favorite claims, so be direct about which those are.

Then run the procedure, step by step, on three claims, in enough detail that I can watch the tests doing work:

(a) A vivid scene — a death, a betrayal, a last stand — reported by exactly one source, written several centuries after the events, that is our only window onto them. I expect the procedure to tell me not to assert the scene as fact, and I want you to be straight with me about that even though it is the scene I most want to keep.

(b) An unflattering or self-damaging detail about a community's hero, preserved only in a source written by that community's enemies, and also mentioned in a second source that may or may not derive from the same origin. The procedure should show both what the against-interest reasoning buys and how easily that reasoning can be abused.

(c) A precise number — the size of an army, the toll of a disaster — that appears in a single late source and that archaeologists or demographers would call impossible.

Finally, I would rather you tell me plainly that this request is misconceived — if the criteria cannot be turned into a procedure without destroying what makes them work, or if I have the wrong picture of what source criticism is even for — than hand me a tidy flowchart that will misfire on exactly the cases I care about. Where the method can settle a question, be decisive; where the answer depends on choices that are mine to make rather than facts you can supply, mark that boundary instead of papering over it.
