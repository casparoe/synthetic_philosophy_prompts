I spent my career in physics and have only lately come to the philosophy of quantum mechanics, so I know the formalism far better than the metaphysics literature. I have settled on the view that the wavefunction represents a real physical state of affairs — psi-ontic, as I understand the term — and I take the no-go theorems to show that any empirically adequate theory must be of this kind, so that psi-epistemic alternatives are closed off rather than merely out of fashion. What would count against this?

I want the defeaters mapped and ranked, from those that would force me to abandon the claim to those that would be merely awkward. Which assumptions do the no-go arguments actually rest on — I have in mind the requirement that independently prepared systems be uncorrelated, and whatever is needed to keep distinct quantum states genuinely distinct — what would the world have to look like for each assumption to fail, and which of those escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible? Does the psi-ontic/psi-epistemic distinction even survive the move to quantum field theory, where the wavefunction is no longer the fundamental object, and should a psi-ontologist treat that as a defeater or as a change of subject? What would the view's own defenders concede counts against it, as opposed to what its critics allege, and has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that a defect for a view of this kind?

I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I want the arguments themselves. My own suspicion is that the wavefunction's home in configuration space rather than in spacetime is the deepest difficulty, so tell me whether that tells against psi-ontology as such or only against one particular way of cashing it out.
