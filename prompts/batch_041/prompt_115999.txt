I am preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on causation and absence, and I want to work slowly through a passage my students invariably read past. It comes from Aristotle's Metaphysics, Book V (Delta), chapter 2, in Ross's translation: "Again, the same thing is the cause of contraries; for that which when present causes a particular thing, we sometimes charge, when absent, with the contrary, e.g. we impute the shipwreck to the absence of the steersman, whose presence was the cause of safety; and both — the presence and the privation — are causes as sources of movement."

Please help me understand it. I do not want a survey of the secondary literature; I want the moves in the argument made explicit. Three things in particular. First, what does Aristotle gain by admitting privation among causes, and how literally should we take the closing phrase — is an absence really a source of movement, or is that phrase doing something looser? Second, does the shipwreck example establish that absences cause, or only that we charge outcomes to them; and if only the latter, does the example still carry the general claim about contraries? Third, a student will certainly object that the storm caused the wreck and that the absent steersman merely failed to prevent it, so that calling his absence a cause is at best a manner of speaking. What is the strongest reply available within Aristotle's own framework, and what should that student nonetheless be granted? Keep the discussion anchored to the passage itself; a sentence or two on how it bears on the present-day dispute over omission is welcome, but no more than that.
