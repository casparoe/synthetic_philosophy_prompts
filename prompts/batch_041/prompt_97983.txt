I am a philosophy professor and I run a small departmental interview series, recorded for our graduate students, in which I interview visiting scholars about positions that are unfashionable but philosophically serious. Next term's guest is a philosopher of value who defends a broadly Moorean account of beauty and friendship: she holds that beauty is a simple, non-natural value property, that the value of a friendship is not merely the sum of the values of its constituent experiences, and that the highest human goods are certain forms of aesthetic enjoyment and personal affection. I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond inviting her to restate these claims. I want at least one question each on the following: (i) the method of absolute isolation and whether it can be applied to something as relationally embedded as a friendship; (ii) the doctrine of organic unities and whether the value of a friendship can be located in the whole rather than in its parts without making that value merely emergent or mysterious; (iii) the relation between Moore's ideal utilitarianism and his aesthetics, especially whether beauty is to be valued for its own sake or for the sake of the experience it occasions; and (iv) whether the Moorean account can accommodate the intuition that friendship has a distinctively moral dimension, for example that friends owe each other partiality and that betraying a friend is a wrong, not merely an aesthetic loss.

For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and where the literature standardly presses, make the follow-up press there too. I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations; I want the questions themselves to be philosophically pointed and to force her to make distinctions rather than to repeat slogans. If you can, include one question that turns on the difference between valuing a friendship as an organic whole and valuing it as a practice with constitutive norms, and one that asks whether Moore's account can explain the asymmetry between the value of a friendship and the disvalue of a betrayal.
