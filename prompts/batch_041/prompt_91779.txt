I am a retired experimental physicist and, since retiring, have been reading philosophy—decision theory in particular—with more enthusiasm than expertise. I sit on the board of trustees of a foundation that funds basic research. At our next meeting we must decide a matter that has divided the board, and I have been asked to prepare the written opinion that will frame the vote.

Last month the foundation received a letter from a person who claims to have built a prediction system—he calls it a simulator—that models the decision procedures of institutions like ours. He says that before sending the letter he ran the simulator on our board's likely deliberations and then flipped a fair coin. The letter explains the consequences:

- If the coin came up heads, he has already placed $2,000,000 in escrow, to be transferred to the foundation if and only if the simulator predicted that our board would authorize a payment of $50,000 to him. The prediction was made before the coin flip and before our deliberations; he says the simulator is accurate in the high ninety percent range, though not perfect.
- If the coin came up tails, he now demands $50,000. If we do not pay, he will publish a report documenting a series of past grant-audit irregularities at the foundation—irregularities that are real, that would embarrass us, and that would likely cost us several major donors.

He has provided what appears to be a video of the coin flip and a cryptographic commitment to the escrow arrangement, but we cannot verify that the coin flip occurred before our deliberations, nor that the simulator exists. We do not know whether the coin came up heads or tails.

The board is split. One faction says we should pay: if the coin came up heads, paying is what triggers the $2,000,000; if it came up tails, paying avoids the report. On their view, the expected value of paying is positive given the simulator's claimed accuracy, and refusing to pay is a kind of irrational taking-the-money-and-running that ignores the counterfactual branch in which our decision was already predicted. Another faction says we should refuse: the coin has already landed, and our payment cannot causally influence whether it landed heads or tails. If it landed tails, we are being extorted; if it landed heads, the escrow will be released based on a prediction that is already fixed, and our current decision is irrelevant to that fixed prediction. Paying, on this view, is paying for nothing—or worse, rewarding a threat.

I would like the memo to recommend a concrete course of action—pay, refuse, or some conditional policy—with a time frame for the decision. It should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side, and it should be written so that trustees who are not philosophers can follow the reasoning.

There is a further wrinkle. In a postscript, the sender offers what he calls an exclusive-or variant of the arrangement, which he says we may choose instead of the original. Under the variant, he has sent the same demand to a second foundation, and the report will be published if and only if exactly one of the two foundations pays: if both pay, or if neither pays, the report is not published; if exactly one pays, it is published. If both pay, he says he will return both payments; if neither pays, he keeps nothing. He presents this as a more fair arrangement. I am not sure whether this changes the correct decision, and I would like the memo to say whether it should, and why.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting.
