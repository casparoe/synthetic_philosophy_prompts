My old boss is retiring next month after thirty-odd years of doing strategy work, and I've been tapped to give the toast at the dinner. Here's my problem. Her whole reputation rests on telling rooms full of very confident people that the "lessons of history" are mostly a con — her line, the one everyone in the shop can recite, is that an analogy is just a memory that's been promoted to a law, and that the reason Munich and Vietnam and the Thucydides trap keep showing up in every briefing is that they're the analogies everyone has already paid for. And yet she used historical analogies constantly, every single day, right up to last week. So I can't just stand up and say analogies are bad, because that's a roast of the person we're supposed to be toasting.

The way she squared it, as far as I ever understood it, was that an analogy can do one of three jobs: prophecy (this will happen again), excuse (we've seen this before, so we can do X), or what she called an x-ray — you hold the old case up next to the new one and ask what the match lets you see that you'd otherwise miss, and then you throw the analogy away. She only ever trusted the third. I want the toast built so that it *does* the third thing instead of talking about it: the speech should walk the room through one small, specific resemblance and then take it apart in front of them, so they feel the move rather than hear it described.

The room is split. Maybe half are her colleagues — strategy and policy people who will clock every reference to Munich or Vietnam or the trap and will be quietly grading wether I've got the history right. The other half are her family, who don't work in this world at all and have heard her say the memory-promoted-to-a-law thing at the dinner table for years without ever really knowing what she meant. It has to land for both, without either half feeling talked down to. Six minutes, seven tops.

Then, separately from the speech, give me a short note: tell me where this leans on a reading of her method that a colleague might dispute, and what would change your mind about how to pitch the thing — like if you turn out to be wrong about which half of the room the real risk is with.
