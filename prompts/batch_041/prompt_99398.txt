I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I keep getting into dumb arguments at work about who's actually at fault when our ML stuff misbehaves. I'd like to see it as a short story so it stops being so abstract.

Write me a short story, 2000 words or so, built around this one idea: when an autonomous-ish system does something bad, you can walk the causal chain and hand every single person a legitimate excuse. The labeler was following the spec. The PM hit the metric she was told to hit. The model just did what the training run rewarded. The on-call engineer didn't have the context. The person who pushed the deploy was going through a pipeline that had been green for months. Add up all those excuses and you're left with nobody responsible, even though something clearly happened and someone (or something) should answer for it. I want the story to actually feel that pincer — every person individually looks reasonable, even conscientious, and the gap only shows up when you look at the whole thing at once. That's the part I can't get my head around and the part I want the story to make me feel.

A few things I'd like folded in, but naturally, not with characters giving little lectures:
- the system's behavior came out of a training/optimization process that nobody can fully reconstruct after the fact, so "what it was trying to do" isn't available to anyone, including its own authors;
- one character who's supposed to be the fall guy — the reviewer who clicked approve, or the operator on shift — and who slowly realizes he's being treated like the crumple zone of a car, the part designed to absorb the impact so the expensive part survives;
- a scene where someone proposes a fix, like a responsibility ledger where everyone has to log, at the time, the reason they believed their piece was safe, and someone else points out why that could just turn into paperwork that launders the excuse;
- no villain. If it's a story about a gap, the whole point is that nobody's the bad guy and thats the problem.

Don't use real companies, real incidents, or real philosophers. Keep it grounded in ordinary engineering life — standups, tickets, dashboards, a postmortem, a bad Tuesday — not sci-fi.

Then, seperate from the story, give me a short note on where it takes liberties with the actual philosophical issue or leans on a contested reading of it. In particular: is "nobody is responsible" a conclusion people actually dispute, and am I stacking the deck to get there? And tell me what would have to be different in the setup for the story to honestly earn that ending instead of just asserting it.
