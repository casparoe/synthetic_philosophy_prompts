I've been trying to write a short piece about the universal prior — not the usual 'isn't it cool that its incomputable' stuff, more about what it does to decision theory — and I've reached the point where I can't tell whether the argument is real or whether I've just talked myself into a mood. I'd like you to go at it hard. It's all below, in rough form.

**The setup.** Suppose I take seriously that I might be a computation, and that my uncertainty about which computation I am is modeled by something in the neighbourhood of a Solomonoff prior: programs, each weighted by roughly 2^(-length), where a program 'predicts' my observations by generating them.

**The familiar bit.** Conditional on my entire history, the high-weight programs are short. A short program that generates a long history tends to do it by a trick — a lookup table, a rule that happens to coincide with what I see, a few moving parts producing a lot of apparent detail. These programs mostly don't have rich continuations. The table runs out. The rule starts repeating. So the weight on hypotheses where I have a long, complicated future is tiny. That's the standard malignity claim and I think it's roughly right as far as it goes.

**Where I want to push it.** Prediction isn't the interesting case; agency is. Suppose I'm not just predicting but acting — a consequentialist trying to make some value function big. Notice that in the prior, my action is just another part of the program. And here's the asymmetry I care about: a program in which I deliberate, form a multi-step plan, execute it, and the world responds in a complicated way is *long*. A program in which I deliberate and then the world hands me the outcome is *short*. So the high-weight hypotheses are ones where the link between my deliberation and my outcome is simple. Either my outcome is fixed no matter what I do — a puppet world, a dream, a recording — or my outcome is exactly what I intend, with no friction at all.

**Why I think that's the real problem.** Both of those make effort look pointless, for different reasons. If the outcome is fixed, effort is irrelevant. If the outcome is frictionlessly whatever I intend, effort is redundant — the same outcome follows from a shorter intention. So the expected value of any particular plan is dominated by hypotheses on which the plan's details don't matter, and the expected value of just intending the good outcome is barely lower. The prior doesn't merely make me expect a boring future; it makes the derivative of expected value with respect to my effort come out at roughly zero. It isn't a trickster prior, it's an indulgent one, and I suspect an indulgent prior is worse for an agent than a hostile one.

**The escape hatch.** You can restrict the hypothesis space: only programs containing a persistent agent; only programs with a causal structure in which outcomes depend non-degenerately on the agent's actions; only programs that keep the agent around for many steps. But every restriction of that kind is either ad hoc, or so strong that it stops being a prior over computations and becomes a substantive theory of the world — which is fine, except that then it's doing all the work and the Solomonoff machinery is decoration.

**The worry I can't shake.** Maybe I'm conflating 'measure over programs that generate my observations' with 'measure over worlds I should care about'. Someone will say: you're not a program, you're a pattern, and pattern-measure doesn't privilege short futures the same way. But I don't know how to make pattern-measure precise enough to check that, and I suspect it just inherits the problem.

What I want from you, and please dont summarise it back to me:

- Is this just the simulation argument wearing a hat, or is there a real difference between 'I'm probably simulated' and 'the expected value of my effort is about zero'?
- If the conclusion generalises to any simplicity-favouring prior, say so — that would be interesting to me, because it would mean the problem was never about Solomonoff.
- Tell me which single sentence is doing the most illegitimate work. I'd rather cut one sentence than rewrite the piece.
