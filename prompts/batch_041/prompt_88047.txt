I am a homeschooling parent, and I have been writing my daughter's end-of-unit exams myself since she was about twelve. She is seventeen now. This term's unit is on the arguments people give for and against school choice, private schooling, and homeschooling, and she has read a good deal of advocacy from both directions — enough that she could produce the standard for-and-against lists in her sleep, which is exactly what I do not want to assess. I would like one essay question for her final, answerable in about sixty minutes, that she cannot get through by reciting those lists.

The crux I want the question to force on her is this. Arguments on every side of this debate quietly assume a picture of what kind of good education is, and they are rarely careful about which picture they are using. I want her to have to separate, in her own words: education as an ordinary private good that happens to produce benefits for people other than the family buying it; education as a good whose value is largely a matter of rank, so that one family's gain is substantially another family's loss; and education as something partly created by everyone participating in it together, so that it is weakened for those who remain when others leave. She should then say which of the standard arguments on each side actually depends on which of those pictures, and which arguments would survive even if the picture they lean on turned out to be wrong. I also want the question to make her commit to a view about the parent's authority over a child's education — whether it is more like a trustee's authority, held on the child's behalf, or an owner's — and to say what that view implies about whether homeschooling should be subject to outside requirements at all. She has not studied economics formally, so the question has to set these distinctions up in plain language rather than assuming she knows the textbook terms.

Please give me the question as it would be printed on the exam; a model answer of roughly 600 words; and then a marking scheme as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, saying for each what move the answer has to make to earn the marks and what the common way of missing it looks like. The question is worth 20 marks in total. No scholars' names and no citations anywhere — I want the marking scheme to be about the reasoning, not about who said what.
