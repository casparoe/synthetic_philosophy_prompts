I'm a computational cognitive scientist by training, and next month I'm giving a keynote at a type-design conference. The room will be almost entirely working type designers and type historians — people who have drawn hundreds of alphabets by hand and who think about letterforms the way a cabinetmaker thinks about wood — with a handful of cognitive psychologists who study reading and visual categorization sitting in the back. What I'll defend is a claim I've been developing for a few years and have come to call the letter's spirit: that for each letter of the alphabet there is a determinate set of constraints that any acceptable instance of that letter must satisfy, and that this spirit is a genuinely different thing from the letter's style, which is the set of parameters left free once the spirit is fixed. On my picture, every 'a' is an 'a' because it realizes the 'a'-spirit, and two 'a's can look wildly different while both remaining 'a's because the spirit constrains only the skeleton and leaves the flesh to style. I've built a small model that encodes a spirit as a graph of rigid and flexible relations among strokes and generates instances by sampling styles, and I'll show it for a few minutes. My rough argument runs: (i) designers converge on the same identity judgments across styles; (ii) that convergence can't be explained by raw visual similarity, since a two-storey and a single-storey 'a' can be less similar to each other than either is to some other letter; so (iii) some shared abstract structure must be doing the work, and style is what varies over it.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet for this particular room: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people — phrased the way a type designer or a type historian would put it, not the way a philosopher of language would — ordered by how much damage they'd do to the thesis, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. I expect the historians to go straight for the genealogy of the alphabet and for the two-storey-versus-single-storey problem with 'a' and 'g', and the psychologists to go for graded categorization and the empirical literature on letter perception, but I care most about the objections that come out of the craft, since those are the ones I haven't rehearsed; if you think I'm wrong about which camp will say what, say so. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Finally, be candid about the load-bearing point rather than reassuring me: tell me which single objection, if it turned out to be right, would force me to abandon the spirit/style distinction altogether rather than merely patch the model, and what argument or evidence would move you toward thinking the distinction tracks a real feature of how letterforms work rather than an artifact of my own formalism.
