Last week my friend Ines and I got into it about Rousseau's letter on whether Geneva should be allowed a theatre — the one he wrote at d'Alembert, not the Social Contract — and we ended up building a tidier version of his case than anything he actually says, and now I can't tell if it's a good argument or a machine for generating absurd conclusions. Here's what we wrote down. (1) A play moves us by getting us to feel what the characters feel; that's the pleasure of it. (2) Feeling something is a substitute for doing something about it — while I'm weeping over the heroine I'm not helping anybody, and the weeping is itself the payoff, so I walk out satisfied. (3) The better the play is at getting me inside a character, the more completely the feeling gets spent. (4) Sympathy is a limited stock: what's spent in the stalls isn't available on the street. (5) So a city with a great theatre has less sympathy left over for its actual poor, and the better its theatre, the worse off it is. (6) What the play is about makes no difference here — one that makes me adore virtue spends the same stock as one that flatters my worst instincts. (7) And in any case the stage has to give the audience what it already likes, so it can follow the general taste but never raise it. (8) Geneva is a small republic where every citizen's contribution counts, so it can least afford (5). (9) Therefore Geneva shouldn't build a theatre.

My problem is that (2) through (5) seem to prove way too much. If spending the feeling is what's wrong, the same goes for novels, films, documentaries about famine, and standing in a crowd singing — and Rousseau wrote a novel himself, and says he was basically formed by the books he read as a kid, which is just (1) plus a book. At the end of the same letter, unless I'm misremembering it, he proposes public festivals instead, where everyone is up and moving and nobody is just sitting watching; but I can't see how a festival isn't also a spectacle, and if the difference is that at the festival the spectators and the performers are the same people, then the real work is being done by something that never shows up in (1)-(6). Meanwhile (6) and (7) look like they contradict each other: (6) says the content is irrelevant, (7) says the content is precisely what does the damage, the play only mirrors the audience it's written for. And if (7) is the load-bearing step, then (1)-(5) are dead weight and the conclusion shrinks a lot — not 'the theatre is bad' but 'the theatre is bad when the audience is already rotten', which is much less exciting for Geneva.

So which step do I cut? Ines says (3) is where it breaks, I'd rather keep (2) if I possibly can because without it I don't have an argument at all, and I suspect (4) is the shakiest thing on the list but I can't say why without sounding like I'm just refusing to believe that feelings have costs. Tell me where it actually goes wrong, why that step looked so obvious when we were writing it, and what I have to give up along with it — and if the honest fix is that Rousseau has a different reason for disliking the theatre that we've left out entirely, tell me which one that is and what it costs to hold it. No reading list, I just want the diagnosis.
