So two people in my dept got into it after a seminar on mental causation and I've been chewing on it all week. I can't refere it and it's bugging me.

Setup as I understood it: the exclusion worry. When I decide to raise my arm, there's some neural state N that seems to be enough on its own to bring the arm up. If N is enough, the intention did no work — it's a dangler. Either the intention just is N, in which case there's no distinct mental causation to save, or it's something over and above N, in which case the arm going up is overdetermined and the mental cause is redundant. Either way mental causation as a distinctive thing is in trouble. That's roughly the Kim thing, right?

Person A (exclusionist) ran that. Person B said it's rigged. Sufficient is doing all the work, she said: N is sufficient only against a background, and the background includes whether I intend it or not — change the intention and N isn't even the same state anymore, so the counterfactual A needs (if the intention hadn't been there, N would still have produced the movement) never gets going. And anyway sufficiency isn't exclusion: one level of description being enough doesn't mean lower levels aren't also causes. She said we should seperate producing from making a difference — N produces the movement, the intention makes the difference between raising and not raising, and those aren't rivals.

A came back: fine, but making a difference has to bottom out somewhere physical. If the intention makes a difference, there's a physical difference it makes, and that physical difference is already in the N story. So the mental difference-maker is either identical to a physical one (then it's not distinct) or it's floating free of the physical (then it's not doing anything). He called this the understudy principle: a cause that only ever goes on stage when the lead is already on isn't a cause, it's a standby.

B: then your argument eats everything. If being already included at the physical level disqualifies a cause, N is disqualified too, because the microphysical state is already sufficient for N. Chemistry and biology and economics go out with the bathwater. And you've got a weird view where the only real causes are whatever's fundamental, which nobody believes and which makes every special science a fiction. Also you keep sliding between determined by and pre-empted — a determined cause isn't a pre-empted cause.

A: I'll take that bullet, sort of. Maybe the honest conclusion is that causation is a fundamental-level notion and the higher levels give explanation, not causation. And she can't have it both ways either: if the mental is distinct, she has to say how a distinct mental difference-maker gets to push anything around without there being an extra physical push — and if she says it doesn't push, just makes a difference, I want to know what a difference is if it isn't a physical difference.

B: and if you say the mental is identical to the physical, you've solved the letter of the problem and not the spirit — the whole worry was that supervenient properties get excluded, and identity just tells me the mental cause is the physical cause wearing a hat. Which is what I wanted anyway, so thanks I guess. The interesting question is whether you can get distinctness and efficacy without overdetermination, and I think you can, because overdetermination needs two independently sufficient causes, and the mental is never independently sufficient.

A: never independently sufficient is just another way of saying it's not a cause.

B: no, it's a way of saying causation is difference-making under intervention, and the mental passes that test while failing the sufficiency test, and those are two different tests.

A: but the intervention test would need to wiggle the intention while holding the brain fixed, which supervenience forbids. So the test either can't be run or it's really just wiggling the brain.
