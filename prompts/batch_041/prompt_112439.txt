I have been teaching a seminar this term on truth and the growth of knowledge, and last week's session stalled on a question I could not answer. A student asked what we could possibly mean by saying that a later theory is nearer the truth, when every theory we have held is, strictly speaking, false. I offered the standard reply — verisimilitude, roughly truth-content minus falsity-content — and then had to concede, under her questioning, that the notion collapses on Popper's own definition: the objection published in 1974 by Tichý, Harris and Miller shows that a false theory with more truth-content carries more falsity-content along with it, so that no false theory can come out closer to the truth than any other. The room could feel that we have got warmer; the formalism says there is no warmer. I want to open next week's session with a poem rather than a summary, and I would rather it came from you than from me.

Write sixteen lines in four quatrains in which almost nothing rhymes properly, and in which every end-word is an imperfect echo of at least two others, so that the poem can be heard as carrying two rival rhyme schemes and no reader can say which of them is the true one. Those two schemes should carry the two halves of what we could not settle: the felt sense of drawing nearer, and the demonstration that the rivals doing the drawing cannot be ranked. Let the last line either complete the rhyme it has been missing throughout or refuse to; choose whichever the poem needs, and let that choice do the arguing. Someone who knows the 1974 objection should recognise it without its being named, and someone who does not should still find a good poem about being almost right.
