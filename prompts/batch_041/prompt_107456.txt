I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about individual contributions to collective harms, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, fits the verdicts below, where that principle disagrees with me, and what it implies for a case I haven't listed. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my cases.

Some context on where I'm coming from: I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and my instincts are marginalist — I think in terms of expected value, deltas, and thresholds. That is probably why the cases below bother me, and I'd rather you not resolve them by appealing to an empirical fact about supply chains or voter turnout that I can't check without going online. If a verdict of mine turns out to depend on such a fact, say so and tell me what the principle says conditional on each possibility.

My verdicts:

1. Driving five miles for a pointless errand in a gas car: mildly wrong — a small mark against me, not a serious one.
2. Eating a beef burger at a restaurant: wrong, and I'd say more wrong than the drive in (1), even though I have no idea how the two compare in emissions or animal suffering.
3. Flying and then buying a carbon offset I'm fairly confident genuinely cancels the emissions: still somewhat wrong, though less wrong than flying without it. This annoys me, because if the offset really cancels it, the net harm looks like zero.
4. Eating a burger the restaurant has already cooked, is about to throw out, and will not replace: fine, or nearly fine. Nothing I do changes how many patties were made.
5. Voting in a national election: somewhat wrong to skip out of laziness, but not because my vote might decide it — I'm near certain it won't.
6. Voting in a local race that was decided by three votes last time: clearly wrong to stay home. Same expected-impact math as (5), different feeling.
7. A lake that absorbs 1,000 kg of a pollutant before it collapses, with 1,001 factories each emitting 1 kg. If I'm one of the first 1,000, my kilogram feels like a minor wrong. If I'm the 1,001st and mine is what tips the lake, I feel far more culpable — though the increment is the same 1 kg either way.
8. Signing a pledge where my signature is the one that pushes a supplier past a threshold and gets them to switch to a cleaner default: feels like a real contribution and clearly worth doing. Had I signed one person earlier, so the threshold was crossed without me, it would have felt like nothing.
9. The case I can't handle: the harm is already fully determined by others — everyone else has already driven, voted, and eaten, nothing I do changes the outcome, and I know all this. I still feel I shouldn't add my own contribution, and I can't say what that "shouldn't" is tracking.

A principle I've been toying with: an action is wrong if it would be wrong for everyone in my position to do it. But this seems to cheat on (4) — if everyone ate the throwaway burger, restaurants would stop having throwaways, so the universalized version isn't the same case — and it seems beside the point on (8).

What I want from you: a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause handles which case; the verdicts it forces me to give up, stated honestly; and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — namely, one where I both do the marginal thing and recruit two friends to do it too, so my example may multiply the harm even if my own increment doesn't.
