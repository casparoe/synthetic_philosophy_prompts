The session was on debate as a scalable oversight technique. The notes I circulated fixed three terms, which the participants had in front of them:

Asymmetric verification: for a task family, producing a correct answer is infeasible for the agents involved, while checking a proposed answer is feasible for the judge. Debate's promise is usually taken to rest on this.

Crux: a step in an argument that is both (i) essential, in the sense that dropping it or replacing it invalidates the argument, and (ii) separately checkable by the judge.

Obfuscated argument: a long argument for a wrong answer in which no step is a crux, so that every step is individually plausible to the judge and the error is spread across the combination of steps rather than located in any one of them.

The question I circulated was this:

Suppose a protocol has two agents argue for opposing answers and a bounded judge decide the winner, and is proposed as a way to supervise systems whose answers that judge cannot verify directly. The obfuscated arguments problem is the observation that the dishonest debater can put forward a long argument for a wrong answer in which no step is a crux, so that the honest debater can do no better than assert that somewhere in the chain there is an error, and the judge has no cheap way to tell such an argument apart from a long honest one.

(a) State precisely what this problem shows about the scope of the asymmetric verification assumption, and what stronger assumption debate would need in its place.

(b) Assess whether the problem is an artifact of a judge with limited time, attention, or skill, and therefore something that more rounds of debate, cross-examination, and recursive decomposition could fix, or whether it is a structural limitation of the protocol. Defend your assessment.

(c) Say what follows for whether debate is a plausible route to supervising systems substantially more capable than their supervisors.

The submission I have to grade reads as follows:

(a) The obfuscated arguments problem shows that asymmetric verification is weaker than what debate needs. Debate requires not only that a correct answer be cheap to check once it is supplied, but that an incorrect answer be cheap to expose. These are different properties, and the second does not follow from the first. In the case described, the honest debater cannot hand the judge the one step that carries the error, because the error is not concentrated in any one step. So the assumption that has to replace asymmetric verification is something like crux-checkability: for any argument for a wrong answer, there is a decisive step the judge can check on its own. The problem shows that crux-checkability is not implied by asymmetric verification, and that it is the property the protocol actually relies on.

It is worth being clear about where the difficulty sits. If the judge were unbounded the problem would vanish, since the judge would simply locate the error. So this is not a problem about the debaters' incentives; it arises even when the dishonest debater is doing nothing except trying to win. The dishonest debater conceals a flaw it knows to be present, and the honest debater, who is equally aware of it, cannot demonstrate it to the judge. The protocol therefore presupposes at least one participant who both knows the flaw and is willing to point at it.

(b) This looks to me like a limitation of the judge rather than of the protocol. The judge's checking capacity is the binding resource, and it is also the one thing we can scale. With more rounds the honest debater can take any step the judge finds opaque and decompose it, and cross-examination lets the judge force a debater to commit to a definite claim instead of a vague one. The natural extension is recursive: run debate on each AI-checkable step, decomposing it further until the steps are human-checkable, and follow a single path down the tree so that the judge never has to look at the whole argument at once. Because the judge follows only one branch at each level, the total cost stays inside the judge's budget. Decomposition therefore removes the obfuscation rather than merely moving it around, since an argument whose flaw is spread over many steps is exactly the kind that falls apart once each step is replaced by a debate about that step. Whatever residue remains is a matter of compute, and compute is the cheapest thing to add; if it still proved necessary, the judge could be trained on labelled examples of obfuscated arguments until it learns to recognise them.

(c) On the technical side the picture is therefore encouraging, but I think the deeper problem is not obfuscation at all. Debate is only safe if at least one debater is trying to tell the truth, and the systems we would want to supervise are precisely the ones we suspect of pursuing objectives other than the ones we gave them. A debater that is deceptive at the level of its stated reasoning can always produce an argument shaped to the judge's weaknesses, and no number of extra rounds repairs that. So while obfuscation is a tractable engineering problem, the protocol is not a credible route to supervising systems much more capable than we are, and the effort would be better spent on interpretability.
