I am building predictive models of artificial agents that must act under time pressure and cannot derive everything their information entails, and before I commit to any account of their belief states I want the standard of adequacy written out. Set out the desiderata for a theory of belief for such agents: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must remain consistent with, each with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference of one camp. I care especially about these cases: an agent that stores a universally quantified conditional together with its antecedent but has never performed the instantiation, so that it is silent about the consequent; an agent that did derive the consequent and then had the conclusion overwritten in working memory; an agent that believes the general claim that every tautology is true while failing to believe some particular monstrous tautology, so that nothing about it is closed under anything yet its contents do not contradict one another; an agent holding contradictory entries from two subsystems that never interact; and an agent whose degrees of belief must be defined for the purposes of choice even though it holds no attitude toward most of the consequences of what it believes. I expect some of these requirements cannot be had together, and that is the part I most want from you: say which pairs you judge jointly unsatisfiable and whether the conflict is a theorem or a matter of engineering, in particular whether closure under the agent's own inference rules can be required at all without collapsing back into the omniscience the theory was built to avoid, and whether any representation of belief rich enough to compose with probability, decision, and communication can escape licensing the substitution of logical equivalents. Then name the single requirement that does the most to exclude the existing families of accounts, and score those families briefly against the list.
