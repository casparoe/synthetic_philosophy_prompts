I run a small philosophy-of-mind reading group that also puts out a podcast, and I have booked a guest for next month who has spent several years defending a version of epiphenomenalism about phenomenal properties — the thesis that experiences are caused by physical events in the brain but are themselves causally inert, never figuring among the causes of anything, including our own reports of them. What makes him worth an episode is not the base view, which we have already covered, but his answer to the objection usually called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experiences never cause anything, then my judgment that I am now seeing red is caused entirely by whatever brain state produced the experience, and it begins to look like a coincidence — a small miracle — that the judgment should be an accurate judgment about the experience rather than a blind firing that merely runs alongside it. His reply has two parts. First, he denies that the experience and the judgment stand in a causal relation at all: they are joint effects of a common neural cause, so whatever reliability the judgment enjoys is underwritten by the shared physical base rather than by any causal link between the two. Second, he holds that in the first-person case the judgment is not a separate inner utterance that must somehow be hooked up to the experience; the experience partly constitutes the judgment, so there is no gap for a causal connection to bridge. He likes to add that his position leaves the epistemology of consciousness exactly where externalist theories of content leave the epistemology of ordinary perception, and that those who accept the latter while rejecting the former are applying a double standard. He is careful and slightly combative: he will readily concede that zombies are conceivable and that his view cannot explain why the correlation holds rather than not, and he will then observe that no one else can either.

Please draft questions for a forty-minute interview that lets him state the view once and then spends its time where the pressure actually is. Give me the material in this order. A "Framing" section of two or three sentences I can read aloud to set him up for the audience. A "The common cause reply" section of three questions that force him to say precisely what reliability is doing in the epistemology — including at least one that asks what becomes of the judgment if the neural common cause fires in the absence of any experience, whether the judgment is then unjustified, false, or merely ungrounded, and why that does not simply relocate the paradox. A "Constitution and the first person" section of three questions about the second move, one of which presses whether a judgment partly constituted by the experience can still be mistaken, and one of which asks whether the constitutive relation can do epistemic work without covertly being a causal relation after all. A "The cost" section of two questions: one on whether his view can distinguish the reports of experience we treat as authoritative from those we treat as confabulated, and one that asks him to name the single strongest consideration against him and to say what evidence would change his mind. And a closing "The last word" of a single question inviting him to say, in a paragraph, what the dispute over epiphenomenalism is really about once the standard objection is set aside. For every question after the framing, append a bracketed follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, so that I am never left without a next move if he takes the expected line. Keep everything concrete and answerable by someone reasoning from the position itself: nothing that requires him to cite the literature, and nothing that asks him to name names.
