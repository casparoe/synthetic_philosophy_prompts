I got into a stupidly long argument with my friend Nadia last night — she does social choice — about the will of the people, and the reason it went on for three hours is that I'm presenting at the political theory workshop in two weeks and I'm now not sure Im defending the right thing. My thesis is this: the will of the people is neither a pre-existing fact that good institutions are supposed to go and discover, nor a cynical fiction that populists just wave around to dress up their demands, but something institutions *enact* through their procedures — and, more contentiously, that this is what makes some appeals to the will of the people legitimate even in cases where there is obviously no single unified will sitting there to be appealed to. Nadia kept insisting the whole debate is just Arrow's theorem in a trench coat, that the moment you admit there's no unified preference ordering the phrase does zero normative work, and that my "enactment" move is really just a way of smuggling in whatever outcome I happen to like and then calling it the people's will. Her sharpest one was that the second you say the will is enacted and not found, you've handed the populists a weapon, because now any movement that grabs the machinery gets to declare itself the people. I half agree with her and thats the problem.

The audience is going to be rough in a very specific way, which is why I want to prep properly. The workshop is mostly empirical political scientists who study populist movements as a phenomenon — they'll treat the will of the people as a frame or a rhetorical resource and be faintly irritated at anyone doing normative theory at them — plus two deliberative democrats who think legitimacy comes out of deliberation and who will hear my enactment line as a rival story about the same thing, and one Kant scholar who wandered in from a different workshop and always asks about the status of the fiction. The empirical people will want to know what my claim predicts or explains; the deliberative people will want to know why their procedural account isn't just a cleaner version of mine; and the Kant scholar will ask whether the fiction is constitutive or merely regulative, which I have a sinking feeling is the question that actually kills me.

So please write me the Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by how much damage they'd really do — not by how famous the objection is — and phrase each one the way that particular person would actually put it, because I want to hear it in their vocabulary before I hear it in the room. For each one give me a reply I can get through in about two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is soft or question-begging. Tell me what I should just concede up front instead of dying on, and finish with the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
