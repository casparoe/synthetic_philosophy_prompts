Having spent a career in chemistry and only recently turned to philosophy of science, I have agreed to interview, for a small podcast on the philosophy of probability, a philosopher who defends a propensity interpretation of chance — the thesis that probabilities are real dispositional features of physical setups rather than summaries of frequencies or measures of credence. My audience already knows the textbook contrast with frequentism and subjectivism, so I want questions that press the position where it is genuinely vulnerable rather than elicit a restatement. Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give. I want questions that cover, at least: what kind of entity a propensity is meant to be — a disposition, a modal fact, a physical quantity — and whether it is observable in the demanding sense my scientific training imposes; whether the view can accommodate single-case chances that no repeatable setup underwrites, such as the decay of one particular radium atom; the objection that propensities stop behaving like probabilities once one conditionalizes, appearing not to satisfy the calculus under Bayes's rule; and whether propensities can exist in a deterministic world or whether determinism simply collapses the view into subjectivism. Include one question that forces the guest to say what evidence could count against the propensity interpretation, and, since the position is contested, attribute it to an unnamed defender rather than to any living person. End with a one-sentence statement of the interview's through-line, on a final line beginning "Through-line:".
