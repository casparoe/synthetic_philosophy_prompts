I spent 35 years as a chemist before I retired, and for the last couple of years I've been reading philosophy of mind. There's one analogy I keep running into and it bugs me, and I'd like you to take it apart.

The analogy goes like this: beliefs and desires are supposed to be to future neuroscience what phlogiston was to chemistry, or caloric to thermodynamics. Folk psychology — the everyday practice of explaining people by what they believe and want — is a theory. It posits inner states, a belief that it's raining, a desire for coffee, and uses them to predict behavior. And like phlogiston, it might turn out that nothing actually answers to those posits. So we shouldn't expect neuroscience to vindicate belief-talk by finding the brain state that is the belief; we should expect it to scrap the vocabulary the way we scrapped phlogiston and caloric. I've seen this used by a neuroscientist friend of mine to argue we should stop treating "belief" as a thing to be found in the brain at all.

I get that this is a real position and not just a put-down, so I want an honest audit, not a defense of common sense.

First, give me the mapping, a table is fine. What corresponds to phlogiston, what to combustion, what to oxygen or the kinetic theory of heat? Who's playing the role of the phlogiston theorists, and what's supposed to be the anomaly that kills the theory?

Second, where does the mapping break? Be specific, and tell me which breaks actually matter. A few I've already wondered about: as a chemist, phlogiston was a substance-posit inside a fairly mature quantitative theory, whereas a belief is a content-bearing state, and I don't see the analogy carrying any of that content over. Second, "heat" survived the death of caloric with a new referent, so is the fate of "belief" elimination or reinterpretation? Third, phlogiston theory made sharp predictions and had a rival that explained the same data better — does folk psychology have a rival doing that, or is the analogy running ahead of the evidence? And is there a difference between eliminating a hidden posit that only ever got inferred and eliminating states people report from the inside?

Third, which of those breaks are load-bearing for the conclusion and which are just curiosities.

Fourth, does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If the analogy only shows the theory is false, not that the phenomena are unreal, what's left? And is the analogy doing any work that the self-defeating worry doesn't already do — that the eliminativist seems to need beliefs about what scientists will beleive and do?

Also, the phlogiston story as usually told is alot cleaner than the actual history. In my own field, "elimination" was messy and gradual, and some of the discarded framework's mathematical content survived under a new reading. Does that help the analogy or hurt it?

If there's a better analogy for the same conclusion, or a better one for the opposite conclusion, say what it is and why it's better.
