my chevruta and i have been going back and forth on this for a week and we need an outside read. it started with the Guide, the chapter where Maimonides explains the sacrifices. my rough version: the nations around israel all worshipped by bringing offerings, israel came out of that world and couldn't have jumped straight to some pure unbloody worship (people don't move from one extreme to its opposite in one step), so God took the practice over instead of abolishing it — offerings, but to Me, and in one place, so the habit gets transferred and the idolatry gets worked out of it gradually. that reading is the one thing that finally made the whole sacrificial system make sense to me. so here's the arguement we built out of it, and neither of us can accept where it lands:

(1) The reason the sacrificial commandments were given is a fact about the audience: they were in the habit of sacrificing, like everyone around them, and the law had to work with that habit rather than against it.
(2) If the reason a commandment was given is a contingent fact about the audience's habits, then the commandment's authority is tied to that fact — it binds because, and for as long as, the circumstance it was meant to address is in place.
(3) That circumstance was a stage: just out of egypt, surrounded by cults, israel could not have gone straight to a worship of the heart.
(4) Stages end. A generation raised on the prophets' complaints about sacrifice, taught that God wants the heart and not the smoke, is no longer in that circumstance.
(5) So the sacrificial commandments could lapse, or be replaced by prayer and study, with no change in what God wants — and the tradition half admits this already when it says prayer stands in the place of the offerings.
(6) None of (1)-(5) is special to sacrifices. The same shape of explanation seems to fit the law of the king (the people demanded one and got one), the dietary laws and the ban on mixtures (fences against idolatrous practice), the festivals, prayer itself.
(7) So by (2) each of those binds only while its circumstance lasts, and the whole law is a temporary arrangement.
(8) But the Torah is eternal and no commandment lapses. Maimonides holds this himself — it's one of his principles — and it's why the offerings come back in the messianic age.
(9) Therefore (1) is false: the sacrifices must have an intrinsic reason of their own rather than a borrowed historical one, which i'm told is roughly the Ramban's line against him.

The two things i'd hate to give up are (1) (nothing else explains why the Torah builds its central rite out of the same material as every pagan cult) and (8) (without it i don't know what i'm doing). So: which step actually breaks, why did it look fine, and what does dropping it cost? If the honest answer is that (2) is doing something illicit, say exactly what, then tell me what a defensible replacement for (2) would look like and which commandments would still fall under it. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and which step you're least sure about.
