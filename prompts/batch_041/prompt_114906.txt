I'm a high school student, and I've been asked to contribute a piece to our school's philosophy magazine. I want it to be a dialogue, and I'd like you to write it for me.

Please take on the role of someone who has followed the anti-mechanist literature in the philosophy of mathematics closely — the family of arguments that the human mind cannot be a Turing machine, made famous by Lucas and by Penrose on the basis of Gödel's incompleteness theorems — and who also knows the strongest replies to those arguments. Before you write anything, brainstorm briefly: lay out the best version of each side and identify precisely where the two cases collide. Then write the dialogue itself, keeping it tight.

Both characters are professional philosophers and both are entirely competent. Neither is a strawman, and neither is allowed to talk past the other.

Character A is an anti-mechanist in the Lucas–Penrose tradition. Her claim: for any Turing machine you care to nominate as a model of a human mathematician, there is a sentence the machine cannot prove — its Gödel sentence — that a human mathematician can nonetheless see to be true; therefore the mind is not a machine. She is careful, and she does not think the mind needs anything mysterious or quantum-mechanical; she is happy to say the mind is a physical system, just not a computable one.

Character B is a mechanist who accepts Gödel's theorems in full and thinks A's argument fails. His central move is that A needs the human mathematician to know that the nominated machine is consistent, and a machine cannot prove its own consistency, so A cannot reach her conclusion without somehow stepping outside the machine she is. I don't want him to stop there, though. Please have him develop two further points at length, and have A respond to each of them rather than ignore them:

(i) the self-identification problem — even granting that a mathematician can see the truth of the Gödel sentence of any system whose rules she can survey, she can only do this for systems she can identify, and we have no way of identifying the program we ourselves are running, so the argument never actually gets applied to us;

(ii) the unsound-machine point — we might be a machine that is not consistent at all, and an inconsistent machine can prove anything, including its own consistency, so whatever confidence we have in our own consistency cannot rest on our having surveyed our own rules.

I'd also like A to press a suggestion of her own: that the argument really establishes a disjunction — either the human mind is not a machine, or there are mathematical questions that are absolutely undecidable, in the sense that no possible mind could ever settle them no matter how much mathematics it did — and that the second horn is far worse to accept than the first. Have B answer that too, and have the two of them argue about what "seeing" that a Gödel sentence is true actually amounts to: is it mathematical knowledge that genuinely outruns proof, or merely a conditional belief (if the system is consistent, then the sentence is true) that a machine can hold just as well?

Don't let them converge, and don't let one of them win. End with each character stating, in a sentence or two, what would have to be true for the other to be right, with the disagreement still open. No citations, please — I only want the arguments. Roughly 1200–1500 words.
