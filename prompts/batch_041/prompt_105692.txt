Here is my opening. I have been trying to make precise something I keep gesturing at, and I want to know whether it survives contact with someone who knows the formal literature. I want to distinguish two kinds of trap. In the first, the bad outcome is an equilibrium of a fixed game among a fixed set of players; escape means changing the payoffs — a contract, an enforcer, side payments, repetition, a change in what is common knowledge. Call these matrix traps. In the second, the set of players is endogenous: the bad outcome persists because the strategies that would escape are selected out of the population over time, not because any particular player finds escape unprofitable in the situation she actually faces. Cartel members who honor the agreement get undercut by entrants; the firm that refuses to advertise is driven out by the firms that do; the state that regulates loses out to the state that does not. Call these composition traps. My claim is that the second class is not a special case of the first, and that this is where the Moloch label earns its keep. The reason is not that repetition fails, but that the escape condition differs in kind. In a matrix trap the existing players can in principle bind themselves; in a composition trap any binding arrangement among the existing players is itself a strategy in the larger selection process, so it is stable only if it is stable against the entry and exit of precisely the types it was meant to exclude. That, I think, explains why so many proposed escapes look sound on paper and then quietly fail: they are solutions to matrix traps applied to composition traps. So where does this break? Give me the sharpest reason to think the distinction either collapses or is already fully captured by some standard piece of game theory I should have known about.
