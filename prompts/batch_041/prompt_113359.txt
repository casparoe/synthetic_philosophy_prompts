I am trying to work through the twenty-fifth chapter of Machiavelli's The Prince — the chapter on fortune — and I can follow the Italian well enough to know what happens in it, but I cannot make the argument hang together. What follows is the chapter's core as I read it, with the two phrases I keep stumbling over left in the original.

Machiavelli opens by granting that many people have held, and still hold, that the world's affairs are governed by fortune and by God in such a way that human prudence can neither correct nor remedy them, and that this opinion has gained credit in his own time because of the great upheavals everyone has witnessed. He then refuses to let this abolish free will: "io giudico potere esser vero, che la fortuna sia arbitra della metà delle azioni nostre, ma che ancora ella ne lasci governare l'altra metà, o poco meno, a noi." Fortune, he says, is like a raging river that floods the plains, uproots trees, and destroys buildings when it is angry; everyone flees before it and no one can resist. And yet in quiet times men can prepare defences — embankments and dykes — so that when the river rises again it is confined to a channel and its force is less licentious and less damaging. So too with fortune, which shows its power where there is no "ordinata virtù" to resist it, and directs its assaults where it knows no dykes or defences have been built. Italy, he says, is a country without dykes, which is why it has suffered the great upheavals; had it been defended by suitable virtù, as Germany, Spain, and France were, the flood would not have caused the changes it did.

He then narrows to particulars. We see one prince prosper today and be ruined tomorrow without any change in his nature or qualities. This happens because a prince who leans entirely on fortune falls as fortune varies, and because a man is happy when his mode of proceeding matches the quality of the times and unhappy when they disagree. Men pursue glory and riches in different ways — one with caution, another with impetuosity; one by violence, another by craft; one with patience, another with the opposite — and each of these ways can succeed. Two cautious men can fare differently; two men can succeed with opposite styles. The cause is nothing but the fit between the times and their proceedings. And since no man is prudent enough to adapt himself to this — partly because he cannot deviate from what his nature inclines him to, partly because a man who has always prospered on one path cannot be persuaded to leave it — the cautious man fails when the time comes for impetuosity. If he could change his nature with the times, fortune would not change for him. He gives Pope Julius II as an example of impetuosity succeeding because the times happened to conform to it. He concludes that, since fortune varies and men stay obstinately in their modes, men are happy while the two agree and unhappy when they disagree. Finally he judges that it is better to be impetuous than cautious, because fortune is a woman, and to keep her under one must beat and strike her; and one sees that she lets herself be won by the young, because they are less cautious and more ferocious and command her with more audacity.

What I cannot work out is how these pieces are supposed to fit.

First, the arithmetic. If fortune is the arbiter of half our actions and leaves us the other half, what exactly is the half left to us? The dyke image suggests that fortune's power is not a fixed quota at all but is conditional: it shows its force where no defences have been built and passes harmlessly where they have. But if the damage fortune does depends on whether virtù has already prepared, then either virtù's own preparation was among the actions fortune controls, or fortune is not really governing half our actions — it governs a proportion that virtù can shrink. Which reading does the chapter support, and is the half a piece of arithmetic or a rhetorical hedge?

Second, what "ordinata virtù" is supposed to be. The phrase suggests virtù that is ordered, or ordering. But the later part of the chapter describes men's modes of proceeding as fixed by nature, something a man cannot deviate from even when the times demand it. If virtù is a natural inclination you cannot change, how can it be the thing that builds dykes in quiet times? Building dykes is precisely foresight and deliberate preparation, and the cautious man, who is the one best suited to prepare, is the one who fails when the times turn. So is virtù the capacity to prepare, or is it the disposition that happens to fit the times? These seem to require different things.

Third, the two metaphors. In the river image the remedy for fortune is prudence: anticipate, build in quiet times, prepare. In the final image the remedy for fortune, a woman, is to beat and strike her, and the young succeed because they are less cautious and more ferocious. Prudence and impetuosity are being recommended in the same chapter, and the second seems to be recommended precisely at the expense of the first. Is the ending a genuine revision of the river image, an unstable metaphor he does not notice, or a rhetorical flourish the argument does not need? And does the claim that the impetuous man is happier follow from the earlier claim that success depends on the match between one's mode and the times — since by that earlier claim no mode is intrinsically better than another, and impetuosity would be better only if the times happen to be impetuous?

Fourth, the conclusion. The chapter ends by saying that, fortune varying and men obstinate, men are happy while their modes agree with the times and unhappy when they disagree, and then immediately says it is better to be impetuous. But if the fit with the times determines success, and the impetuous man is only lucky when the times suit him, why is it better to be impetuous? Is this an empirical claim about which times are more common, a normative claim about what a man should be, or a concession that virtù cannot do what the dyke image says it can?

I do not want a reading list or citations; I want you to work through the text's argument. Tell me how the pieces are supposed to hang together, what the chapter is committed to about the relation between virtù and fortune, and whether the ending is consistent with the river image or a break with it.
