Two of my friends got into a long argument at dinner, and I have not been able to settle it in my own mind since. I was only a bystander, and what I want from you is a judgement on the argument as it was actually made, not a general essay on the subject.

One of them comes from a country that spent much of the last century under foreign rule and has since rebuilt its sense of itself around a very old past. His claim was that his people have been a nation for more than a thousand years. They have had a name of their own, a language, a church, a line of kings, and a body of stories about where they came from, and he said you do not get a list like that out of nowhere. The other, who reads a great deal of history, said that all of this is raw material and not a nation. For most of those centuries, he said, the people living on that territory did not know or care which nation they belonged to; they knew their village, their landlord, their priest, and their local speech. Mass national feeling, on his account, is a product of the last two hundred years, and it needed cheap print, railways, conscription, and state schooling to spread. Nations are recent things that were built, often deliberately, and then dressed in a costume and a pedigree that make them look timeless. He pointed out that a great deal of what looks immemorial in Europe turns out to be the work of the last two or three hundred years, from the "ancient" clan dress and clan tartans to the bardic ceremonies and the royal pageantry.

His friend replied that this only pushes the question back. Whoever built those schools and states had to know whom to include and whom to leave out, and to know that they must already have been drawing on something: a shared name, a story of common descent, a memory of a kingdom that had once existed. Point to a name and a shared myth of origin, he said, and you have a nation in the making, whatever the peasants happened to think. He added that "invented" is not the same as "false," and that "recent" is not an insult, since almost everything he cares about — individual rights, democracy, the idea that the people should rule — is recent too.

The other said this was precisely the confusion he had been complaining about. "Nation" was being used in two senses: one is a community that exists in people's heads, which may well be old, and the other is the claim that this community ought to have a state of its own and that the state belongs to it, which is modern and is the one that has produced the wars. When his friend said "we have been a nation for a thousand years," he was smuggling the modern political claim into the old cultural one. He also mentioned that he had read of a long-ruling imperial house whose oldest chronicle was compiled at the court's own direction, centuries after the events it describes, in order to give the ruling family a divine pedigree; the machinery of manufacturing a past, he said, is very old, and what is modern is only the scale of it and its marriage to the state.

His friend answered that if the political claim is modern, then it cannot be the source of the feeling, and the feeling is what actually moves people to fight and die: the modernist story explains the flag but not the blood. The other replied that the traditional story explains the blood but not the flag, since it cannot say why people who had shared a language for centuries only began killing one another over it after 1800. The first then said that the belief that nations are modern inventions became popular at a particular moment, when old empires were being broken up and new international bodies were being built, and that it is a very convenient belief for anyone who would prefer people to think of themselves as something else. The other said that this was an argument about who finds a thesis agreeable, not about whether it is true — the very mistake he had been warned against a few minutes earlier — and the first said it was not about truth but about which questions get asked. At that point they were both repeating themselves, and the table moved on.

Please go through their exchange and tell me who is right at each step, not merely who has the better case overall. Where one of them makes a point that is true but does not support what he thinks it supports, say so. Where one of them is answering a position the other never held, say so. And tell me what both of them are leaving out, especially if the whole dispute turns on something neither has mentioned. Please do not settle the history for me; take their examples as given. What I want is an adjudication of the argument as it stands.
