I design programming languages for a living — mostly small domain-specific languages for data pipelines — and I am reasonably fluent in the theoretical vocabulary that comes with the job: Turing completeness and what it does and does not buy you, the gap between what a language can express and what it makes easy, type systems and evaluation order, implicit coercions, the distinction between accidental and essential complexity, and the old joke about writing Fortran in whatever language you happen to be handed. A colleague with a linguistics background keeps insisting that our design arguments are the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis in miniature — that a notation shapes what its users readily think, not merely what they can write — and I have begun to suspect he is right in a way that is worth pinning down. What made me take it seriously was watching a team migrate from a lazy, expression-oriented language to an eager imperative one: within a few months they had simply stopped reaching for certain recursive formulations, even though nothing had become impossible and nobody had told them to stop.

I would like you to take the linguistic relativity hypothesis in the two forms it is usually put in — the strong reading, on which the language you speak determines or bounds the thoughts available to you, and the weak reading, on which it shapes habits of attention and what is salient by default — and restate each as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use, without quietly importing my framework's assumptions and calling that a translation. Be concrete about which piece of the Whorfian picture maps onto which piece of language design: obligatory grammatical categories onto whatever a language forces on you whether you want it or not (evaluation order, overflow behaviour, implicit coercions); the translation argument — that anything sayable in one language is sayable in another — onto the observation that anything computable can be implemented in any complete language; and the expressibility/idiom distinction onto the difference between what a language makes possible and what it makes natural. Where a mapping is forced rather than clean, say so instead of smoothing it over. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what in the Whorfian position has no counterpart in how I think about languages, and conversely what my vocabulary has no counterpart for. I have a suspicion I want tested rather than confirmed — that my framework cannot state the strong thesis at all except as a claim about ease, and that this is either a real gap in language design (it has no room for the thinker) or evidence that the strong thesis was only ever a claim about ease in disguise. Finally, I want an honest accounting of the status of what you are telling me: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, do not present a contested finding or reading as the consensus, and mark where your own confidence is low.
