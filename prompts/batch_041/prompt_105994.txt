Right, I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years now — LessWrong, the old OB stuff, the whole extended network of people who argue about priors in comment sections — and I keep circling back to the uncertain-evidence thing. You know the move: you don't always learn something for certain, you learn it partially, and the standard fix is to reweight your credences across the cells of some partition instead of conditioning on a single cell, in proportion to how the evidence shifted your probabilities over those cells. I get the mechanics. What I can't get anyone to tell me straight is whether it's actually correct, or whether it's just a tidy bit of formalism for a situation where there's no fact of the matter about what you should do.

So here's what I want, and please write it in British English. Give me your actual credence that this reweighting rule — reweighting a partition and then conditioning within the cells, rather than conditioning on the evidence directly — gives the uniquely correct updated credences for an agent whose evidence is genuinely uncertain. A number, not a shrug about how it depends on your philosophy.

But the number's the easy bit; what I really want is the decomposition. Break it into the sub-questions the number hangs on and give me a rough probability for each, with a line of justification, then show me how they combine. The ones I can see are: how likely is it that all rational belief change is of this partial-reweighting form at all (versus there being some cases where it just doesn't apply); how likely is it that the constraint usually imposed on it — that your conditional credences within each cell stay put while the cell weights shift — is the right constraint rather than an arbitrary one; how likely is it that the fact that doing it twice in different orders can land you in different places is a real problem rather than a harmless artefact; and how likely is it that "the correct thing to do" is even well-defined for uncertain evidence, rather than there being a family of defensible responses with no unique member. Add any sub-question you think I've missed.

Then two things. First, tell me which single sub-question my final number is most sensitive to — where a modest change in that term moves the answer most. Second, tell me what would actually move that term for you: a proof, a counterexample, a Dutch-book style argument, or just a change of heart about what "correct" means here. And I want a concrete case to hang it on, so run the whole thing on this: I glance at a dim gauge and my credence splits 0.6/0.4 over two possible readings, and I want to know what I should believe afterwards and whether the rule earns its keep there or just launders my confusion. If the honest answer is that the rule only looks clean because the toy cases are clean, say so.
