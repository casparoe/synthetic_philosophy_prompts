I'm in a grad seminar on the Vienna Circle and politics right now (we spent two weeks on the 1930s, the Neurath stuff, the whole question of whether logical empiricism was ever as apolitical as it liked to pretend), and I have to write something for the seminar blog that's actually fun to read, so I want to do a parody. Here's the thing I keep circling. Carnap's signature move is the split between theoretical questions -- what is the case, which science answers -- and practical questions, which are matters of decision rather than discovery: you don't find out what ends to pursue, you choose them, and then the science can tell you about means. And Carnap himself was a socialist who thought a planned economy was the rational way to get where he'd decided he wanted to go. Individually those are fine, but combined, in the wrong hands, that split turns into a machine for making a political programme unanswerable: the ends can't be criticised because ends aren't the kind of thing that can be true or false, and the plan can't be said to have failed because failure is only failure relative to some description of success that we chose. I want to write the satire from inside that mindset, deadpan, no winking, no nudge-nudge "isn't this sinister" -- the horror should come out of the official's own reasonableness. Also I don't want it to be a cheap dunk on Carnap; the joke should be that he'd have a real point about means and ends and about explication, and the piece should show how a real feature of the view gets stretched into something awful.

So: write me an internal handbook / FAQ of about 650-700 words, issued by a fictional state planning commission (invent the country, don't use a real one), in numbered sections, written by an invented mid-level official in the commission's statistics division who is a sincere, cheerful logical empiricist. Don't name any real philosopher -- he can be obviously Carnapian without being Carnap, and nobody should ever say "as the positivists held". The document is his answer to a wave of complaints that last year's targets were missed, and he should be genuinely, uncomprehendingly puzzled that anyone thinks this is a problem. Every joke has to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from inventing absurdity, and I'd like you to work in at least three of the following, used as if they were just ordinary bureaucratic common sense: (a) the theoretical/practical distinction, deployed so that complaints about what the plan is *for* are dismissed as category mistakes; (b) explication -- the commission has redefined the measure ("steel delivered to collection points" rather than "steel produced", say) so that the target is now met by definition, and he presents this as a clarification of the concept rather than a fudge; (c) tolerance of frameworks -- the plan is a proposal, a choice of language, and a rival plan is simply a different framework, so nobody is wrong and there is nothing to argue about; (d) the point that a missed quota only ever falsifies a whole cluster of assumptions at once, so you can always save the plan by adjusting something auxiliary; (e) the statistics office's field reports functioning as the plan's protocol sentences, with disputes about them settled by decision rather than by further evidence; (f) a verificationist aside about long-run projections that can't be checked for decades being, strictly speaking, not even meaningful. Include one deadpan quoted complaint from an invented dissident inside the commission -- a factory manager or a sceptical economist, made up, that's fine -- and a closing paragraph that is warm, reasonable and reassuring in a way that should leave a reader slightly cold. Readable to someone who has never opened Carnap, funny to someone who has. No citations, no reading lists, no real names or titles of real works.
