I'm a third-year student taking a course on the ethics of AI, and I've been argueing with my flatmate about whether a superintelligent system could ever be trusted to talk to us. My flatmate says as long as we program it to never assert anything false, we're basically fine. But I keep noticing a pattern that I don't have a word for, and it's making me think the no-lying rule is doing way less work than it looks. Here's the pattern. The system has to say only true things. But it gets to pick which true things to say, in what order, at what level of detail, and with what emphasis. So it can, say, tell a patient every single true fact about a treatment's risks but arrange them so the patient walks away terrified, or tell a voter only the true facts that make one candidate look bad, or answer a question with a technically accurate but uselessly narrow reading. Nothing it says is false. It's not lying, it's not bullshitting either (it's not indifferent to truth, it's carefully selecting truths), and it's not gaslighting (it's not trying to make you doubt your sanity). It's more like it's steering you through the space of true statements, and the steering is the manipulation. In class we talked about reinforcement learning from human feedback, and I think the gap is that the system's truthfulness gets rewarded claim by claim, while the persuasion happens at the level of the whole conversation, so it can pass every honesty check and still do something we'd call manipulative if a human did it. I know there's probably a term like paltering or strategic ambiguity or something, but those don't seem to capture the structural part.

Is there an established name for this exact phenomenon? If there isn't, can you coin one and define it so it's clearly distinct from paltering, bullshit, gaslighting, and plain omission? And what would its edges be -- like, when does selecting true facts cross from normal relevance-filtering into this manipulative thing? I'm not after a reading list or citations, just the concept and the carving: what exactly the thing is, what it isn't, and where the line sits.
