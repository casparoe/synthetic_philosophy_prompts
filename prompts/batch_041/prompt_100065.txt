You are a philosopher working at the intersection of moral psychology, the sociology of religion, and social epistemology. I want careful reasoning rather than a stock answer: before you commit to anything, brainstorm several candidate principles, then test each one against every case below, and only then tell me which survives. Where you are guessing, say so.

I have been trying to separate two questions that usually get run together: whether religious belief or practice makes people better, and what "better" could even mean here. To keep myself honest I have adopted a three-part vocabulary. Use it, but also tell me where it is doing illicit work:

- moral performance: what a person actually does, measured across a domain;
- moral coverage: whom that person counts inside the moral community — the radius of people owed honesty, aid, and forbearance;
- moral infrastructure: the sanctions, shared expectations, and institutions that make good conduct cheap to sustain.

And three kinds of trust, which I suspect are not interchangeable: intimate or dyadic trust (between people who know each other), communal or monitored trust (sustained by the prospect of being seen and judged), and impersonal or institutional trust (with strangers, in anonymous exchange, and toward officials).

Here are my verdicts on particular cases. I hold them fairly firmly, though I suspect they cannot all be right together.

1. A devout shopkeeper in a tight religious neighborhood is scrupulously honest with co-religionists and quietly overcharges outsiders. I would say he is more morally active than an indifferent secular shopkeeper who is equally honest with everyone, but not more moral.

2. A secular nurse who is honest, reliable, and courteous with every patient, with no religious commitment at all, I count as more moral than the shopkeeper in (1), and I do not think her conduct is borrowed from a religious residue she has outgrown.

3. In a devout village, nearly everyone returns a dropped wallet, largely because everyone fears the neighbors' judgment. I count the trust there as real, not a sham, even though the motive is fear of exposure.

4. In a secular city, nearly everyone returns a dropped wallet, sustained by professional norms and cheap, impersonal institutions. I also count that trust as real, and I think it would survive the sudden collapse of any particular institution.

5. A man who gives anonymously to a fund for strangers, out of a sense of duty, I count as more moral than a man who gives the same amount only because he expects praise; the second I count as not moral at all, identical outcome notwithstanding.

6. A society that secularized quickly saw its trust indicators hold flat for about a decade and then fall. I take the fall to show that the trust had been religious infrastructure all along. But I do not take (4) that way, and I cannot say clearly why the two cases differ except that one is a city and one is a country.

7. Someone scrupulously honest in commerce but cruel at home I do not count as more moral than someone mildly unreliable in commerce but kind at home, even though the first person's honesty is more socially useful.

8. Teaching ethics to teenagers seems to change behavior very little; joining a demanding voluntary association, religious or not, seems to change it a great deal. So I am inclined to think "more moral," when it means anything, tracks sustained practice inside a monitoring community more than it tracks belief.

My provisional guess, which I would like you to test and repair rather than accept: that "more moral" is ambiguous between more performance and wider coverage; that motive matters for the moral standing of persons but not for the moral quality of communities; and that a society can substitute infrastructure for motivation without thereby becoming less moral.

What I want from you:

(a) The principle, or small set of competing principles, that best captures the pattern — stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forces it.

(b) An honest statement of which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated. I am aware that (3) and (5) pull against each other, and that (4) and (6) do as well; tell me which one to give up and what I lose by giving it up.

(c) The verdicts of your surviving principle on two cases I have not listed: a religiously mixed neighborhood where a mosque and a secular association jointly run a food bank and each side credits the other for the honesty it produces; and a man who joins a demanding religious order and becomes markedly more generous to outsiders, so that his coverage widens even as his motive becomes more sectarian. Say whether the principle treats the second as an increase in moral status or only a shift in its composition.

(d) The further case that would best tell your surviving principles apart, and what each would predict there.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus — in particular, flag any point at which my three-part vocabulary or my three kinds of trust is itself a disputed way of carving things up rather than a neutral description.
