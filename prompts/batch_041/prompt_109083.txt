I am giving the retirement toast for my former dissertation advisor, a philosopher who spent forty years defending the view that knowledge can be practically certain and that a knower may sometimes stop treating counterevidence as a standing demand. The room is split: half are epistemologists who will hear the dogmatism paradox in every sentence, and half are her family, who know her only as magnificently stubborn. Write a five-minute toast that uses the paradox to distinguish two kinds of dogmatism—the vice of refusing to hear an objection and the discipline of having settled a question well enough to act—without mentioning the paradox by name until the final line. The pivot should be the difference between dogmatism about a proposition and dogmatism about one's own fallibility; the epistemologists should hear a serious point about closure and misleading evidence, and the family should hear a portrait of her they recognize. No quotations, citations, or reading lists.
