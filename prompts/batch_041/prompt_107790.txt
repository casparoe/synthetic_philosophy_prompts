I chair the grant committee at a small environmental funder — we do about $3m a year, mostly wetlands and coastal stuff — and our next meeting is a week from tuesday, so I need my written opinion circulating before then. Here's the case. We're considering funding the re-flooding of 900 hectares of drained farmland in the delta, plus a ten-year monitoring program; the ecologists on the committee are thrilled, the bird counts go up, it's a textbook restoration. But one member, Priya, has thrown a wrenck in it. She reads a lot of animal-welfare philosophy and not much ecology, and her line is that re-flooding will bring an enormous number of new animals into existence — mostly r-strategists with near-total juvenile mortality, plus all the predation, parasitism, starvation and exposure that comes with a functioning wetland — and that from a welfare standpoint the drained farmland, with its very low animal density, may actually be the kinder world. She's not making the stupid version of the argument ("nature is cruel, so pave it"); she's saying the burden of proof runs the other way, that we'd be proposing to create a vast number of sentient-ish lives and should have to show their expected lives are net positive. Half the committee thinks this is a reductio and the other half thinks it's the most philosophically serious thing anyones said in a year, and I genuinely don't know which half I'm in.

Our charter has two clauses that are going to end up doing most of the work, and I want you to actually use them instead of gesturing vaguely at net welfare. The first is a creation clause: the funder is answerable for welfare effects on animals whose existence is contingent on our grant. The second is a baseline clause: we evaluate against the counterfactual state of the world, not against some ideal of untouched nature. Past committees have also gotten into fights about whether we're accounting in aggregate welfare or per-animal welfare, so please be explicit about which one you're using and whether your recommendation survives under the other.

Please draft the opinion itself. I want a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions (and if conditions, make them concrete, with numbers and a time frame we could actually put in a grant agreement) — with the reasoning laid out so a skeptic can follow it step by step. Include an honest statement of the strongest version of the case against my recommendation, not a strawman, and something Priya could genuinely respond to rather than a restatement of conservation orthodoxy. Finally, say plainly which parts of this are empirical questions we can't settle before the vote and which are the value judgments the committee is really making, because I'd rather we lose honestly than win by pretending the ecology decides it.
