I am giving a workshop talk next month defending a version of the performance-legitimacy thesis. My thesis, in brief: a regime that is not an electoral democracy can possess full legitimacy, not merely prudential acceptability, when it satisfies a three-part test. The attribution condition: the prosperity and order it delivers must be credibly attributable to the regime’s own actions, not merely to exogenous luck, resource rents, or preexisting social capital. The distribution condition: the benefits must be broadly shared and not merely aggregate, and the order must be the kind of order that protects ordinary people rather than merely protecting the regime. The supersession condition: the regime must maintain a credible, institutionalized path to its own supersession or to meaningful contestation, so that its performance claim is not a permanent license. I call this conditional performance legitimacy. I use China and Singapore as my two main cases, though I argue the test is general and that Singapore comes closer to passing it than China does, and that neither passes it fully. I also distinguish between performance as a ground of legitimacy and performance as a ground of obedience; I expect the audience to collapse them, and part of my task is to keep them apart. I expect the hardest line of attack to be what I call the horizon problem: the performance that legitimates is assessed over a long horizon, but the regime’s incentive to produce it is short-horizon, and the supersession condition is meant to bridge that gap.

The audience will include six liberal-democratic political theorists, two comparative authoritarianism specialists, one development economist, one legal philosopher who works on the rule of law, and one former Singaporean civil servant. The liberal theorists are mostly Rawlsians, deliberative democrats, and neo-republicans; they believe legitimacy requires consent, accountability, or non-domination. The comparative specialists are skeptical that performance legitimacy is anything more than a post-hoc rationalization for authoritarian rule. The economist will press on identification and counterfactuals. The legal philosopher will distinguish rule by law from rule of law. The former civil servant will know the Singapore case from the inside and will object to any framework that treats it as a mere case study.
