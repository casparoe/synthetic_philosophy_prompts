In discussions of frontier AI development, I keep running into the following argument, and I want to know whether it survives scrutiny. The argument runs roughly as follows. A lab that spends real resources on alignment — holding a model back until it can be evaluated, funding interpretability and red-teaming, accepting a slower capability trajectory — is like a manufacturer that installs emissions controls or pays a carbon price while its competitors do not. The responsible firm absorbs a cost its rivals avoid, customers cannot easily tell the safer product from the cheaper, dirtier one, and so, absent a rule binding everyone at once, the responsible firm cedes market share to the irresponsible ones and eventually either capitulates or exits. The conclusion drawn is that the alignment tax is not genuinely a choice any single actor can make, that exhortations to slow down voluntarily are naive, and that only a binding international regime changes anything — until such a regime exists, the competitiveness constraint simply dominates.

Please audit the analogy. Begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly, ideally as a table: what plays the role of the firm, the product, the pollutant, the abatement technology, the price signal, the regulator, and the treaty. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in three points. First, whether leakage works the same way when the good in question is a model rather than a physical product: can a frontier capability relocate to a permissive jurisdiction the way a factory can, does the release of open weights function like a rival's cheap imports, and is there any analogue to the fact that a factory's output is tied to a particular place? Second, whether the harm behaves like a stock pollutant that accumulates with output or like a low-probability, high-severity event that may never occur — and what that difference does to the strategic logic, given that the carbon analogy is built on marginal, continuous, roughly linear damage. Third, whether framing alignment work as a pure tax is even correct: if interpretability or evaluation sometimes unlocks capability, then the cost is not a clean tax and the incentive structure shifts accordingly.

Distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the conclusion — that unilateral restraint is futile and coordination is the only real lever — from those that are merely decorative. Then say whether the conclusion survives as stated, whether some weaker version of it survives, and, if a better analogy exists for the competitiveness constraint, what it is and why it carries the weight more honestly than the environmental-regulation picture does.
