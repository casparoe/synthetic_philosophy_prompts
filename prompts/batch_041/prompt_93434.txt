I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs, and I've fallen into a recurring argument with a close friend — a math graduate student I otherwise admire — that began at a reading group on whether anyone can be justified in trusting logic and has since grown into a general dispute about what it even means to accept an argument. Reconstructed as fairly as I can manage, his position is this: whenever I infer a conclusion from two premises — I affirm that if it's raining the street is wet, I affirm that it's raining, I conclude the street is wet — I am implicitly leaning on a further principle, that from a conditional and its antecedent one may infer the consequent. If that principle is doing any work, he says, it has to be something I accept, and accepting something is just having a belief; so the principle is really one more premise, and the argument now needs a further principle to carry me from the enlarged set of premises to the conclusion, and so on without end. Hence, on his view, no inference is ever justified, and my confidence in deduction is a leap of faith no better than his aunt's faith in her crystals. He adds, for good measure, that even if logic could be justified, the justification would have to use logic, so the whole enterprise is circular and therefore empty.

I've read enough to know that a famous nineteenth-century dialogue sits squarely on this spot, and I can see the shape of the regress he's describing, but I cannot get a grip on what the actual reply is supposed to be, or whether there is one. My half-formed instinct is that his mistake is treating my acceptance of a rule as a belief I could hold in the way I hold a belief about the weather — that there's a difference between believing a proposition and being disposed to reason in a certain way, and that the regress only gets started if you flatten that difference. But I can't make the distinction precise, and I suspect that the moment I try, I've either smuggled in a premise of my own or merely relabeled the problem. I've also absorbed the usual rationalist talking points about logical omniscience and about putting priors over logical facts, and neither of them seems to touch his argument at all.

What I want from you is a committed answer, not a tour of the literature. Tell me plainly whether his regress is fatal to the justification of deduction or whether it rests on a mistake, and if it rests on a mistake, name the mistake and defend the claim that it is one — don't just tell me that some philosophers think rules aren't premises. I want your actual verdict on whether my instinct about belief-versus-disposition is the right thread to pull or a dead end, on whether the circularity he raises at the end is vicious or benign, and on what I should say to him the next time he asks me to justify modus ponens. Where you think I've misdescribed the dispute, or where the answer turns on a distinction I haven't noticed, say so rather than letting it pass.
