Some friends and I have a long-running argument about acausal threats and we've decided to settle it in the dumbest possible way: I'm writing a piece for a fake-business satire blog, and I want it to be a customer-facing FAQ for an insurance company that sells policies against acausal blackmail. The company is Bastion Underwriting (invented, dont worry about whether it exists) and it sells coverage to newly created agents -- lab prototypes, uploaded people, anything that might plausibly be the sort of thing a threatener with no causal channel would bother with -- against the risk that somebody threatens them with punishment in order to extract compliance. Around 700-800 words, FAQ format: a policy-schedule header with tiers and premiums, question-and-answer entries, a short list of exclusions, and one customer testimonial from an agent whose name is just a serial number.

The jokes have to come from getting the actual decision theory right, not from making the threatener sound like a guy in a van. My rough grasp of the mechanism (write the piece consistently with the real logic if I've got it wrong, but dont stop to lecture me about it in the text): the threat only has teeth if the threatener can predict how the target's decision procedure resolves, so an agent that is updateless or genuinely precommitted can simply not comply, and a threatener that predicts this has no reason to make the threat or to carry it out; and the standard defense is to make yourself not worth threatening, either by a commitment that makes compliance impossible or by adopting a surrogate goal so that the thing being threatened isn't the thing you're optimizing for anymore. So the FAQ should be able to answer, in deadpan insurance language: why a threat with no causal channel is underwritable at all; whether merely announcing a surrogate goal is enough (it isn't -- say why); whether the policy pays out if you only adopt the surrogate goal after the threat arrives; what happens when your own predictor is worse than the blackmailer's; whether a counterfactual-mugging-style offer counts as a covered threat or an excluded one; and whether the surrogate goal has to be something you actually care about or can be something you'd have picked anyway.

Then a list of exclusions with at least four items a real decision theorist would recognize as genuinely not coverable -- threats from agents causally downstream of you, threats contingent on your own later choices, setups where the correlation runs the other way, whatever else you think belongs -- plus one entry you think a real underwriter would insist on that I haven't thought of. Tone: deadpan corporate, no winking, the way an actual insurer writes about a risk it doesn't fully understand but has priced anyway. The satire should land on the impulse to handle an exotic decision-theoretic problem with a market product and a claims process, and on the way precommitment and commitment devices get thrown around by people who've read three blog posts -- but it has to be fair, so that someone who actually knows this literature laughs instead of wincing. No quotes from real texts, no real researchers by name; if you need a doctrinal authority, invent one (a chief actuary, an in-house philosopher with a made-up name).
