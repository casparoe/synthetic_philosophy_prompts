Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about the norm of assertion, and I would rather have the question pressed properly than win the argument. I hold the knowledge account: the constitutive norm of assertion is knowledge — one may assert that p only if one knows that p, and the criticism "you don't know that" is the distinctively apt criticism of a defective assertion, in something like the way "but you promised" is the apt criticism of a broken promise. My friend holds that the operative norm is weaker — warranted reliance, or the epistemic standing the utterance itself signals — and that the knowledge account only looks right because we read our preferred theory of knowledge back into our verdicts about particular assertions.

I want a map of the knowledge account's exposure. What would count against it? I am after the defeater structure, not a literature survey: which cases, which patterns of intuition, which formal or theoretical results, and which developments in adjacent areas would tell against the view, ranked from what should genuinely shake it to what would be merely awkward.

Four things I would especially like worked through.

First, cases. Take a speaker who reliably arrives at true beliefs through a process she cannot articulate or check — a clinician whose diagnostic judgments outrun any justification she could give for them, or someone reading off the verdict of an opaque instrument. She asserts, she is right, she cannot defend herself. Does the knowledge account call her assertion improper, and does that verdict have any content independent of a controversial theory of knowledge? Then take a speaker who asserts on the strength of a division of cognitive labor — a researcher reporting a result she is entitled to rely on but could not herself verify. If the account must invoke group knowledge to license her assertion, does it quietly collapse into my friend's weaker norm?

Second, the gap between violating a norm and being blameworthy. If a speaker asserts something false through no fault of her own, is her assertion defective, or merely unlucky? The knowledge account appears committed to the former. Tell me whether that commitment is a cost or a virtue, and whether its defenders themselves treat it as a concession.

Third, the insulation problem. Every apparent counterexample can be met either by denying that the speaker knew or by denying that the utterance was a full-blooded assertion — it was a guess, a surmise, a hedge, a joke, a rehearsal. Say plainly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, whether that is a vice in a constitutive-norm claim or the ordinary fate of one, and what the account predicts that its rivals do not: about lottery assertions, about assertions grounded only in known statistics, and about whether assertability should inherit the context-sensitivity of knowledge.

Fourth, the social enforcement angle. The criticism "you don't know that" is not applied evenly; two speakers in the same epistemic position may draw it at very different rates because of how an audience reads them. If the norm is normative rather than descriptive, does that leave the knowledge account untouched, or does it show that what actually regulates the practice is something about standing and credibility rather than knowledge?

Finally, tell me what the defenders would concede — not what critics say about the view, but what its own proponents should admit counts against it: the cases they cannot explain away, the rivals they cannot beat on the phenomena, and the parts of the account doing no explanatory work at all. End with a verdict on whether the knowledge account is well insulated, and if it is, whether that is a problem for it. What would move me most is a case in which the account's verdict about a speaker is one I cannot bring myself to accept even after granting the friendliest possible theory of knowledge.
