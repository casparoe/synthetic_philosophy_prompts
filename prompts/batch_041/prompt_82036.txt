Ok so I work as a policy analyst at a state law reform commission -- we're the ones who get handed a reference like "should X be a crime" and have to hand back a recommendation to the Attorney-General, and I'm drowning because the references keep arriving with moral arguments stapled to them and nobody on the commission can reliably tell a good one from a bad one. We get maybe four or five references a year. The last one was adult incest, before that a private member's bill to recriminalise something to do with public nudity, and there's an assisted dying reference coming in the spring. Historically we've just kind of vibed it, cited Hart and Devlin in a footnote, and moved on.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run on a reference, in the spirit of the Hart-Devlin debate but usable by people who've read neither of them. You know the shape of it -- Devlin's line that a shared morality is what holds a society together and the majority is entitled to enforce it, Hart's reply that you can't criminalise conduct just because it disgusts the majority, that you need harm to others or at least something more than the fact that people dislike it. I don't want a summary of the debate. I want the operational version: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no", and where it hands the decision back to the commission because it's a values question and not a fact question. Assume the analyst running it is smart but not trained in philosophy, has about a week per reference, and has to defend the result in front of a committee containing both a religious conservative and a civil libertarian, so it has to be honest in both directions and not just smuggle in a preference.

Two things about our internal process you should build in. First, the chair has ruled that we can't recommend criminalisation on a pure "shared morality would disintegrate" argument unless the cohesion claim comes with evidence that tolerating the conduct would cause identifiable harm to third parties within a five-year horizon -- so your procedure needs a step that either meets that bar or fails it explicitly, not one that quietly ignores it. Second, whatever we recommend has to fit in a two-page "criminalisation justification" with a mandatory section on who would actually be prosecuted and whether the law would be enforced, plus a section where we state plainly what moral position we're taking, because the AG wants us to stop pretending the values are facts.

Then run the procedure on three references: adult incest between siblings, the public nudity bill, and assisted dying. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like -- I'd rather find out now than in committee. Tell me where the procedure misfires, and be upfront about which of its steps are really just judgment calls in a costume.

One more thing: tell me how confident you are in each step of the procedure, and single out the one step you're least sure survives contact with the actual cases.
