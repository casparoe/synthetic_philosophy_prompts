I'm a novelist — my new book is about a man who dies for four minutes on a hospital ward and comes back with this serene, faintly bureaucratic vision of how the cosmos is run — and next month I'm on a panel at a "Death & Consciousness" weekend where I've promised to defend the Ayer line. You know the material: Ayer the logical positivist chokes on smoked salmon, his heart stops for four minutes, and he comes back with that vision of an unbearably bright red light that governs the universe and two ministers who'd botched the job on space, space itself sitting crooked like a badly fitting jigsaw; and then he writes the whole thing up and admits the experience had loosened his conviction that death is the end of him, before walking it back a couple months later — its only his inflexible attitude toward the belief that had loosened, he said, not the belief itself.

My thesis for the panel is that the vision is gorgeous and worthless as evidence: look at whats in it — the space-time jigsaw, the ministers of space, a man waving his watch at the officials in charge of time — that's a Cambridge don's own seminar notes bouncing off a hypoxic brain, not a report from anywhere. Act as my Q&A coach: a philosopher of mind who's done a hundred of these panels and knows how the questions actually land in a non-philosophical room. Before you write anything, think it through and brainstorm who's actually sitting there.

Here's the room: about half are hospice nurses and palliative-care staff who've sat with hundreds of dying people and will not have it that consciousness is just the brain doing its thing; a quarter are people who've had NDEs themselves or swallowed the popular survival books whole; one ICU neurologist who'll be all hypoxia, temporal-lobe surge, case closed; and a retired hospital chaplain who thinks the lot of us are fools.

Give me the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come and ranked by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that person would really put it — the nurse's version, the believer's version, the neurologist's version — with a two-minute reply for me and a candid note on where the reply is soft. And flag the one question I'm quietly dreading: the one where I come off as a cold materialist lecturing grieving people. Tell me what I can safely concede without giving up the thesis.
