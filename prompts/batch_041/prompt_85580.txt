I am preparing a long-form interview for a philosophy-of-mind podcast, and my guest is a cognitive scientist who defends what she calls the loop thesis: a system is self-aware just when it builds an internal model of its own processing and that model is causally looped back into the processing it models. She treats the Gödelian construction at the centre of Gödel, Escher, Bach — a formal system that, through an arithmetization of its own syntax, yields a sentence that is about itself — as more than a suggestive metaphor: on her view, the shape of that construction is the shape of self-awareness. She is emphatic that she is not reviving the Lucas–Penrose argument; she is not claiming that minds escape machinery, only that self-reference inside a formal system displays the structure of self-knowledge.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions, ordered so that the pressure accumulates rather than clustering, and give each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: state in a clause what you take that answer to be, then write the follow-up that makes it costly. At least one question must press each of the following:

- The coding problem. Self-reference in the formal case is manufactured by a numbering scheme imposed from outside, and nothing in the system itself marks the diagonal sentence as being about itself. Ask what plays the role of the code in a mind, and whether the analogy survives if the code belongs to the theorist rather than to the system.
- The gap between self-reference and self-knowledge. The system does not see its own diagonal; only the outside observer does. Ask what turns the loop from a fact about a system into something the system has, and press on whether that requires representing one's own representations — and whether that invites a regress.
- Why incompleteness is needed at all. If the explanatory work is done by causal looping, ask what a thermostat or a governor lacks that the Gödelian construction supplies, and what her view would lose if the theorem were dropped from it.
- Triviality and levels. Any physical system can be described, at some level of abstraction, as containing a model of itself. Ask what constraint blocks that reading, and how the privileged level is to be identified without appealing to the description the theorist has already chosen.
- A case to test the view. A system whose self-model is entirely accurate but causally inert, and a system whose self-model is crude but wired into control. Ask which of the two is self-aware, and use her answer to probe whether the view is really about self-reference or about control.
- The blind spot. The Gödelian sentence is true only if the system is consistent, and the system cannot establish that of itself. Ask whether the loop thesis predicts that self-awareness is essentially accompanied by an unprovable assumption of one's own coherence, and whether that is a prediction or a pun on the word "incompleteness."
- Empirical content. Ask what would count against the thesis, and whether it is so undemanding that existing systems which generate descriptions of their own processing already qualify, or so demanding that nothing short of the Gödelian apparatus does.

Close with the question about disconfirming evidence. The questions should be answerable by reasoning about the position itself: do not ask her to rehearse the thesis, do not ask about her career or her influences, and do not ask her to recommend reading or to say who has argued what in print.
