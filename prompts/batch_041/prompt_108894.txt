I'm not a philosopher or anything, I just read a lot of pop science and I've gotten stuck on something. You hear it all the time: evolution "designed" the eye, a bee "knows" which flowers to visit, prices "tell" you what's scarce, a thermostat "senses" the cold. And then someone says no, none of that is real, it's just stuff happening, there's nobody home. But other people say you can have a reason without a reasoner — that the reason a spider builds its web that way exists whether or not the spider thinks about it, and same for a kidney, same for a market. I think I read this in some book about evolution but I can't remember who wrote it, so don't give me a reading list, I won't read the papers. I just want your judgement.

What I want to know is which cases actually pull their weight. Here's my list — some I made up, some I stole from documentaries:

1. A thermostat turning the heat on when it gets cold
2. A bacterium swimming toward sugar by tumbling more when things get worse
3. A spider's web being the shape it is
4. The immune system "recognising" a virus it has seen before
5. An orchid that looks and smells like a female bee so males try to mate with it
6. A cuckoo chick shoving its foster siblings out of the nest
7. A river finding the steepest way downhill
8. Market prices "signalling" that something is scarce
9. A slime mold finding the shortest route through a maze between two bits of food
10. A chess engine sacrificing its queen
11. A reinforcement-learning agent "exploring" a maze
12. A kidney "deciding" which molecules to keep
13. A shrimp jerking away from a hot surface
14. Natural selection "preferring" one trait over another
15. A plant turning toward the light

Sort these into tiers by how well each one supports the idea that there can be a reason — a purpose, a "for", a bit of meaning — without anybody doing the reasoning. Tier 1 means the case really does show it, tier 5 means it's pure metaphor and anyone who talks like that is confused. For each one I want to know whether the reason-talk is literally true, literally false but still a useful way of talking, or just a figure of speech, and what specifically about the case puts it in that tier — not just "it isn't conscious", that's the boring answer. If two of my cases turn out to be the same case, merge them and say which. I'm especially interested in the ones that sit right on a border.

Give it to me as JSON and nothing else, with these fields: tier_definitions (a short sentence for each of the five tiers in your own words); cases, an array where each entry has case (the name as I gave it), tier (1-5), status (one of "literally true", "literally false but useful", "just a figure of speech", "genuinely undecided"), why_this_tier (two or three sentences), what_would_move_it (what evidence or argument would push it up or down a tier), most_like (which other case on the list it clusters with and why, in a clause), and confidence (a number from 0 to 1); fuzzy_boundaries (where the tier lines are softest); hardest_case, an object with case and why; and one_sentence_takeaway. If you merged any cases, add a merges array saying which and why.
