I am writing a novel that follows one family through four generations in a country which, in the grandfather's lifetime, did something monstrous, and I have reached the point where the book turns on my characters holding genuinely different positions rather than four shades of unease. What I need from you is an instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree — a taxonomy of the positions one can take on whether a nation, or anything like it, can be guilty across generations — which I can use to keep my characters from collapsing into one another, and which I may later print as an appendix for readers who want to locate themselves.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names. Every internal node should be a question a person could answer without already knowing where the answer leads, and every branch should be labelled with the claim that divides it from its siblings. Leaves should carry the name of a position where a recognisable one exists and 'unnamed' where none does, together with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment — the thing its holder must swallow in order to keep it.

The distinctions I care about most are these: who or what bears the guilt (an individual, the state as a continuing agent, the nation as a plural subject, a lineage, a culture, a generation); what it is that descends, if anything — guilt proper, or something adjacent, such as liability, inherited debt, responsibility without guilt, shame, taint, or an obligation arising from unjust enrichment; what the descendant's connection to the deed must be for any of this to attach, whether her own acts, her identity, her benefits, her continuing institutions, or nothing at all; whether the live question is what the ancestors did or what the descendants are still doing; and whether the response is owed to the dead, to living victims, to no one in particular, or to the descendants themselves. I am aware of the fourfold taxonomy of guilt Jaspers sets out — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — and I do not want it as the spine of the tree. If it falls out of the tree somewhere, so be it, but the branching must be mine.

Please test the tree against these cases, placing each at the node where it does real work rather than illustrating a leaf after the fact: the farmer who still works land his grandfather acquired when its owners were deported; the woman who left the country in the fifties, whose own family lost nothing, and who keeps her grandfather's photograph in uniform in a drawer; the town that maintains a monument out of a municipal budget to which the descendants still contribute; the great-granddaughter who has taken up the victims' faith and claims a share of the guilt; the grandson who works for the ministry that once administered the wrong; the man who renounces an inheritance the moment he learns how it was made; and the ceremony abroad at which a woman who has never lived in the country is asked to apologise on her family's behalf. Where two positions cannot be separated by any question available to me, say so plainly instead of inventing a distinction to keep them apart; where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Aim for ten to fourteen leaves and no more than six levels of branching. Do not cite literature or propose reading — name positions rather than authors, and attach an author's name only where the association is beyond dispute. I should be able to hand a character's answers to a reader and have the tree return a position, not a mood.
