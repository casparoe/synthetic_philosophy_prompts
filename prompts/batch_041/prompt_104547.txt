I'm a TA for a big intro-philosophy lecture and I also run a smaller weekly discussion section on the side (about twelve people, mostly undergrads plus a couple of regulars from a local skeptics meetup). I've got one 75-minute slot with them and I want to use it on this. Game, not lecture.

Why it's suddenly personal: my younger brother has gotten involved with a group online that's talked him into handing over most of his savings, on the grounds that there's a tiny chance of some astronomically huge payoff somewhere down the line. I've been trying to argue him out of it and I keep hitting a wall, because the expected-value math actually favors the long shot, by a lot, and I don't have a good non-hand-wavy story about why he should refuse anyway. Someone in my department said the general shape of this is called Pascal's mugging, and I've read a bit since, but reading about it hasn't made me feel it and I doubt it'll make the group feel it. I want them to walk into the problem with their own two feet.

What I want them to come away seeing, without me saying it up front: that turning down the long shot isn't just innumeracy — the arithmetic genuinely does say take the bet — and that whatever makes refusal seem right has to come from somewhere other than the arithmetic (what a probability is doing here, whether the offer can be checked, whether accepting once commits you to a policy you can't afford). I'd rather they notice that themselves at the end than hear it at the start. Don't let the name "Pascal's mugging" into the room until the debrief.

Practical stuff: no real money. Snacks as the payout, or chips that buy snacks at the end. Dice for the odds, index cards, a whiteboard, and one printed sheet I can hand out. Keep the stakes small enough that nobody feels actually pressured.

One mechanic I want built in, because I think it's the thing that'll make it land: before anything starts, everyone writes on an index card (and seals it) the most they'd be willing to pay for some fixed tiny chance at the jackpot. We open the cards at the very end and compare them to what they actually did during the game. The jackpot should escalate over the rounds, in units people can picture, until the offer is so lopsided on paper that no one's sealed card can survive it.

And a second thing: somewhere in the middle, one player becomes the Mugger. The Mugger is only allowed to make offers that are positive expected value for the person being offered, but is otherwise free to scale the jackpot up as far as they want — and if someone accepts, the Mugger may keep charging them the same cost in later rounds without offering anything new. I want the group to discover for themselves what it means to accept once, in front of a mugger who's still standing there.

Give it to me in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- **The setup** — who's in the room, what they're told before anything starts, what's on the sealed cards. ~150 words.
- **The rules** — the bets, the dice, the token economy, the roles, and the exact numbers I need for the payoff table so the escalation actually works (tell me what to print). ~500 words.
- **Phase two: the Mugger** — the offer, the escalation, the repeat-charge rule, and what the Mugger is and isn't allowed to do. ~300 words.
- **The debrief** — map each moment of the game onto the part of the idea it's doing, including the sealed cards, and give me the questions to ask rather than the answers to deliver. ~400 words.
- **What will predictably go wrong the first time** — and how I should handle each thing. ~250 words.
- **What this game can't reproduce** — be honest about it and tell me what I'll have to supply myself. ~150 words.
