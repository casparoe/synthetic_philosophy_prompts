A colleague of mine is arguing against a proposal to add a foundations-of-physics component to our graduate curriculum, and his central move is an analogy I keep hearing from working scientists: philosophy of science is to science as ornithology is to birds. The ornithologists may study the birds as much as they please, but the birds fly perfectly well without them, and nothing an ornithologist discovers changes how a bird flies. On his account, philosophers can describe what scientists do from the outside, but they cannot contribute to the doing, so training in the subject is at best an ornament. He adds a historical claim in support: where conceptual questions have mattered in physics—the operational definition of simultaneity, the interpretation of probability, the notion of a natural kind—the work was done by physicists, and the philosophical vocabulary was applied retroactively.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict for one side or the other. First, set out the correspondence explicitly, in a table if that helps: what plays the role of the birds, of the ornithologists, of flying, of the bird's physiology, and of whatever the analogy takes a scientist's practice to be. Second, identify where the mapping breaks down—which features of the ornithology case are actually carrying the argument, and which of them fail to transfer. Third, sort the disanalogies into those that genuinely threaten his conclusion and those that are merely incidental, and say whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table. If it does not, say what would be needed to rescue it. If a different analogy would serve better—philosophy as mathematics to physics, or as grammar to speech, or something else—say what it is and why it does the job more honestly.

Two cautions. The analogy trades on a picture of scientific practice as a self-contained activity that needs no external clarification of its own concepts; press on whether that picture is a premise the argument relies on rather than a result it establishes. And be careful not to present as settled what is genuinely contested: whether philosophical work has ever fed back into scientific practice, and what should count as a contribution, are disputed among people who know the history. Distinguish the parts of your answer that are uncontroversial from the parts where informed people reasonably disagree, and mark the latter as contested rather than as consensus.
