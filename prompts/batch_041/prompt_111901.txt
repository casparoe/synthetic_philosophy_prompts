I run the interpretation side at a county museum service — I'm the person who signs off on gallery labels, on the "what we know about these people" panels, and increasingly on the archaeological sections of planning documents where a developer has to say what the site they're about to bulldoze means before they bulldoze it. My problem is that I've got four writers, none trained past a decent undergrad degree, and they keep making claims the evidence can't carry. Someone finds a pit of deliberately smashed pots and writes "a ritual of destruction expressing grief." Someone finds forty burials all oriented east and writes "a shared belief in rebirth." Someone finds the same style of brooch spread over two hundred miles and writes "a kingdom with a central administration." Sometimes those are fine. Usually they're guesses in confident prose, and my options are to wave them through or to argue with a colleague who just says "well, it's a reasonable interpretation" — and I never have a better answer ready on the spot. So what I want from you is a decision procedure my writers can actually run, half an hour per claim, in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point where it just stops and says the objects can't settle this, and the point where it hands the call to me because it's a judgment about values or about what we owe the descendant communities we consult, not a fact about the dirt. I'd much rather it return too little than too much — I can live with a boring label.

I know there's an old textbook picture where inference gets shakier as you climb from technology up through subsistence and economy to social organization and finally to religion and belief, and I know the general point that you need some bridging argument to get from a pattern in the ground to a meaning, and that the same pattern can be produced by very different beliefs. Don't lecture me on those — turn them into boxes to tick. In particular I want the procedure to force the writer to state the bridging premise out loud and say what would have to be true for it to hold; to distinguish a claim that people *did* something from a claim that they *believed* or *instituted* something, and to say what a belief leaves behind that a mere practice doesn't; to require more than one line of evidence and to check whether the lines are really independent or are all just the same assumption wearing different hats; and to say when an ethnographic analogy is allowed, which analogy, and what would break it. It should end in a fixed verdict — something like supported, provisional, or not supportable as written — with the conditions for upgrading later.

Then run it on three: the smashed pots, the east-facing burials, and the brooch distribution. I want to see where it bites, and I want at least one where it tells me the thing I like is not supportable, because if it never does that it's useless to me. And be honest about where the procedure misfires — I suspect it will be bad with anything that hinges on scale or on absence of evidence, and I'd rather know that going in than find out in a planning inquiry.
