I'm in a grad seminar on consciousness and a few of us ended up arguing after a movie night (we watched Blade Runner, quite late, so forgive the ramble). The fight was about epiphenomenalism — the view that phenomenal properties are perfectly real but do no causal work — and the thing people call the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experiences don't cause anything, then my belief that I'm having one can't be caused by the experience it's about; it has to be caused by whatever physical state happens to realise it. Half the room thought that's fatal, because then the belief isn't really about the experience at all and certainly isn't justified by it; the other half said it's fine so long as the physical state reliably co-occurs with the phenomenal one and the belief is caused by that, so the correlation is nomological rather than causal. Someone else kept insisting the view is self-undermining anyway, since the epiphenomenalist's own belief in it would be epiphenomenal too and so not held for reasons. We got nowhere, and then a guy said the film settles it, which annoyed me enough to want it written out properly.

So: read Blade Runner through that lens and tell me what it actually shows. The obvious thread is the Voight-Kampff test — a machine that watches for blush responses and pupil dilation, i.e. it assumes the difference between a person and a replicant has a measurable causal footprint. If epiphenomenalism is true there's nothing of the kind to measure: whatever the difference is, by hypothesis it can't show up in behaviour or physiology, so either the film's plot quietly requires epiphenomenalism to be false, or the test is just a crude physical proxy that would work either way. I'm more interested in Rachael, though, because her self-narrative is systematically disconnected from her actual history — she has sincere, load-bearing judgments about a past she never had. That strikes me as a much more literal picture of the paradox than the zombie case: a judgment that is about one thing and caused by another, and it still matters enormously to her, and to Deckard. And Deckard's own status (I know the different cuts push you in different directions, so treat it as an ambiguity rather than a puzzle to solve) makes the point reflexively: whatever he is, his judgments about what he is are caused by something, and it isn't obviously the thing they're about.

What I want out of this, beyond a reading: does the film dramatise something the philosophical discussion misses? My hunch is that it does — that even if experiences are causally idle, judgments about who has them are violently causally active, which turns the paradox from an epistemological curiosity into something with bodies attached, since people get retired on the strength of a test result. And then the honest part I actually need: where does the film resist the reading? The replicants aren't zombies in the philosopher's sense — they grieve, they're afraid, Roy's last speech is about experiences he's about to lose — and I suspect the film's images and score make inner life present to the audience in a way no argument could, which is either the strongest counterexample to epiphenomenalism going or just one more causal input into our judgments about minds. Tell me which, and don't be gentle.
