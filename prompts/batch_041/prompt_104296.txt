I'm a journalist who writes features on science and ideas, and for the better part of a year I've been reporting on what I keep calling, in my notes, the strange afterlife of willpower. The same scene replays in every round of interviews. I sit down with a behavioral economist, who explains that weakness of will is really just present bias: the discount function steepens for the near future, preferences reverse as the moment of choice approaches, and the whole thing falls out of the same hyperbolic model that predicts why someone takes the smaller reward now over the larger one later. A week later I sit down with a philosopher of action, who tells me that none of this is akrasia. Akrasia, on that account, is acting against one's own best judgment, and it is puzzling in a way that a mere preference reversal is not — it seems to require both that the agent judge some course of action best and that the agent not take it, and the live question is how that is even possible and what, if anything, is defective about it. The two interviews never touch. The economist treats the philosopher's puzzle as a verbal residue, a word game left over from an older vocabulary; the philosopher treats the model as a description of a different phenomenon that happens to share a name.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not another survey of the field. Is this a merely verbal disagreement, in which two groups attach the term weakness of will to different phenomena and could part as friends? Is it a disagreement about what a theory of action is even for — prediction and control on one side, the rationalizing explanation of what a person did and whether it makes sense on the other — in which case both projects might be legitimate while answering different questions under one heading? Or is there a genuinely substantive disagreement underneath: about whether there is any such thing as acting against one's considered judgment, about whether akrasia must be irrational, or about which cases count as the phenomenon in the first place? I have a working hypothesis I'd like you to test rather than assume — that the impasse comes from the two sides disagreeing about the unit of analysis, since the economist's preference reversal is diachronic and observable while the philosopher's akrasia is a relation between a judgment and an action inside a single agent at a single time, and that this mismatch is why their evidence never overlaps. Tell me whether that is right and where it is too neat. Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here: a distinction that dissolves the standoff, a piece of evidence that could settle part of it, or an argument that the two sides should stop pretending to be in the same conversation at all.
