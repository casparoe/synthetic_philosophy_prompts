For a few years I've been part of a large online community, a few thousand members, where any single member is free, on their own initiative, to send an alert to the entire group: a warning about a scam, a safety concern, a person to be avoided. No one's permission is needed and nothing has to be checked first. Individually, each alert is easy to justify; the person sending it is usually quite sure. But the volume is relentless, most alerts turn out to be mistaken or wildly exaggerated, and the group has slowly stopped believing any of them, which means the one accurate warning that eventually arrives will be ignored along with the rest. The people who bear the cost, the wrongly accused and the members who have learned to distrust the channel, are not the people who sent the alerts, and each sender, looking only at their own case, can say with a straight face that they did nothing wrong.

What bothers me is not simply that a large group will eventually contain someone who is wrong. It is that the option seems to select for the wrong people. The members who would weigh an alert carefully mostly hold back, partly because they sense how easily the channel gets flooded and partly because they would rather not add to it. The members who do not hold back are, as far as I can tell, disproportionately the ones with the least sense of proportion. So the freedom to act on one's own does not draw from the group at random; it draws from the confident end of it, and confidence here has little to do with being right. And because an alert cannot be unsent, the careful majority can only react after the damage is done.

A second, smaller case: in a neighborhood group, any one household can unilaterally post a notice about a suspected prowler. The same thing happens. The notices come from the households most given to alarm, and everyone else's quieter good judgment never reaches the board at all, because quiet judgment produces no notices.

I keep feeling that this is close to something that must already have a name. There is the arithmetic of many people each able to act alone, and there is the way the most optimistic party ends up determining an outcome when parties compete. But neither of those captures what I am after, because in those versions the actors are assumed to be roughly interchangeable in how well they judge, and the harm comes from there simply being many of them, or from their competing. Here the point is that the option itself sorts people, and it sorts them in the wrong direction.

Is there an established term for this exact configuration, or is it a gap? If there is, I want to know how it differs from its nearest neighbors and which of them fits best. If there is not, I would like a name proposed and defined, with the boundaries drawn: what counts as an instance and what does not, whether irreversibility is required, and whether the sorting I described is essential to the phenomenon or merely a feature it often happens to have.
