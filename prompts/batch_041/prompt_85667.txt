I have no background in academic philosophy, but I have spent years arguing with people about what is true — about history, medicine, politics — and I have come to suspect that the word "true" does almost no work of its own. When someone says "that's true," I want to know what they have added to the sentence they are agreeing with, and nobody has ever given me an answer I could check. Before I read any more about it, I want the specification: what any account of what it is for a claim to be true has to be able to do in order to count as adequate. Write the list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that puts it there.

I have my own candidates, and I would like you to sort them rather than simply adopt them:

- The cases that make the word necessary at all. My favorite is agreeing with a long, careful answer when I cannot remember it sentence by sentence: I say "what she said is true." Or a review that says "some of what the expert claims is true, but I could not tell you which parts." If an account cannot say what is happening in those two cases, it has not explained why we have the word.
- The same claim turning up twice: a sentence in one language and its translation in another, both of which we call true. Does the account have to say there is one thing that is true in both cases, and does that thing attach to the sentence, to the saying of it, or to what the sentence says?
- Made-up people: what is going on when we say something about a fictional detective is true, and true only inside the story.
- Claims with nothing there to be true or false — a statement about the husband of someone who has no husband — as against claims that are merely on a borderline, like how many hairs it takes to stop being bald.
- Statements that are accurate but misleading, where the fact reported is real and the impression given is false. Does the account of truth owe us anything about this, or is it a separate matter?
- The sentence that says of itself that it is not true.
- The demand that "true" not become a magic word: if it is supposed to be doing nothing beyond standing in for the claim itself, then an account of it should not let us prove anything about the world that we could not prove without it. I want this made precise, because I think this is where the accounts begin to pull against each other.
- The opposite pressure: the word shows up in places where it looks like real work — in saying what a sentence means, in saying what an investigation is aiming at, in saying what is wrong with a lie. If it is empty, why does it hold those jobs?
- What the account owes us about truth and knowledge: whether it must say anything about claims that are true but could never be found out.

Then tell me which items on the list are genuine requirements and which are just the habits of one group of people; flag any pair you think cannot be met at once, since I suspect at least two of these pull in opposite directions and I care about that more than about the rest; and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate accounts that people actually hold.

Two conditions on the answer. Do not give me names of books, articles, or authors, and do not cite anything: I cannot look anything up, and a guess at a title is worse than useless to me. If you need to refer to a named position, describe it in your own words. And finish with a one-sentence summary of the whole answer, followed by a final line beginning "Verdict:" that gives the requirement you say eliminates the most, in five words or fewer.
