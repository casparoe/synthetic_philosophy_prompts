My flatmate and I have a running argument about a game we play most Fridays, and its got to the point where I care more about the argument than the game. It's a simple two-player thing, zero-sum, so whatever I win she loses. I choose between three moves — call them A, B and C — and she chooses between two, left and right. Against left, A gets me 3 and B gets me 0; against right it's the other way round; C gets me a flat 1 no matter what she does. She says C is a move I should never make, and her reason isn't that A or B beats it — on its own, neither does. Her reason is that if I toss a coin and play A or B at random, I get 1.5 whatever she does, which beats the flat 1 in both cases. So C is dead, she says, and honestly I can't argue with that bit.

But then she goes further, and this is where I get off the bus. She says that with C gone the game is just a guessing game between A and B, so the answer is to randomise fifty-fifty and there's nothing more to say. And she reckons the same trick solves any game at all: you just keep crossing off moves that fail the coin-flip test, in whatever order you happen to fancy, until there's nothing left to cross off, and whatever survives is what sensible people play. My worries are these. She's killing C on the strength of a coin toss she never actually performs — neither of us ever sits there flipping anything. The second step only works because of the first, so I don't see why the order we cross things off in shouldn't change where we end up. And the whole thing seems to lean on the assumption that we're both perfectly sensible and each know it about the other, which, having lived with her, I have my doubts about. She's got alot of confidence for someone who lost four weeks running, I'll say that much.

Could you sort out who's right, and be straight with me about how much of this is actually settled? In particular: is crossing off a move that's beaten by a coin toss legitimate in the same way as crossing off one that's beaten by another single move; can the order of deletion change what survives; and what work is the 'we're both sensible and we know it' assumption really doing — is it doing anything, or is it just decoration? I did one term of economics years ago and I remember the word Nash floating about, but that's about my limit, so take me slowly. And please don't make a contested view sound like the consensus — I'd rather have the bits that everyone who knows the area agrees on clearly separated from the bits people still argue about, even if that means telling me the answer is mostly sand. British English, please.
