I recently moved into a forecasting role, and a large part of the job is putting numbers on events that have never happened before — a supplier defaulting, a regulation passing, a rival launching early. There is no frequency data to lean on, so I have been told more than once to fall back on the principle of indifference: if I have no reason to prefer one outcome to another, I should split my credence evenly. I was never confident about how far that rule can be pushed, and a dispute I overheard last week at a small seminar left me less sure rather than more, so I would like an adjudication.

The occasion was Bertrand's paradox. Two of the more senior people in the room, whom I will call Dana and Marcus, took opposite sides. Dana set it up this way. Pick a chord of a circle at random. What is the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? She said that if you choose the chord by picking two independent points on the circumference and connecting them, you get one third; if you pick a random radius and then a random point along it and take the chord perpendicular there, you get one half; if you pick a random point inside the disk and take it as the chord's midpoint, you get one quarter. Same problem, three answers, and each step used nothing but indifference about the choice. For her this is a reductio ad absurdum: the principle of indifference licenses contradictory credences from identical information, so it cannot be a requirement of rationality at all, and the forecasting advice I keep receiving is simply wrong.

Marcus did not accept that. He said the three answers do not come from indifference alone; each comes from indifference plus a hidden choice of how the chord is generated. A random chord is not a single thing until you say what physical process produces it, and different processes yield different distributions, so the honest conclusion is that the original question was under-specified, not that indifference is incoherent. Specify the experiment, he said, and the probability becomes perfectly well-defined, with indifference doing its usual work at each stage.

Dana came back that this concedes her point. The entire value of indifference was supposed to be that it gives you a probability when you have no procedure — when your information is nothing but symmetry. If you must first choose a procedure, you have imported a substantive assumption that indifference did not supply and cannot evaluate, so the rule is doing no real work. Choosing among procedures is itself a choice that indifference cannot guide.

Marcus replied that indifference should not be applied to just any way of describing the situation, but to the quantity that is invariant under the symmetries the problem actually has. In the chord case, the problem has rotational symmetry, so one can demand that the induced distribution be invariant under rotations and use that requirement to single out a principled method of generation. More generally, he said, when the unknown quantity is a scale, like a length or a rate, invariance under scaling forces one particular prior, and when it is a location, a different one, so the criterion does yield definite answers rather than none.

Dana said that just moves the bump in the rug. The chord problem is invariant under more than rotations — it is also invariant under rescaling and under re-describing the disk in other coordinates — and imposing different invariance requirements yields different priors, so invariance is not a unique criterion but one more place where an arbitrary choice gets hidden. Marcus answered that making the assumptions explicit and testable is exactly what one wants, and that in real cases there is usually a natural symmetry that settles the matter.

I would like a verdict on this, point by point, because I suspect both of them are right about different things and that the forecasting rule I was given sits somewhere in between. Please go through the exchange move by move and rate each move, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, saying briefly why. I would also like you to be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested, and not to present a contested position as if it were the consensus. And please write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; the way you would explain it to me in an email.
