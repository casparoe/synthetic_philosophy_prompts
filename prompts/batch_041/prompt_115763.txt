I run a monthly AI reading group at the software company where I work: about 14 engineers, 90 minutes, most with no philosophy or machine-learning-research background. I want to spend one session on iterated amplification and distillation -- whether breaking a hard task into smaller overseen pieces, then compressing the result back into a single policy, preserves both the original intent and the competence. I don't have a philosophy background, so I want them to build the pipeline and watch it drift rather than hear me summarize the literature.

Design one concrete session, fully specified: the task, the roles, the materials I need to prepare beforehand, the scoring, a timeline, and the debrief. Structure it in three stages -- a principal who briefs a small team on a task with a nuanced policy; a distiller who never speaks to the principal and must compress that team's behavior into a short written rule sheet; and a fresh team that works only from the sheet. Give me a task (triage or labeling with genuinely ambiguous cases seems promising) and a point scheme where the fresh team is rewarded for speed and for a visible test set I hand the distiller, while the principal privately knows which cases matter most -- so a good sheet can buy accuracy on the visible set and quietly lose exactly those cases. Add a third stage where the sheet gets re-distilled once more from the second team's behavior, so they can see whether drift compounds. Keep it runnable in 90 minutes with no computers.

The idea should land in the debrief, not in a lecture: map what happened onto amplification, distillation, and the alignment-versus-competence gap, and tell me how to stage the reveal so the second team feels how fast and confident they were while failing the cases that mattered. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- where participants will silently narrow the task, or where the principal will keep talking when they shouldn't -- and how to handle each. Be frank about what this format cannot reproduce: there is no reward model, no training, and no self-improvement loop, so the drift here comes from lossy human handoffs rather than optimization pressure, and the game cannot show a distilled model learning to fool its overseer.

Give me one design, not a menu, and make the judgment calls yourself; where you are guessing about my group, commit and say why, and flag the single design choice you are least confident in.
