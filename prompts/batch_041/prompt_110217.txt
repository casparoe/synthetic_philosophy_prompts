I have no philosophical training, but a question has been bothering me for a while, and I would like to think it through properly. It seems to me that a great many of the rules I follow from day to day are arbitrary at bottom. Whether one queues for a bus or crowds on, whether a tip should be fifteen percent or twenty, whether one stands on the right of an escalator or the left, whether one waits for the light on an empty street — none of these arrangements has any intrinsic justification. They are simply the customs that happen to have grown up where I live, and a different place might have settled on the opposite ones for no worse reason. Because the content of these customs is arbitrary, I have come to think they cannot be genuine moral obligations at all: the only reasons to observe them are the law and the wish not to appear rude or strange, and neither of those seems to me a matter of morality. So I would like to ask: when a visitor arrives somewhere whose customs differ from their own, are they under any real moral obligation to adopt the local ones, or is it merely a question of etiquette and personal taste? If some local customs bind a visitor and others do not, what distinguishes the ones that do?

I have a rough rule of my own and would like to know whether it is on the right track: follow the local custom whenever ignoring it would upset an expectation that someone else is reasonably relying on, and otherwise do as you please. By that rule, a queue and the side of the road we drive on seem to bind, while how much I tip seems not to — though I cannot say clearly why. And I am unsure what to make of customs that appear to work against somebody, such as a tipping custom that, as I understand it, shifts part of a restaurant's labour costs onto the diner and leaves the server dependent on the goodwill of strangers; would a visitor be entitled, or perhaps obliged, to refuse that one?
