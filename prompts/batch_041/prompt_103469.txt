For the past year a small reading group I run on decision theory has been going around and around Newcomb's problem, and I have become more interested in why we cannot converge than in who is right. We all accept the setup and we all know both standard lines: that taking both boxes dominates, because whatever is already in the boxes, taking the extra sum cannot change it; and that taking only the opaque box is what the evidence recommends, because those who take both tend to find it empty. What unsettles me is that I can predict which line each member will take from their antecedent sympathies before hearing their argument, that no one has shifted in a year, and that the two camps do not seem to make contact even when they agree on every number. So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about what rationality requires, a verbal one about the word rational or about what an ought ranges over, or two groups answering two different questions under one name — one about which act maximizes expected value given the evidence, the other about which act is licensed by the causal structure? Or is the real disagreement elsewhere: about whether such a predictor is possible at all, about the semantics of the subjunctive conditional describing what would happen were you to take both boxes, or about whether the object to be assessed is the single act or the whole policy and set of dispositions the agent brings to the table? One of us keeps insisting that a genuinely reliable predictor must be reading a cause of our choice rather than the choice itself, and that this dissolves the problem; I suspect that move is doing more work than it admits, but I cannot say exactly what.

My working hypothesis — which I would like tested rather than confirmed — is that the impasse is not really about decision theory but about what may count as evidence for a counterfactual, so that the two sides are running incompatible theories of subjunctive conditionals and then reporting the results as verdicts about rationality. To probe this we built a variant I have not seen discussed: the predictor is stipulated to be perfectly reliable and to have derived its prediction from the agent's decision procedure rather than from the agent's act; the agent is told all of this and knows the boxes are already filled. In one version the agent is an ordinary person; in another the agent is a program whose source it can read. Tell me what the split, if it survives in both versions, actually shows. If it shows that the disagreement is at bottom about the metaphysics of the predictor or about the semantics of the counterfactual, say which, and say what would count as progress from there — a distinction, a formal result fixing the right decision theory, a proof that the case is incoherent, or a candid admission that the question is under-specified. If instead it shows that we have misdescribed our own intuitions, say that too. I do not want a survey or a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement, argued from what each side actually says, together with a straight answer about whether anything could end it.
