I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades — the Sequences, the free-will megathreads, the recurring fights over whether "you could have done otherwise" is even the right question — and I've finally written up something of my own: a reconstruction of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility that I think survives the standard rebuttals. Next month I'm presenting it to a departmental reading group, and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a summary of the paper.

Take the role of a professional philosopher of action and moral responsibility who has spent years in the compatibilist trenches and who is also a careful, unsentimental reader of amateur work. Think step by step, and begin by brainstorming: before you draft anything, list for yourself the distinct ways this audience could come at the paper, then decide which of those are real threats and which are noise.

Here is the position I'll be defending. Strawson's argument, in my telling, is not really about determinism, and I've tried to strip out anything that depends on free will in the metaphysical sense. I put it as a thesis about the source of action: for an agent to be *deserving* of blame or credit in the backward-looking sense — to have it be a fact about them that they merit a response, independent of any forward-looking reasons we might have to sanction or praise — there must be no brute fact about the agent that fully accounts for why they acted as they did. By "brute fact" I mean a fact about their constitution, history, or circumstances that is not itself something they are responsible for. I then argue that every candidate source of an action bottoms out in such a brute fact, including the agent's own past acts of self-making, each of which traces back to an earlier constitution the agent did not author. The conclusion is that desert is impossible, but — and this is the move I care most about — that this leaves ordinary practices of holding one another accountable almost entirely intact, because those practices can be justified forward-looking. So I'm a skeptic about desert and a quietist about blame. I should say plainly that I expect to be read as having reinvented the wheel; my claim is only that the wheel as I've rebuilt it has fewer loose spokes than the ones on offer.

The audience is a mid-sized philosophy department's reading group. Most of them are compatibilists of the Frankfurt-school, reasons-responsive stripe; two are card-carrying libertarians who work on agent causation; one works on moral luck and the epistemology of blame; and there is usually a legal theorist or two from the law school who show up and ask about criminal responsibility. They are collegial but they are not going to be gentle, and several of them have heard Strawson's argument many times and think it is a trick.

What I want from you:

1. The eight or so objections I will actually get from *this* audience — phrased the way they would phrase them, in their vocabulary, not the way a Strawsonian would state them. Distinguish the ones the compatibilists will press from the ones the libertarians will press from the ones the legal theorists will press.

2. Order them by how much damage they'd do if I fumble them, and say for each whether I should concede, hold, or reframe.

3. For each objection, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, plus a candid note on where that reply is weakest — the follow-up I should expect and the place where I'm most likely to be bluffing.

4. The one objection I am hoping nobody raises, stated as sharply as it deserves, and what I should do if it comes.

5. A short list of the places where my own reconstruction is doing more work than I've admitted, so I'm not surprised by them in the room.

Write it as a working document I can rehearse from. Do not pad it with throat-clearing about how interesting the debate is; assume I know the literature and want the friction.
