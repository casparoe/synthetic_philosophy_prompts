ok so I'm putting together a thing and I need a short story, and I want it to be about the moral equality of combatants, but not the usual "every soldier thinks he's the good guy" war story, I've read that one a hundred times.

Here's the setup I want you to build on. Near future. Somebody — an NGO, a defence contractor, doesn't matter — has built a small wearable that runs a live estimate of whether the war your side is fighting satisfies the ad bellum conditions: just cause, last resort, proportionality, right intention, proper authority, that whole checklist. It's not oracle-magic, it's a forecast with error bars, but it's genuinely good, and both sides have it, because it leaked. It doesn't disable anything. It just shows you a number, like a smoke alarm for the justice of your cause. So when a war that started as self-defence slides into something else — reprisal, conquest, an occupation that nobody can justify anymore — the soldiers on that side can see it happen in real time. Some of them keep fighting anyway.

What I want the story to live inside is the gap between two positions. One says the guy in the other uniform is your moral equal: he's doing his job for his polity, you don't get to hate him, and anyway he can't know whether his cause is just, so it'd be unfair to hold him to it. The other says no, that's too easy — if his war is unjust he isn't permitted to kill, even if he's excused, even if he's a decent man with a mortgage; excused is not the same as justified, and what he's liable to isn't the same as what he's guilty of. The device is basically a machine for taking away the "he couldn't know" escape hatch, so I want the story to actually feel that removal.

And here's the wrinkle I care most about: the device only speaks to the cause, the ad bellum side of things. So a soldier whose war is just can watch the number stay green while he does something monstrous at the tactical level, and the machine will keep cheerfully telling him his side is in the right. The justice of the war and the justice of the act are seperate questions and the gadget collapses them. Let that do real work in the story. Give me two soldiers, one on each side, both decent, plus whoever else you need — maybe a legal adviser, maybe a chaplain, maybe the engineer who built the thing.

Five numbered scenes, about 150–250 words each, spare prose, no intro and no summing-up paragraph at the end. The one thing I'll allow outside the five scenes is a single short line at the very end, off to the side, where you tell me how confident you are in the picture of the debate you're working with and single out the bit you're least sure about — where you're leaning on a contested reading, or taking a liberty with the ideas. Don't let it resolve into "both sides are the same," that's the cop-out I'm trying to avoid.
