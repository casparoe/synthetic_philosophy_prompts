Last night I spent three hours arguing with a close friend — a former professional poker player who now trades for a living — about whether the Kelly criterion settles how anyone ought to bet on anything that compounds. His position, as best I can state it: maximizing expected wealth is simply an error, the error is non-ergodicity, the correct objective is expected log wealth, this is a theory of growth rather than of utility, and fractional Kelly is a concession to people who cannot stomach variance. I am a theoretical ecologist. I have spent my career on stochastic population growth, and throughout the argument I had the strong, possibly smug feeling that I was being told in an unfamiliar dialect something my own field has had for decades. I want you to test that feeling rather than flatter it.

Restate his position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in: per-capita growth rates in randomly varying environments, arithmetic versus geometric mean fitness, bet-hedging and variance-discounting, the almost-sure growth rate of a branching process as against the dominant eigenvalue of the mean matrix, selection as against optimization, extinction as an absorbing state, and evolutionarily stable strategies. Assume I am fluent; do not explain my own vocabulary back to me.

For each plank, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Cover at least these four: the claim that the ensemble average is a fantasy because he gets one life; the claim that log wealth is the uniquely correct objective; the claim that the real justification is the long-run argument — that any other strategy is eventually overtaken — rather than a utility calculation; and the practice of betting a fixed fraction of the growth-optimal stake. As a concrete case to run through both vocabularies, take a bet that multiplies wealth by 1.5 or 0.6 with equal probability: arithmetic mean above one, geometric mean below one, so that expected wealth rises while any actual sequence of bets decays.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Where his framework says should and mine can only say will, is the should doing work that selection cannot do, or is that the entire disagreement dressed up in moral vocabulary? Does my framework smuggle in a chooser when it speaks of what selection favors, and does his smuggle in a population when it speaks of a single life? And where does my vocabulary make his problem look easier than it is — for instance by treating ruin as a clean absorbing boundary, when losing your stake is plainly not the same thing as a lineage going extinct?

I would rather be told I was being condescending than that the translation is perfect. If it carries everything, say so and explain why the two literatures nonetheless developed separately; if it carries most of it, be specific about the remainder, and tell me whether the remainder is a defect of my framework or evidence that his claim was less clear than it sounded.
