A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a pattern I can describe but cannot name, and I would like your help either identifying it or, failing that, defining it properly. The pattern concerns how we sort harmful outcomes into bringing about and letting happen, and what struck me is that the sorting does not seem to track whether the agent acted at all, but rather whether the agent started a chain of events or merely kept one running. My friend offered a lighthouse keeper: one who lights a lamp for the first time is said to have caused the wreck, while one who has kept the lamp dark for years is said merely to have let it happen — yet keeping a lamp dark for years is plainly more work than lighting it once. The same shape appears in medicine: a surgeon who switches off a ventilator is treated as killing, while one who declines to start it is treated as allowing a death, even though both involve a deliberate policy sustained over time. Is there a name for this asymmetry — the habit of counting the initiation of a harmful causal chain as an act and its continued maintenance as a mere omission, even when the maintenance costs more effort than the initiation? If the established act-and-omission literature already covers it, tell me which strand fits and precisely where the fit breaks down; if it does not, coin a term and define it, marking its boundaries against the ordinary doing/allowing distinction and against omission bias. I am not after a bare label — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. Please answer in British English.
