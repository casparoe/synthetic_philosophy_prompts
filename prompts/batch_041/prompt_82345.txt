I belong to a fortnightly reading group on the philosophy of probability, and a recent session left me with an argument I can't settle on my own. Two of the other members — Ines, who was trained as a statistician, and Marcus, who works on the history of economic thought — fell into a dispute about whether Keynes's notion of the weight of an argument does any independent work, or whether it collapses into machinery we already have. Ines opened by saying that whenever someone insists two hypotheses have the same probability but different weight, what is really being described is a difference in how much evidence stands behind each probability, and that a subjectivist can capture this as a second-order distribution over the first-order probability; once you represent it that way, the two cases come apart in just the way Keynes wanted, so weight is a convenient label rather than a separate dimension of evidential support. Marcus replied that the reduction fails precisely where it matters: the second-order distribution is itself something one may have more or less evidence for, so the regress never terminates, and in any case Keynes's point was not merely that the quantity of evidence varies but that it need not be probabilistically measurable at all — an agent may hold a determinate degree of belief with no determinate second-order distribution behind it. He added that when weight is low the rational response is not to average over the second-order spread and act as though the resulting number were as trustworthy as one backed by a great deal of evidence, but to hedge, defer, or go and acquire more evidence.

Ines came back that this last point is a claim about how agents should respond to ambiguity, not a claim about evidence: model the decision-maker as ambiguity-averse — with a non-additive weighting, a maximin rule, or whatever else you prefer — and the phenomenon is captured inside ordinary decision theory, which shows that what Marcus is protecting is a preference rather than an epistemic primitive; she suggested that Keynes himself ran these two things together, wanting weight to be both an evidential quantity and something that rationally governs action. Marcus answered that this is redescription rather than reduction, and that the cases which interested Keynes — long-term investment, the formation of expectations about the distant economic future — are ones in which there is no well-defined probability to be second-guessed in the first place, so a second-order distribution presupposes exactly what is missing; he conceded that in simple urn-style cases the subjectivist move may be harmless, but insisted the interesting cases are the ones where it isn't. Ines closed by saying that if there really is no well-defined probability, then adding weight buys nothing, because one has simply traded one probability-free vocabulary for another; and that where a probability does exist, the second-order construction does the work; so weight is either idle or redundant.

The example they kept returning to was this: two people are deciding whether to act on a claim that a particular compound works, and in one version a large body of consistent evidence supports a posterior of about 0.8, while in the other a single flimsy study and a good deal of guesswork support the same 0.8; the second situation plainly calls for something different, and the question is what exactly. Could you work through their exchange move by move, telling me for each move whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and justifying each verdict briefly? I would especially like to know where the two of them are talking past one another, what each is missing, and whether there is a formulation of the issue on which both are substantially correct. Finish by saying who is more right overall; and if the answer really does turn on how the terms are defined, tell me which definition is doing the work and why we should prefer it, rather than leaving it there. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or a reading list — I want the arguments as made assessed on their merits.
