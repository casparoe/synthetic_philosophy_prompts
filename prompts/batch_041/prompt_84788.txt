I'm a retired plant pathologist — thirty-odd years on fungal diseases of cereals — and now that I've got the time I've been reading philosophy, which has been a strange experience, because half of what you people argue about are things I used to measure for a living. I want to use you as a tutor for one of them.

So set yourself up as someone who knows the famine literature and Amartya Sen's entitlement approach from the inside — not a summarizer, someone who has thought about where the thing creaks. And think carefully before you write; if it helps, start by brainstorming the candidate mechanisms on the table, then work out which of them the theory can actually accommodate, and only then write the analysis. I'd rather have the working than the verdict.

The episode: the Bengal famine of 1943. Comparators: Ireland in the 1840s, China in the late 1950s. The theory: the entitlement approach — famines as failures of people's command over food (what they grow, what their labour buys, what they can trade for, what they're given) rather than failures of total supply.

I know the headline version. Bengal's supply wasn't dramatically down; the war cut off the rice coming in from Burma; there was a cyclone; and the actual killer was that whole categories of people lost the ability to get at rice that existed — wages against prices, food pulled toward Calcutta, boats confiscated, and so on. I don't need that recited. What I want is the disciplined mapping: who the groups were, which entitlement each was leaning on, which ones failed and in what order, and then an honest account of what the theory can't reach.

Here's the part I actually care about, and it comes out of my old trade. In plant pathology we worked with a triangle — host, pathogen, environment — and you could never explain an epidemic by pointing at any one corner. The whole art was assigning weight, and it was routinely abused by whoever had a budget to protect. So I want to know whether the entitlement approach is best read as a claim about which corner famines live in. Is Sen in effect saying: famines are host-and-environment phenomena, and the pathogen is usually a red herring? If so, does that reading survive contact with the cases? Because I've come across arguments that the 1943 rice crop really was down — hurricane damage, plant disease, the lot — and that Sen's figures tilt the picture. And my biologist's instinct says "was the crop down" and "was the crop the cause of the famine" are two entirely different questions. So: is the entitlement approach equipped to keep those apart, or does it have to deny the first in order to make the second work? That's the thing I want most.

Then the comparisons. Ireland looks to me like a case where the host was absurdly narrow — one crop, one pathogen — and the environment was ideological, food walking out of the country while people starved in it. China looks like a case where the supply genuinely did fall and the system then actively blocked the information and the movement of people that would have capped the damage. What does the entitlement approach say about each, and where do the three cases pull it apart? Does Bengal end up confirming the theory, or does it start to look like a description of one particular kind of famine that got generalized too far?

And please flag where the theory simply has nothing to say — the moral vocabulary, the question of whether a famine is a natural event or a crime, whether "entitlement failure" is a description or an accusation. I suspect it's silent there and I'd rather hear that than watch it get stretched.

Don't bother with citations or reading lists, I'm not going to chase them down — just argue it out.
