My two roommates got into it for like an hour last night about wasps and free will and I genuinely couldn't tell who was making sense, so I'm outsourcing the verdict.

It started because Maya had been reading about this wasp -- a "sphex" wasp, I think -- that hunts a cricket, paralyzes it, drags it to its burrow, leaves the cricket on the doorstep, goes inside to check the burrow is empty, and then comes back out to pull the cricket in. The famous bit, she says, is that if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside checking, she comes back out, drags it to the doorstep again, and goes back inside to check again -- and if you move it again she does it again, and she'll apparently loop like that dozens of times without ever catching on. Maya's take: that's the whole story. Nobody's home. The wasp is running a subroutine, the subroutine has a bug, and it loops forever. And she says the scary part is we're the same -- find the right lever on a person and you can make them loop too, and the only difference is that the wasp's loop is short and ours is long and has more branches. She said "sphexish" is a real word for this that philosophers actually use.

Jonah said that proves nothing about free will. He said the wasp looping doesn't show there's no one home; it shows her behavior is being triggered by a cue she doesn't represent. But a person can be made to loop by a cue they don't represent too -- the guy who checks the lock again and again -- and we don't conclude he's a machine, we conclude he's anxious. Jonah said what matters isn't whether behavior loops, it's whether there's some process that can notice the loop and change it from the inside. Maya said fine, name one for the wasp. Jonah said name one for the anxiety guy that isn't itself just a longer loop.

Then Maya came back with a point where I couldn't tell if it was devastating or cheap. She said that even if Jonah's right that "flexibility" is what matters, "flexible" is doing the same job as "ruinous" does in arguments about insurance -- there's no line. Every system is flexible relative to some class of perturbations and rigid relative to another; a person happily reroutes around traffic but not around being asked to change their favorite band; a wasp might adapt to wind but not to a moved cricket. So saying humans are flexible and wasps aren't is just picking the perturbations that flatter us. Jonah said that's cheap -- that there's an obvious difference between adapting to a cue you can't even detect and actually deliberating, and that if Maya can't tell those apart she can't tell a thermostat from a person either.

Maya's last thing was that the wasp story is probably puffed up anyway. She said she'd heard the "dozens of times" bit is folk science and that real wasps do eventually give up or switch tactics when you mess with them, and that the whole "sphexishness" idea rests on a story that got passed around secondhand and may not even be true -- which she said is itself funny, since the go-to example of mindless repetition is being mindlessly repeated. Jonah said it doesn't matter whether the wasp story is true. It's a thought experiment, and if the wasp doesn't do it then some thermostat or some worm does, and the argument goes through anyway.

Can you go through this move by move -- the wasp claim, Jonah's "cue she doesn't represent" reply, Maya's "no line for flexibility" point, Jonah's thermostat comeback, and the last round about whether the story being shaky matters -- and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a short justification? Then say who's more right overall, and what both of them are missing.
