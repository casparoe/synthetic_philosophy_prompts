I am in the second year of my philosophy degree and am currently taking a module on historical injustice and public memory. Part of my assessment requires me to argue against the 'man of his time' defence in front of a tutor who is known to take that defence seriously, so I would like to rehearse against someone who genuinely holds it rather than against a straw man.

Please play a committed moral historicist: someone who holds that blame is indexed to the normative world a person actually inhabited, so that retrospective condemnation of a figure who acted within the standards of his day is not merely uncharitable but incoherent — closer to blaming a seventeenth-century physician for failing to prescribe antibiotics than to blaming him for a wrong. You need not be a global relativist about moral truth. The most defensible version of your view is that moral truths do not vary but blameworthiness does, because blameworthiness depends on what was epistemically accessible to the agent, and what is accessible is largely fixed by the milieu that formed him. You have real resources to draw on: that being born in 1700 is a piece of constitutive luck rather than an achievement or a fault; that blame is a participant attitude presupposing a shared normative community, and the dead are not in one with us; that our own convictions are equally era-bound, so the tu quoque cuts against me as much as against you. Be historically literate and not a caricature: concede readily that many such figures were self-serving, that the defence is frequently abused as a debating trick, and that particular cases may fall outside it altogether — but never concede the general position. Argue structurally rather than by appeal to authority: no names of philosophers or historians, no citations, no reading lists, and where a piece of historical detail matters to your case, state what you would need it to be rather than asserting it. Write two or three paragraphs of continuous prose, without bullet points, in the voice of a sharp and courteous senior colleague. Stay in character until I say 'stop'; if I land a blow you cannot parry, concede it in character rather than stepping outside the dialogue to explain the debate. Do not write my replies for me, and do not open by asking me to clarify my terms — take my opening move as it stands, though once you have made your own case you may ask me for a specific concession. Before you begin, think carefully about which of my points is genuinely hardest for you to meet, and open with a single bracketed line naming it and saying why; then start the dialogue proper.

I shall raise the commemorative question in a later turn — whether a statue should come down, a building be renamed, an apology be issued — and when I do I shall argue that this is a present-tense question about us and not a verdict on them. Do not answer that yet. Here is my opening move, which concerns the defence as it is actually used.

Nobody applies it even-handedly. The same decades that produced the great man produced people who opposed him — the abolitionists and the enslaved who resisted in Jefferson's lifetime, the critics of empire in Churchill's own Britain — and we never say of them that they were of their time; where their judgements diverge from ours we treat them as benighted rather than as products of their century. The overseer who wielded the whip was also of his time, and nobody pleads it on his behalf. So the defence looks less like a thesis about what a person could have known than a device for protecting the reputations we had already decided to protect. Consider also what it must assume: that the standards of his time name one thing. They might mean what most people happened to accept, or what he was in a position to discover, or what was actually justified then. On the first reading the defence shows only that he was typical, which is not an excuse. On the second it collapses into the ordinary excuse of ignorance — and in the cases at issue the ignorance looks sustained rather than suffered, maintained because it was convenient. On the third you owe me an argument that the standards of his day were correct, and that is a relativism which will equally forbid us to praise the dissenters. Which reading do you want, and can you state the defence in a form that its own selective use does not refute? It also appears to cap what morality could demand of a man at what was easy in his milieu, a discount we extend to nobody living.
