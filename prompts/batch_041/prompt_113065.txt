I've been running a reading group with a German colleague for about two years now — half the group is German-trained, half American-trained — and we keep having the exact same fight about whether a decent person is someone who keeps the rules of a decent order or someone who answers to their own conscience, and which of those two moralities actually has the better claim on us. It's not a fight about any particular issue: we agree about the facts of jaywalking, draft resistance, whistleblowing, tax forms, all of it. Nobody in the room is confused about what the other side is saying. We just never move an inch.

The German side says an ethic that makes individual conscience the final court of appeal is a recipe for a society in which everyone is a special case, and that the real moral achievement is an order people can rely on and hold each other to; the American side says an order that can't be questioned by the person who has to live under it is just coercion with good manners, and that a rule nobody's conscience actually endorses isn't a moral rule at all, it's a fence. Both sides can produce cases where the other looks monstrous, and both sides shrug at the other's cases.

My working hypothesis — and I want you to treat it as just a hypothesis — is that this isn't one disagreement but two wearing the same coat: the Germans are answering "what makes a society morally decent?", the Americans are answering "what makes a person morally decent?", and neither question can adjudicate the other. But it might just as well be that we mean different things by a moral reason (does a rule give you a reason just because it's a rule, or only if it's backed by something you can endorse?), or that we're each doing induction from a different stock of cases and calling our own sample the data. One of my grad students thinks it's really the agent-relative versus agent-neutral thing in a costume, and that the old conviction-versus-responsibility distinction is doing all the work without anyone naming it. Or maybe it's a straightforwardly substantive disagreement about the grounds of obligation, in which case the impasse is just the question being hard and we should all go home.

Diagnose it for me. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is, argued from what each side says rather than from a survey of who said it, and then, given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a changed question, a case neither side has thought about, a piece of empirical work, or an honest admission that nothing will move it and here's why. If my hypothesis is right, say so and say what follows from it; if it's a dodge that lets both sides off the hook, say that instead. Don't give me a reading list, I've read the stuff — I want your reasoning, and I'd rather have a definately-wrong diagnosis with the reasoning shown than a balanced one that seperates nothing.
