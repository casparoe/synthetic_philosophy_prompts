I have been arguing with a colleague about judicial review, and he keeps falling back on the umpire analogy — specifically the version John Roberts offered at his 2005 confirmation hearings, where he said that judges are like umpires, that "Umpires don't make the rules, they apply them," and that it is a justice's job "to call balls and strikes, and not to pitch or bat." His view is that the analogy is sturdier than its critics allow, and that it dissolves the counter-majoritarian objection: unelected judges no more overrule the legislature than an umpire overrules the players.

What he thinks rescues it is a refinement I had not heard before. The strike zone, he says, is not a boundary that umpires discover and occasionally misreport. The rulebook zone is a nominal specification; the operative zone is whatever umpires consistently call, and pitchers and hitters calibrate to the operative zone rather than to the text. The official's judgment therefore constitutes the standard it appears merely to apply, and there is no independent fact of the matter for a call to be wrong about. Constitutional meaning, he says, works identically: the text fixes a nominal boundary, the operative content is the settled practice of courts, and judicial review is an unremarkable continuation of the game rather than an intrusion on democratic self-government.

I suspect the refinement is a reductio rather than a rescue, but I cannot say precisely why, and I want the analogy audited rather than dismissed. Please do the following. First, give me the correspondence table as explicitly as you can: what plays the role of the umpire, the rulebook, the league and its rules committee, the players, the fans, the appeal, and the league's power to hire, fire, and replace officials. Second, say where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the use he is putting it to — defusing the counter-majoritarian objection — from the ones that are merely decorative. Third, tell me what features of the baseball case the analogy quietly imports that the constitutional case lacks. I notice, for instance, that the sport classifies ball-and-strike calls as subjective and has long kept them outside replay review, while simultaneously developing an automated ball-strike system, which suggests the sport treats the call as answerable to a measurable fact in a way that constitutional interpretation is not; and that in baseball there is a body, independent of the umpires, that can amend the rules and replace the officials, whereas in the constitutional case that body is, at best, a nearly unusable amendment process. Fourth, adjudicate the tension inside his own position: can the modesty version of the analogy ("they only apply the rules") and the constitutive-practice version ("their practice is the rule") both be true at once, or does the refinement undermine the conclusion it was introduced to save? Finally, say whether any conclusion about the legitimacy of judicial review survives the audit, and whether some better analogy — or none — would carry what he wants it to carry.
