I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on concepts and categorization, and I'd like help with something that has been bothering me since our session on the Bongard problems — the visual puzzles, originally posed by the Soviet researcher Mikhail Bongard and later popularized by Hofstadter, in which you are shown six boxes on the left and six on the right and asked to say what concept separates the two sets. Our instructor used them to illustrate concept induction from very few examples. I think I followed the lecture, but the more I press on the idea the less I feel I understand what is actually being claimed, and I'd like a careful conceptual explanation rather than a survey. Please don't send me to a reading list or give me citations; I want to get the idea straight in my own head first.

Here is what puzzles me. Take a problem whose intended rule is relational: in each left-hand box, some part of the figure is a rotated copy of another part, and in each right-hand box no part is a rotated copy of any other. Suppose the designer, carelessly, also made it true that every left figure has an odd number of strokes and every right figure an even number, and that the left figures share a symmetry the right ones lack. A solver who announces that the rule is odd-versus-even has not solved the problem. Every one of the twelve boxes satisfies that rule, yet we want to say the concept has been missed. So the data by itself never picks out the concept; something else does the work.

What I'd like you to explain is the following.

First, what exactly is that something else? Is the intended rule privileged by a fact about the figures themselves, by a fact about human perceivers and the concepts they happen to share, or by a fact about the designer's intentions — and can these three be pulled apart without the explanation becoming circular? Our teaching assistant insisted that a puzzle is only ever solved relative to a community of solvers and that there is no fact of the matter about which rule is "the" rule. I want to know whether that is a deep point or a deflationary one.

Second, what does this imply for the phrase "inducing a concept from a few examples"? If the examples are consistent with indefinitely many rules, what is a successful solver actually doing when they get the concept, and is that achievement logical or psychological? I keep feeling that if the answer is "they bring a great deal of background knowledge to bear," then the interesting question has simply been relocated into where the background knowledge came from — I'd like you to either vindicate that suspicion or show me why it's a mistake.

Third, in the Copycat tradition the emphasis falls on fluidity: the same input can be perceived under many descriptions, and which description you settle on governs which generalization you reach. I'd like that made concrete — what work does the notion of a description actually do here, beyond being a slogan about seeing-as? Is there any principled sense in which the intended rule is simpler than the accidental one, or is "simpler" just our bias wearing a lab coat? I know the standard grue-style version of this problem from the philosophy of induction; I'd like to know whether the visual case adds anything to it or is really the same problem in a different costume.

Please explain this intuitively — I don't need formalism, and I'd rather have a clear grip on the issue than a complete taxonomy. Around 900 words would be ideal.
