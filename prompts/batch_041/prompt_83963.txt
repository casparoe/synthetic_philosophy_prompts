I read Hofstadter on how the 'I' isn't stuck in one skull -- that a fair chunk of you gets copied into the people who know you best, so when you die something of you is still running in their heads, not as a memory but as a real partial instance of you. I want to take that seriously, and I'm trying to work out what a theory of personal identity would have to deliver to survive it. So give me the desiderata -- not a theory, the spec: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't flatten, the things it must not be allowed to say. With the case or consideration that forces each one.

Don't only give me the usual suspects (fission, gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement, the split-brain patients). It also has to handle: a widow who can finish her late husband's sentences better than he could and gets his jokes right when nobody else does; a man who is, on any honest reading, about 40% his mother and 15% his oldest friend; identical twins raised apart who end up with the same laugh; two people who each carry a copy of the same dead friend and now disagree about what he would have wanted. Throw in your own case if you've got one that does alot more work than mine.

Then say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to like, and flag any pair I can't have at once -- my suspicion is that 'being the same person comes in degrees' fights with 'identity is one relation, not a similarity score', but you tell me. Skip the scorecard of rival theories, I just want the list. 600 words max.
