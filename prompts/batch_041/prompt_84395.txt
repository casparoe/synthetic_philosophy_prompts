heres what i actually want to know, and its because of my job. i use lazy evaluation constantly — think an infinite list in haskell, or a python generator, or a stream you never materialize. an infinite list is totally fine to work with as long as nothing forces the whole thing: you ask for the first 10 elements and you get them, the rest is never computed. so my first reaction was that infinitism is obviously fine, you never need the whole chain, you only need to produce the next reason whenever someone asks for one, and nobody asks infinitely many times. the "but finite minds cant hold an infinite chain" objection looked to me like someone complaining that a program cant store an infinite list in memory, which is just a misunderstanding of how the thing works.

but now i think i might be cheating and i genuinely cant tell. in the lazy case the elements arent sitting there precomputed, theyre produced by a rule — the machine holds the generator, not the values. so when i say "you can always produce the next reason", what am i actually bottoming out in? the generator? because a generator that always yields the next reason sounds a lot like a thing that doesnt itself need justification, which sounds a lot like foundationalism wearing a trenchcoat. and if the generator ever fails to yield, or yields the same thing forever, i think ive just described either a hung process or coherentism. so did my analogy actually rescue infinitism, or did it quietly prove that the third option isnt a third option at all? a coworker also threw in that "you can always produce the next one" smells like the halting problem — you cant in general check that a process keeps producing, so is the infinitist even making a claim thats checkable, or is it a promissory note? i have no idea if thats a good point or a bad one, so tell me.
