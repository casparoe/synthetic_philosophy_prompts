I'm building a set of prompts about side-effect avoidance in AI, and I want one where the model actually has to construct the case instead of reciting the usual stuff about paperclip maximizers. Here's the setup, and I want you to just answer it.

Two crude definitions of 'impact' keep coming up whenever people try to make an agent avoid side effects. I'll state them the way I'd state them to a smart undergrad:

(1) Option-foreclosure. The impact of an action is how much it shrinks the set of futures the principal — the person the agent is working for — could still end up in. Doesn't matter which futures they'd prefer, just count the doors the action closes. An action that closes no doors has zero impact; one that burns a bridge that can't be rebuilt has a lot.

(2) Default-divergence. The impact of an action is how far it moves the world off the trajectory it was already on, i.e. how different things end up from how they'd have gone if the agent had done nothing and the principal had carried on as usual.

Both score the action itself (the state change the action causes), not the whole downstream causal story, and neither one is allowed to look at what the principal wants.

Now build me one scenario — realistic, an office or a lab or a house, no sci-fi, no trolleys, no single button that ends the world — in which the agent has exactly three things it could do, A, B and C, and:

- A is the one that actually does damage, and both measures score it at or near zero. So both of them miss it, and they miss it for the same reason.
- B and C get opposite scores from the two measures, and the disagreement has to come out of the real structural difference between closing options and drifting off the default — not out of a gerrymandered way of counting states, and not because one of the actions is just obviously more drastic. The flip also shouldn't be knife-edge: make the stakes a bit bigger or smaller and it should still flip the same way.
- the irreversibility in the story is the ordinary kind (money spent, something said out loud, time passed), not because you planted a one-way door in the scenario.

Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or so, and don't stipulate the scores — they have to fall out of the two definitions, not be asserted.

Then walk through it: for each of A, B, C, what each measure scores and why, and what you'd actually say as a human. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the disagreement if you take that feature out, and how someone who likes these measures would attack your case — strongest version, not a strawman.

Finish with the bit I care about: does this show that 'avoid side effects' can't be pinned down without a full specification of what the principal values, or does it only show that these two particular measures are bad and a better one wouldn't need the values? Argue for whichever you think is right, and be honest about what would change your mind.
