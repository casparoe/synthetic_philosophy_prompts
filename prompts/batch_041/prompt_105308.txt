Six weeks ago I accepted a role that had never existed at my employer: I am now the company's first full-time technology-ethics lead. We sell a workforce-management platform that schedules shifts, scores worker productivity, and quietly shapes who gets promoted; the next release will automate several decisions that managers currently make by hand. My first assignment is to advise whether this redesign should be treated as a purely technical question — a matter of engineering trade-offs — or as a political one, and, if the latter, where exactly I should press to have any effect. I have been reading my way into the long-running argument over whether artifacts have politics, and I find that it is usually conducted at a level of abstraction that never tells a practitioner where to stand. What I need from you is a prioritization, not a tour.

Concretely: among the open questions in this area — whether an artifact's politics is best located in its origins (who built it, under what constraints, to whose benefit), in its effects (what it does to the people caught up in it), in its affordances (what it renders easy or hard, visible or invisible), or in the interpretive flexibility of those who use and repurpose it — which is the most upstream? Which one, if settled, would do the most to clarify or dissolve the others, and which of the rest are downstream disputes that only look independent? Rank them, and defend the ranking on explicit criteria: how much other work depends on the answer, how tractable each question is for someone who will not be running experiments, and what a good answer would actually let me do on Monday. Tell me what would change your ordering — what evidence, argument, or shift in my situation should move a question up or down the list.

Two further things I want you to take a definite position on rather than hedge. First, is there a version of the claim that an artifact can be inherently political — political by virtue of its design rather than its use — that survives the usual objections, and does it apply to something like an automated scheduling system, or is that a category mistake? Second, given your ranking, where should a person in my position spend her limited credibility: on changing the artifact, on changing the rules around it, or on changing who gets to decide?

Give me a committed answer with reasons, not a menu of options left for me to sort through. And if you think the whole framing is wrong — that I am asking a design question in the vocabulary of political theory and would do better to abandon it — say so plainly and tell me what to think instead.
