I have no academic background, but I've been reading on my own about the dispute between endurantism, on which a person is wholly present at every moment of their life, and perdurantism, on which a person is a four-dimensional whole composed of distinct temporal parts, one per moment. There is a version of the second view that strikes me as both genuinely puzzling and a little absurd: that you are not the whole but only the current moment's part, so that the person who made yesterday's promise is not you but merely your temporal counterpart. I'd like a satirical piece written from inside that position — a deadpan self-help column, in the "learn to let go" register, by a coach who has absorbed the view completely and is advising readers on guilt, promise-keeping, and planning for a future that, strictly speaking, concerns strangers. The humour should come from the metaphysics being handled accurately and sustained without a slip: the coach should treat each moment's part as its own thing, insist that counterpart relations rather than identity connect us across time, and never quietly relapse into speaking as though he were one continuous person (he may dismiss the endurantist's picture of a single enduring self, but only in passing). A reader who knows the debate should recognize that the author understands the position and find the satire fair, while a reader who does not should still be able to follow it. About 500–550 words, with a title and one reader's letter that the coach answers.
