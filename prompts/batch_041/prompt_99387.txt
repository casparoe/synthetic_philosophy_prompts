(a) Explain the distinction Ryle draws between knowing how and knowing that, and set out his main argument against the intellectualist view that knowing how is a species of knowing that. (b) Do recent intellectualists such as Stanley and Williamson succeed in showing that knowing how is propositional knowledge?

Here's what the student wrote:

Ryle's distinction is between knowing that something is the case and knowing how to do something. In The Concept of Mind he attacks what he calls the 'intellectualist legend' — the view that knowing how is really just a kind of knowing that. His main argument is the regress: if every intelligent performance required you first to know a true proposition about how to do it, then acting intelligently would require you to contemplate that proposition first, and contemplating it is itself something you'd have to do intelligently, so you'd need another proposition, and another, and so on forever. Since no one can actually run through an infinite series of thoughts before they act, intellectualism has to be false. Ryle concludes that knowing how is a disposition to behave intelligently rather than a proposition one knows.

Stanley and Williamson (2001) bring intellectualism back. They point out that knowing how to swim is grammatically a case of knowing something — knowing a way to swim — so knowing how is knowing a proposition. Their distinctive move is that the proposition is known under a practical mode of presentation, which is why it doesn't feel like knowing a fact. This shows that Ryle was wrong: knowing how really is a form of knowing that, just a practically-presented one. So the anti-intellectualism that was the received view for decades is refuted.
