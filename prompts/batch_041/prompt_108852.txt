I've been reading a lot of the wild-animal-suffering material that circulates on the rationalist and effective-altruism blogs, and I'm trying to write up my own position for a discussion group before I commit it to paper. I'd like a hard audit of whether the package actually hangs together. Please act as a careful philosopher of population ethics and animal welfare — someone fluent in the technical machinery of welfare aggregation, the harm of death, and the ethics of bringing beings into existence — and think through this slowly rather than reaching for the standard talking points. It may help to begin by restating each commitment below in your own terms and, for each pair, flagging whether they genuinely pull against one another or merely look like they do.

Here is what I hold. (1) I'm a totalist welfarist: the value of an outcome is the sum of the welfare of every sentient being, and an increment of welfare counts the same regardless of species. (2) I think the typical life of a wild animal — and I have in mind especially the r-strategists, the insects, the small mammals, the fish and the songbirds that make up the overwhelming majority of sentient biomass — is on balance bad for the animal: the juvenile mortality, the hunger, the predation and the disease swamp whatever positive experience there is. (3) I take the harm of death seriously, in roughly the deprivation sense: dying is bad for the one who dies because of the good it takes away. (4) I'm a habitat preservationist — I give to land trusts, I think clearing rainforest for pasture is a grave wrong, and I count biodiversity loss among the great moral disasters of our time. (5) I think it's bad to bring a being into existence whose life will be net negative, even though that being is not thereby made worse off by never existing. (6) I think the extinction of a species is a serious loss in its own right, not merely a shorthand for the summed welfare of its members. (7) I'm a reluctant interventionist: I think we should be prepared to intervene in wild nature to reduce suffering — contraceptives, vaccines, gene drives, and if it came to it, measures that reduce the numbers of the most fecund and most miserable species.

What I want is not a survey of the literature but an audit. Tell me which of these are actually in tension and how badly, ordering them from most to least severe; for each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. I suspect at least some of these only look inconsistent, so be careful to distinguish the real conflicts from the apparent ones, and say explicitly when a pair turns out to be compatible rather than straining to find friction in it. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most trouble at the least cost. Throughout, please state how confident you are in each claim you make, and at the end single out the one point you're least sure about and explain why it's the shakiest.
