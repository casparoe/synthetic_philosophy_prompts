I have been reading and arguing about the special composition question for years now, and I want a diagnosis rather than another round of the usual moves. The question, as I understand it, is this: under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? The live answers are familiar. The universalist says: always -- any plurality whatsoever has a sum. The nihilist says: never -- strictly speaking there are no composite objects, only simples arranged chairwise, catwise, and so on. And the organicist says: sometimes -- composition occurs precisely when the activities of the parts constitute a life, so organisms exist but chairs and statues do not. Each camp has its standard battery. The universalist and the nihilist both press the vagueness argument: any restrictive answer needs a sharp, non-arbitrary line, and there seems to be no such line in a continuous series of cases, so we are pushed to one of the two extremes. The organicist replies that a life is not an arbitrary line but a real joint, and that universalism and nihilism both mangle the manifest image -- chairs and all. Then the universalist asks why a life should be privileged over a chair, and the nihilist asks the organicist to say what "constituting a life" adds that the arrangement of simples did not already give us, and everyone goes home unconvinced. I have watched this loop for a decade, in print and in my department, and nobody has moved an inch.

Here is my hypothesis, which I would like you to evaluate rather than assume. I suspect the participants are not really disagreeing about composition at all. They are disagreeing about which explanatory virtue is supposed to govern the answer. The universalist and the nihilist are both optimizing for "no arbitrary distinctions," and they are willing to pay in common sense to get it; the organicist is optimizing for "save the manifest image," and is willing to pay in arbitrariness (or to insist that a life is not arbitrary) to get that. If that is right, the debate is not a disagreement about what composes what -- nobody is disputing any particular case's microstructure -- but a disagreement about the aim of the inquiry, descriptive versus revisionary, and about whether "no arbitrary distinctions" is even a desideratum on a metaphysical answer rather than an aesthetic preference carried over from physics. A second possibility, which I hold more loosely, is that the two extreme camps are answering different questions under one heading: the nihilist may be making a claim about the fundamental inventory of the world, while the universalist may be making a claim about which of our ordinary count-noun classifications are legitimate -- and those can come apart, so that both could be right about their own question while neither addresses the other's.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the impasse a substantive metaphysical disagreement, a verbal dispute about the word "object" or "exists," a difference in bedrock intuitions about whether a continuous series can have a non-arbitrary cut, or a case of two camps answering two different questions? Engage the actual positions as stated above, say which of my two hypotheses (or which third diagnosis) best fits the structure of the standoff, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction, a formal result, a decision about what the question is for -- or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why.
