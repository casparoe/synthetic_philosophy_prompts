so im a junior and i run the philosophy club at my school. we're about 14 people, 50 minutes after school, normal classroom, no projector and definately no money for prizes (candy at best).

my teacher mentioned Moore's paradox in class last week - the thing where you say its raining but you dont beleive its raining - and everyone laughed and moved on. i keep thinking about it and i want the club to actually feel it. the problem is that when i explain it everyone immediately says ok so its a lie, or ok so its a contradiction, and then theyre bored. but neither of those is right, i think: both halves can be true at the same time, and the person isnt lying about the weather or about their own head. so i dont want to explain it at all - i want them to walk into it themselves.

what i want is a game. my rough idea: everyone plays lie detector. there are cards with sentences on them, someone reads one out loud, and the group has to decide - lying, confused, or just stating two true things. some cards are flat contradictions so they have a baseline for what a real contradiction feels like. some are the dont-beleive kind and some are the beleive-the-opposite kind (i heard those are two different flavours and i want both, because i suspect theyre wierd for different reasons). and at least one card has to be a case where the person reading it is really in that state and the first half is actually true, so the group convicts them and then we check at the end and it turns out they were just... right. my idea for that one is whoever is actually the tallest person in the room reading a card that says theyre the tallest and they dont think they are, but you might have something better.

can you build the whole thing out? rules, materials, how many rounds, a scoring system that makes them actually want to argue, roles if you need them, and a timeline that fits 50 minutes. then the debrief: where the sentence isnt contradictory, what exactly is wrong with it, and why it feels wrong even when its true.

two more rounds i want in there. one on the knowledge version - say the thing and then say you dont know the thing - which i think is nastier. and a final round that breaks whatever explanation the group settled on, so nobody leaves thinking its just a rude thing to say.

and be honest with me about two things: what predictably goes wrong the first time (i have no idea how to stop 14 teenagers yelling LIAR at everything), and what the game cant show - the part i'll have to just tell them.
