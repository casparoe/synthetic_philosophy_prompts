I'm putting together materials for a graduate seminar on experiment design in the cognitive sciences, and one session is on how benchmark-style evaluations smuggle in unexamined background assumptions about the world. My students found Turing's brief discussion of clairvoyance and telepathy in his 1950 paper on machine intelligence genuinely arresting: he treats the possibility of mind-to-mind information transfer not as a fringe curiosity but as a live methodological hazard for any test that has a human interrogator judging a remote respondent, and he proposes that the whole thing could be settled by conducting the test in a shielded chamber with the participants physically separated and no possibility of covert transmission. What interests me is that almost everyone remembers the imitation game and almost nobody remembers that he was willing to grant parapsychological effects enough standing that they might contaminate the results — and that this concession, whether or not one shares it, is a perfectly respectable point about confounding variables and experimenter effects.

So here is what I'd like from you: write a satirical institutional document — specifically, a formal methodological standards bulletin, in the dry register of an official best-practice guideline, issued by an invented regulatory body for AI evaluation laboratories in a world where extrasensory perception has been quietly, unglamorously established as a real but small effect. The target of the satire is the way benchmark culture handles confounds: the pretense that once you have a controlled protocol, blinding, and a sufficiently large sample, all the messy epistemic residue has been cleaned up. The humor should come from the institution treating telepathy exactly the way it treats radio-frequency interference or experimenter bias — as a routine nuisance to be scheduled around, budgeted for, and written into the compliance annex, never as anything metaphysically remarkable. I want the bulletin to cover, in numbered sections, matters such as: required shielding specifications for interrogator and respondent chambers; the distinction between leakage that inflates machine scores and leakage that deflates them, and why only one of these gets labs audited; a rule about whether a subject who reports receiving impressions must be believed or referred for retesting; the awkward case where a human confederate scores below chance, which the document treats as an administrative problem rather than a finding; and a short appendix conceding that if the effect size is zero in a given lab, the lab is not thereby in compliance, because the absence of a signal is itself an unexplained result. The comedy has to be earned from getting the methodology right: a reader who actually runs evaluations should recognize the real anxieties about blinding, demand characteristics, and the difference between a failed replication and a null result, and should find the piece fair rather than silly.

Aim for roughly 700 to 850 words. Keep the voice deadpan and bureaucratic throughout — no winking, no narrator stepping outside the document, no jokes that require the reader to be told they are jokes. An invented signatory, a document number, and a revision date are welcome; avoid naming any real person, institution, or regulatory body, and avoid quoting or attributing any specific sentence to a real text. If the document needs to gesture at its intellectual ancestor, do so obliquely, by describing a historical evaluation protocol that assumed a shielded room would settle the matter, without naming who proposed it. The reader should close the document unsure whether to laugh at the parapsychology or at the compliance culture, and that uncertainty is the point.
