Write a dialogue between two philosophers who have agreed to spend an afternoon disputing whether the pictorial statistics that Otto Neurath developed at the Social and Economic Museum in Red Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s — the method later called Isotype, in which greater quantities are shown not by enlarging a pictogram but by multiplying the same pictogram, so that a thousand unemployed workers become a row of a thousand identical worker figures — can be a medium in which genuine statements are made.

Speaker A is an advocate. She holds that a picture statistics of this kind is a real instrument of knowledge: that it makes social and economic facts legible to people who would never read a statistical table, that it can cross boundaries of language and class, and that it belongs to Neurath's larger programme of a unified, physicalist science. Speaker B is a critic, trained in formal logic and the philosophy of language. He is happy to grant the charts their rhetorical force, but he doubts they constitute a language, and he suspects that their air of neutrality conceals a series of conventions and editorial choices that a written sentence would have been obliged to defend.

Set the scene in front of one wall-sized chart, assembled from hundreds of small identical figures, that is meant to display something about unemployment or housing over time. The two should argue over the concrete features of the chart in front of them rather than in the abstract. Let the exchange turn, at a minimum, on the following questions, with each speaker answering the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them:

- Can the chart assert anything at all, and if it can, can it assert something false? What would a negated or mendacious pictorial statistic look like, and what would distinguish it from an accurate one?
- Does a picture language have a syntax? Can it express negation, disjunction, quantification, or the difference between a rising trend and a high level — or does everything the chart seems to say have to be supplied in words by whoever designed it?
- Is the rule of serial repetition a discovery about how perception works, or an arbitrary stipulation? And is the choice of the pictogram itself — which worker, drawn in what style, with what posture and what possessions — a merely decorative decision or a substantive one?
- The critic's strongest card: Neurath's own anti-foundationalism, his insistence that there is no theory-neutral observation language and no clean slate on which experience is recorded, appears to undercut the claim that pictures supply a neutral medium. How can the advocate answer this without abandoning either thesis?
- The advocate's strongest card: the critic's demand that a language be sentential may itself be a parochial preference, and a medium that a worker can read without a gymnasium education has epistemic and political virtues that the logician's formalism conspicuously lacks.

A complication both speakers must reckon with: Neurath himself declined to call Isotype a full language, describing it instead as a "language-like technique" and a helping language that was always to be accompanied by words. The dialogue should make clear why each speaker thinks that concession either saves the project or gives it away.

Neither speaker should be a strawman. Each should concede at least one real point, and at least one exchange should be left genuinely unresolved. About 1,000 words. Neither character should quote from any text; any view attributed to a historical figure should be paraphrased.
