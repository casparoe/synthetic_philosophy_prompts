I do cost-effectiveness modeling for a health technology assessment group — QALYs, DALYs when I am working with global health colleagues, ICERs measured against a cost-per-QALY threshold, health-state utility weights elicited by time trade-off and standard gamble, severity modifiers, end-of-life premiums, discounting, probabilistic sensitivity analysis, cost-effectiveness acceptability curves, the whole apparatus. A close friend is a philosopher who has spent the last couple of years working out a view she calls lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, and our arguments have stopped being productive because we keep sliding past each other. Here is her position as carefully as I can state it. First, suffering and pleasure are not commensurable: there is no fact of the matter about how much pleasure is equivalent to a given amount of suffering, so any exchange rate between them is an artifact of the measuring instrument rather than a discovery. Second, there is a threshold of suffering — she usually cashes it out as a life worse than not existing, or severe unrelievable chronic pain — such that reducing suffering above that threshold takes strict lexical priority over any amount of pleasure gained elsewhere, however large, while below the threshold ordinary trade-offs are permissible. Third, the priority is not merely a matter of suffering being more intense or more urgent: the bad and the good are different kinds of thing, so the absence of pleasure is not a harm in the way the presence of pain is.
