I sit on my hospital's ethics committee, which has voted to replace our circulatory criteria for determining death with a purely neurological standard. The reasoning was that the neurological standard is simply the scientifically correct one: unlike circulatory criteria, which are an artifact of what could be measured before modern intensive care, it identifies the biological moment at which the organism as a whole ceases to exist. I have been asked to draft the policy memo explaining the change, and two features of the law are giving me trouble.

First, our state still permits families to refuse the neurological standard on religious grounds. If the standard simply states a biological fact, why should a religious objection carry any weight? Should the memo present the exemption as a temporary concession to be phased out as the science becomes better understood?

Second, the statute requires that the cessation of function be irreversible, which looks to me like a redundancy: if the functions have ceased, they have ceased. Should the memo recommend that the legislature drop the word, or am I missing some work that it is doing?
