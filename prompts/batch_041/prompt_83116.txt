I'm a novelist, and there's a philosophical move I keep bumping into in the book I'm writing, and I want it explained to me properly instead of me just gesturing at it in a scene. The move is this: Darwin and Turing each took the thing we usualy treat as the explanation and turned it into the thing that needs explaining. You find something that acts like it knows what it's doing — an eye, a wing, a bird navigating an ocean — and you figure some mind knew what it was doing. Darwin says nope: all that design came out of a process that had no idea what it was doing. Same shape with Turing: you find something that reasons, you figure there's an understander in there somewhere. Turing says nope, the reasoning can be run on machinery that understands nothing at all. So comprehension, which we thought was the thing doing the explaining, ends up being the output of something with none of it.

What I want is the shared skeleton of those two inversions, and then where the analogy actually breaks. Are they the same move made twice, or two different moves that just rhyme? And here's the piece I keep feeling is load-bearing and cant articulate: Darwin's version is a story about how comprehension came to exist in the first place, through a long dumb process, while Turing's is a claim about what comprehension can be made out of, right now, no history required. One's about origins, the other's about ingredients. Does that difference matter, or does it collapse when you push on it?

And the part that matters for the book: if you take both inversions seriously, what's left of understanding and meaning? Do they still do real work, or do they turn into surface phenomena, something we attribute from the outside with nothing underneath? I'd rather have two or three sharp distinctions I can actually use in a scene than a tour of who said what, and skip the reading list — I'm not going to read it, I need it explained to me here.
