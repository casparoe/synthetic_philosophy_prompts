I'm in a small bargaining-theory reading group (three of us, we meet in a bar) and last week we did the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, which I now can't stop thinking about. The set-up: two players, a convex feasible set of utility pairs, a disagreement point, and an "ideal point" that's the best each side can get while the other is at least at their disagreement payoff. You draw the ray from the disagreement point through the ideal point and take the top-right point where it hits the feasible set — so the whole split is pinned to just two facts, where you'd land without a deal and how big a claim you can file. It's sold on its axioms: Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance to rescaling the players' utilities, and monotonicity (if your options improve across the board, your share can't drop). What it gives up relative to Nash is independence of irrelevant alternatives — put a third option on the table and the entire split can move, because this thing insists that every alternative available counts for something.
