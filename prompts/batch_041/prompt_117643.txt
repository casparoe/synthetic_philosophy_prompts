At my lab's reading group, a visiting statistician and our reliability engineer spent an hour going at each other after a seminar on safety cases, and I still cannot say who had the better of it. The speaker had estimated the probability that at least one of a system's three redundant safety subsystems fails, treating the three failures as independent. Five moves stuck with me.

(1) The statistician said the treatment was sound in principle: independence is the one assumption that contributes nothing of its own, since among all joint distributions with the given marginals the independent one is the maximum-entropy choice, so it is the right default whenever the dependence structure is unknown.

(2) The engineer said the default is not neutral in practice: redundant subsystems share power supplies, maintenance crews, and manufacturing defects, so their failures are positively correlated, and presuming independence makes redundancy look more effective than it is — the wrong direction to be wrong in a safety argument.

(3) The statistician replied that if she is right, the presumption is not a presumption but a substantive empirical claim about this system, licensed only locally: either by a causal model in which the subsystems are conditionally independent once common causes are conditioned on, or by independence that has actually been engineered into the design. Demand that, he said, and you are left with no default that lets you compute anything when you know nothing.

(4) The engineer said she would rather have no default than one that manufactures confidence, and added that independence cannot be the source of the familiar error of judging a conjunction more probable than its conjuncts: independence makes a conjunction less probable than either part, whereas the combination mistake that inflates probability is treating overlapping outcomes as mutually exclusive and adding them. She suspected the conjunction effect comes from judging how well a story fits rather than from how probabilities combine.

(5) The statistician said that even granting all that, the maximum-entropy argument settles what to do under ignorance, and that an engineer who refuses a default is quietly adopting a dependence model she cannot quantify.

Take the role of a philosopher of probability who also knows reliability engineering well. Work out the strongest version of each position before committing to anything, then give me your verdict as exactly five numbered items, one per move, each a sentence or two, each rating that move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying the rating. Where the two of them share a blind spot, fold it into the item where it first matters. No introduction, no conclusion.
