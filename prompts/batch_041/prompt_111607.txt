I'm leading a reading group session on Hobbes's chapter on the liberty of subjects and I'm stuck on the central move. Rather than quote it I'll paraphrase, because the 17th-century spelling in my edition is a nightmare: Hobbes defines liberty as the absence of external impediments to motion, and he means it literally, so that water is free when it isn't dammed and a person is free when nothing outside them blocks what they're trying to do. He then says this makes liberty and fear compatible: someone who jettisons cargo in a storm, or pays a fine because they're afraid of prison, acts freely, because nothing is stopping them; the fear is inside them, not in the world. And the liberty of subjects in a commonwealth comes down to whatever the sovereign has left unregulated, the silence of the law. What I can't get straight is what he thinks he's doing with this. Is he offering a genuine account of what freedom is, or is he rigging a definition so that obedience under threat stops counting as unfreedom, and if it's the latter, is there any independent reason to accept the definition? Then how do I place him against the usual three-way split, negative liberty as non-interference, positive liberty as self-mastery, and the republican idea that you're unfree when you live at someone's mercy even if they never actually interfere with you? Take a worker whose employer could fire them at any moment but never has, and who does everything they want to do: Hobbes seems to have to call them free, and I want to know whether thats a reductio or something a Hobbesian can live with. Please help me see what the passage is actually claiming before I decide what to tell the group. Answer in British English.
