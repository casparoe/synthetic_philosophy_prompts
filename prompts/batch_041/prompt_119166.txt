ok here's a view I've talked myself into over the last couple years and I want you to try to wreck it.

Background: I've been reading rationalist blogs since forever, so I'm soaked in base rates and the outside view, and I've always been annoyed by the thing where you pick a reference class, someone picks a narrower or wider one, and the number moves. But I've come around to thinking the reference class problem isn't a real problem. The position: for any single-case question there is a determinate right class — the one that's homogeneous with respect to everything causally upstream of the outcome. Probabilities are facts about chance setups, so which class is the correct one is a fact about the world, not a convention. What we have is an epistemic problem, sometimes an unsolvable one: we can't usually tell which properties are causally relevant, or we don't have any data in the cell. "Which reference class?" has an answer. We just often can't get at it.

So what would count against this? I want the actual defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a defender of my line would themselves concede, not just the ones critics like to push. Some things I half suspect are the real threats, so say something about each:

- the specificity limit. Push refinement far enough and you get a class of one, so the frequency is 0 or 1 and tells you nothing. Is that a reductio, or is it just the point where frequency-talk runs out and chance-talk is supposed to take over? And if the latter, does chance actually earn its keep here, or is it a relabeling?

- whether the homogeneous class even exists. Statistical relevance isn't obviously well-founded — the relevant properties may be indefinitely many, and the intersection of all of them might be empty, or might not settle anything. Is there a clean counterexample where two equally specific, equally causally relevant classes give different answers and neither screens off the other?

- Simpson-style cases where the finer class flips the coarser one. If more specificity is supposed to be better, is anything actually guaranteeing that refinement is monotone in usefulness, or is that just a slogan?

- the old propensity objection, that a setup's propensity seems to shift once you learn the setup is in place, so it doesn't behave like a probability under conditioning. Does that kill the chance-y reading of "the right class," or is it a bug in one particular version of propensities?

- circularity. Knowing the causally right class requires knowing the causal structure, which is usually the very thing the probability was supposed to tell me in the first place.

And then the meta-question I actually care most about: has my view been qualified into unfalsifiability? "Your class was wrong" is always available as a reply to any disagreement, and "you just didn't condition on enough" is always available as a reply to any counterexample. If a defender can say that forever, is the view doing any work at all — or is that just what any realist about objective chance has to say, and fine? Also, what does my view predict that its rivals don't? The rivals being credences-only (no objective class needed) and plain "pick a class, it's a modelling choice, stop worrying."

I'd find it most persuasive if someone showed me a case where the causal facts are fully known and two maximally specific classes still disagree. But I suspect that's impossible by construction, which is either the view's vindication or its unfalsifiability, and I honestly can't tell which.
