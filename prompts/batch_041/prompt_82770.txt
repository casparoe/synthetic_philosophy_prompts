Draft a chapter for the induction handbook of an invented public body — give it a dull, plausible name of your own — whose sole statutory standard answers the following question: is it better that one person be tortured for fifty years, or that a number of people so large it cannot be written down each receive, for a moment, a speck of dust in the eye? The Office's founding answer was that the second outcome is plainly better, and the handbook exists to teach newly hired adjudicators to apply that answer as routine administrative procedure.

Write the chapter in 650–800 words, as numbered sections, including at least one worked example with figures and one paragraph of scripted language for use with a complainant. The figures in the worked example must be internally consistent; the arithmetic should be calm and correct even where the result is monstrous. The voice throughout is the flat, patient, faintly reassuring register of a procedural manual written for junior staff. The chapter may never acknowledge that it is a joke, that the standard might be mistaken, or that any of its authors had a sense of humour. The comedy must come from the arithmetic and the procedure rather than from comic naming: the Office and its units of measurement should be named as dully as a real regulator would name them. The target is the practice of running a single scalar total as a decision procedure, and the genre of standards documents that convert an intuition pump into a form to be filled in.

Without ever naming them as objections, the chapter must make the following difficulties legible. First, that the comparison requires a unit of harm, and that whoever fixes the unit — its magnitude, its duration, whether it is counted per person or per person-moment — is the one actually deciding the cases; let the Office have revised its unit more than once, and let each revision have changed outcomes. Second, that the speck side of the question requires more persons than exist, so the ledger must either spread the specks across time or reuse the same persons; show, without embarrassment, how the Office does it. Third, that a standard which always prefers the speck side will in practice never select the torture, so that the torture survives in the statute as a boundary marker rather than as anything the Office will ever do; have the handbook defend the retention of that boundary marker as precisely what keeps the standard honest. Fourth, that the Office decides between policies rather than between particular acts, and answers individual complaints by reference to the total. Fifth, that the appeal procedure imposes a cost on the appellant, which the Office books as a further speck.

End with a three-question self-check for the trainee, together with the Office's own model answers. A specialist should be able to reconstruct the standard objections — the separateness of persons, the intuition that certain harms are not exchangeable against any quantity of trivial ones, the slide toward a being whose interests outweigh everyone else's — from the handbook's attempts to forestall them; a reader who has never encountered the puzzle should still find the piece funny and faintly sickening. Do not cite, quote, or allude to any real work, author, or study; invent whatever precedents, committees, and consultation exercises you require.
