I chair the restitution advisory panel of a mid-sized encyclopedic museum in a European city. We hold about a million objects; a few thousand entered the collection between 1880 and 1930 from territories under colonial administration or military occupation, or from the private collections of officials who served in them. The panel meets monthly and disposes of roughly twenty matters a year. Some arrive as claims from states, some from communities, royal houses, or diaspora organisations, some from individual descendants of donors or of the people an object was taken from; some we open ourselves when a curator finds a hole in the record and nobody is asking for anything. We get one ninety-minute slot per matter. The panel is a lawyer, two curators, a historian, and me; none of us has a background in the ethics of cultural property, and the files we read are written by claimants, so they argue. Anything that transfers title needs the trustees, and every decision is reported in the press in at least two countries.

The council has adopted a premise I need turned into something we can actually run: for objects of the kind above, our holding is a stewardship and not an ownership, and legal title is where the inquiry ends only when the law compels it to. It has also adopted three commitments that I suspect contradict one another, and I want the procedure to expose the contradiction rather than smooth it over: a wrongful taking is not cured by the passage of time; an object's significance may lie in its relation to a living practice rather than in the object considered alone, in which case the question is where that relation can be sustained; and we remain answerable to a public that pays for the building and to donors' heirs whose future gifts we need.

Please write the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the matter to someone else because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to fit on two pages, plus a one-page intake sheet listing what we must establish before the procedure starts (facts, not characterisations), and be usable by a curator without a law degree. Every step should produce a line in the minutes that would survive a freedom-of-information request.

It must handle, at minimum: sorting claims by kind — remedy for a wrong, recognition of belonging, integrity of a dismembered ensemble, restriction of access to material a community treats as sacred, and a dispute among heirs — and saying what each kind requires, including whether a claim that alleges no wrong at all can succeed on any other ground; competing claimants, including the case where a state and the community of origin want different things; who counts as the successor of a community when the taking is a hundred and thirty years old and the community's own institutions have changed, and what to do when the claimant community is itself split; whether the recipient's capacity to care for and display the object is ever a legitimate consideration, and where it is a pretext; the non-return outcomes (loan-back, joint title, shared custody, transfer of title with a continuing loan, transfer to a body other than the claimant, digital return) and when each is a real answer rather than a way of avoiding the hard one; and precedent — the trustees will ask whether returning this one means returning everything.

Then run it on three files. First, objects taken in a punitive military expedition in the 1890s, claimed now by the successor state's national commission and separately by the historic royal court, the two disagreeing about where the objects should end up. Second, architectural sculptures removed from a monument with the permission of the then-sovereign power, bought by a private buyer and later sold to us; the claimant state does not say the removal was illegal by the standards of its time, but that the monument is a single work whose meaning the division destroys. Third, an object whose sacred or restricted character means the claimant community wants it back and wants it kept from general view, which collides with our access mission and our funders. You will recognise the first two; I have left the names out deliberately, and I do not want the public arguments rehearsed — run the procedure on the facts as I have stated them, and tell me if my statement of the facts is incomplete in a way that matters.

For each file, give the outcome and name the point at which a value judgement rather than a fact decided it. Then give me one case where the procedure yields an answer I will not like — my instinct is that the first should be returned and the second declined — and one class of case where the procedure misfires and I should not trust it. No citations or reading lists; if you name a real case, use only ones whose broad facts are common knowledge, and do not invent dates or figures.
