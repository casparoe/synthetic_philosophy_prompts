I've been reading about the extended mind thesis — the Clark and Chalmers thing where, if a notebook or a phone plays the right kind of role in your cognitive life, it isn't just a tool you use but literally part of your mind, same as biological memory. The classic illustration is the guy with Alzheimer's who writes everything down in a notebook versus the woman who just remembers her friend's address; the claim is there's no principled difference in the cognitive work being done. Anyway, me and two friends got into it last night and now I want the full picture.

My friend Ravi has a wearable pendant that records everything and lets him ask it "what did Priya say about the lease on Tuesday," and it answers instantly in his ear, and he says it's part of his mind now, and he's genuinely freaked out beacuse the startup behind it might fold. So here's what I want: what are the most important implications of the extended mind thesis? Not the abstract debate about whether it's true — I want the downstream consequences that actually matter, and I want them worked out for the messy cases rather than the textbook ones. In particular: (a) if the pendant really is part of Ravi's mind, what follows for moral responsibility when it malfunctions or when someone else messes with it — is sabotaging his pendant more like stealing his phone, or more like giving him brain damage? (b) what does it do to personal identity across upgrades and platform migrations — if he switches to a competitor's device and the old corpus doesn't come with him, has part of *him* died? (c) does it change what counts as cheating in an exam, or what a school is allowed to confiscate, and (d) does it license any kind of claim against the company for pulling the service? Ravi also floated a rule he calls the "long-coupling criterion" — that a tool only counts as part of your mind once removing it produces a measurable, lasting cognitive deficit — so please tell me whether that criterion does any real work or just collapses into "how attached you happen to be."

Don't just list abstract principles. For each implication, tell me what it would actually commit us to in these specific cases, and flag wherever the thesis gives a surprising or counterintuitive answer.
