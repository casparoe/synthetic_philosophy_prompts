Write a villanelle in the voice of a patient with pain asymbolia: someone whose pain report is intact and accurate and whose flinch is not. The sensory side of the state survives in full — location, quality, intensity — while the aversion that ordinarily travels with it has gone, and gone neurologically rather than psychologically. This is not stoicism, not dissociation, not a coping strategy, and the poem must make that difference felt rather than assert it. The patient's report is never that the pain is absent, only that it is present and does not matter; keep that distinction in view on every line.

Let the two refrains be the sensory report and the missing sting. The villanelle is the form whose whole character is a line that will not leave you alone — the literary shape of what pain ordinarily is — so turn that against itself: the line that keeps returning should come back emptied. No line may wince, and neither refrain may be ironic.

In the closing quatrain, where the two refrains finally stand side by side, the poem should lean — briefly, without arguing — toward one of the three accounts of why pain is bad: that the badness is intrinsic to the sensation itself; that it consists in the desire to be rid of it; or that it lies in pain's character as a command to act. The poem need not settle which is right, but a reader should be able to see which account it leans on and why the case embarrasses all three: a pain that is present, real, and minded by no one is awkward for each of them. Aim for the strict nineteen lines. Someone who works on the sensory/affective distinction in pain should recognize the phenomenon; someone who has never heard of it should still find a good poem. No title, no epigraph, no explanation of the neurology.
