I'm putting together a humor piece and I need you to write it. Format: an old-school newspaper advice column — the kind with a matronly headshot and the letters in italics — except the columnist, "Dr. Prue Sample," is a committed true believer in the self-indication assumption, and every one of her correspondents has a self-locating-belief problem. Title it "Ask Prue Sample." About 650 words. Deadpan the whole way, no winking, and nobody inside the piece ever remarks that anthropic reasoning is silly.

The technical stuff has to be right, because the fun is in getting it right. Quick reminder in case it helps: SSA says reason as if you're a random draw from the observers that actually exist in your reference class; SIA says a random draw from all possible observers, weighted by how likely each is to exist, so SIA gives extra weight to worlds containing more observers — which is what defuses the doomsday argument, and also what lets a certain kind of philosopher announce the size of the universe without leaving his chair. Prue is an SIA absolutist. Every reply has to actually follow from SIA, consistently, including the ones where the SIA answer is upsetting. She does not get to quietly switch to SSA when it's convenient — thats the move I'm making fun of, so if a correspondent pulls it she should correct them. Same with reference classes: she has a stable policy about hers, states it once, and sticks to it.

Four letters, each a real puzzle done properly:
1. "Presumptuous Phil," who is confident the universe is vast and therefore refuses to actually check things — his partner insists he open the envelope / read the result before announcing it, and he wants to know whether looking is even necessary.
2. A reader who has worked out she's roughly the hundred-billionth human and is using that to decide whether to keep paying into her pension. (She's reasoning SSA-style without knowing there's a name for it.)
3. Someone in a Sleeping Beauty situation — an amnesia protocol, two wakings, a bet on the coin — asking how to split a bill with the other waking.
4. A man who suspects he's a Boltzmann brain and wants to know whether he should propose to his girlfriend.

Prue's advice should be calm, warm, and frequently monstrous, and the monstrousness should come out of the assumption rather than out of her being a bad person. At least one answer should be a flat probability with no hedging. You can have her cite an invented authority on reference-class questions (a fictional board, a fictional handbook) — make up the names, don't cite real papers or people, and don't put words in any real author's mouth. She can have a catchphrase, thats fine, just make it up.

End with the little teaser line for next week's column.
