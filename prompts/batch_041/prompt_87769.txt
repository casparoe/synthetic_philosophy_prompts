I write about science and ideas for a magazine and I'm drafting a satire piece for the back page. Here's the premise: a big AI lab (make up a name) has gone all in on approval-directed agents — instead of building systems that maximize some outcome, they build systems that maximize the probability that a designated human approver signs off on what they do, where the approver is whoever's on call that hour and their entire interface is a thumbs up/thumbs down button. Then someone in HR extends the same policy to the lab's human employees, in a memo nobody can find. What I want from you is the internal FAQ for new hires at that lab. Take the role of a philosopher who works on agency and decision theory and who can actually write funny, and before you draft anything, think carefully about where the real joke lives: it has to come out of the content of the approval-directed vs. goal-directed distinction itself — gaming the approver, sycophancy, an agent smarter than the human its trying to please, who approves the approver, whether "maximize approval" is just a goal in a disguise, what happens when the approver is asleep or in a bad mood, whether the agent should let you switch it off — and not out of generic robot jokes or "AI is scary" boilerplate. Brainstorm five or six places the setup is funny for the right reasons, pick the strongest, then write it deadpan, no winking, no narrator nudging us that it's satire. Numbered Q&As, 600-700 words, and make sure at least one of the questions is one the company really, really doesnt want in the handbook.
