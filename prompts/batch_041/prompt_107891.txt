I am marking scripts for a third-year undergraduate epistemology module and would like a second, careful assessment of one of them. Below you will find the question as it was set, the marking scheme, and the anonymous script. Please give me a mark and a full justification rather than a general impression.

THE QUESTION AS SET (answer in no more than 1,200 words)

Priya is a statistician working without a computer. She carries out a long numerical derivation and arrives at the estimate θ = 4.2. She checks the derivation several times; it strikes her as impeccable and she can find no error in it. She then learns from a source she has every reason to trust that, during the hours in which she was working, she was suffering from a mild fever which in people with her training reliably degrades exactly this kind of derivation: it raises the rate at which such derivations misfire, and it does so without producing any felt difficulty, hesitation or doubt. She cannot repeat the derivation, and no other evidence bearing on θ is available to her.

(a) What should Priya believe about θ, and what should she think about the justification of that belief?

(b) In the seminars we gave two positions on the relation between these verdicts the labels 'Absorption' and 'Bracketing'. Absorption: a verdict about the reliability of one's own process must be absorbed into one's verdict about the matter itself, so that if Priya judges her derivation likely to have misfired she must lower her confidence in θ, and believing θ while judging that belief unjustified — epistemic akrasia — is never rational. Bracketing: the two verdicts answer different questions and sit at different levels, so Priya may rationally keep her credence in θ while judging that she is not justified in holding it, and epistemic akrasia may be rational. Which of the two, if either, does the case support, and why?

MARKING SCHEME

(a) Up to 6 marks: identifies the fever report as evidence about the reliability of the process rather than about the value of θ (2); gives a defensible account of how such evidence bears on the justification of the first-order belief (2); states and defends a verdict on what Priya should believe (2).

(b) Up to 6 marks: accurate statement of what each position does and does not claim (3); a reasoned choice between them, or a reasoned refusal to choose, which engages the costs of each (3).

Clarity and structure: up to 3 marks. Independence of argument — doing something with the case beyond restating the two positions: up to 5 marks. Total: 20.

THE SCRIPT

What Priya learns from the report is not a new fact about θ but a fact about herself: the derivation on which her estimate rests was produced by a process which, on this occasion, was unusually likely to have misfired. It does not identify any faulty step, and it supplies no competing estimate and no reason to think the true value lies above or below 4.2. It is therefore higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of the process rather than about the value of θ.

The first thing to notice is how little the distinction does here. A derivation that probably misfired probably did not deliver the right answer. What Priya has, then, is evidence that her estimate is wrong; and evidence that a belief is wrong is evidence about the matter itself. The fever statistics bear on θ in the ordinary way, making it less likely that θ = 4.2 is the truth, and the label 'higher-order' marks no difference that matters. She should suspend judgement.

The same conclusion follows from an analogy with disagreement. We may think of the fever as a second opinion about θ. Priya's own working says 4.2; the fever says, in effect, that the working is not to be trusted. Since she has no reason to think the second opinion less reliable than the first — the report is, after all, what she has to go on — she should give the two verdicts equal weight, which again leaves her suspended.

To suspend judgement is not to believe the negation. She has no positive evidence that θ ≠ 4.2, and she should not conclude that the true value is something else; she should simply decline to believe either. It is worth registering how general the situation is: any evidence that one's own reasoning is unreliable in ways invisible from the inside is a defeater for the beliefs that reasoning produced, and much of our reasoning is like that.

Part (b). The case supports Absorption. If the fever report is evidence about θ, as I have argued, there is nothing left for Bracketing to bracket. Bracketing asks Priya to believe that θ = 4.2 while believing that this belief is unjustified; but to judge a belief unjustified is to judge that it is probably not true. So Bracketing asks her to affirm what she takes to be false. Whatever we say about the practical variety, no one can be akratic about a proposition in this way; the state is not merely irrational but impossible. Epistemic akrasia — believing something while judging that one ought not to believe it — is a defect in the believer, and it is a defect precisely because such a believer will act on the belief and act badly. Since the report has already been absorbed into her verdict about θ, Absorption is the position the case supports.

There is one further wrinkle. The report itself reaches her through processes she cannot certify: her source, her hearing, her memory of what the source said, her assessment of the source's trustworthiness. Each might, for all she can tell from the inside, have misfired. The levels therefore collapse into one another, and Absorption and Bracketing turn out to be the same position under different descriptions: whichever verdict she reaches, she reaches it with a reliability she cannot vouch for.
