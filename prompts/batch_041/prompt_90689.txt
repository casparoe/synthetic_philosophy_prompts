I have no training in philosophy, but I have been chewing on A Clockwork Orange for about fifteen years, and I have reached the point where I think almost every discussion of it that I have read or overheard goes wrong in the same way. I want to know whether I am the one who is wrong.

The scene I keep returning to is the demonstration. Alex has been conditioned into an inability to do harm. The prison chaplain objects that the technique has taken away his ability to choose, and the Minister answers him in the language of consequences: crime will fall, the prisons will empty, there will be room for the people the government actually wants locked up. Everyone I have ever talked to about this treats the chaplain as the film's conscience and the Minister as its villain, and the film as a defence of free will against a therapeutic state. I no longer believe that. The chaplain is a prison chaplain; his objection would be just as available to someone who simply wanted Alex to serve out his full fourteen years. And the Minister's answer is not stupid. It is a real answer with real costs attached, and the film shows us the prison producing nothing but more violence. So the film seems to me to be staging the retributivist and consequentialist positions against each other and then refusing to settle it. I would like a reading that takes both theories seriously and says what the film actually does to them.

I have some specific things I want the reading to handle, because these are where my own attempts keep breaking down.

First: the Ludovico technique is not a sentence with a term. It is an alteration that keeps operating after Alex is released. Whatever else it is, it is a punishment he cannot finish serving, administered not by the state but by his own nervous system, and its duration is not fixed by anything. I have a feeling this matters enormously to the retributivist side and I cannot say exactly why.

Second: the aversion catches Beethoven. Alex loses a good that was never a crime. A consequentialist can book this as a cost of the policy. I do not see how a retributivist can say anything at all, because the suffering is not deserved and its amount is not measured to anything he did.

Third: Alex never repents. He complies. At the demonstration he is exactly as willing to do harm as he was before, minus the ability. If the point of punishment is desert, the state has not given him what he deserves. If the point is reform, it has not reformed him. If the point is deterrence, it has achieved that, and it has achieved it by making a man who cannot do otherwise.

Fourth, and this is the one I care about most: I keep wanting to say that a punishment has to be addressed to someone capable of taking it up, that it has to speak to the offender as a member of the same moral community, someone who could in principle understand why it is being done and answer for what he did. Alex after the treatment cannot do that. He cannot even be addressed; he can only be triggered. I do not know whether this is a third position or whether it is retributivism with the theology left in. Tell me plainly which it is, and what the position is usually called if it has a name.

Fifth: the film keeps showing me retributive feeling in its actual, unattractive form. The vagrant and the two policemen who used to be Alex's friends beat him well past what he did to them, and they enjoy it. Mr. Alexander, who has the strongest claim of anyone in the film, does not seek justice; he locks Alex in a room and plays the music that tortures him, in order to use him against the government. So when the film does let the retributive impulse run, it makes it look like vengeance wearing justice's clothes. I want the reading to say whether that is the film's verdict on retributivism, or a separate point about how retributive institutions get captured by politics.

Finally, the ending. I have read that the American edition of the novel dropped the last chapter, in which Alex outgrows violence on his own, and that the film follows the shorter version, so that Alex ends cured of the cure, back to his old self, and being offered a job by the Minister in exchange for his cooperation. If I have that textual history wrong, tell me and work with the film as it actually ends. What I want to know is what the last shot does to the argument. If the restoration of Alex's capacity for choice is the happy ending, then the film has sided with the chaplain and against the Minister, and with the retributivists, and it has done so knowing perfectly well that what is restored is a rapist and a murderer. If that is not a happy ending, then the film is saying something considerably darker about what the value of choice is worth, and I want that said out loud.

Please do not give me line readings or quotations. I do not have the film or the book in front of me and I would rather have the argument. Take the scenes I have named and use them, and name any others you think I have missed or misremembered.

Two things about how I would like this done. I am not a student and I do not have the vocabulary; explain the actual retributivist and consequentialist positions in plain terms rather than assuming I know the standard moves, but do not let that turn into a summary of the debate. I want the film read against the theories, not the theories recited. And I want you to be explicit about what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who actually work on this. If the reading I am reaching for is one contested interpretation of the film among several, say so, and say what the others are, rather than presenting it as what the film obviously means. I would much rather be told that a question I think has an answer does not have one than be given a confident answer that turns out to be one side of a live disagreement.

End by telling me what the film adds that the arguments alone do not, if it adds anything.
