In his opening statement at his 2005 Senate confirmation hearings, John Roberts offered what has become the standard defense of judicial restraint: "Judges are like umpires. Umpires don't make the rules; they apply them." He went on to call judging a "limited role," to insist that judges "operate within a system of precedent, shaped by other judges," and to remark that "nobody ever went to a ball game to see the umpire." The image is routinely enlisted to answer the objection that unelected judges who invalidate legislation are overriding the democratic will: on this view the judge contributes no content of his own, only the application of rules laid down by others, so the countermajoritarian objection misfires.

Audit the analogy. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — saying what in the constitutional order plays the role of the rulebook, the umpire, the strike zone, the teams, the league that writes the rules and employs the official, and the spectators, and stating what conclusion the image is being made to license. Then locate where the mapping breaks down, and sort the disanalogies into those that are incidental and those that bear on that conclusion. Among the matters worth pressing: whether the strike zone is fixed by the rulebook or constituted by the umpire's calls; whether, once precedent is admitted, the umpire's decisions do not themselves become part of the rulebook, so that the official legislates after all; whether baseball's rules are determinate at the points that matter in the way that open-textured constitutional clauses are not; whether the umpire's authority rests on a structure (the league) logically prior to and independent of the contest, whereas judicial authority is contested by the very political branches whose enactments it voids; and whether "nobody ever went to a ball game to see the umpire" imports a substantive conception of what judging should look like rather than deriving one from the game.

Then say which features of the source the image quietly imports that the target lacks — a prior and stable rulebook, an official with no stake in the outcome, a review above the umpire, and participants who accept the official's authority antecedently — and assess whether, with those removed, the conclusion the analogy was meant to support (that unelected judging poses no distinctive democratic problem) survives on its own or was being carried by the analogy all along. Close by saying whether any analogy could carry that conclusion, and if a better one exists, state it and explain what it captures that the umpire does not.
