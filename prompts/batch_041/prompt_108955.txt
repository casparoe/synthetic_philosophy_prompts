I am in the second year of a philosophy degree and I also work two shifts a week as an assistant in an outpatient physiotherapy clinic, which is where most of my thinking gets done. One of my modules covers the view that a person's life goes well to the extent that it contains more pleasure than pain; separately, for the last eight months I have been keeping a ledger of my own pleasures on the advice of a therapist, after coming out of a long stretch of depression in which nothing registered at all. The two things have collided in a way I cannot sort out. There are about four hundred entries now, and the longer the list gets the less its items seem to be about the same thing: the moment a fever breaks and thinking becomes possible again; being told news you had already feared, and feeling oddly lighter; the smell of a school corridor I hated at eleven and now go out of my way for; the relief of a book I hate being over; a stranger's ringtone that is the same as my father's; fury at a referee in a match I do not care about; a scalding bath; being proved right about something bad. If I ask what these have in common — a feeling, a kind of wanting, a yes — I cannot say, and the theory I am being taught appears to need an answer. My tutor thinks they are unified at a level beneath the surface. The friend I share a flat with, who is on the same course and is less patient than my tutor, thinks the word "pleasure" is doing no work at all and that the theory is empty.

What I would like from you is a short story that puts this problem into events rather than into exposition. Set it in a small seaside town in February, mostly in a rented flat and in the clinic where the protagonist works: she is a physiotherapist in her forties, she has a back injury of her own, and she keeps the ledger. Please do not use the words "hedonism" or "well-being" anywhere in it, do not name or quote any philosopher, and do not let any character deliver a lecture — the disagreement should live in what people do, notice and refuse to say. I want at least one scene in which she and someone else disagree about whether two particular entries belong on the same list (use entries from the list above, or ones at least as specific), and one scene in which the ledger is put to some practical use — a form, a referral, an argument with a doctor — and turns out to be useless, or worse than useless. Somebody who has never read any philosophy should be able to follow it. Around two thousand words, and please keep the ledger from becoming a stand-in for an argument; it has to stay a person's.

The story has to end somewhere. Whichever way you go, the protagonist should make a definite decision about what her list is a list of: either that there is something its items share, in which case show her finding it at some cost to herself, or that there is not, in which case show what she loses by admitting it. The last paragraph should not hedge between the two, and I would rather have a wrong commitment than a balanced non-commitment. Then add a note of no more than three hundred words telling me which contested reading of the problem you have leaned on, where the story had to fudge the philosophy to make a scene work, and which side you actually think is right, with your reasons.
