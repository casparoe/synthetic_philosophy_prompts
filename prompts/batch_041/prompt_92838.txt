Alright, I've been goign in circles on the Pascal's mugging thing and I want someone to just tell me what I actually believe. Here's my gut on a pile of cases, and I want to know what single principle — or small set — captures them, what it says about a case I haven't thought of, and which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.

1. A stranger stops me, says he's a matrix lord, and says that unless I hand over my wallet he'll torture 10^50 people for 10^50 years. He says the odds he can actually do it are 1 in 10^50. I keep the wallet. I don't even slow down.

2. Same guy, same threat, but now the odds are 1 in a million. I hand it over.

3. Same guy, same 1-in-a-million odds, but the threatened harm is just that he breaks my arm. I still hand it over, and this one doesn't feel weird to me at all — a one-in-a-million chance of a broken arm is just worse than losing the wallet, so it feels like ordinary decision-making.

4. Reverse mugging. A guy offers to save 10^40 people from torture, and all I have to do is mail him $20. Same credibility as case 1: 1 in 10^40. I don't mail the $20. But this is case 1 with the sign flipped, and I can't really say why it feels different. Maybe I just don't like being played.

5. Not a person — a physics thing. There's a known process that, as far as anyone can tell, has a 1-in-10^20 chance of triggering a chain that kills everyone. A lab says it can be shielded for $1,000. I pay the $1,000 without hesitation, even though 1 in 10^20 is a way smaller number than the 1 in 10^50 I blow off in case 1.

6. Muggers, plural. A different guy shows up every morning with the case-1 story. Paying each time would ruin me inside a year, and they obviously can't all be as likely as they claim. I refuse all of them, and honestly I feel better about that than about case 1, because now it's a policy question and not a one-off.

7. The mugger who asks for a penny. Case 1 again, except he only wants one cent and the odds are still 1 in 10^50. I give him the penny. Pretty sure that's just because a penny is nothing to me and not because of the math.

My tentative guess is that I'm an expected-utility maximizer who quietly caps how much a tiny probability gets to move me — like anything under some threshold counts as zero, and the threshold drops as the stakes go up. But case 5 breaks that, since there I act on a probability way smaller than one I refuse in case 1, and the only real difference is who's telling me the number. So maybe my actual rule is about whether I trust the source of the probability, not the size of it. Is there one principle that gets all seven? What does it say about a case I haven't listed — say, a mugger who isn't a person but a message from a source I have good reason to think is honest, at 1 in 10^30? And if I want the rule to stay simple, which of my seven do I have to give up? I'd rather keep 5 and 6 than 4, if that matters.
