Common knowledge is when everyone knows something, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on forever. The idea was introduced by Lewis in his book on convention and then given a clean mathematical form by Aumann in the seventies. The formal trick is that you don't actually need the infinite regress: an event is common knowledge if everyone's information somehow already includes the fact that it's common knowledge, so it's a kind of fixed point, and that's how the infinite hierarchy gets collapsed into one condition.

The blue-eyes / muddy-children puzzle is the standard illustration. The visitor announces something everyone already knew, and yet it sets off a chain of deductions that ends with the blue-eyed people leaving. The point of the puzzle is that the announcement creates new knowledge out of thin air: before the announcement nobody knew there was a blue-eyed person, after it everyone does, and that's what lets them start reasoning.

Aumann's famous theorem is that if rationality is common knowledge in a game of perfect information, then every player picks the backward-induction action at every one of their decision nodes, including the nodes that never actually get reached. So in the centipede game, if both players know each other to be rational and know that they know it and so on, they both take immediately, and this isn't an equilibrium-selection story where you pick the backward-induction equilibrium out of several — it's forced by the epistemic assumptions alone.

Then the electronic mail game shows the flip side. If you only have almost common knowledge — a long but finite chain of confirmation emails, each with a tiny chance of getting lost — then the players are close enough to common knowledge that they go ahead and coordinate on the risky action, since the chance of a failure is negligible. So the moral is that in practice almost common knowledge is as good as common knowledge, and the infinitary idealization is harmless.

He wraps up by saying the whole literature is basically a technical curiosity: real people never actually have common knowledge of each other's rationality, so the theorems don't describe anything that happens in real strategic situations, and the apparatus is interesting mainly as pure math.
