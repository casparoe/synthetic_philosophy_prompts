I'm a retired geologist — 35 years, mostly on the exploration side — and since I hung up the boots I've been working through philosophy on my own, no uni, just books and podcasts, so I'm always half-worried that what I'm absorbing is subtly off and I won't find out for years.

Here's the thing. I listened to a long episode about Nietzsche's essay on history — the second of the Untimely Meditations, the 1874 one — and I wrote down the host's summary because it was so tidy. The gist was this:

Nietzsche says our relationship to the past comes in three flavours, and which one you need depends on what sort of person you are. Monumental history is for the person of action, the one who wants to do something great: it holds up the great figures of the past as models, showing that greatness was possible once and so can be again. Antiquarian history is for the person who loves what he's inherited and wants to keep it: it preserves a people's identity, but its weak spot is that it can curdle into a dusty reverence for everything old just because it's old, until the whole culture turns into a museum. And critical history is for the person who's weighed down by his past and needs to get free of it: it puts the past on trial, condemns it, and clears the ground for a fresh start. All three, he said, can go bad — but Nietzsche thinks critical history is the one we should mostly be using, because it's the only one that really serves life instead of deadening it. And the big point under the whole essay is that too much history is bad for you: a healthy person has to be able to forget, and modern education, with its endless pile of historical facts, is turning out people who can't act anymore. So the remedy, in the end, is to become unhistorical — to live like the grazing animal he's so fond of, in a happy sort of forgetfulness.

Then the host drew the conclusion that if Nietzsche were advising a bright kid today, he'd tell them not to go study history at university.

I'm inclined to believe the forgetting part, because in my own field I've known plenty of people who'd have done better science if they'd read fewer papers and spent more time looking at rocks. But the ranking of the three, and that ending, I'm less sure about — it doesn't quite match the Nietzsche I've run into elsewhere, though I couldn't tell you why.
