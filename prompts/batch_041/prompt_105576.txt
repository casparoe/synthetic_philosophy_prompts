The reading group I'm in has spent a term on the dispute between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws of nature without anyone changing position, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of it. As I understand the two sides: the Humean treats a law as whatever regularity earns its place in the deductive systematization of all particular fact that best balances strength, simplicity, and fit, so that lawhood is nothing over and above the pattern; the anti-Humean holds that this reverses the order of explanation, that regularities obtain because laws constrain what can happen, and that no inventory of non-modal facts about what does happen can by itself fix what must or may happen. Each side has the other's standard objections memorized: the anti-Humean says the Humean has no genuine modality and that the best-system competition is a fact about our theorizing rather than about the world; the Humean says necessitation is an unexplicated primitive that earns nothing in prediction, and that any anti-Humean intuition about a case can be reabsorbed by adding it to the desiderata the systematization must satisfy. That last move is what worries me most, since if it always works the debate threatens to become a contest over the weights of theoretical virtues that neither side argues for.

My hypothesis — tell me whether it survives — is that this is not a hidden empirical question and not merely verbal, but a disagreement about what an explanation of a regularity has to deliver: subsumption under a pattern versus grounding in something that makes the regularity hold, and downstream of that, about whether simplicity and the other virtues have any claim to track the world. Diagnose it concretely: which parts are substantive and on what (facts, standards of evidence, which cases count as data, starting intuitions), which parts are the two sides answering different questions under the single word law, and which parts are evaluative standards neither side can argue for. Then say what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected.
