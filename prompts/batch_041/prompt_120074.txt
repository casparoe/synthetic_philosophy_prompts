So this is going to sound like a small thing, but it's been bugging me since Thursday. We had a faculty meeting about whether the department should adopt some kind of statement about what we're for — the humanities dean wants everyone to have a mission, god help us — and one of my collegues shut the whole thing down with 'the right is prior to the good'. Apparantly that's supposed to mean we can't endorse any particular picture of what a flourishing life looks like. Everyone nodded. I nodded. I have no idea what he actually claimed.

Give me the menu. What precise claims could 'the right is prior to the good' be making? I want the full spread, from the ones that are almost trivially true to the ones that would cost you a lot to accept, and I want to know which is which. Things I've been chewing on: that we can justify principles of justice without any prior account of what's good for people (that one can't be right as stated — Rawls himself needs a thin theory of the good, and primary goods along with it, to get the whole thing off the ground); that the state, or in his version the department, may not presuppose any citizen's conception of the good; that justice constrains the pursuit of good outcomes and never trades off against them; that the self comes before its ends, so a person's identity isn't constituted by her commitments — I take it that's what the communitarians were really swinging at, not the priority thesis as such; that the right is prior in the order of justification while the good is prior in the order of value; that the basic structure gets assessed first, before individual life plans. Some of those look like they might not even be consistent with each other, and some may not be claims about the same 'good'.

So for each reading: platitude, real thesis, or just false? Which ones would actually support what my colleague was doing with it, i.e. ruling out a departmental endorsement of a vision of flourishing? And does he need the slogan at all, or could someone who thinks the state may legitimately promote worthwhile ways of life — a perfectionist liberal, say — reach the same conclusion while denying it? Also tell me who denies each reading, and whether the communitarian critique lands on the priority claim itself or only on its most deontological version.

Lay it out as a list I can bring to him on Monday. Casual is fine, I just need the joints to be visible.
