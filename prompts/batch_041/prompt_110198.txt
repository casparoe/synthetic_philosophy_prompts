A colleague of mine in the law faculty has, over several years of discussion, given me her verdicts on a series of cases about causation by omission. I have recorded the verdicts but not her reasons, which is deliberate: I want to reconstruct the rule she is running from the pattern alone. She is careful to separate causation from blameworthiness, and in every case she is answering only whether the omission caused the outcome, not whether the agent was at fault. These are her verdicts.

1. An anaesthetist on call during an operation leaves the theatre to take a personal telephone call and is absent when the patient arrests; the patient dies. She holds that the anaesthetist's failure to be present caused the death.

2. A parent drives to work having forgotten that a sleeping infant is in the back of the car; the infant dies of the heat. She holds that the parent's failure to remove the child caused the death.

3. A shipping company's designated safety officer, whose role is to report hull defects to the master, notices a defect and says nothing; the vessel founders. She holds that the officer's silence caused the loss of the vessel.

4. A man declines to wade into a fast-flowing river to pull out a drowning stranger, fearing for his own safety; the stranger drowns. She holds that his failure did not cause the death.

5. A wealthy woman does not send money to a charity that would have saved a child's life for the price of a meal; the child dies. She holds that her omission did not cause the death.

6. A commuter watches a stranger being pickpocketed and does nothing; the stranger loses her purse. She holds that his omission did not cause the loss.

Her pattern, then, is that the first three are cases of causation and the last three are not.

She has now put two new cases to me and asked me to predict her verdicts before she gives them. In the first, on a hospital ward the settled custom — contrary to the hospital's written protocol, but in accordance with what every nurse on the ward does every day — is not to have a second nurse independently re-check a drug dosage that a doctor has prescribed. A nurse follows that custom; the dose she gives is ten times too high, as a second check would have revealed; the patient is harmed. In the second, on a beach where a dozen other bystanders have linked arms to form a human chain reaching a struggling swimmer, a man alone remains seated and continues reading; he could have joined the chain at no risk to himself; there is no statute requiring him to help, and he is a stranger to the swimmer.

Please do five things. First, state the rule she appears to be running. Second, state the other rules that fit her six earlier verdicts just as well as the one you have named, including any rule her verdicts rule out, and why. Third, say what each surviving rule predicts about the two new cases, being explicit about where the rules diverge. Fourth, give your best guess at her verdict on each new case, with your reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals. Fifth, name a further case — one she has not already given — that would best tell the surviving rules apart, and give the verdict each rule would deliver on it. I am less interested in whether the nurse or the beachgoer is to be blamed than in whether their omissions caused the harm, and in what her pattern of verdicts reveals about which of the candidate rules she is in fact applying.
