I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on the ethics of everyday life, and I am assembling the handout for the session on whether ordinary conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking and their relatives — are moral obligations, and whether a visitor is bound by the local versions of them. Please construct the classification instrument for that session: a decision tree over the positions, built out of the logical space rather than out of a list of doctrinal labels.

The framework I want the tree to respect, because my students will have met it in the first hour, is a three-step decomposition, and I would like the branch labels to use my vocabulary for it rather than synonyms:

- the Grounding step: does the practice carry a 'practice-independent' moral ground — a stake that would be there whether or not the local rule existed, such as a harm, a fairness claim, or a reasonable expectation the other party has actually formed — or is it 'practice-dependent', its normative force coming from the fact that the local rule exists and is generally complied with?
- the Scope step: is the resulting requirement 'place-indexed', binding whoever is standing here, or 'insider-indexed', binding those who participate in the practice and share its benefits?
- the Weight step: is the requirement a duty, a defeasible reason that trivial inconvenience can outweigh, or a 'membership fee' — something one pays to be a decent participant, whose breach is a faux pas rather than a wrong?

Build the tree under the following conditions.

1. Every internal node is a question a respondent can answer without knowing which position it leads to. No node may take the form of asking whether one is a conventionalist, a realist, or the like; the first node should be a case, not a doctrine.
2. The cases must include at least: a visitor from a country without a tipping norm dining where the tip is assumed by both employer and server; jaywalking at three in the morning on an empty street; jaywalking across a busy street while a small child watches; holding a place in a queue for a friend who has not yet arrived; a visitor declining food a host has offered; and a visitor complying with a local convention she believes props up an unjust arrangement. At least two leaves should come apart only on that last case.
3. The visitor question needs at least three distinct positions: the visitor is bound exactly as a local is; the visitor is bound only by the practice-independent core and not by the practice as such; and the visitor is bound more strictly than a local, on a guest's-burden rationale.
4. Aim for nine to thirteen leaves, with no path longer than five questions and every node a yes/no or three-way split. Each leaf gets a short descriptive name, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a one-line verdict on tipping, on queueing, on jaywalking, and on visitor scope. Where a leaf corresponds to a position nobody defends, mark it 'unnamed' and say why it is empty.
5. Close with two short sections: the combinations of answers the tree generates that remain unoccupied, each with a one-line diagnosis — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and the pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, each with the further case that would separate them.

Two constraints on the instrument itself. Name positions descriptively and attribute none of them to an author or a work; I want the tree to stand on its arguments rather than on labels the students can look up, and I want the handout to be self-contained without readings. And write the nodes so that a respondent with genuinely mixed intuitions gets visibly stuck at one identifiable point — tell me which node that is and why the mixed respondent cannot get past it.
