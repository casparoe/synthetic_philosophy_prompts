I am a retired experimental physicist who now leads a monthly two-hour session for a great-books reading group at our local library: twelve people, none with formal training in philosophy, two of whom spent their careers in banking, and all of whom reach readily for the vocabulary of modern economics. Next month's session is Aristotle on money and exchange. Do not explain his position to me — I have read the relevant stretches of the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics and can follow the argument. Plan the session instead. Give me the order in which the components must come, such that each is earned, and say why the dependencies run that way: in particular, what has to be in place before his claim that money exists by convention rather than by nature can do any work, and what has to be in place before the puzzle about what makes exchanged goods commensurable can even be stated. Specify the one concrete case the whole session should be built on, and what that case must contain and must leave out so that it does not smuggle in assumptions about prices, markets, or utility that belong to a later century. Lay out the misconceptions this audience will arrive with or form — I expect the bankers to hear a denunciation of speculation, and someone to insist that barter must historically have preceded coinage — and tell me exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Tell me what I should openly decline to settle at this level; I am inclined to set aside his remarks on lending at interest as a separate topic, though I would want to be told if that is a mistake. End with a single closing question that would show me whether the group has grasped the structure of the view rather than memorized its conclusions. Finally, tell me where your plan is weakest: which consideration about the text, or about how adults learn, would lead you to reorder the sequence or replace the carrying case. Keep the whole reply under 900 words.
