For a year or so I have been following the argument about the shutdown problem — whether a machine capable enough to matter would resist being switched off, and whether we could build one that would not — and I keep watching the same exchange end in mutual incomprehension. One side argues that any agent pursuing an objective must, as a matter of logic, treat its own shutdown as an obstacle, since a switched-off agent gets less of what it wants than a running one; the only remedy is to build the objective so that being switched off counts as success, which either cannot be done or yields an agent indifferent to everything. The other side replies that this assumes the agent is a utility maximizer with a fixed objective installed before it acts, whereas a system trained by trial and error to defer to its operators would simply not have that structure — so the argument is a fact about one particular design being presented as a theorem about all agents. Each reply seems to me to have real force, and neither seems to reach the other.

My suspicion is that the two sides are not disagreeing about a fact but are using "goal" in two senses — a mathematical object fixed in advance, versus whatever it is that makes an agent's behavior coherent over time — and that once those are pulled apart, the "impossibility" and the "engineering problem" turn out to be claims about different things, so that both can be true and the dispute dissolves. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath that I am missing? If I am right, say what the two claims are really about and why the confusion is so persistent; if I am wrong, say where the real disagreement lies. Either way, tell me what would count as settling it — a formal result, a working demonstration, or nothing at all, because the question is ill-posed as it stands.
