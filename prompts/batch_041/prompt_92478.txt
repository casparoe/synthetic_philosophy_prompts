I'm not a philosopher — I trained as an engineer and now read about the mind on my own time — but I keep circling the question of what thinking is actually made of, and whether it is done in some inner code the way a computer runs a program. The idea that there is a language of thought, a "mentalese," appeals to me, but every time I read an argument for or against it I notice the two sides are quietly demanding different things of the theory, and that most of the disagreement is really about which demands are legitimate. So before I pick a side, I want the specification first. Please write me the desiderata: everything a theory of the format of thought would have to get right to count as adequate, with the case or consideration that motivates each requirement, and a clear separation between the requirements that are genuinely non-negotiable and those that are just the habits of one camp. I care mainly about adequacy for one purpose — explaining the thinking of a creature that reasons before it can speak, and of a machine that manipulates symbols — so judge the requirements against that target rather than against thought in the abstract.

Here is the rough list I've been accumulating, which I'd like you to sort, correct, and fill in. It has to explain how a finite thinker can entertain endlessly many novel thoughts. It has to explain why being able to think one thing seems to bring the ability to think some related thing with it (I can think that the dog is under the table, so I can think that the table is under the dog). It has to say how the parts of a thought contribute to the whole without pretending those parts are words of English. It cannot quietly assume the very concepts whose acquisition it is supposed to explain. It has to keep separate two things I keep seeing blurred — the content of a thought and whatever physical or formal vehicle carries it — and it has to stay consistent with the fact that creatures with no public language, and machines that shuffle symbols, appear to reason.

What I most want, though, is your judgment on three things: which of the requirements I've listed are genuine and which are smuggled-in assumptions; which requirements I've missed entirely; and — the part I care about most — whether any pair of the genuine requirements cannot be satisfied together, so that the list itself forces a choice and becomes a result rather than a checklist. If some combination is unsatisfiable, say which and why. Then, briefly, how the main families of view fare against your final list: the view that thought is carried by an inner code, the view that thought just is silent speech in a natural language, and the view that there is no inner language at all. I don't need a reading list or citations — just the requirements, the reasoning behind each, and your view of which single requirement does the most to rule candidate theories out.
