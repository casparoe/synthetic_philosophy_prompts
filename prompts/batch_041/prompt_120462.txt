I am a graduate student in a political philosophy seminar on distributive justice, and I am writing a term paper on the leveling-down objection. Over the semester my professor has been issuing verdicts, in passing, on pairs of possible futures, where each number in a distribution is one person's well-being (higher is better), the two options in a pair concern the same people, and her verdict is about which world is impersonally better rather than more just. She has been remarkably consistent. She says that (10, 50, 50) is a better world than (10, 20, 20), which is just its leveled-down version; that (8, 40, 40) is better than (8, 15, 15), even though the first is far less equal; that (18, 25, 25) is better than (10, 30, 30), even though its total is smaller; and that (10, 30, 30) is better than (10, 20, 40), even though the two have the same total and the same worst-off person. She tells us the leveling-down objection shows that equality cannot be a value in its own right, and she seems entirely unmoved by the thought that inequality above the worst-off position is bad in itself.

What I need is not a survey of the literature but a prediction and a diagnosis. The case I have been arguing about with a friend is this: a population of one hundred people in which a single person is at 10 and the other ninety-nine are at 60, versus a world in which that worst-off person is at 12 and the other ninety-nine are each at 58. I have been assuming my professor is some kind of prioritarian, but I cannot tell whether her priority for the worse-off is absolute — the sort of view that treats the worst-off person's level as lexically prior, so that any gain to that person settles the matter — or merely strong, so that a sufficiently large aggregate loss to others can outweigh a small gain to the worst-off.

Please tell me which candidate rules fit all four of her earlier verdicts, what each of those rules predicts about my hundred-person case, which verdict you would bet on and why, and which further case would do the most to separate the surviving rules from one another. I would also like to know what her verdicts so far do and do not pin down about how much a benefit to the worst-off counts for relative to losses to the better-off. Since I will have to defend this in a seminar without appealing to authorities, give me the reasoning rather than pointers to who has said what.
