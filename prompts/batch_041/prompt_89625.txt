For the past five months I have chaired the priority-setting panel of a small charitable trust that pays for a fixed number of intensive treatments each year. My father spent his final year in a pain I could not compare to anything I had felt, and I took the position because I wanted to understand what it means to say that one person's suffering is greater than another's. I have signed off on decisions that turn on exactly that, and I have not been able to give a clean account of the claim I was making.

Two of my colleagues have argued about this for a year. The first, a health economist, holds the measurement view: a statement of the form that A is worse off than B can be true or false, and our inability to measure it well is a practical limitation rather than a metaphysical one. The second, a philosopher, holds the construction view: such a statement is meaningful only relative to a scale or decision rule we have adopted, and adopting the scale is a normative act rather than a discovery. I suspect they agree on more than they admit and that at least one of their disputes is about words, but I cannot tell which.

The panel has been using a device I want the joint statement to be organized around. Call it the anchor ledger. Before an allocation, each patient is asked to place their current state relative to a fixed set of anchor vignettes, each described in plain, non-numerical terms: a year of unrelieved moderate pain; a month of severe nausea; the death of a close friend; the relief of a long-standing toothache; a week of ordinary contentment. The patient may place themselves anywhere relative to the anchors and may space the anchors however they like. The panel reads off an ordering and, when the patient permits, a rough magnitude. The economist treats the ledger as a crude measuring instrument that could be improved. The philosopher treats it as a constitutional convention dressed as a measurement, with the anchors manufacturing the numbers rather than revealing them. When we re-administered the ledger to the same patients after a mood manipulation, some placements moved by more than the differences between patients; both colleagues agree this fact matters, but they draw opposite lessons from it.

Write the joint statement the two of them could put their names to. It should have four parts.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what the ledger does and does not establish; what the logical structure of a comparison commits you to; what both sides accept about the roles of transitivity, interpersonal spacing, and the difference between knowing who is worse off and knowing by how much; and what both would count as genuine progress.

Second, a disagreements table. For each live disagreement, give the claim as each side would phrase it for itself, the issue restated in neutral words, and your judgment as to whether the dispute is substantive or merely verbal. Give each position its strongest formulation; where a position looks unattractive, do not let that be an artifact of how you have stated it.

Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect one is whether a comparison can be approximately correct without a prior agreed scale; another is whether the veil-of-ignorance device, in which you choose between two lives without knowing which would be yours, reveals a comparison or constitutes one; a third is whether willingness to pay can serve as a common currency or merely relocates the problem; and a fourth is whether intransitive ledger rankings show the instrument to be broken or the target to be indeterminate. For each crux, give a concrete case, test, or argument that both colleagues agree would bear on it, state in advance what each side predicts, and say what result each side would have to accept as evidence against its own position.

Fourth, a short section on what would change each colleague's mind and what would not, even if it occurred. Then add a brief note addressed to me rather than to them, saying which of their disagreements you believe is real, which is verbal, and where you think the truth lies.

Two constraints. Do not cite literature, supply a reading list, or name any author; I want the argument, not the bibliography, and the document must stand on its own for someone who has read nothing. And keep the two colleagues equally competent throughout: no straw men, no cheap wins, and no ending that merely restates the disagreement in a politer tone.
