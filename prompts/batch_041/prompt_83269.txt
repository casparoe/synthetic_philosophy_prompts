Two of my housemates got into an argument that I was unable to settle, and I would like you to referee it point by point. I had raised this case at dinner: my cousin, after nine hours on the road, could not tell whether she was merely tired or too tired to drive safely; she pulled over at the next exit and later said she had known she was too tired. Nina holds that if my cousin knew she was too tired, she must also have been in a position to know that she knew it — otherwise her knowledge was a fluke she could not vouch for, and we would be holding her responsible for acting on something she had no way of checking. Omar says that gets it backwards: knowing that she was too tired would require a margin over the nearest case in which she was not, and no one can detect their own margin, so at the moment she acted she was in a position to know neither that she was too tired nor that she knew it. He adds that this generalizes — no condition that comes in imperceptible steps, no degree of fatigue or shade of feeling, is one whose possession puts you in a position to know that you possess it. Nina replies that this is an artifact of the example: the same reasoning would show that my cousin could not know she was exhausted, or that she was in pain, which is absurd; and that the fact she pulled over is itself evidence that she did know. Please adjudicate: which of their moves are sound, where does each of them overreach, what is each of them missing, and who has the better of the argument overall?
