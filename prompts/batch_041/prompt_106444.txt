I am a graduate teaching assistant for an introductory course on rational choice and decision theory, and I am preparing a handout on the Allais paradox for students who have just seen the expected utility representation theorem. In the standard pair of choices, most people prefer a sure million to a lottery that gives an eighty-nine percent chance at a million, a ten percent chance at five million, and a one percent chance at nothing; yet in the second pair they prefer a ten percent chance at five million with a ninety percent chance at nothing to an eleven percent chance at a million with an eighty-nine percent chance at nothing. The instructor says this pattern violates the independence axiom and therefore shows that expected utility theory is empirically false. My question is what a student should conclude from that. Since independence is not a testable hypothesis about how people happen to behave but a consistency requirement on preferences -- if you prefer one lottery to another, adding the same independent chance to both should not reverse the preference -- is the Allais pattern simply a logical mistake, on the order of a straightforward inconsistency like preferring A to B while also preferring B to A? And if it is, should the handout tell students that the only rational choice is the one with the higher expected monetary value, so that anyone who takes the sure million and then the ten percent shot has been tricked by framing?

A student pushed back on this in office hours: she said that taking the sure million might be a reasonable form of risk aversion rather than an inconsistency, and another student confused the case with the Ellsberg paradox and said it shows people dislike ambiguous probabilities. A third classmate claims the whole thing is an artifact of describing the outcomes as gains rather than as final wealth, and that if the options were presented as total wealth levels the inconsistency would disappear. I want to know what the Allais choices actually establish, whether the independence axiom is a logical truth or a substantive normative postulate, how the sure-thing principle and independence are related, and what the strongest defenses of the apparently inconsistent choices say. Please give me the distinctions I need for the handout, including the difference between the descriptive failure of expected utility theory and the normative question of whether the choices are irrational, and tell me where the student who wants to know whether her own choices were irrational should come out.
