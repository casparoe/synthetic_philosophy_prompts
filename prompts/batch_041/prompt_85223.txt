I've been going around in circles about vagueness for weeks and I want to know if there's a single story that fits what I actually think, or if I'm just of two minds and have to pick. Not asking who said what — just the principle. Here's my gut on a pile of cases.

1. Bald, the usual forced march. Full head of hair, one hair removed at a time. At every single step I think there's a fact about whether the guy is bald. Not a fact we could ever discover, and not one the community gets to settle by vote — just a fact, fixed by what the word means plus how many hairs are on his head. The idea that at some point he's neither bald nor not-bald strikes me as a cop-out. On this one I'm a hidden-line person.

2. Blue drifting to turquoise, a chip at a time. Here I'm sure there's no hidden line. Some chips really are neither, and I don't think that's us failing to know something; I think the world just hasn't decided, and thats fine, the words still work.

3. Rude. Whether borrowing a colleague's mug without asking was, on that particular Tuesday, rude. I think there's no fact of the matter. But I also want to say "either it was rude or it wasn't" is true, which is odd, because neither half of it is.

4. Tall. Give me the comparison class and a threshold — tall for a twelve-year-old gymnast, cut-off 150cm — and my sense that there's no fact mostly evaporates. So a lot of what I call vagueness might really be me not knowing what I'm comparing to.

5. The cloud. Cloud in the sky, made of droplets, no single droplet is the cloud, and there are lots of overlapping bunches of droplets that would each look exactly like the cloud from the ground. I don't think there's a fact about which bunch is the cloud. But it doesn't feel like the same kind of gap as case 1 — nobody's failing to know anything.

6. Just to pin 1 down: a man with 4,872 hairs. I think he's either bald or not and I'd bet on it — but if you showed me the actual line I'd find it absurd that one hair flips it.

7. And whatever line there is, I don't think there's a sharp line between the clearly bald and the borderline ones. The edge of the edge is fuzzy too. I can't tell if that's a deep point or me being confused.

My tentative rule, which I dont trust: if the word tracks a quantity you could in principle measure (hairs, nanometres of wavelength), there's a hidden line and we just can't see it (1, 6). If it tracks a loose bundle of features or a social convension (rudeness, sandwichhood, being a game), there's genuinely no fact (3, 5). Case 2 wrecks it, since colour is as measurable as anything and yet I'm sure there's no line. Case 4 makes me think half of this is just context. And I have no idea where 7 fits.

So: is there a principle that gets 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6, and if so what does it have to say about 4 and 7 — or does it just have to drop one of my verdicts? If the answer splits vagueness into two kinds, say exactly what the difference is supposed to be, because measurable-vs-social obviously isn't it. Also tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't given you: is a slowly cooling cup of coffee 'hot', and is someone who's been asleep for forty seconds 'awake'. And be honest about which of my seven verdicts it can't keep.
