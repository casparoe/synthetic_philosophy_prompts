For about a year now, a reading group I belong to — half philosophy graduate students, half people from an evolutionary anthropology lab — has been circling the evolutionary debunking argument against moral realism, and I have come to think that the stall itself is more interesting than either side's position. The argument, in the form we keep rehearsing, is simple enough: our moral beliefs are substantially shaped by natural selection; selection favored beliefs that promoted cooperation and reproductive success rather than beliefs that latched onto independent moral truths; therefore those beliefs are unjustified, or at least do not amount to knowledge, even if moral facts exist. The philosophers in the room grant the empirical premise for the sake of argument and then split over the epistemic step. Some hold that a genealogy undermines a belief only if it shows the belief-forming process to be unreliable, and that the debunker therefore owes a counterfactual or tracking claim that no empirical evidence could establish; others hold that the genealogy does its work simply by showing the belief would have been the same whether or not it was true, so that it fails a modal condition on knowledge regardless of its truth. Meanwhile the anthropologists keep producing evidence that moral norms covary with ecology, subsistence mode, and the intensity of intergroup competition, and the philosophers keep replying, in effect, so what? After a year I genuinely cannot tell whether we are having one disagreement or three.

My own hypothesis — which I would like you to test rather than assume — is that this is not a disagreement about the empirical facts, nor merely about the strength of an inference, but about what a debunking is supposed to be directed at. One camp treats the target as the belief's truth (does the causal history make the belief probably false?); another treats the target as the believer's entitlement (does the history strip her of the right to rely on the belief even if it is true?); a third treats the target as the belief's modal profile (would she have believed it anyway?). If that is right, the debate looks stalled partly because each camp keeps answering a question the others are not asking, so that much of the apparent clash is verbal — though I suspect a residue of genuinely unargued disagreement survives about whether justification is etiological at all, that is, whether how one came by a belief can bear on whether one may hold it. One of the anthropologists, impatient with all of this, has proposed a test case that has become our white whale: a lineage whose members are selected to believe that abandoning the weak is permissible, in an environment where that belief really does maximize inclusive fitness, and who inhabit a world in which the moral facts, if there are any, run the other way. She wants to know which side that case is supposed to embarrass, and none of us can agree — which I take to be a symptom of the deeper confusion rather than a defect in the case.

Please diagnose the dispute as carefully as you can. Is it verbal, a hidden empirical or formal question, two or three different questions trading under one heading, or a bedrock disagreement about what epistemic justification is and whether a belief's pedigree is even the right kind of thing to bear on its standing? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says and why it says it, not in a survey of positions, and tell me where my hypothesis is wrong or incomplete. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a piece of evidence, a reformulation of the question — or argue that nothing should be expected to move it, and why.
