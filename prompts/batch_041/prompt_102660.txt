I recently moved from a statistics doctorate into a forecasting role at a small policy-research group, and the transition has forced me to notice how much of my working philosophy of probability I had never actually made explicit. Before I have to defend my practice to colleagues from other backgrounds, I want to know whether the views I have drifted into are mutually consistent, and I would rather you audit them than explain the textbook positions to me.

Here, as plainly as I can put them, are the four things I find myself believing. First, that objective chances are real features of the world and are best understood as propensities: the chance that a given radium atom decays within the hour is a genuine single-case disposition of that particular physical setup, not merely a fact about a reference class of atoms. Second, that when an agent knows the objective chance of an event, her credence in that event ought simply to equal that chance — that to know a chance is to know how confident to be. Third, that the probability belonging to scientific inference is frequency within a repeatable collective, and that importing prior probabilities into statistical practice is a subjectivist concession that quietly undermines the objectivity of the method. Fourth, that for a genuinely unique event — say, whether a particular fiscal decision will be vindicated by history — there is no objective probability at all, so any credence one forms is an expression of opinion rather than a grasp of a fact.

What I would like is a consistency audit of this package. Tell me which of the four commitments are in genuine tension with one another, which only appear to conflict and why the appearance is misleading, and, for each genuine tension, how severe it is and what the least costly repair would be — that is, which commitment I would have to abandon or weaken, and what I would forfeit by doing so. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as uniformly bad, and be explicit about which commitment is doing the work that renders another untenable. I am not attached to any one of the four in particular; I only want to be told where the load-bearing contradiction lies, and whether resolving the most obvious conflict leaves a residual one standing.
