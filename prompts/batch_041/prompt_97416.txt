I am not a philosopher by training and have no access to a university library, but I have been reading about the philosophy of mind on my own for a couple of years and have written an essay I would like you to comment on. I will paste the whole thing below. It is short, and I would rather have hard criticism than encouragement.

The essay is called "Not Everything You Think With Is Part of Your Mind." Its thesis is that the extended mind thesis is right about memory and wrong about judgment. Here is the argument as I have written it.

The standard example is the one about Otto, who has Alzheimer's and carries a notebook with his directions in it, and Inga, who simply remembers the way. Clark and Chalmers argue that if a resource is always at hand, trusted, and used without hesitation, it belongs to the cognitive system just as much as a brain process does, so Otto's notebook is part of his mind. I accept this for the case as given. My complaint is that the case quietly runs together two different things: a resource that holds what I would otherwise have had to remember, and a resource that decides what I would otherwise have had to work out. The first is storage. The second is judgment, and judgment is not storage.

My claim is that storage can be moved outside the head without any real loss, but judgment cannot be externalized in the same way. Nothing about who I am requires that the map be inside my skull; a map in my pocket is still my map, and there is no leftover mental act that I am failing to perform. Judgment is different, because judgment just is the activity of weighing reasons, and if something else does the weighing, then the weighing is not mine — it is the tool's, and I am only the one who accepts the result. So the notebook extends Otto's memory; a chess engine that tells me the move does not extend my chess judgment, it replaces it.

To make this more than a feeling, I propose what I call the disagreement test. Ask what happens when you and the tool part ways. If the tool produces X and you produce Y, and you treat X as the answer and Y as a nuisance to be corrected, then the tool is doing the judging. If you treat Y as the answer and X as a suggestion to be overruled, then you are judging and the tool is only a display. On this test the notebook passes, because a notebook never disagrees with you; it only reminds you of what you already endorsed. The chess engine fails, because in any real game you defer to it. I apply the test to a few other cases. A shared family calendar looks at first like one extended memory, but because several people can edit it and none of them fully endorses the others' entries, I think it is better described as a small institution than as anybody's mind. Phone search extends me when it retrieves something I already meant to look up, and does not extend me when the autocomplete finishes my sentence and I simply keep the sentence.

The objection I cannot get past is this. Maybe I have just redefined "extension" to mean the part I still do myself, in which case my thesis is true by definition and says nothing. If the disagreement test is really a test of who is in charge rather than of what belongs to the mind, then I have smuggled in a claim about ownership and dressed it up as a claim about cognition. I do not know how to tell whether the distinction between storage and judgment is a real joint in nature or only a line I drew because it suits my conclusion.

The consequence I care about is responsibility. If I have delegated judgment rather than extended it, then a decision I reach by deferring to a tool is not fully mine, and that is either an excuse I should not be allowed to use or a warning I should be made to heed, depending on how the argument goes.

What I want from you is not a reading list and not citations; I can look things up later and I would rather you engage with the argument as it stands. I would like your comments on four things, in order of importance to me. First, is the storage-versus-judgment distinction a genuine distinction, or have I just described the difference between a tool that constrains an answer and a tool that supplies one? Second, does the disagreement test actually do the work I ask of it, or is it circular — does it already assume the conclusion about ownership? Third, am I reinventing something that people in this debate settled long ago, and if so, what is the objection I have not seen coming? Fourth, is any of this interesting to someone who already knows the literature, or is the whole essay a restatement of the standard position with new labels? Please be specific about which sentences are doing real work and which are padding, and tell me plainly where the argument fails.
