My reading group has been stuck on procreative beneficence for about four months and I'd like an outside diagnosis instead of another round of the same arguing. The principle, as Savulescu and Kahane lay it out, is roughly that couples choosing between possible children have a reason — they say an obligation, not just a permission — to pick the one expected to have the best life, or the best chance at the best life, and it was never restricted to disease: any trait that makes a life go better counts, memory and intelligence included, on the idea that these are all-purpose means to whatever the kid ends up wanting. On the defenders' side you get the move that refusing to choose is itself a choice with consequences, that the non-identity problem means the unselected child isn't harmed, and that if you already accept the uncontroversial thought that we have reasons to make things go better then PB just falls out of it. On the critics' side you get the disability and expressivist crowd saying that selecting against a trait — deafness is the example everybody reaches for, with the signing community and all — expresses that lives with that trait are worth less, and that no amount of welfare arithmetic answers that; and you get the people who just call the whole thing liberal eugenics and mean it as a charge, with the defenders replying that a non-coercive eugenics aimed at the child's own good is just parenting with better tools. Fifteen-odd years of papers and neither side has conceded a thing; each thinks the other is either confused or morally obtuse.

Here's my hypothesis and I'd like you to either sharpen it or knock it down. I think these are two different questions wearing one name. PB is comparative and welfarist and it's about the child who gets selected — is this one better off than that one. The expressivist objection isn't comparative and isn't about the selected child's welfare at all; it's about what the practice means and what it does to people who already have the trait. If that's right then the defenders keep proving that nobody is harmed while the critics keep proving that the practice demeans, and each argument isn't even addressed to the other's question, which would make the stalemate look less like a hard problem and more like a category slip. But I dont fully buy my own story, because there seem to be real disagreements underneath it too: about whether the best life is a welfarist notion at all, about whether a reason to prefer an outcome is a reason to bring it about, about whether PB is a duty or merely a permission, and about that alternative one of us keeps pushing, which he calls procreative altruism, where the reason to select isnt the selected child's own good but the effect on everyone else who has to live alongside them. So: lay out the actual positions and where they really bite, then tell me what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, about facts, about what counts as a moral reason, or just two sets of starting intuitions neither side can argue for. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, a counterexample, a concession about the scope of the principle — or whether the honest answer is that nothing will move it because the thing was never one question to begin with. I dont want a survey of the literature or a verdict on who's right; I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it.
