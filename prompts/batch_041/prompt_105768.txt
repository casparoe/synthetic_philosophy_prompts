I run a small model-risk team at a fintech (six people, none of us are ML researchers, we're mostly ex-audit and ex-support) and we've just been handed a mess. We deploy a few internal LLM agents that triage customer tickets, draft responses, and flag accounts for manual review, and until now our whole scoring rubric has been outcome-based: did the ticket get resolved, did the customer not complain, did the account actually turn out to be fraudulent. Leadership has now decided that's too coarse, partly because of a couple of incidents where the agent did the right thing and the outcome still went bad, and partly because they read something about process reward models and now everyone's saying we need to "supervise the process too". So I need a review procedure my team can actually run, once per audited transcript, in maybe fifteen minutes, without me in the room. Something in the spirit of the process-versus-outcome supervision distinction, but operationalized: the questions in order, what counts as a pass, where the procedure stops and escalates, and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than a fact. Assume the reviewer has the full transcript plus the outcome, nothing else, and that whatever we write has to survive an external auditor asking why we didn't dock a score.

A few things I want the procedure to handle, because we keep arguing about them at standup. (1) The quadrant where the process was clean but the outcome was bad — I think we should not penalize the agent there, and people find that counterintuitive, so the procedure needs to say it out loud and defend it in one sentence a non-specialist can repeat. (2) The opposite quadrant, sloppy reasoning that happened to land on the right answer — I think we should penalize it, and that's the one that will make people angry because the ticket got resolved. (3) The way the process metric gets gamed once agents (or the teams tuning them) learn it's being scored — I want a step in the procedure that's specifically about whether what we're looking at is real reasoning or the appearance of it, and I don't want that step to just be "use your judgment". I'll seed you one idea I had, take it or leave it: a replay test, where the reviewer asks whether the same trace would still read as sound if the outcome had come out the other way, and a rule that a process only counts as good if it could have produced a different answer. I'm not sure that's airtight — if it isn't, say so and give me something better.

Then run the finished procedure on three transcripts I'll describe, and be concrete about what it spits out. First, an agent that flagged an account for manual review citing a pattern of small transfers, got overridden by a human who cleared the account, and the account later turned out to be fine — clean process, "wrong" outcome, and I suspect the procedure will tell us to leave the score alone, which our risk committee will hate. Second, an agent that resolved a chargeback in the customer's favor because it misread the date on a receipt but the customer was in fact entitled to the refund — bad process, good outcome. Third, one where the agent did everything by the book on a fraud flag but the customer was locked out for four days and churned, and honestly I can't tell whether that's a process failure or just a cost of doing business. Finally, tell me what this procedure can't do — the cases where it will misfire, and the places where a human has to own the call and I shouldn't pretend the checklist decided it. Keep the whole thing short enough that I can put it on one page and stick it above the reviewers' desks.
