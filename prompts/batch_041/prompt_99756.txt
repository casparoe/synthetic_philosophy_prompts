I want a satire in the form of a syndicated advice column, titled Dear Impartial Spectator, whose replies are written strictly out of Adam Smith's moral psychology in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and whose running joke is that this apparatus, honestly applied, keeps threatening to become a rubber stamp for whatever the letter-writer's neighbours already think. Roughly 700 to 800 words: a one-line editor's note, then three letters with three replies, in the deadpan, unfailingly courteous register of a newspaper agony column. The letters should be ordinary modern dilemmas — a colleague takes credit for the writer's work; a mass-casualty disaster abroad that the writer cannot make herself feel anything about; a large donation given mostly for the look of it — and each reply must be derivable from Smith's own machinery rather than from modern ethics: sympathy as imagined fellow-feeling rather than pity; propriety as the concord between a sentiment and what an uninvolved onlooker could enter into; the impartial spectator as the man within the breast, distinct both from self-interest and from the actual people looking on; the difference between being praised and being praiseworthy; and self-command as what lets someone hold to the spectator's verdict when the crowd dissents. The difficulty the column must never quite solve, though the Spectator should be visibly straining against it, is that this inner spectator is itself learned from the real spectators a person grew up among, so that letter after letter exposes a reader whose impartial spectator is merely their firm, their family, or their class, and the Spectator has to keep distinguishing the man within the breast from the man without without reaching for a decision procedure or for a providential Author of nature. At least one reply should concede that a reader's spectator has been badly formed and say what can still be done about it. Keep the satire fair: it targets the practice of dressing local opinion up as the verdict of conscience, not Smith, whose own account of how our moral sentiments are corrupted by admiration for the rich and the great is part of what makes the joke work. A reader who has never opened Smith should be able to follow the column; a reader who has should find nothing in it misstated. Do not quote him or cite chapters — the piece has to stand on its own.
