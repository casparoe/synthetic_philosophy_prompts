I teach an upper-level seminar in ethics and public policy — sixteen students, mostly philosophy and public-policy majors, one ninety-minute session a week — and I keep hitting the same wall. When I lay out the case that compromise between people with different value systems can be a mutual gain rather than a mutual dilution, they follow the argument and agree with it in the abstract; a week later they are back to treating every compromise as a defeat for whoever conceded, and treating the person who traded as the one who lost something. What I want is a session in which the group talks its way into a package that is better for every single one of them by their own values, and that still feels like a betrayal to several of them, so that the resistance is something they lived through rather than something I told them about. They should not hear the terms of art (gains from trade, logrolling, taboo trade-offs) until the debrief, and nothing should be explained up front.

Here is the shape I have in mind, though I would be glad to have it improved. Cast the students as sixteen constituencies negotiating a single policy package over six ballot items, each item with three options, each role holding a private payoff card with points for each option and different weights across items and across roles, so the trade is real rather than hypothetical. I want two currencies in play and not one: the points on the card, and a public pledge that a few constituencies have made, which costs nothing on the card but would have to be broken in front of everyone if they accept the relevant option. Build the preference profile so that no single package gives every role its top priority; so that issue-by-issue majority voting produces a package that some available logrolled package strictly beats for every participant (I want a strict Pareto improvement, not merely a majority gain); and so that the improvement is reachable only if nearly everyone votes, at least once, against a position they actually hold. The point I want them to feel in their hands is that the trade requires no agreement about values and no common measure of them — only that each party can rank whole packages by its own lights and be given a justification in its own terms — so I would like the trading phase to force exactly that: every role must state, in one sentence and in its own value terms, why it is voting as it does, and the sentence must be one it could defend to the constituency it represents. Tag four or five roles as delegates, bound not to accept an outcome contrary to their constituency's flagship commitment though free to trade elsewhere, and the rest as trustees free to trade anything if the package is better for their people overall; include a role whose preferences are nearly flat, so the group discovers that a trade needs an intensity difference to trade against, and put one pledge item on precisely the option the logroll requires, so that the Pareto improvement gets blocked by a refusal that costs the refuser points.

Please give me the rules and materials (role cards, ballot, tally sheets, a one-page facilitator script), the six items with their three options each, and a payoff scheme worked out in enough detail that I can see why the majority package loses and the logroll wins, together with the checks I should run the night before — that the majority package is not Pareto optimal, and that the roles do not line up on a single left-right axis, since if they do the second phase resolves everything and there is nothing to feel. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes across the three phases (silent ideal ballots and the announcement that no ideal package can be chosen; debate and issue-by-issue majority vote with trades forbidden; then trades permitted under the public-justification rule), and a debrief that maps each phase onto the structure of the idea: what the trading phase reveals about what gains from trade do and do not require; what the pledge item reveals about costs that sit outside the payoff matrix, and about whether the refuser's refusal is best modelled as a very high price or as a constraint that has no price, with what follows for the trade picture in each case; and what the final tally reveals about the difference between a compromise and an exchange. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — my guess is that they will argue the merits and never open the cards, and that someone will pool everyone's payoffs and turn the whole thing into a welfare-maximization exercise, which would kill exactly what I am after — and be frank about which features of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I still have to supply myself in the last fifteen minutes.
