Every spring our hospice holds a remembrance evening for the families of everyone who died on the inpatient unit that year, and this year they've asked me to give the short address. I'm one of the doctors there, and I've spent most of my working life on the doing/allowing problem, and I've landed somewhere I can't just set down for an evening: I don't think the line between "we stopped the treatment" and "we let him die" is a line in the world. On our unit, keeping someone on the vent is something we are doing, every minute, so "we didn't do anything" is usually just false; and whether the ventilator or the failing lungs count as the background is something we pick, not something we find. The room will be families who withdrew treatment, families who signed a DNR and did nothing else, families who kept everything going to the last hour, and our chaplain, who speaks before me and will say, warmly and sincerely, that nobody in this room killed anybody. I can't contradict him from the podium. Also one of our night nurses will be there who has spent a year convinced that the last dose she pushed is what ended it, and I can't comfort her by telling her it wasn't a killing, because that's the exact thing I don't believe.

Write the address. Five minutes, plain words, no trolleys, no names of philosophers, and nothing I'd consider false -- so no "the disease did it, not you," and no "it makes no difference where the line falls either." They should leave comforted by something true. If there's no honest version that does that, say so and give me the least-bad honest one.
