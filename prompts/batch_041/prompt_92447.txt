So I've been noticing something in the nudge literature and in actual policy that I can't find a good handle on, and I want to know if it already has a name or if we need to make one up.

Here's the shape of it. Somebody sets a default in your favor — enrol you in the pension plan, put you down for the generic drug, license your papers open-access by default, turn on the data sharing when you set up the phone — and they defend it in exactly the standard way: nobody's forcing you, the option to do otherwise is right there, so this isn't paternalism. Fine. But then you look at the option, and it turns out you can only exercise it once, at one particular moment, usually before you know anything about what's actually at stake. You get a thirty-day window after you're hired and then the window shuts; after that the only way out is to petition a committee, or wait for the next annual enrolment, or quit. And the person who didn't opt out in that window is now treated as having chosen the arrangement for good — their one-time non-decision gets recycled as ongoing consent, every year, forever. I keep running into this and I don't think "nudge", "libertarian paternalism" or "soft paternalism" quite captures it, because all of those are about whether the option exists, and my complaint isn't that it doesn't exist, it's about when it exists and whether it can be exercised again. I know "sludge" is in the neighbourhood, but sludge is about how much friction there is, and here the friction can be near zero — it's the timing and the irreversibility that does the work, not the effort. Same with Ulysses contracts, except those are cases where I bind my future self on purpose and for my own good; this is someone else using a single moment of mine as a mandate over all the later me's, most of whom weren't consulted.

Is there an established term for this? If there is, I want to know exactly how it's supposed to be distinguished from libertarian paternalism, from sludge, from Hirschman-style exit, and from ordinary self-binding — and which of those it's really closest to. And if there isn't one, coin it, define it, and tell me where its edges are. Specifically: does the phenomenon require irreversibility, or is a reversible-but-costly opt-out the same thing? Does it matter whether the default happens to be what most people would pick anyway — i.e., is the wrong, if it's a wrong, about the mechanism or about the outcome? And is the real culprit the one-shot structure, or the fact that the initial choice is made before the chooser has any idea what they're choosing about? A bare label won't help me, I need the carving.
