I want an essay on the Lockheed bribery scandals and what they show about whether corruption is one thing or a family of locally-defined things. Quick refresher on the facts, since you won't be able to look them up: through the 60s and early 70s Lockheed paid out something like $20-odd million to officials and middlemen abroad to move aircraft -- F-104s to West Germany, C-130s to Italy, Tristars to All Nippon Airways in Japan, and payments that reached Prince Bernhard in the Netherlands. It surfaced in US Senate hearings in the mid-70s. In Japan it led to Kakuei Tanaka's arrest and trial; in Italy it helped force President Leone out; in the Netherlands a government inquiry found Bernhard had accepted improper gifts and he gave up his business and military posts; the German side is the murkiest, with a Bundestag inquiry that ran for over a year and mostly dead-ended after it turned out the relevant ministry files had been destroyed back in 1962. The US then criminalized paying foreign officials with the 1977 Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, and it took until the late 90s for the OECD anti-bribery convention to get the rest of the rich world to follow -- before that, in several European countries you could write off payments to foreign officials as ordinary business expenses.

Now run that through the theory. Work with the standard competing definitions of corruption -- abuse of public office, corruption as a market for influence, corruption as betrayal of public interest -- plus the principal-agent vs collective-action vs social-exchange/clientelist readings of what's actually happening when an official takes money. Map the case onto each: who's the principal, what's the office, is Kodama a bribe-taker or a paid consultant in a system where influence is a traded commodity, is a Dutch prince taking gifts a corrupt official or just a friend of industry in a country that drew the line somewhere else. Say what each reading explains and where it goes silent -- I suspect the German dead-end is where several of them have nothing to say, but tell me.

The question I actually want answered: is the it's-just-how-business-is-done-there defense a description of a different moral world or an excuse, and does the move from unilateral US law to a multilateral convention vindicate the universalist reading of corruption, or just show that the universal standard happened to be the one the hegemon could enforce? Reach a verdict, don't hedge, and say what would move you off it. Then tell me what the episode shows about the theory itself -- are these definitions picking out a natural kind, or carving the world along the lines of whoever's writing the statute?
