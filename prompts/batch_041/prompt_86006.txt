I have two years of postdoctoral research time, no teaching obligations, and a small budget to bring in one visitor for a term, and I intend to spend it on the paradox of phenomenal judgment: the difficulty of explaining how a creature could come to judge, report, or know anything about its experiences if those experiences themselves do no causal work. I want a research agenda, not a survey. Decide what I should work on, in what order, and defend the ordering on grounds I can check.

I have found it useful to distinguish three versions of the difficulty, and I would like the agenda organized around them, though I am open to being told that the distinction is the wrong one.

The production version: how could an experience that does no causal work be responsible for the judgment about it, and what is actually lost if we say that the judgment is produced by the neural state that also produces the experience?

The aboutness version: can a judgment be about a property that plays no part in bringing that judgment about, or does a judgment's content depend on the causal or informational contribution of what it is about?

The warrant version: granting that the judgment is produced in the ordinary way and is about what it seems to be about, can it amount to knowledge, or is it at best a reliably produced true belief for which the subject has no justification?

Two considerations should constrain the ranking. First, an epiphenomenalist and an interactionist predict exactly the same judgments, reports, and introspective evidence; the paradox therefore cannot be run as an argument for interactionism, and whatever the epiphenomenalist loses, she loses somewhere other than in the predictions. I want the ordering to reflect where you think the real cost falls, and I want you to say plainly if you think it falls nowhere, so that the paradox is an artifact of a causal requirement on knowledge or on content that we should simply abandon. Second, I care about upstreamness far more than about intrinsic interest: I would rather spend two years on a question that the others turn on than on the most gripping question in the area. I am also unsure whether the primary object of explanation should be the judgment itself or the felt obviousness of the problem, and whether settling that is upstream of everything else or a distraction; place it rather than assume it.

Give me a ranked list of four to six items, and for each tell me why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone working from an armchair rather than a laboratory, how neglected it is, and what a result over the next two years would look like that would move it up or down the list. Then tell me which of the questions you considered are red herrings, which one, if settled, would make the rest largely moot, and what would change the ordering. If the three-way split I have proposed distorts the terrain, say so and rebuild the agenda on a carving you prefer. I am not looking for a reading list; I am looking for the argument for the ordering.
