I hold a strong version of the coherence argument for expected utility maximization, and I want an honest map of its exposure rather than a survey of the literature.

The position, stated flatly: an agent counts as practically rational only if her preferences over prospects admit a representation as a complete, transitive, and suitably continuous ordering, with her choices maximizing expected utility relative to some probability and utility assignment. What makes this a requirement rather than a convenience is exploitability. If your preferences are incoherent — a cycle, a violation of the independence condition, a set of pairwise choices with no coherent completion — then there exists a sequence of transactions, each of which you prefer to the position you are in just before it, that leaves you strictly worse off than you began. The pump is not a rhetorical flourish attached to the real argument; on this view it is the whole normative content of the requirement. If an incoherence cannot be pumped, it is not irrational.

I am not asking for the standard list of objections. I want to know which of them a committed defender should actually concede, ranked by how much of the position each one removes. To make that tractable, please work with two distinctions I have drawn, and tell me whether they survive scrutiny or turn out to be unstable.

First, separate two senses of exploitability. In the adversarial sense, some other agent designs a sequence that you will accept. In the self-pumping sense, no adversary is needed: your own dispositions carry you around a cycle, or into a dominated position, without anyone arranging it. Which sense does the argument need, which sense does it actually establish, and does the difference change the verdict about rationality? My suspicion is that the adversarial version proves less than it is asked to prove, since a pump is only a pump if the agent keeps paying, and an agent may simply decline the first trade, or decline to treat the sequence as a single object of choice.

Second, separate global from local coherence. Suppose an agent's preferences are coherent over every option she will ever actually confront, but incoherent over the wider space of conceivable options. Is that agent irrational? If not, the coherence requirement is doing considerably less work than its defenders claim.

With those in hand, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I would like at least these considered: the resolute-choice response, and whether it saves the agent's rationality or merely relocates the irrationality; incomplete preferences that never trade and are therefore never pumped; intransitive indifference and just-noticeable differences, where transitivity may be an idealization no finite agent can satisfy; the possibility that the pump covertly requires an independent irrationality, such as foresight failure or an inability to commit, so that it exploits only agents who are already irrational for reasons having nothing to do with coherence; and cases like ambiguity aversion, where the supposedly incoherent choice looks defensible on reflection and the defender is forced to call a great many careful people irrational.

Finally, the question I care most about. Is the position falsifiable? What observation, proof, or case would make a defender abandon the claim that coherence is required because incoherence is exploitable? If nothing would, say whether that is a defect of the position or simply what a normative claim of this kind looks like. And consider the standard retreat — that coherence is a requirement on representation rather than on rationality, so a violation shows only that no utility function exists — and tell me whether that leaves the view with any content that its rivals lack.
