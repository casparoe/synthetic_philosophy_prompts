I'm in a philosophy of physics course this term, and last week the lecturer gave us his bird's-eye summary of the measurement problem and of the many-worlds interpretation (the view associated with Everett). I took it down as faithfully as I could, and I would like to know how much of it I should actually believe. Here is the gist: 'On many-worlds, the Schrödinger equation is the whole story. The wavefunction never collapses, and whenever a measurement occurs the world branches, one branch for each possible outcome. So the theory is fully deterministic, and all the apparent randomness is in us: when you and I end up in different branches, neither of us knew in advance which branch he would turn out to be in, and the probabilities we quote are simply our ignorance about which branch we occupy. That is why many-worlds does not need the Born rule as a separate postulate — once you grant that all the branches are equally real, branch counting and ordinary probability theory deliver the familiar weights. And since decoherence explains why the branches stop interfering with one another, the measurement problem is genuinely solved: no collapse, no hidden variables, and no special role for observers.'

I have been repeating this to friends as though it were settled, and one of them, who is a physicist, told me it is mostly right but sloppy in a way that matters — which is not an answer I can do anything with. So I would like a careful audit. Go through it claim by claim, and tell me which parts a working proponent of many-worlds would endorse without qualification, which parts are common simplifications that a proponent would want to restate more carefully, and which parts are straightforwardly wrong or at least not supported by anything in the interpretation itself. I am especially interested in the probability claim, since that is the part I find most surprising: I gather that serious attempts to derive the Born rule from the formalism exist and are contested, and I want to know whether my lecturer's version is a fair precis of the best version of that project or a caricature of it — and in particular whether the move from 'all branches are equally real' to 'the familiar weights follow' is a step anyone has actually made good on.

Finally, tell me whether the closing verdict — that the measurement problem is solved, so there is nothing left to worry about — follows from the accurate parts of the summary, or whether it is doing work the rest of the summary does not license. Where you think a proponent and a critic would simply talk past each other, say so, rather than resolving the dispute for me.
