the two positions, as concretely as i can state them:

classical person: for any definite proposition A, "A or not-A" is just true. denying it is a weird self-imposed handicap, you're giving up perfectly good reasoning for no gain. and "not not A implies A" is obviously fine for anything with a determinate truth value.

intuitionist person: a proof of "A or B" that doesn't hand me a proof of one disjunct or the other isn't a proof i can do anything with, its just a promissory note. same with "not not A" -- if it doesn't convert into an actual construction of an A, it's not a proof of A. and i don't think math is a domain of pre-existing facts sitting there with determinate truth values that our proofs just report on.

here's my hypothesis, and i want you to tell me if its right or if im fooling myself. i think this is a "two questions under one name" situation and mostly a verbal disagreement, in the taboo-the-word sense. the classicist is answering "what is true of a fixed domain of determinate facts," the intuitionist is answering "what does a construction entitle me to assert," and the words "or", "not", "exists", "implies" are just doing different jobs in the two games. so nobody is wrong about LEM, the word "or" has two roles and the two sides keep grabbing different ones. a friend pushed back hard and said no, this is substantive all the way down -- it's really a metaphysical fight about whether mathematical objects exist independently of our constructions, and the logic just falls out of whichever way you answer that. and a third person said the real crux is the conditional, that the two sides agree on the elimination rules and split on what counts as a proof of an implication, so no amount of staring at the derivations will ever settle it.
