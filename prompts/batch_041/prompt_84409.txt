Hey — I'm deep in a chapter on whether the special sciences' disposition-talk (resistance, robustness, plasticity, "capacity to self-repair") is anything more than shorthand, and I keep discovering I don't actually have a clear spec of what a theory of dispositions is even supposed to deliver. Everyone in the literature picks a favourite case, builds a theory that handles it, and declares victory, and I'm supposed to grade the lot of them in chapter 3. So before I do: give me the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of dispositions have to get right?

Not platitudes — the actual constraints, with the case behind each one. To be concrete, here are the cases I care about, a few of which I've been cooking up myself and haven't seen anyone discuss:

- the boring baseline: a glass is fragile, sugar is soluble. Anything that can't even do these is out.
- the anti-case family: the glass struck inside a machine that strips its fragility a split second before impact, so it doesn't break, though intuitively it's still fragile; the sugar with a coating that blocks dissolving; the rubber ball with a hidden charge that shatters when dropped, so it looks fragile but isn't. Plus a variant I've been chewing on: a vase rigged so that the instant anything touches it, it's vaporised and swapped for a look-alike made of tough plastic. Nothing ever breaks. The original never stops being fragile — it just doesn't survive long enough to matter. Does a theory have to say the vase is fragile? Does it have to say the counterfactual 'if struck, it would break' is false? Can it say both, or is that the whole game right there?
- a never-manifests case: an enzyme whose substrate, whenever it turns up, binds in a way that jams the active site, so catalysis is physically impossible under every circumstance. Biochemists will cheerfully call it catalytically active but inhibited. Should the theory call it a disposition at all, and if so what content does the ascription have?
- immunity/resistance-type cases: the disposition comes in degrees, the manifestation depends on dose and on the rest of the organism, and "it's resistant" is only true relative to a reference class and a set of normal conditions. Nothing in the glass and sugar cases prepares you for that.

So: the requirements list. The cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse (disposition vs. its base, having the disposition vs. manifesting it, dispositional vs. categorical, degrees vs. kinds), what it owes on the counterfactual front now that the plain conditional analysis is dead, what it owes about composites (is a bridge's strength anything over and above its beams'?) and about dispositions to acquire dispositions, and which bits of actual scientific practice it has to stay consistent with — including the awkward fact that the base is usually a black box and that we infer dispositions from single cases and from statistics.

Then flag the pairs you think can't be had together. I'm fairly sure there's at least one; I have a suspicion about intrinsicness plus the normal-conditions clause, and another about a disposition being genuinely distinct from its base while remaining causally non-redundant given that base. Say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just house rules of one camp (the "no brute modality" one smells like a house rule to me). And tell me which single requirement does the most excluding. Skim the main families against the list at the end if you want, but keep that part short — I know the literature, so no reading list, no citations, just the argument. A table for the list is definately fine, and if you need to split it into negotiable/non-negotiable columns, even better.
