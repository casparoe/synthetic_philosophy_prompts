I write about politics and ideas, and I have been trying to work out what I actually think about unfreedom, going by my gut rather than by any theory. A tenant whose landlord never interferes but could evict her on a whim, for no reason and with no appeal: unfree. A driver held at a red light: free. A citizen bound by a law she voted for and can openly contest: free, the constraint notwithstanding. A salaried employee whose boss could dismiss her at will but is fair and has never threatened to: free — that is simply what a job is. A woman living under a government that could detain her without charge, though it never has: unfree. And a woman whose thermostat, feed and phone quietly steer her choices, never forbidding anything, never explaining themselves, and offering no way to see or contest the shaping: unfree.

The tenant and the employee are the two that bother me. Both are subject to an unaccountable private power that happens not to be exercised, yet I call one unfree and the other free. My hunch is that the difference lies in whether the power is arbitrary and unaccountable, but that seems to make the employee unfree too, and I am reluctant to say that. Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? What would it say about the algorithmic case, and if it cannot save both the tenant and the employee, which of my verdicts should I give up? Please answer in British English.
