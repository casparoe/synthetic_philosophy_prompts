I built this argument for a seminar on the socialist calculation debate and at the time I thought it was a clean win for Mises and Hayek, but now I'm worried it proves too much and I can't tell if I've found a real problem or just tied myself in knots. Here it is:

(1) Allocating resources rationally means sending them to their most valuable uses.
(2) Resources only get sent to their most valuable uses when there are prices that reflect opportunity costs — what you give up by putting a resource here rather than there.
(3) Prices that reflect genuine opportunity costs only come out of real exchange, where independent owners who carry the consequences of their own choices bid against one another.
(4) Under Lange's model the Central Planning Board sets the prices of capital goods itself and just tells the managers to minimise cost and produce up to demand; nobody owns the capital, nobody carries the residual, and there's no genuine exchange of capital goods anywhere.
(5) So the Board's prices can't reflect genuine opportunity costs.
(6) So Lange's socialism can't allocate rationally, and the calculation problem stands.

Heres my worry. A big company — pick any car maker — is basically a little planned economy. The divisions don't own anything, no independent owners are bidding for the factories, they trade with each other at transfer prices that head office sets, and the managers just follow rules from above. And yet the company shifts resources around internally and nobody thinks it's irrational. So (3) and (4) look like they prove that big firms can't allocate rationally either, which is absurd. So either (3) is doing something sneaky, or the argument's fine and there's a real difference between a firm and an economy that I'm not seeing.

I'd like to keep (6) — I do think there's something to the calculation problem and I don't want to just hand Lange the win. But I can't deny that firms allocate internally. So where's the mistake — is it one of the premises, or is the firm comparison just a bad analogy? And whatever I have to drop, does the argument still get me to (6), or does it fall apart? And whats the actual difference between a firm and a whole economy here — is it just scale, or something structural?
