My friend Nadia is finishing a dissertation on labour organizing in a region where basically the only surviving records are police files, and last night we got into a long, increasingly heated argument (two bottles of cheap wine in) about which kinds of archival silence actually do the most damage. Her line was that records that were never made are the deepest problem, because no amount of clever reading recovers a document that was never written, whereas I said a document sitting in a box with no finding aid is functionally just as gone — and for most purposes worse, since at least everyone agrees the never-written stuff is missing. We never realy settled it and I'm still annoyed.

So I want your own judgment, reasoned out, not a reading list. Take the following as six distinct ways a person or an activity can go missing from the record: (1) nothing was ever written down in the first place; (2) something was written but nothing survived — discarded, purged, lost, destroyed on purpose or by routine housekeeping; (3) it survived and sits in a repository but was never catalogued or described, so nobody can find it; (4) it's catalogued but practically unusable — wrong language, unreadable hand, degraded film, needs equipment nobody has; (5) it's findable and readable but its genre distorts it, e.g. testimony produced under interrogation, or a form that forces messy lives into tidy categories; (6) it's right there, well described, and the field still doesn't read it because everyone cites the same three collections. Sort these into tiers by how much each one should lower a historian's confidence in any general claim about the period, with a sentence or two each on why it lands where it does. Then tell me which one you think gets misread most often as simply 'these people weren't there' rather than 'these people were written out', and whether that changes where it belongs in your ranking.
