I am assembling materials for a two-day workshop on advance directives, and the document I actually need is a joint statement that the two camps in this dispute could both sign. The dispute is the one about whether a directive written by someone who now lacks decision-making capacity continues to govern that person's treatment. For the purposes of the document, call the camps the entrenchment view, on which a directive is an act of self-legislation whose authority is fixed at the moment of writing and does not depend on any later endorsement, and the contemporaneous-will view, on which authority tracks the present will of the person, so that a directive functions as evidence of that will and is defeasible by it. Do not attribute either view to any named philosopher or work, living or dead, and include no citations of any kind; I will supply the scholarly apparatus myself, and I would rather have the positions than the personnel.

Draft the statement. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — propositions both camps can sign about the structure of the problem, not truisms. These should cover at least: that a directive is issued by one temporally located self and applied to a later one; that the two central cases are later endorsement and later repudiation, and that a theory handling only one of them is incomplete; that neither camp may treat the loss of capacity as a premise it gets for free; and that the question at issue is the directive's moral authority rather than its legal validity, with the relation between the two left as an open item if the camps disagree about it.

2. A shared taxonomy of three directive-types — a precommitment directive (one that aims to defeat a choice the writer expects to want to make later), a testamentary directive (one that settles a matter the writer expects to be indifferent or incompetent about), and a default directive (a standing preference that governs unless the present person actively opts out) — together with each camp's position on which types its disagreement is actually about. If one camp denies that the taxonomy carves at the joints, record that as a disagreement rather than papering over it.

3. A disagreements table with a row for each live disagreement. Each row should give the disagreement in wording that the side holding that position would accept as a fair statement of its own view; the crux, meaning the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp; the case, argument, or piece of evidence both camps agree bears on that crux; and each camp's prediction about how it would come out. I expect at least these cruxes to surface, though add others if the drafting requires them: whether the ground of authority is the writer's autonomy at the time of writing or the interests of the person later; whether an expression of preference by a person with impaired capacity counts as a will or as a symptom, and by what criterion; and whether the writer's ignorance, at the time of writing, of what the later state is like undermines the directive or is simply the ordinary condition of all planning.

4. An honest closing section on what remains unresolved even after the drafting: any claim I have asked for as agreed that turns out to be contested, and any part of the dispute that is verbal, naming the disputed term and the sense each camp is using.

My own suspicion, which I would like the document to test rather than assume, is that the two camps are answering different questions — one about who holds authority over the body and one about who is the author of the life — and that once those are separated the residual disagreement is smaller than either side believes. If the drafting shows this is false, say so plainly and show where the separation breaks down.

End the document with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in at most fifteen words, the single crux on which the dispute most turns.
