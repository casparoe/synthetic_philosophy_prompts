You are a philosopher of science with a specialism in evolutionary biology and the philosophy of the behavioural sciences. I would like you to diagnose an impasse, not adjudicate it, so please begin by thinking carefully about the structure of the disagreement before committing to any verdict; brainstorm the candidate diagnoses first, then argue for the one you find most defensible.

For roughly four decades, evolutionary psychology has been dogged by the charge that its hypotheses about human behaviour — mate preferences, sexual jealousy, cheater detection, the allegedly modular architecture of the mind — are just-so stories. The critics and the practitioners have been talking past one another for as long as anyone can remember. The practitioners reply that their hypotheses are testable and have in fact been tested, that they generate predictions, and that cross-cultural data confirm them. The critics reply that a story which can be told about any outcome explains nothing, that the evidence is correlational or reconstructed after the fact, and that the whole programme rests on an unexamined assumption that every interesting feature of the mind is an adaptation. Nobody moves.

My working hypothesis — which I would like you to treat as a hypothesis to be tested against what the two sides actually say, rather than as something to be confirmed — is that the phrase just-so story is doing at least three separable jobs in this dispute, and that part of the impasse is a failure to keep them apart: first, the charge that a hypothesis is untestable in principle; second, the charge that it is testable but not uniquely supported, that it is merely one explanation among several compatible with the same data; and third, the charge that even a well-supported hypothesis is not the same thing as a demonstrated history of selection, because showing that a trait currently does something useful is not showing that it was selected for. It may help to add a fourth contrast: a genuine adaptationist explanation should be reverse-engineered from the design of the trait, whereas a just-so story is forward-engineered from a problem to a convenient solution. If this diagnosis is on the right lines, the two sides may be answering different questions under a single term of abuse, and some of the apparent disagreement may be verbal. But it may equally be that a genuine methodological or even metaphysical disagreement is hiding underneath — about what a selection explanation is, about whether the notion of an environment of evolutionary adaptedness is a legitimate construct or a fiction, and about what could count as evidence for a psychological adaptation when one can neither run the experiment nor read the ancestral genome.

Please do the following. First, reconstruct as fairly and concretely as you can what each side is actually claiming and what each takes itself to have shown, without reducing either to a caricature. Second, diagnose the impasse: is it verbal; is it a disagreement about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, about the correct explanatory target, or about bedrock assumptions that neither side can argue for? Where a dispute that looks substantive turns out to be verbal, identify exactly which term is equivocating and how; where it turns out to be substantive, state what the real crux is. Third, and most importantly, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a clarification, a distinction, a piece of evidence, a formal or experimental result, or a decision to stop asking one question and start asking another — and be honest if you think no progress should be expected, and why.

Be concrete and engage the actual shape of the arguments rather than gesturing that both sides have a point. I am not asking for a literature survey, a reading list, or citations; I want the diagnosis itself, argued from the positions as you understand them. Write the whole answer in British English.
