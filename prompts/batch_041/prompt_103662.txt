I have been arguing about infinite sequences of tasks ever since a reading group I am in drifted from the history of mathematics into metaphysics, and I have arrived at a set of verdicts that I hold to individually but cannot get to sit still together. A runner who must first reach the halfway point of the course, then the three-quarter point, then the seven-eighths point, and so on, does finish the race, and Achilles, who must first reach the spot the tortoise has just left, then the spot it has reached by the time he gets there, and so on, does catch the tortoise; in both cases the infinitely many stages are run off inside a finite span, and the motion completes. But a lamp bothers me: it starts off, and at one minute before the hour it is switched on, at half a minute before the hour it is switched off, at a quarter of a minute on again, and so on, halving the interval each time so that infinitely many switchings are crowded into that last minute. I want to say that at the hour there is simply no fact about whether it is on — that the description leaves the matter unsettled — even though I am uneasy about denying that a lamp is either on or off. And then there is the urn. At each step I drop in ten balls and take one out. In the first version I always remove the lowest-numbered ball, and I am confident that at the end the urn is empty, since ball number n is taken out at step n and never comes back. In the second version I always remove the highest-numbered ball, the one I have just put in, and I am equally confident the urn ends with infinitely many balls, since every ball numbered n stays in from step n onward. Those last two strike me as the same process with a different piece of clerical detail, and yet I cannot bring myself to give them the same verdict.

My best attempt at a unifying principle is this: a sequence of tasks of this kind has a determinate outcome exactly when the quantity that describes the relevant feature of the world converges as the steps proceed, and when it converges the outcome is simply its limit. On that principle the lamp has no outcome, because its state oscillates and never settles, while each urn has one, because the sequence of its contents does settle — in the one case on the empty set, in the other on an infinite set. I would like you to test that principle against the verdicts above and repair it where it needs repairing. In particular, does it genuinely reconcile the two urns, or does it merely redescribe the disagreement in other words; does it force me to abandon the thought that the lamp must after all be either on or off, or can some more complicated principle keep both that thought and the urn verdicts; and what does it predict for cases I have not yet considered — say a dial that is set at half a turn, then three-quarters, then seven-eighths, and so on, so that it converges on a full turn it is never actually given, or a snail that each minute covers half the remaining distance to a lamp while the lamp is being switched as in the lamp case above? Tell me which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and say plainly which one I should give up.
