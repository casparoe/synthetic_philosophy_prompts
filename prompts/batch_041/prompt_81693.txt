I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about coherent extrapolated volition, and I have come away less sure of my footing than I was when we started. She has read a good deal more of the rationalist and effective-altruism literature than I have, so I had assumed she would be a reliable guide, but the account she gave me does not match the fragments I have picked up elsewhere, and I cannot tell whether that is because she is wrong or because I am.

Her summary, as close to her words as I can reconstruct them, ran like this. "CEV is the proposal that we should build a superintelligence whose goal is to do what humanity would want if we were smarter and better informed. Concretely, you take everyone's preferences, correct them for factual ignorance and for logical error, so that nobody's preference is resting on a false belief and the resulting set is internally consistent, and then you maximize the result. So it is preference utilitarianism with an extra step that fixes everyone's beliefs, and that step is doing all the work, because there is no such thing as the preference you would have if you were smarter that is not just the preference of whoever gets to specify what 'smarter' means. You either end up with the programmers' values wearing a costume, or with no determinate answer at all, and the literature knows this — it is the standard objection. Which is why I do not see why people still treat it as a serious target for alignment work. And the same move shows up in ethics: 'what you would want if you were fully informed' accounts of a person's good have the same hole in them."

Several things in that seem off to me, and I would like to know which of them actually matter.

First, she describes CEV as adding up corrected individual preferences. I have a vague sense that this family of proposals contains both an aggregating variant and a variant that somehow merges volitions into a single coherent set, and that CEV is meant to be the latter, but I may be inventing that distinction, and I cannot tell whether it is load-bearing.

Second, the extrapolation she describes corrects only beliefs. My impression is that the proposal also extrapolates the values themselves, toward what we would want if we had become more nearly the people we would like to be, which would seem to make the view more vulnerable to her objection rather than less, even though she presents her objection as aimed at the real thing.

Third, she reads "coherent" as meaning logically consistent, which lets her assume there is a single well-defined target that either exists or does not, and then treats the possibility that it does not as a refutation rather than as a problem.

Fourth, she says the idealization objection is standard and decisive, and then concludes that we should stop treating CEV as a serious target and rely instead on techniques that train systems on what people actually approve of. It is not obvious to me that her objection, if it lands, would not land at least as hard on what she recommends.

Fifth, the analogy to full-information accounts of a person's good. I half-remember an objection to those accounts to the effect that the idealized version of you is not you, and that idealizing can change what the thing desired even is, so the fact that your idealized self would want something does not show that it is good for you. If that is the right shape of the objection, does it transfer to CEV, and does her version resemble it?

What I want is an audit, not a verdict on CEV. Go through her summary claim by claim and tell me what someone who defends the proposal would accept as fair, what they would wince at as a distortion, and what is simply wrong; tell me which of her simplifications are harmless and which are the ones doing the work in her conclusion; and then say whether that conclusion follows from the parts of the summary that survive. Where I have garbled something, straighten me out as well. If the framing makes the audit impossible — if I am conflating two different debates, or if the "standard objection" she invokes is not one thing — say so plainly rather than manufacturing a tidy answer, and tell me what I would need to understand to do better. Do not retreat into "both sides have a point": if her summary is unfair, I want to know where and why.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and do not send me off to a reading list. I want the argument, not a bibliography.
