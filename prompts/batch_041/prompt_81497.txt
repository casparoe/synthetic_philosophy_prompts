I'm a politics student here in the UK, but I spent last year on exchange in Zurich and I've been argueing about Swiss military service with my flatmate ever since. You're a political philosopher who works on the ethics of war and on citizenship, so I'd like you to think carefully rather than give me the standard lecture-hall summary. Please start by just brainstorming the candidate presuppositions in rough order of how confident you are about them, and only then commit to a view.

Here's what puzzles me. Switzerland is famously neutral — no military alliances, no taking sides — and yet it's also one of the most thoroughly militarised societies in Europe: conscription for men, a large share of the adult population with military training, service weapons kept at home for years afterwards, and a referendum every so often about whether to scrap the whole arrangement. So neutrality there doesn't seem to mean disarmament or even keeping a low profile; it means being armed, heavily. Then there's the objection side of it: if you don't want to serve, you can do civilian service instead, but I'm fairly sure you have to do it for considerably longer than the military stint, and you have to make some sort of case that it's your conscience talking rather than convenience. Compare Sweden, which went professional and then, a few years later, brought conscription back, and Finland, where conscription is just normal and uncontroversial and the alternative is again civilian service.

What I want to know is what all of this presupposes philosophically. Does armed neutrality presuppose that a political community has a right — or a duty — of self-defence that stands independent of any alliance or international body, i.e. that the nation-state is the basic unit of security and owes this to itself alone? What does it presuppose about deterrence: that it's legitimate to threaten force you've forsworn using except in response? And conscription itself — the idea that a citizen's body can be requisitioned by the state, that it's men's bodies in particular, that it lasts only up to a certain age, and that this is a duty of citizenship rather than a job. Contrast the professional army, where it is a job you can resign from, which looks like it presupposes that military service is labour like any other and that the state is more employer than community. Then the civilian-service option: if conscientious objection is a genuine right, why is the alternative longer — doesn't that presuppose that objection is a concession the state grants and prices accordingly, so that the objector has to pay for their conscience? Or does the longer term presuppose something else entirely, namely that the underlying duty is one of service to the community in some form, so that the objector simply owes the same quantity of service by another route? Those two readings seem to pull apart, and I can't tell which one the practice is actually committed to. Please be careful to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with — I suspect a lot of this is underdetermined and that several incompatible theories would license the same arrangements. Tell me where the practice looks internally inconsistent, and what would have to change if we swapped the presupposed view for a rival one, say if we decided military service was simply a job, or that the state has no claim on anyone's person beyond what they've consented to.
