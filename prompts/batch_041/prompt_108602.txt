OK so I've been reading about Bronston v. United States, the 1973 Supreme Court perjury case, and I want you to run it through Grice's stuff on implicature and speaker meaning. Here's the setup so you don't have to go looking: Bronston ran a film company that had gone into bankruptcy, and at a hearing a creditor's lawyer asked him about his Swiss bank accounts. The exchange went like this: "Do you have any bank accounts in Swiss banks, Mr. Bronston?" "No, sir." "Have you ever?" "The company had an account there for about six months, in Zurich." "Have you any nominees who have bank accounts in Swiss banks?" "No, sir." "Have you ever?" "No, sir." Turns out he'd personally had an account in Geneva for nearly five years, and everyone agreed every one of his answers were literally true. The Court held a literally true but unresponsive answer isn't perjury even if he meant to mislead, because the questioner has to frame his questions acutely and the safeguard is cross-examination, not the criminal law.
