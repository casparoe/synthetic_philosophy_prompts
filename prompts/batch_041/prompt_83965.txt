I have recently joined the safety team at a laboratory that trains large language models, and I cannot reconcile four parts of what we do. The model's basic dispositions are settled before we touch it, by choices of architecture and training data that nobody now revisits; we then shape its behaviour by rewarding the outputs our raters prefer and penalising those they dislike; we push it to generate candidate answers, inspect them and discard the weak ones, so that its mistakes are caught in the course of thinking rather than punished after the fact; and we deploy it with calculators, search tools and scratchpads, so that much of what it does is done by things outside it. Each part seems to assume a different sort of creature: one whose nature is fixed for it, one that is shaped by consequences, one that can try things out in its head before acting, and one whose thinking is made partly of tools it has taken from others. Do these together presuppose a single layered picture of mind, or are we running four incompatible regimes and calling the result a model? Tell me what this practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined; then say what would have to change if we took the last of those four assumptions seriously — if we treated the model's mind as reaching into its tools. Please answer in British English, and end with a final line beginning 'Verdict:' that states in one sentence which picture of mind the practice presupposes.
