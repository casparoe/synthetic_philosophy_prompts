I'm putting together a little collection of things about how psychiatric categories and the people inside them keep chasing each other, and I need a funny piece for it. So: write me a mock newsletter column — the chair of a made-up diagnostic working group writing to the membership in his professional association's quarterly bulletin, deadpan, around 600-700 words, in the voice of a man who is extremely pleased with how things are going and has no idea what he's admitting to.

The idea underneath it should be Ian Hacking's looping effects — classifying people changes the people being classified, so the category and the classified keep moving each other, the category gets revised to catch up, and round it goes. But don't explain any of that in the piece. The chair should just live inside it, cheerfully, never noticing.

Stuff for him to cover, all of it as bragging or boring housekeeping:

- the category's prevalence has climbed since the manual came out, and he takes this as the category being vindicated
- the patients turning up now don't look like the ones in the field trials — they describe the symptoms differently, they arrive already fluent in the vocabulary — and he's noticed, but reads it as the field maturing
- the group revised a criterion to match the new presentation, framed as being responsive to the community
- at least one criterion is quietly self-referential (knowing about the category, having been assessed for it, having a name for it, that kind of thing) and he doesn't see the problem
- the thing has been exported and looks different elsewhere; he calls this robustness
- a paragraph pushing back on critics who say the category is 'made up'. He insists he's no social constructionist, and then describes, approvingly, something that is exactly social construction
- a small tell about industry ties, dropped in the way people drop those in
- and near the end a modest proposal — he wants to publish the criteria earlier next time, or start designing the next category around the patients he'd prefer to have, or something in that direction — pitched as a dull procedural improvement

Couple of constraints. Don't name any real disorder, real manual, or real person; invent the disorder and the group, but keep everything else recongisable, so the joke lands on the practice rather than on patients. The patients aren't the punchline, the working group is. No winking, no asides to the reader, no 'of course this is absurd' — he should read as entirely straight, and someone who's never heard of Hacking should still find it funny.
