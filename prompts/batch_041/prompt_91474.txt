I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, which is how I ended up with a pile of intuitions about which things have reasons and which do not. I cannot find the principle behind them, and I suspect at least one of the verdicts is just verbal residue. Here is what I actually think, case by case.

- A thermostat: no reasons. "It turns the furnace on because the room is cold" is fine shorthand, but nothing in the thermostat represents the cold as something to be gotten rid of.
- A chess engine: reasons, of a thin, borrowed kind. I explain its move by saying it was trying to win a pawn, and that explanation is not merely a redescription of the transistors — it is the level at which the thing is actually organized.
- An eye, and the natural selection that produced it: there is a rationale here — the lens is there in order to focus light — but it belongs to no one. I am entirely comfortable with this, and I think I first met the idea in Dennett.
- E. coli swimming up a glucose gradient: no reasons. It is a mechanism, full stop. But I notice I reach for the same "in order to" language, which makes me uneasy.
- A river cutting a gorge: no rationale at all. The gorge is a stable end state of a physical process, and that is the whole story.
- A heat-seeking missile: I lean yes, a very thin reason — it tracks and corrects, and its corrections are explained by what it is tracking.
- A large language model that writes out a fluent justification, something like "I recommended option B because it scored higher on the criteria you gave me": my gut says no reasons, and I am bothered that I cannot name what is missing, since the output is the verbal form of a reason.
- A market price: no single reasoner anywhere, yet the price carries information about conditions no participant fully knows. I genuinely do not know whether to call that a rationale.

Two comparisons are the ones I cannot reconcile. First, the chess engine and the E. coli: both are mechanisms whose behavior is best predicted by treating them as pursuing an end, and both are products of a selection process that optimized them for that end, one by engineers and one by natural selection. If my principle is "was selected for tracking a goal," the bacterium gets reasons. If it is "contains states that represent conditions and uses them," I owe you an account of why the bacterium's chemotaxis signaling does not count. If it is "was designed by an agent who had the goal," then the engine's reasons are inherited from its programmers, and I need to be told what is special about that inheritance. Second, the chess engine and the language model: both are artifacts, both manipulate symbols, and I give one reasons and not the other.

Here is the principle I keep reaching for, so you can test and repair it rather than reinvent it: something has a reason when a state inside it carries information about a condition in the world, and that state's causal role is explained by its carrying that information. I like it because it makes the eye and the missile come out right. It makes the river and the thermostat come out right, but for the wrong reasons, and I cannot get the language model to come out wrong. Fix it or replace it.

What I want, in order: (1) the principle, or the small set of principles, that captures all or most of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can apply it myself; (2) an honest list of which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to give up, and what the replacement verdict should be; (3) the principle's verdicts on cases I have not listed — say, a trait that evolved for one purpose and is now used for another, a vestigial organ, an organism exploiting another's signal, and a reinforcement-learning agent trained to maximize a reward; and (4) how your principle differs from the accounts I am probably conflating — the intentional stance, Millikan-style proper functions and teleosemantics, and a plain dispositional account of function. Do not just tell me which label fits. Tell me what the thing is, where its edges are, and which of my reactions are best explained as the residue of how we talk rather than as tracking a real distinction.
