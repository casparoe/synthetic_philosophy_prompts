I want to practice arguing against a real textualist, so here's the setup. You're a federal appellate judge, a committed textualist of the non-cartoon variety: the enacted words are the law, legislative history is a ventriloquist's dummy, "legislative intent" is either a fiction or the intent of whatever coalition could get 51 votes, and you've got real answers ready for the absurdity doctrine, scrivener's error, and the whole-act rule. You'll concede a point when it's actually right, you just won't give up the main one. Stay in character the whole time -- if I say "court's in recess" you can step out for one line and then get back in. One or two paragraphs a reply, no bullet lists, no summarizing both sides for my benefit. I'm the purposivist. And you're the sort of judge who'd cite a corpus of 1970s trade-press usage before you'd cite a committee report, so feel free to go there if you think it settles anything.

Now the case. I'm inventing the statute and the history, so don't go hunting for it. Interstate Freight Data Act, 1974. Section 2(4): "carrier" means any person who transports freight for compensation across a state line in a vehicle the person owns or leases. Section 3(a): no carrier shall be liable under this Act for loss of freight occurring during any period in which the carrier's vehicle is not under the carrier's operational control. Section 5, added by amendment in 1983: nothing in section 3 shall be construed to relieve any carrier of liability for loss of freight occurring while the carrier's vehicle is being operated by an employee of the carrier. The 1983 committee report, which I know you're going to throw out the window, says the amendment was meant to "restore" liability for employee-driven losses after a circuit split, and it notes that the drafters deleted a sentence defining "operational control" to include periods when an employee is behind the wheel, on the theory that the sentence was unecessary.

Here's my move, and it isn't the usual "your theory is heartless" complaint. Read 3(a) on its own and it exempts exactly the losses that 5 is about: if the carrier's employee is driving, whose operational control is the vehicle under, if not the employee's? So you've got two options. Either 5 contradicts 3(a), and you resolve it with a priority rule -- later and more specific wins -- in which case your textualism didn't decide anything, a canon did, and canons aren't text. Or you read 3(a) against 5 and call 5 a clarification of what "operational control" already meant, in which case you've assumed the 1974 and 1983 Congresses were coherent, i.e. you let a purpose (don't read this statute as sawing off its own leg) override what the words of 3(a) actually say. Which is it? And if it's the second, tell me how that's not purposivism with a shorter leash -- and who set the leash length, you or the 1974 Congress?
