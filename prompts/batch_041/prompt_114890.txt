I am a Year 12 student, I edit the history pages of my school's academic magazine, and I help run the sixth-form history society. We keep receiving essays and debate motions that turn on counterfactual claims of the form: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? The discussion usually collapses into either a romantic Great Man story or a vague appeal to structural forces such as the Depression, the fragility of the Weimar Republic, or the legacy of Versailles. I have been trying to think about this using some distinctions from the philosophy of history that came up in a philosophy extension class: the difference between a cause as a difference-maker and a cause as part of a standing causal field; the notions of necessary, sufficient and INUS conditions, where an INUS condition is an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition; overdetermination; equifinality, or multiple paths to the same outcome; and the idea of a minimal counterfactual rewrite, in which one alters as little as possible from the actual world. What I want from you is a decision procedure that a sixth-form student with no specialist training could actually run in about ten minutes before a society meeting, not a lecture on the historiography. The procedure should tell me which questions to ask in order, what would count as a sufficient answer at each stage, where it should stop and return a verdict of not warranted or warranted enough to discuss, and where it must hand the matter over to judgment rather than pretending to settle it.

Then run the procedure on two claims: first, that without Hitler there would have been no Nazi Germany; second, that without the Great Depression the Nazi Party would never have come to power. I am especially interested in the case where the procedure gives an answer that a committed Great Man theorist or a committed structuralist would dislike, and in what the procedure cannot do. Please give me the procedure as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and make sure the two applications are visible within those items rather than in a separate section; write in British English, and do not give me a bibliography, reading list or citations.
