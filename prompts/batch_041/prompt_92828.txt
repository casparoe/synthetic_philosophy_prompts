I would like a careful audit of an analogy that a colleague of mine leans on heavily, because I suspect it is doing more rhetorical work than he realizes.

His argument concerns the prospects for rapid machine self-improvement. He grants that present-day systems are built and tuned by people, but he contends that once a system can modify its own code or weights, the improvement will become rapid and largely self-sustaining. The analogy he offers is the self-hosting compiler. A compiler is a program that translates programs written in some language into an executable form; if one writes a compiler for a language L in L itself, the first version must be produced by some pre-existing compiler written in another language, but thereafter the compiler compiles itself, so that the output of one round becomes the tool for the next and the original host language can be retired. The system, he says, has closed the loop and no longer depends on its makers. He reads this as a model of machine self-improvement: an artificial system able to rewrite itself should, after a small number of rounds, shed every external bottleneck, with each round running faster than the last. He draws the moral that a fast, discontinuous takeoff is the natural expectation, and that human oversight of such a system would become decorative within a few iterations.

Please audit the analogy rather than simply agreeing or objecting. Specifically:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly, in a table if that is clearer: what plays the role of the host compiler, the source language, the compilation step, the executable, the bootstrap chain, the discarded host, and the moment of closure?

2. Identify where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in whether the analogy supplies any mechanism of improvement at all, as opposed to a mechanism for removing a dependency; whether the compiler case has a fixed point that the target case lacks; what the analogy quietly imports about the cost of verifying a self-modification, the determinism of the step, and the availability of a well-defined source and executable on the target side.

3. Separate the disanalogies that matter for his intended conclusion from those that are merely incidental.

4. Say plainly whether the fast-takeoff conclusion survives the audit, or whether the analogy, properly read, points elsewhere — including, if you think so, toward a plateau.

5. If the analogy is worth keeping in a repaired form, say what repair would be needed; if a different comparison would serve better, propose one and give both its mapping and its own limits.

You need not cite any literature. Argue from the structure of the analogy and from what is actually known about how compilers and machine-learning systems behave.
