I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about the identity of indiscernibles, and we ended exactly where we began—not, I think, because either of us was being stupid, but because each of us kept treating the other’s central move as a cheat. The occasion was Max Black’s two-spheres case: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, identical in diameter, color, temperature, and every other qualitative respect, symmetrically placed. My friend says the case is obviously possible and therefore refutes any interesting version of the principle that there cannot be numerical difference without extra-numerical difference. I say that once every qualitative and relational property is stipulated to be shared, there is no further fact that could make them two rather than one, and that his obvious two-ness is an intuition imported from worlds like ours, where objects are always individuated by differences. He calls that a verificationist refusal to countenance haecceities or bare particulars; I call his haecceities a device for saving the principle by fiat. We kept reaching for distinctions we half-remembered—weak versus strong formulations, qualitative versus non-qualitative properties, impure properties such as being identical with sphere A, haecceitism and anti-haecceitism, the bundle theory versus substratum—and each distinction seemed to help for a minute and then became the next thing we disagreed about. The literature since Black has not exactly settled it either, which is part of why I am asking.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive metaphysical disagreement, a verbal dispute about property or indiscernible, or two people answering different questions under one heading—one about whether a qualitatively symmetric world is possible, the other about what individuates objects within a world? Which version of the principle is actually at issue, and does the choice among versions dissolve the impasse or merely relocate it? Is the real disagreement a clash of modal intuitions, a disagreement about what counts as a property, or a hidden formal question about symmetric structures and automorphisms that leave every qualitative fact fixed? Does the debate stall because one side treats numerical identity as a primitive, non-qualitative fact and the other treats it as supervening on qualitative facts—and if so, is that a disagreement argument could settle or a choice of framework? I have a hypothesis: that the impasse is not really about the spheres but about whether numerical identity needs a ground, and that once that is made explicit the dispute changes character. I want to know whether that hypothesis is right, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis—a distinction, a formal result, a decision about the legitimacy of haecceities, a change of question—or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why.
