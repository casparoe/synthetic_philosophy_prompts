I'm in a reading group that's been chewing on Condorcet's jury theorem, and someone built an argument I can't dismantle. Its been bugging me all week. Background so you don't have to guess: the theorem says that if a group votes on a yes/no question, there's a single correct answer, and each voter is independently a bit better than a coin flip at getting it, then the probability that the majority is correct goes to 1 as the group grows. Fine. I also know the Condorcet paradox -- three voters, three options, and you can get a majority for A over B, B over C, and C over A at the same time. Also fine. Now the argument:

(1) Take 10,000 voters who each, on any yes/no question, are right with probability 0.6, independently of each other, and suppose there's a fact of the matter about which answer is right.
(2) Have them vote pairwise on three options: A vs B, B vs C, C vs A.
(3) By the theorem, the majority is right about A vs B with probability essentially 1. Same for B vs C. Same for C vs A.
(4) If each of the three has probability essentially 1, then all three together do too -- near-certain events can't be mutually exclusive.
(5) But being right about all three means endorsing A over B, B over C, and C over A at once. That's a cycle, and a cycle can't be the truth about anything. So the probability in (4) is 0.
(6) Contradiction. So no group of any size can satisfy the theorem's conditions once there are three or more options, and majority rule is never epistemically justified when it actually matters.

Someone in the group said the events in (3) aren't independent of each other, but I don't see how that rescues anything, since three near-certain events have to overlap. I'd most like to keep the theorem and keep the thought that majority rule is sometimes epistemically justified; I'll give up whatever has to go, I just want to know the price. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what breaks downstream? If the answer is that (3) is smuggling in a sense of "right" that pairwise preference votes can't have, spell out what sense the theorem needs and where that leaves arguments that majority rule is epistemically good -- including in settings where people are literally judging facts rather than expressing preferences. If the answer is (5), tell me what the event in (4) actually is. And if the whole thing is really just valid as a conditional, say whether that's a problem for the theorem or only for people who oversell it. Also say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the bit you're least sure of -- I don't want to get shot down on a technicality at the next meeting. American English, please.
