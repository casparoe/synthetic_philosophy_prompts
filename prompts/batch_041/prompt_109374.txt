I'm a novelist and I've written myself into a corner. My character is a retired actuary who has to convince her sceptical brother — and, more importantly, the reader — that the fact that things have always went one way tells you nothing on its own about tomorrow, and I want to do the sharp modern version of that: the no-free-lunch thing, where if you average over all possible problems, no learning rule beats any other. I don't want you to explain the theorem, I want a plan for the explanation, because there are no equations in this scene, it's all talk. So: what order do the pieces have to come in for the punchline to feel earned rather than cheap? What single example carries the whole thing — I was going to reach for the sunrise, or the turkey who's been fed every morning of his life, but I suspect both are dead on arrival — and what does the example have to have and not have? What will the reader wrongly conclude (my guess is 'so everything's arbitrary' or 'so induction is irrational, and Hume wins'), and at which beat do I need to head each of those off? I'd like to build it around a line I've coined for her: there are no free lunches, and no free assumptions. Tell me straight whether that's honest or whether it oversells, and if it's honest, exactly where in the sequence it lands. Then give me one line the brother can say at the end that proves he got it and one that proves he didn't. And tell me what the actuary should refuse to explain at this level, so I can have her refuse it out loud. Commit to one plan and defend it — don't hand me three options and leave me to pick.
