Last night a friend and I argued for like two hours about the two-bitser — you know the Dennett thing where a vending machine built to take US quarters gets shipped off to Panama and happily takes the local coins that are close enough, and now the question is whether the machine ever believed anything about quarters at all, or whether that was just us talking. He's a Searle guy: he thinks there's a real line between my beliefs and the machine's, because mine are original — caused by a brain, about actual stuff — and the machine's are derived, lent to it by us the way a sentence on a page gets its meaning lent to it by a reader. I said the line keeps moving, that once you can predict the thing with the intentional stance as well as you can predict me, calling mine original and its derived is a distinction that isn't doing any work, it's just flattering me. We didnt resolve it. He kept saying syntax by itself can't add up to meaning, and I kept saying fine, then show me the extra ingredient, and we just stared at each other.

Anyway, I want a poem out of this, and I'd rather it stage the disagreement formally than just describe it. So: alternating quatrains, where the odd stanzas describe the machine purely in the design stance — weights, magnets, tolerances, the coin falling through, the little arm swinging back — and the even stanzas describe the exact same events in the intentional stance: it wants, it takes, it is fooled, it believes. The catch is that as the poem goes on the two descriptions should start to bleed into each other, so that by the last stanza I honestly can't tell which stance I'm reading, and the it-believes line should be one I'd be willing to say about myself without changing a word. Somewhere in there the Searle objection should arrive as a line that stops the poem for a second — the thought that all of this is marks on a page and marks don't mean anything on their own — and the poem should either answer it or fail to answer it, in a way that feels honest rather than triumphant. No quoting anyone, no naming theories inside the poem, no footnotes.

Someone who knows the two-bitser and the original/derived distinction should see exactly what's happening; someone who doesn't should still get a poem about a machine that gets fooled by money and a person who maybe does too. Keep it to 32 lines max, and after the poem you can add one single line — no more, no italics, no lecture — saying whether the poem comes down on there being a difference in kind or only a difference in bookkeeping.
