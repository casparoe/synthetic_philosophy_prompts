I've been asked to give the commencement address at the small liberal arts college where I teach — seven minutes, a couple thousend people in a tent, mostly parents who haven't done math since 11th grade, plus a dean who already suspects I'm going to be clever at them. I want to hang the whole thing on the strategy-stealing argument: in a symmetric game with no draws where an extra move never hurts you, the first player can't lose — the second player can't have a winning strategy, because the first player just makes some throwaway opening move and then steals it. Two things I want the speech to actually deal with instead of papering over. First, it's a pure existence argument; I can promise the graduates they can't be beaten and I cannot show them a single move of the strategy. Second, every assumption in it fails in a life — especially "an extra move never hurts," which is just false once you've met zugzwang, where the options you'd rather not have are exactly what loses you the game and you have to move anyway. So the address has to keep the promise real without ever cashing it, and can't pretend the symmetry holds. Don't make anyone in the tent learn game theory; the zugzwang part should land as something they already know from their own lives. Write the speech, then underneath it add a short note: how confident you are in the game-theory claims you leaned on, and which one you're least sure of.
