I edit a tiny fake journal for my philosophy-of-mind reading group -- one issue a year, print run about nine -- and this year I want the whole thing built around Dennett's heterophenomenology. You know the move: the scientist doesn't ask whether the subject's reports are true, just transcribes them, treats the subject as a sincere speaker whose utterances are evidence the way a novelist's characters' speeches are evidence about a fictional world, and builds up a "heterophenomenological world" out of them. The researcher stays officially neutral about whether any of it answers to anything anyone actually experiences; the whole point is to get the science of consciousness going without having to settle the metaphysics first.

What I want from you is the Instructions to Authors page for this journal, written completely straight, like a boring house style guide that somebody actually had to sit down and write -- 600-700 words. The funny has to come from taking the method seriously and following it all the way down into the paperwork, never from winking at the reader. So give me a scope statement, a submission checklist, formatting rules for the heterophenomenological world (is it a separate file? how do you cite an entity that exists only in one subject's report? give at least one worked example, say for a reported pain or a dream), whatever compliance declarations you think the method would generate -- I'd love a sincerity affidavit, and something like a "no Cartesian theatre" disclosure -- rules about what authors may and may not say about their subjects, and a short note on the questions reviewers are forbidden to ask.

The guidelines should keep colliding with the fact that the journal is about consciousness, and should never quite be able to avoid the first person, and should never admit that this is a problem. Stay in character the whole way through: no ironic asides, no "of course, this is absurd," no narrator peeking out. Where the rules have to presuppose that subjects have experiences, let them do it in a footnote, as a formatting matter. And it shouldn't read like a list of gags -- it should read like a real style page that solves real editorial problems, so that someone who knows the literature recognizes the method and someone who doesn't still gets why the rules are strange.

Just give me the page, start to finish, no preamble and no commentary before or after -- begin with the journal's name and the first heading.
