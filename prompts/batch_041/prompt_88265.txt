I have no training in biology or philosophy; I have been thinking about this on my own for a few years, mostly while walking, and I have ended up with a set of convictions that I cannot fit together. The subject is what habitat change does to animal suffering, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, lies behind my verdicts, and which of them I should abandon.

My starting point, which I take to be uncontroversial, is that in most wild populations the great majority of individuals die young and die badly: frogspawn and tadpoles, a songbird nest where one chick in five fledges, voles, insects in numbers I cannot picture. If that is the ordinary shape of a wild animal's life, then how many such lives exist at any moment matters enormously, and habitat change is chiefly a way of changing that number.

Here are my verdicts.

Draining a marsh to build a car park: this leaves less suffering in the world. There are fewer animals afterwards, and the ones that were there died, mostly quickly, but they were going to die soon regardless. I dislike saying this.

Capping a landfill and letting gulls, rats and weeds take it over: this is bad, even though it becomes a thriving habitat crowded with animals that do well around people.

Restoring a drained marsh, or replanting a meadow with wildflowers: this is good, and I feel good about it. It is the sort of thing I give money to.

Clearing rainforest for cattle pasture: this is bad, but I suspect my reasons are about the cows and about species disappearing rather than about net suffering.

Shooting deer in an overcrowded park so they do not starve in winter: this is acceptable, and I suspect it does some good.

Keeping a bird feeder filled through the winter: I am uneasy about it and suspect it is bad, though I cannot defend that against someone who says the birds are simply better off fed.

Fencing a reserve to keep foxes and cats out: my instinct says good; my reasoning says the animals inside will starve or sicken instead, and that there will be more of them to do it.

Some of these cannot all be right. Restoring the marsh and capping the landfill both put many new animals where there were few, and I call one good and the other bad. Draining the marsh and clearing the rainforest both cut the number of wild animals, and I call one good and the other bad. And if I truly believe a wild animal's life is on average not worth living, I seem committed to saying that the conservation I pay for is a harm.

What I want is the principle, or the two or three competing principles, that save the most of these verdicts; what each of them implies about cases I have not considered — saving a species by keeping it in a zoo, a beaver dam that floods a valley, a wind farm that kills bats; and a frank statement of which verdicts must go. If the honest answer is that none of this can be settled without deciding what an insect's life is like, or whether bringing a new animal into existence is a harm, a benefit, or neither, say so plainly and tell me which way each answer would push the verdicts. I am not after references; I would rather have the argument.
