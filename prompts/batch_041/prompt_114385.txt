I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the governance of risky technologies, and I want to open the unit on the competitiveness constraint with Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove (1964). I need a piece of criticism to circulate as required reading before the screening: not a synopsis, not a survey of what other commentators have said, and not a policy brief. Assume my students have seen the film once and read nothing else about it.

Two ideas are to be brought to the film, and I will state them as I want them used, since I do not want the essay to depend on any body of literature. The competitiveness constraint: where several actors race for the same prize or against the same threat, no actor can unilaterally carry a cost its rivals will not carry, so restraint, verification, and safety are selected against even when every actor would privately prefer the world in which all of them paid. The alignment tax: the capability, speed, or discretion a builder surrenders in order to make a system do reliably what its designers intend, which a rival who forgoes the safeguard does not pay. I have been telling students that this tax can be paid in three currencies — capability, discretion, and legibility — the last being the odd case in which the safeguard costs its builder nothing except its concealment.

What I want from the essay:

First, read the Soviet doomsday machine as a safety technology, and show that the film supplies two separable arguments for its failure. On the credibility argument, a deterrent nobody believes in deters nobody, so the machine had to be announced, while announcing it was politically impossible; the film makes the concealment, not the bomb, the fatal design choice. On the competitive argument, the machine is built because a rival might build one first, so that no one's decision is really anyone's. Decide whether these are one argument in two costumes, and say which the film actually dramatizes.

Second, locate where the tax is paid. The catastrophe runs through arrangements that are each individually defensible — launch authority delegated down the chain of command, a recall channel that cannot be used without the code of the officer who ordered the attack, a crew that does its job well. Does the film hold a general thesis, that residual risk always settles into whichever component was left discretionary or kept least legible, or is the failure merely the bad luck of one deranged commander? The film's refusal to settle this is the interesting feature, and I want it addressed rather than tidied away.

Third, say where the analogy to present-day machine learning strains. Deterrence presupposes an adversary who reasons about threats and can be moved by them; a trained model does neither. Is the competitive constraint in AI a relation among rival developers rather than rival arsenals, and if so does the film's argument survive the translation, or does it work only for states, with the parallel breaking at a point you can name?

Fourth, take seriously the reading I am least confident of: that the film satirizes particular Cold War institutions — secrecy, civilian control of the military, masculine panic — rather than advancing a general argument about competition and safety, and that a constraint which assumes incentive-responsive actors is undercut by a story in which nobody is rational.

Close by saying what the film supplies that a memorandum cannot. About fifteen hundred words, with headings, and no citations.
