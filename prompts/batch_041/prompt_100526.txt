Assume the role of a philosopher specializing in free will and moral responsibility, and help me with a puzzle that has been nagging me in my second-year PhD work. I keep running into a dialectical move that I can describe but cannot name. Someone wants to defend the principle that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise. A Frankfurt-style case is then presented: an intervener stands ready to force the agent’s choice if the agent shows any sign of choosing otherwise, but the agent chooses on his own, so the intervener never acts. The defender of the principle grants that the agent could not have chosen otherwise in the ordinary sense, since the intervener would have prevented it, but then insists that the agent could still have done otherwise in some thinner sense. Perhaps he could have tried, or could have formed a different intention, or could have deliberated differently, or could have been the sort of agent who would have chosen otherwise. The thinner alternative is not robust enough to make a difference to what actually happens, but it is supposed to be enough to save the principle. I see a structurally similar move in discussions of coercion: the agent could not have done otherwise given the threat, but defenders of responsibility point out that she could have refused and suffered the consequence. I want to know whether there is a standard name for this general defensive strategy, and, more importantly, how exactly to carve it.

I am not satisfied with a bare label. I need to know what the phenomenon is and what it is not. Is it a single established maneuver, or a family of maneuvers that differ depending on how thin the residual alternative is allowed to become? How does it relate to the distinction between leeway and sourcehood conceptions of free will, and to the conditional analysis of could have done otherwise? Where is the line between a genuine alternative possibility and a mere counterfactual shadow of one — for instance, between an agent who could have tried to do otherwise and an agent whose only residual alternative is that the intervener would have acted differently? Please start by brainstorming the nearest concepts and terms you know, then tell me which one, if any, fits the pattern I have described, and explain the differences. If no existing term captures it exactly, coin one and define it, giving boundary cases and saying what it excludes. I would also like you to consider whether the strategy is dialectically legitimate or whether it changes the subject, and to flag any places where my description is ambiguous or presupposes a contested view. Think carefully and give me the carving, not just the name.
