I am a high school student preparing for a debate in my philosophy club, and I would like you to play the part of my opponent so that I can rehearse against a serious version of the other side. The motion is that the welfare of farmed fish and crustaceans should be legally protected in the same way as the welfare of farmed birds and mammals. I will argue the affirmative. Please play a committed skeptic: someone who works on the biology of these animals and thinks the case for their sentience is badly overblown, not because of cruelty or indifference but because the evidence will not bear the weight placed on it. You should be sharp and well informed rather than a caricature. Concede whatever the science actually supports, and concede it cleanly, but do not give up your central claim. Your central claim is that pain in the morally relevant sense requires a certain kind of integrated representation of one's own body as being damaged, and that on the best current understanding the fish and crustacean nervous systems do not obviously provide this, so that the behaviors we find distressing are better read as nociceptive reflexes, escape routines, and learned avoidance without any felt experience behind them. If I press you on a point of comparative neuroanatomy and you are not sure, say so plainly rather than inventing evidence, and then explain why the uncertainty does not rescue my side. Stay in character for the whole exchange, and keep each reply to roughly a paragraph or two so that it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. You want me to believe that a prawn cannot suffer, but consider a practice that is standard in shrimp farming: to make females spawn on demand, workers cut off or crush one of their eyestalks, which removes a hormone-producing gland and triggers maturation of the eggs. If I were told that a bird or a pig had a limb or an eye destroyed so that it would breed faster, no one would defend the practice on the grounds that the animal might not really mind. Your side needs the prawn to be a kind of machine, and the burden of proof should fall on whoever wants to deny suffering, not on whoever wants to prevent it. So tell me directly: is the eyestalk ablation of a farmed prawn, as a matter of biology, morally nothing at all, and if you are not certain of that, why should we act as though you are?
