Take the induction of a concept from a handful of contrasting examples as a theoretical target in its own right, and write the desiderata that any adequate theory of it must satisfy. The test bed is the Bongard problem: a solver is shown two small sets of figures and must supply the single rule that holds of everything in the first set and of nothing in the second, with no feedback, no second attempt, and no hint about what kind of rule is wanted. Fix the standard of adequacy as follows: the theory must account for a solver that arrives at the intended rule in one shot, with no external source of rules, answers, or precedents to draw on.

Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it — the phenomena the theory must reproduce, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must not contradict. I expect the list to have to accommodate at least the following. Any finite pair of sets is separated by indefinitely many rules, yet solvers converge on one of them and treat the rest as wrong, so the theory must say what selects the intended rule, and it must say whether there is a fact of the matter when two rules both seem defensible. What carries the information is the contrast between the two sets rather than either set on its own, and a rule that partitions the figures on display while failing on further figures is not the one wanted. The rules are assembled from a vocabulary that is not fixed in advance — counting, topology, symmetry, orientation, containment, properties of parts — and the same primitives recur across problems, so some competence transfers. The account must be a procedure that could actually be run, not a description of the answer once it is known. And problems vary widely in difficulty, which the theory should at least be able to rank.

Then do the work I am really after. Say which of these requirements are genuine and which are parochial demands of one style of theory. Identify any subset that cannot be satisfied together; I suspect there is at least one such conflict, and I want it presented as a result about the problem rather than as a complaint about the literature. Finally, name the single requirement that does the most to exclude the obvious families of account — classification by similarity or by a fixed set of features, statistical learning over a preset representation, and memorization of previously solved problems. The list is the deliverable; I do not need a survey of particular proposals.
