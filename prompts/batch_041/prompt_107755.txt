I retired a few years ago from a career in experimental physics, most of it spent on detector and imaging chains: transducers and their transfer functions, front-end electronics, calibration against reference standards, and the model-based inversion that turns raw counts into an estimate of a physical quantity. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of perception, and I keep having the sensation that I already own machinery for the problem — though I cannot tell whether it is the same machinery or merely a look-alike. Assume I am fluent in the measurement vocabulary and a recent, amateur reader of the philosophy.

Please restate the debate over cognitive penetration of perception in the vocabulary of measurement science as I have just sketched it: transducers, signal and noise, gain and bandwidth, calibration and traceability, open-loop versus closed-loop sensing, priors and regularization in model-based inversion, systematic versus random error, and the gap between the quantity measured and the estimate reported. Take the positions one at a time — the encapsulation thesis as defended in the modularist tradition, the claim that perception is genuinely shaped by expectation and concept, and the deflationary suspicion that the striking experimental effects are artefacts of response bias or task demands — and say for each what it becomes in my vocabulary, and where the translation has to be forced.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which of the philosophers' claims have exact counterparts in instrumentation, which survive only in a distorted form, and which cannot be stated in my vocabulary at all? I am particularly interested in two things. First, whether the transducer/processor boundary on which the whole debate seems to rest has any principled analogue in a measurement chain, or whether instrument design simply refuses to draw the line where the philosophers need it drawn. Second, whether the epistemic question at the centre of the dispute — whether a percept can still serve as evidence for the very belief that shaped it — is something my notions of calibration, traceability and uncertainty can express, or something they systematically miss; I notice that in my old trade a prior entering the reconstruction was unremarkable and not thought to compromise the reading, and I want to know whether that is a substantive disagreement or a sign that I have misunderstood the question. Close with your own judgement on the residue: where the translation fails, is my framework too coarse for the phenomenon, or was the original claim doing less work than its defenders suppose?

Please write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to a colleague — no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic for emphasis, and no tables. Take whatever length the answer needs.
