I have been trying to write a short discussion note on the species problem without begging any questions, and I have produced an argument whose premises I can each defend and whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would like you to locate the step I should give up and to tell me what giving it up costs.

(1) A category is a real kind only if its boundaries are found in nature rather than fixed by us. Where a boundary is drawn by stipulation, the category is an artefact of our classificatory practice, not a feature of the world.
(2) Any boundary between species taxa is fixed by some criterion — reproductive isolation, diagnosability, monophyly, or clustering in genotype space — and every such criterion comes in degrees and yields cuts that disagree with the cuts yielded by the others. (Take an anagenetic lineage sampled over a million years: each generation is interfertile with its neighbours, so on the interbreeding criterion the lineage contains either no species or exactly one, and nothing but the taxonomist's decision tells you which.)
(3) Where the criteria disagree with one another and each comes in degrees, nothing in nature compels any particular cut. So the cut is stipulated.
(4) Therefore species taxa are artefacts of classificatory practice rather than real kinds.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(3) is peculiar to species: 'gene', 'organism', 'population' and 'ecosystem' are likewise bounded by criteria that come in degrees and disagree with one another.
(6) So there are no real kinds in biology, and since 'planet', 'mountain' and 'chair' are no better off, there are no real kinds anywhere.
(7) But there must be real kinds: if there were none, no classification could be more correct than another, and 'all ravens are black' would be true by our convention alone.
(8) Hence one of (1)–(7) is false.

I would rather keep (7) than (6), and I am confident of (2) and (5). Please identify the step to reject, explain why it looked compelling, and price the alternatives: in particular, say what each repair leaves me able to claim about particular taxa such as Homo sapiens, and what it forces me to concede about ordinary categories such as 'chair'.

End your reply with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...}, containing the number of the step you reject followed by a label of no more than six words naming the principle you would put in its place.
