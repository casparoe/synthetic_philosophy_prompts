I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I'm running the next session of our department's interdisciplinary reading group (psychologists, a couple of neuroscientists, and philosophers, none of them specialists in self-knowledge). I want to open with a sorting quiz rather than a paper, because the last two discussions collapsed into people talking past one another: everyone cheerfully agreed that self-reports are sometimes confabulated and then drew incompatible morals from it. My diagnosis is that we were answering three different questions under one heading, and I want the quiz to make that visible. The first is the mechanism question: is the report caused by the state it purports to report, or by a separate process that infers what the state must have been? The second is the reliability question: whatever the mechanism, do the reports track the states? The third is the authority question: does the report give the reporter an entitlement others lack, even where the report is not especially reliable? Layered over those I want a target distinction, and this is the part I care most about, so please treat it as the organising spine: reports about the content of a state (what it represents), reports about its causal role (what it does in producing behaviour), and reports about its phenomenal character (what it is like) can come apart sharply in reliability, and I suspect a large share of the apparent disagreement between rival positions is really disagreement about which target is at issue. The three-question breakdown and the target distinction are my own working scaffold rather than anything from the literature, so please use them as given and don't feel obliged to reconcile them with anything you already know.

The deliverable is a quiz of nine questions for non-specialists, each question a concrete case with two or three answer options phrased as verdicts on that case, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The cases must include: the choice-blindness set-up, in which a subject is shown the option they rejected as though it were the one they picked and then justifies it; the split-brain set-up, in which a cue reaches only the hemisphere without speech and the speaking subject offers a reason for the action; the refrigerator-light case, in which someone is asked whether they were experiencing a steady background hum before they attended to it; the tip-of-the-tongue case, in which the report concerns a state the reporter cannot access; the divergence between an implicit measure of attitude and the same person's avowed attitude; and a case in which a movement triggered by an external cue is reported as having been decided upon. The positions that must appear as leaves are: reports are caused by the very states they report, in something like the way perception is caused by its objects; reports are inferences from behaviour, situation, and prior self-theory, with the reported state playing no role in producing the report; a two-route view on which some targets are read out reliably while others are constructed; a skill view on which reliability tracks training and the availability of external feedback; the transparency view on which attitude reports are really world-directed judgements and inherit their reliability from those; and a deflationary view on which there is no distinct faculty and the question reduces to the reliability of verbal report generally. No question may name a position or a researcher, and no question may simply ask whether introspection is reliable; each option must be something a respondent could endorse without knowing which label it leads to. Please also include, for each option, a short note on what endorsing it commits you to, and flag any pair of positions your quiz cannot separate.

Return the whole thing as one JSON object with exactly these fields: `axes` (array of objects with `axis_id`, `name`, `poles` as a two-element array, and `gloss`); `positions` (array with `position_id`, `label`, `target` — one of content, causal_role, phenomenal_character, or any — `core_claim`, and `awkward_commitment`); `questions` (array of nine, each with `id`, `case`, `options` as an array of objects with `key`, `verdict`, `commits_you_to`, and `discriminates`, itself an object with `axis` and `separates` as an array of two position_ids); `scoring_key` (array with `pattern` — the answer keys in question order — `position_id`, and `confidence`); `inseparable_pairs` (array with `pair` and `why`); and `empty_cells` (array with `combination`, `diagnosis` — one of incoherent, unattractive, unexplored — and `why`). Keep every string under forty words, give no citations, no names of researchers, and no suggested readings; I want the conceptual space itself, not a map of who has occupied it.
