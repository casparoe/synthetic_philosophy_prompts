I'm a graduate student in the philosophy of mathematics, and I've been asked to run the Skolem's paradox session at our set theory reading group next week. The room is already split and I can't tell how the pieces fit together: one person insists the paradox is nothing but a theorem about first-order languages, and that anyone who still feels a residual mystery simply hasn't understood what a model is; a second says it shows that "uncountable" isn't a property of sets at all but something closer to an indexical, so Cantor's theorem only ever tells you what some model thinks; a third says it shows that first-order set theory was a mistake from the start and that what we were always after was the real power set; a fourth keeps pointing out that we cheerfully take a countable elementary submodel of some large H(θ) and then reason inside it as though it were the whole universe, which he takes to vindicate the relativist; a fifth thinks the whole thing indicts classical logic, or the power-set axiom, rather than our semantics. What I want for the handout is a decision tree that maps this space rather than a list of names, built up from the logical space first: start with the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, each phrased so that a member of the group can answer it without knowing which position the answer leads to, then locate the actual positions at the leaves and note which combinations of answers nobody occupies.

The cuts I suspect matter — add, drop, or subdivide them as the logic requires — are: whether the model's quantifiers are read as ranging over the whole universe of sets with the model's membership relation supplying a restricted interpretation, or as ranging only over the model's domain; whether "uncountable" is a determinate property of a set or an indexical-like notion; whether there is a unique intended interpretation of the language of set theory, and whether our inability to pin it down in first-order terms is a defect of the language or of the idea; whether the lesson of the paradox is about language or about sets; and whether there is any lesson at all beyond the model theory. I need leaves for at least the deflationary no-lesson position, the relativity position, the absolutist who retains a unique intended interpretation and reaches for second-order resources or an informal full power set, the view that anything uncountable in one model is countable in another and so nothing is uncountable absolutely, the view (of which I have only a hazy memory of seeing defended) that there are simply no uncountable sets and that the appearance to the contrary is an artifact of something in our reading of the quantifiers, and a constructivist or predicativist leaf that blames classical logic or the power-set axiom. If one of my slots is empty, or if you need a leaf I haven't listed, say so and diagnose the gap: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position if there is one people actually use, together with a note on the commitment that would embarrass its holder most, and any two leaves your tree cannot separate should be flagged as such rather than quietly merged. As a test case running across the whole tree, take the elementary-submodel practice: for each leaf, add a clause saying what that position thinks we are doing when we take a countable elementary submodel of a big structure and then reason inside it as if it were the universe. I don't want a reading list or citations — I want the shape of the space and the questions that carve it.
