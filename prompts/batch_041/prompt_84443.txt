I'm a backend engineer, never took a philosophy class, and I'm the one presenting at my film club next week. We're doing The Truman Show. I want to read it as a film about paternalism and nudging, not the usual Plato's cave / free will thing everyone does with it. Please take on the role of someone who actually works on the ethics of influence -- paternalism, nudging, choice architecture -- and brainstorm a few angles for yourself before you start writing, because I'd rather have one honest reading than a survey of the field.

Here's what I keep circling. Christof isn't a villain in the easy sense. He has a justification, and it's the paternalist's justification: Truman will be happier inside Seahaven, the outside world is dangerous and full of liars, and anyway Truman never got to want anything else, because every preference he has was formed inside a world that was built for him. That last part feels like the real problem, and it's the part I suspect the nudge literature can't really answer: if the architect designs the environment from the beginning, there's no "Truman's own ends" left for the nudge to be faithful to, so the promise that nudging just helps people get what they already want kind of evaporates. Test that against the film. Use the distinctions people actually make in this area -- soft vs hard paternalism, paternalism about the means vs about the ends, the argument that some choice architecture is unavoidable so the only real question is who does the architecting -- and tell me where the film dramatizes something real that the theory misses, and where it cheats. My suspicion is that it cheats by making Christof an artist with a god complex rather than a sincere paternalist, which lets us hate him without ever having to answer the strongest version of the paternalist case. Is that fair, or am I being too hard on it?

Also, does the ending settle anything? Truman walks out the door and it plays as a win for autonomy, but the door is a door out of a set, and I'm not sure the film has anyway to show us what an unarchitected choice would even look like -- it seems to just swap one constructed world for another one that's presumably also full of people selling him things.
