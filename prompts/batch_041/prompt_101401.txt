I've been trying to write down, for a reading group, what I actually think about how we learn morals from lives, and I'd like a consistency check before I circulate it. Here is roughly where I've landed.

(1) Moral knowledge travels mainly by exemplars: you come to grasp what a virtue is by seeing it enacted, not by learning a rule that names it.
(2) Biography is the preeminent medium for this, since a well-told life shows a character across many circumstances in a way no principle-statement can.
(3) I accept the is/ought gap: no accumulation of descriptive facts about how someone lived entails a conclusion about how anyone ought to live.
(4) I've come to think a life instructs only when it is presented as actually lived by a specific person, and only when the reader could in principle have been that person. Let's call the second clause the imitability condition.
(5) On that basis I hold that hagiography is not merely unreliable history but pedagogically void: the idealization is precisely what drains it of force.
(6) And yet I take Plutarch's paired lives to be the model of moral pedagogy, legendary subjects and all.
(7) I also think the motivational force of an exemplar comes from the gap between reader and exemplar rather than from resemblance — the distance is the point, not a defect to be closed.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what is the cheapest revision that would restore consistency? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and tell me what each one would cost me to give up.
