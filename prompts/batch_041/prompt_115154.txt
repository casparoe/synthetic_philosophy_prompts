I do causal inference for a living — potential outcomes, identifiability, exchangeability, positivity, transportability — and I keep tripping over transcendental arguments whenever I read philosophy. They look like they're doing my job: naming what you have to assume before the question even means anything. Take the strong anti-sceptical version, where the conclusion is that you couldn't so much as have expereince of a world unless something like persisting objects, or other minds, were really there, and the watered-down version where the conclusion is only about our concepts rather than about reality. Restate both in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: assumptions no data can test, conditions on an estimand being well-defined at all, and the line between an assumption that could fail and a stipulation about what a variable even means (consistency has always felt like the second kind to me, and I'd like to know if that's a real distinction or me flattering myself). Then the residue report: what carries over cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart in my framework at all. My suspicion is that everything hinges on the step from having to presuppose something to its being true, and that my framework can only ever deliver the conditional. Take a definite position: is that residue a hole in the statistician's vocabulary, or a sign the philosophical argument was reaching for something I've correctly learned not to do?
