I spent about a decade in newsrooms before I jumped to covering science and ideas for a magazine, so I've got the trade vocabularly burned into me, and I keep noticing it doesn't map onto the stuff I write about now. Heres what I want.

There's a long-running fight among historians and people who study social memory about what should give way when a community's memory and the documentary record disagree. One camp basically says the record wins — that's the whole point of doing history, to correct what people misremember. The other camp says memory isn't a shabbier version of history, it's a different kind of thing: it's how a group stays a group, it lives in rituals and stories, it isn't even trying to be a neutral account, so treating it as a false claim that needs correcting misses what it is. Restate that fight for me, side by side, in the vocabulary I actually use at work — the morgue (the clip file), the lede, the archive, corrections and clarifications, "we couldn't stand it up," sourcing rules, the anniversary package, the obit desk, "the paper printed it, so that's the record," and the fact that the archive is now searchable and never forgets. I know that vocabulary cold; I don't know the scholars' terms, so translate them rather than assuming I've got them.

Stress-test the translation on one case: a town where the local paper's archive is the only surviving documentary record of something bad that happened, the paper ran a correction decades later, and the town never accepted it — the town's version is what the schools teach and what people say at funerals. Run both positions through that in newsroom terms and see where each one chokes.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. What carried over cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no home in my vocabulary at all. And give me your honest call on whether the leftovers are a blind spot in how newsrooms think about their own archive, or a sign that the philosophers are inflating a distinction that's less sharp than it sounds.

Cap it at 600 words, American English please.
