For the past several months I've been reporting on the quiet machinery of religious finance in Europe, and I keep coming back to Germany's church tax — not as a policy to be described but as a test case for a set of ideas about state neutrality. The arrangement itself is familiar enough, and I'd like you to take these features as given: a surcharge of eight or nine per cent on the income tax bill, withheld at source from anyone registered as a member of one of the churches recognized as corporations under public law, collected by the state's own tax offices (which keep a fee for the service) and remitted to the churches; membership established by religious law rather than by consent, ordinarily through baptism; and the only exit a formal act of civil deregistration before a registrar or local court, which German courts have treated as an all-or-nothing withdrawal from the religious body rather than a partial opt-out from the levy. The whole structure survives largely because the Weimar settlement on church and state was written into the postwar Basic Law instead of being replaced. I'd like two neighbouring cases used as foils: the French law of 1905 that separated church and state but left Alsace-Moselle with a regime of state-salaried clergy that is still in force, and the American settlement, where there is no church tax at all yet churches are exempt from property taxation, an exemption upheld long ago and defended ever since as anything but a subsidy.

What I want is an analysis of that arrangement through three ideas, and I want it to run in both directions — the ideas illuminating the case, the case testing the ideas. First, the distinction between two readings of state neutrality: neutrality as evenhandedness, on which the state may support religion so long as it supports all faiths on comparable terms, and neutrality as abstention, on which the state has no business supporting religion at all. Second, a working device of my own that I'd like you to press hard on: treat establishment not as a yes-or-no property but as a vector of four separable dimensions — symbolic, financial, institutional, and coercive — so that the interesting question about Germany is not whether it is established but which dimensions are engaged and whether any particular combination of them is objectionable. Third, the claim I am least sure of and have been half-convinced by: that what makes a religiously entangled state tolerable is not the absence of entanglement but the cheapness of exit, so that a levy one can walk away from is not an establishment in any sense that matters.

So map the German case onto each framework, say what each one captures and where it distorts or overdescribes, and be candid about where each falls silent rather than forcing it to speak. Then put the exit-based defence under real pressure: how cheap is exit here once you count the canonical and social consequences of deregistration, and does the Alsace-Moselle comparison make exit look cheaper or dearer by contrast? Finally, tell me what the case shows about the master concept itself. If neutrality turns out to be doing no work that the exit condition is not already doing, I want to hear that plainly; if instead the case shows that neutrality has to be read as evenhandedness to say anything useful about Germany, I want to hear that, and what it implies for France and the United States. No citations needed — I'm after the argument, not the literature.
