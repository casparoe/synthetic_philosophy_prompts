I'm a postdoc and next month I have the department seminar — joint philosophy/cogsci thing, about 25 people, only talk I get this year so I actually care how it goes. Title is roughly "How much of you is in my head? A criterion for selves spread across brains," and what I'm defending is a specific version of the Hofstadter-ish picture: when someone dies the pattern that was them doesn't just stop, because a big chunk of it was never in their skull to begin with, it was running in the people around them. I don't want to just do the vibe though, so I've put an actual criterion on it and that's the part I need you to attack. I say a fragment of a person's I is present in another brain only when the host runs the other's practical reasoning from the inside and lets it re-enter their own deliberation — call it loop-closure — and that what gets preserved is not biography but control structure, i.e. what the person would notice, care about, and do. On that criterion a fond memory is not a fragment, a very close friend or a good novelist who's been partially rewritten by someone probably is, and it all comes in degrees. I also want to claim the one-skull-one-self picture is a folk artifact rather than a finding, which I know will annoy people. The example I use is my grandmother — my mother will catch herself making my grandmother's exact gesture and then say a sentence my grandmother would say, and I want to claim that isn't a metaphor.

So write me the Q&A prep sheet. The room is: three or four connectionist/dynamical-systems people who are fine with self-as-pattern but think identity needs causal continuity of a single system; two committed Parfitians who'll say I've reinvented psychological continuity and done it worse, and who'll want to know what matters, and to whom; a Buddhist-philosophy person who thinks my I is a reification and that the distribution question is just badly posed; an eliminativist who thinks the self is folk psychology and that I'm dressing up a metaphor in criteria; someone who knows Hofstadter well and will argue I've made him say something much stronger than he did; a palliative-care researcher who's interested because of the bereavement angle and will worry the criterion gets weaponized — told to grieving families as "they're still here, in you" — and who'll also ask whether it licenses chatbot replicas of the dead; and one guy from a digital-immortality startup who'll want to know if a model trained on someone's texts counts as hosting them. Give me the objections they'll actually raise, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do to the paper rather than how likely they are, each with the two-minute reply and a blunt note on where the reply is soft or where I'm bluffing, plus what I should concede up front and what I should refuse to give up. And include the one I'm hoping nobody asks — you know the one, something about whether any of this is about consciousness at all, or whether I've made hosting a self so cheap that a thermostat does it. I've been at this for like two years and I still can't explain it without alot of handwaving, so be honest with me.
