Ok so I'm 71, spent about 40 years doing biochemistry, and I've lately been reading the old guys — Augustine, on lying. His line, as I understand it, is that every lie is a sin, no exceptions, not even the helpful kind you tell to spare somebody pain, and he's actually rather pleased with himself that you don't have to lie to the man with the knife: you can just keep quiet, or say something true and let the poor bastard run off in the wrong direction. Fine. But I built this arguement to test whether I really buy it and now I can't tell if it's the argument that's broken or me.

(1) Some ends are good and, in the circumstances, obligatory — saving an innocent man is one.
(2) If an end is obligatory, the means necessary to it are at least permitted, since you can't be required to reach something by a route you're forbidden to take.
(3) There are cases where the only way to save him is to tell the knife-man a falsehood.
(4) So in those cases the falsehood is permitted.
(5) But nothing in (1)-(3) is special to saving a life; the same form runs with any good end — sparing a dying woman a brutal last week, keeping a promise you gave, saving a friend from ruin.
(6) So any falsehood in the service of any good end is permitted.
(7) And then no lie is forbidden, which is the exact reverse of what Augustine set out to show, and I don't belive that either.

The bit that really nags me: Augustine already lets you stay silent, or say something true that you know will send the man the wrong way. So he's already fine with me steering someone toward a false belief — he only draws the line when my words themselves are false. What's actually doing the work in that difference, and is it worth the price? So: which premise do I give up? I'd sooner keep (1) and (3) than swallow (6). Tell me what giving up your choice costs me the next time somebody asks me a hard question.
