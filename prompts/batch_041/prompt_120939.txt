Some friends and I have a long-running reading group that began with the Sequences and has drifted steadily toward philosophy of mind, and next month we're doing personal identity — we've already argued the fission case into the ground and someone always brings up the teletransporter, so I want to come at it from a different angle: Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop (2007), specifically the strand where the "I" is not a thing but a self-referential pattern, a strange loop that a brain runs, together with the further move that a pattern is exactly the sort of thing that can be re-instantiated, partially and at low resolution, in other substrates — that the people closest to you each run a rough copy of you, that when someone dies a degraded version of that loop keeps turning over in the heads of those who knew them, and, the claim that reliably starts a fight at our table, that selves therefore come in different sizes, depending on how much of other people a given loop takes in and how many other loops end up carrying a copy of it. I'd like a poem that renders that picture vividly rather than explaining it.

Concretely: write me a sestina. The form is the argument here — six end-words returning in a fixed permutation is precisely what a persistent pattern does when it moves from host to host, and I want a reader to feel that recurrence as continuity rather than as mere repetition. Pick six words flexible enough to slide between noun and verb, standing for the load-bearing elements: the loop itself, the substrate or host that runs it, the pattern considered apart from any host, the copy held in another person, the fidelity or resolution of that copy, and something for degree or part, so that survival comes out as a question of how much rather than whether. Then let the envoi earn its keep: convention gathers the six end-words into three lines, three of them landing at line ends, and I want that compression to read as the low-resolution version of the whole poem — the thing that is recognizably still the same loop though thinner. Please don't name Hofstadter or use his vocabulary as a label; someone who knows the view should recognize it from the inside, and someone who doesn't should still find a good poem. And don't resolve the tension between taking this as a literal claim about what continues after a death and taking it as an elaborate consolation; Hofstadter's own stance is part of what we argue about, and I'd rather the poem leave that open.

Since a poem doesn't get to be right or wrong, what I want in place of a verdict is a short note after it: tell me where the poem takes liberties with the view or leans on a contested reading — whether the differing sizes of soul are being treated as central when they may be an aside, say, or whether the copy-in-another-head is being made to do work it cannot really do — and then what you would have to be persuaded of to write a different poem, for instance if you came to think the pattern-talk was only a metaphor, or that a copy in someone else's head is simply not me in any sense worth the name.
