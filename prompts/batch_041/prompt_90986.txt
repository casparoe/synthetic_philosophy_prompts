I'm a novelist, not an academic, so bear with me. The book I'm writing is about two people — a customs officer and a smuggler on a border that keeps moving — who spend about thirty years almost trusting each other. The whole thing lives or dies on whether the logic underneath their relationship holds up, so Ive been reading around in the evolution-of-cooperation literature. And every account I find does one case beautifully and then goes quiet. Kin selection gets the bees. Reciprocity gets the bats. Network structure gets the villages. Then you get two strangers in a one-shot anonymous game and everyone either shrugs or mumbles something about prosocial preferences and moves on. One of my characters is a biologist and I do not want her sounding like a fool, so before I read any more of this I want the spec.

Write me the desiderata for a theory of cooperation. Not a theory — the list of requirements any theory in that area has to meet to be worth anything. For each requirement I want the case or consideration that puts it on the list, and I want you to mark which of them are non-negotiable and which are really just house preferences of one camp. Include the distinctions the theory cant collapse — altruism vs mutualism, explaining how a cooperative behaviour first appeared vs why it stays around, one-shot vs repeated vs structured populations, and whatever else belongs there — and the findings it isn't allowed to contradict.

Here are four situations I need the theory to survive. I made them up, but I think they're fair, and I'd rather the list be tested against them than against the usual examples:

1. They play again and again, but each round either one can pay a price to make their own move unobservable to the other. So the game repeats, and yet the other player can never actually learn what happened. Is the shadow of the future doing any work here at all? What is a theory supposed to say — that the repetition is worth nothing, or something more interesting?

2. They never meet again, but a third party tells each of them what the other has been up to, and the third party is sometimes mistaken and sometimes has their own reason to shade the report. So reputation is transmitted, but through a channel that is neither reliable nor disinterested.

3. They are wrong about which game they're playing. For a decade the payoffs are a prisoners dilemma, then something shifts and the same two choices become a coordination problem, or a case where cooperating is simply best. Nobody tells them. One of them keeps defecting out of what was, for years, an accurate read of a situation that no longer exists. I need a theory that treats the game itself as something the players are inferring and can get wrong, not as a sign hanging on the wall.

4. One of them helps the other in a way whose size the receiver can't verify, and the helper can't prove it either. The cost is real, the benefit is real, and neither party can audit the ledger.

Then, at the end: tell me which two of your requirements pull against each other and can't both be met as stated — I suspect at least one pair does — and which single requirement does the most to rule out the popular accounts. And please, no reading list. I'll never read it. Just the spec and the arguments.
