You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory and the conceptual foundations of political membership. Think carefully and work through the following at length before answering. Begin by setting out, in your own words, the main families of response to the boundary problem that a well-informed theorist would consider, so that the position you take at the end is an informed one rather than a reflex.

A colleague in my political theory reading group has reconstructed an argument that she says shows democratic legitimacy is either impossible or must be global. Every step looks defensible to me and I cannot find the exit. Here it is as she writes it:

1. A decision is democratically legitimate only if everyone whose interests are authoritatively affected by it has an opportunity to participate in making it. (Call this the inclusion principle.)
2. A decision about who is to be included in a demos is itself a decision that authoritatively affects interests: it fixes whose interests will be counted in later decisions and whose will be subordinated.
3. So, by (1) and (2), the decision to include some people and exclude others must itself be made by a body that includes everyone affected by that inclusion decision.
4. Everyone excluded from a demos is affected by the decision to exclude them, since exclusion just is the assignment of a lesser standing.
5. So any decision to exclude anyone must include the excluded.
6. But a body that includes everyone excludes no one; so no one can be legitimately excluded.
7. Therefore every demos short of the whole of humanity is illegitimate.
8. Nor does stopping at humanity help. If the demos is all living human persons, it excludes future persons, whose interests our decisions affect; extend it to future persons and the same reasoning reaches anyone whose interests any decision touches at all. The only non-arbitrary demos is the set of all interest-bearers across time.
9. No feasible decision procedure constitutes such a body, and any procedure that claimed to would itself be the act of some proper subset. So either democracy is impossible, or every actual democracy is permanently illegitimate.

She reads (9) as a reductio of (1) and concludes that the inclusion principle is a dead end. I would rather keep (1) — the thought that people should not be governed by decisions they had no part in seems to be the entire point of democracy — and give up (9). She thinks the argument can be repaired by adding a revisability condition: a boundary is legitimate so long as those it excludes can contest it and it can be redrawn. I suspect that merely relabels the problem.

Please tell me which step or steps the argument goes wrong at, why the offending step looked so plausible, and what giving it up costs. Address in particular whether the repair requires weakening the inclusion principle from all who are affected to those who are coerced or legally bound, and whether that weakening lets the original problem back in through a side door — for instance, if the question of who counts as coerced or bound is itself settled by a decision that bears on the very people it leaves out. Then, if you had to give up exactly one step, say which, and tell me what I should say to her.
