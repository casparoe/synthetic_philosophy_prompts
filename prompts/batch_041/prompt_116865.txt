I need the specification before I can evaluate any candidate. What must a theory of slurs deliver in order to be adequate — not for exegesis, but for a purpose I will state, since adequacy is relative to it.

The purpose: I work on the policy side of a large platform's content-moderation system. A classifier must decide, at scale and in milliseconds, whether an utterance containing a slur is removed, down-ranked, or left alone; a reviewer must then be able to explain that decision both to the user who made it and to the group it targets; and the system must keep functioning on terms and usages that did not exist when it was built.

Write me the desiderata list: what any adequate theory of slurs has to deliver for that purpose. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it — the datum the theory must accommodate, the distinction it must not collapse, the verdict it must not get wrong. I expect the list will have to cover, among other things: the semantic status of the derogation (truth-conditional content, presupposition, conventional implicature, expressive, or act); the relation between a slur and its neutral counterpart; the difference between a term's being derogatory and its being merely offensive or vulgar, and the boundary between slurs and other identity-directed insults; quotation, report, and counterspeech, where the word occurs without the speaker endorsing it; reclamation, including the question of who is authorized to reclaim and what follows when members of the same group disagree about it; non-derogatory uses in pedagogy and art; diachronic change in both directions; cross-dialect and cross-linguistic cases; and uses that derogate with no member of the target group present.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are artifacts of my particular purpose. Most of all, identify the pairs that cannot be jointly satisfied and argue for each incompatibility, since those are the ones I have to make policy around. Keep the survey of existing positions short: what truth-conditional, presuppositional or conventional-implicature, and purely pragmatic accounts each buy and pay for against the list — a paragraph each at most.

Finally, and this is why I am asking rather than looking it up: I have a working hypothesis that the distinction the system actually needs is not use versus mention but voice — a term is tolerable when the speaker is either speaking as a member of the targeted group or speaking as a conduit for someone else's words, and intolerable when the speaker is speaking in their own voice about the target. Tell me whether that criterion can be made precise enough to survive the desiderata list, what it handles that use versus mention does not, and where it breaks. No literature review; I want the criteria and the conflicts.
