A friend of mine who's doing an econ PhD gave me his one-paragraph take on Aumann's agreement theorem, and I want to know how much of it to believe. He said: 'The theorem is basically that two rational agents who have the same information and both update by Bayes can't disagree about anything. If you and I both know the relevant facts, we're forced to the same probability -- Aumann proved that disagreement between two people who are both being rational is impossible. So whenever two people who've seen the same evidence disagree, at least one of them is being irrational, or else they're starting from different priors, and having different priors is just a polite way of saying one of them hasn't thought it through. Thats also why you never see two rational traders taking opposite sides of the same bet -- if they both knew the same things, one of them would have to know the other was wrong.' I've also heard the phrase 'agreeing to disagree' attached to the theorem, which makes it sound like the opposite of what he's saying. My gut says the trader bit is right and the rest is too strong, but I don't know the result well enough to say. Where is he right, where would someone who actually works on this wince, and does his conclusion -- that disagreement implies somebody's being irrational, and that informed traders shouldn't end up on opposite sides -- follow from the parts that are true?
