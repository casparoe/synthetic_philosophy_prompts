I run a third-year philosophy of biology seminar, 14 students, one 90-minute session, and I want them to actually feel the tension in the claim that DNA is a code — then feel the deflationary counter-pull that it isn't really a code at all, just chemistry that some machinery happens to read. The problem is if I just tell them "information is a metaphor" they nod and forget it in a week, so I want them to run into it. Design me a runnable activity, no lecture up front.

Here's what I'm picturing, but change whatever you think is weak. Split the room into three roles. One group are "genomes": they hold cards bearing short sequences over a four-letter alphabet (say 12 symbols each). One group are the "apparatus": they hold a codon table mapping each triplet to an output token (a color, a letter, whatever) — and crucially you give them a table whose assignments are arbitrary. The third group are "cells": they receive a gene card plus one apparatus table and have to decode the sequence into a product.

Round 1, one shared table, everyone agrees, decoding is deterministic, and predictably the students conclude the sequence "carries the message." Round 2, bring in a second apparatus team with a *different* table and have them decode the exact same sequence — different product. Now the question bites: where was the information, in the DNA or in the table? Round 3, hand a gene to a cell with no apparatus at all; nothing happens, the sequence means nothing without a reader. Round 4, flip a single base so the codon becomes a synonymous one under the table (two triplets, same output) and ask whether that change was even information.

Give me a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money — teams score for correctly predicting a product, and the twist is that predictions only work if you know which table is in play, so the table's arbitrariness has teeth. Include the materials list, the timing, and the exact rules.

The debrief is the important part. Map what happened onto the real distinction: the causal/structural sense in which a sequence covaries with something (Shannon-style, no meaning required) versus the semantic sense of being *about* something for someone. Use the swapped-table moment to get at code-versus-cipher and the frozen-accident idea — that the mapping is arbitrary yet universal, which is exactly why it looks like a language. Use the no-reader round to raise the deflationary view that the "reading" is done by the whole cell, so the information isn't sitting in the DNA like ink on a page; and push them on where the meaning lives if the mapping is external to the sequence. Don't let it collapse into "so it's just a metaphor" — the point is to see what each side has to give up.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time. My guess: someone smuggles in teleology ("the cell wants the protein"), several students insist the sequence alone fixes the product because they've mentally deleted the table, and the scoring pressure makes the fast decoders stop caring about the philosophy. But you'll probably spot failure modes I won't.

And be frank about what the activity can't reproduce. Off the top of my head: the printed table makes the mapping look more conventional than it is, since in the cell the assignments are realized by physical molecules, not by a lookup sheet; and the whole story of how a representation gets its content — how a stretch of DNA comes to be *about* a protein at all — is something I'll have to supply myself, the game won't stage it. Say what else is missing.
