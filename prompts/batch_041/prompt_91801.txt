So my buddy Nate and I have this dumb little arrangement where every month either of us can pull our money out of the project we're doing together, or leave it in and let the other person decide next month, and last week he told me that "rationally" I should cash out on the very first day, and when I asked why he started going on about centipedes and some game theory thing. His version: if we both know we're both rational, the only thing that survives is me taking the money on turn one, which is obviously stupid, since we'd both end up with less than if we just kept going.

The thing is, his whole proof assumes we're each only trying to maximise our own number. But we're friends, I actually care what he ends up with. So I want you to argue that this isn't some cute footnote — that the "rationality forces you to cash out" line is really a claim about being selfish that's gotten dressed up as a claim about reason, and that once you let people care about each other the whole thing falls apart.

Numbers so it's not hand-wavey. Four turns: me, him, me, him. We both know all of this.
- I cash out on turn 1: we get 1 each
- I pass, he cashes out on turn 2: I get 0, he gets 3
- we both pass, I cash out on turn 3: 2 each
- we both pass, he cashes out on turn 4: I get 1, he gets 4
- we both keep passing to the end: 3 each

Work out how much we'd each have to care about the other person's payoff — like, add some fraction of the other guy's number to your own — for the unravelling to stop. Then tell me whether it matters that the caring is lopsided, cause I'm pretty sure I care about him more than he cares about me and I have a hunch that makes things worse for me, not better. Go turn by turn with the reasoning, dont just give me the answer, and at the end say what all this actually shows about whether the centipede thing is a paradox about rationality or just about selfishness.
