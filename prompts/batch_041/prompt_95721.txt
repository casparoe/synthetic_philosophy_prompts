Okay, so this started as a joke question in my reading group (we've been doing modal metaphysics) and now I can't let it go, because I'm pretty sure our arguement proves too much and I can't find where it breaks.

Someone asked whether they could have come from a different sperm and egg. My friend says no, and here's the chain he gave:

(1) I am the organism that developed from a particular zygote Z, and Z is what you get when a particular sperm S fertilizes a particular egg E.
(2) A thing's origin is essential to it: nothing that came from a different origin could have been that very thing.
(3) So I could not have come from a different sperm and egg.

I'd like to keep all three, (3) most of all — it's the whole point of the question and I think it's true. But then he kept going, and this is where I get off the bus:

(4) Z could have split. That's all monozygotic twinning is, and whether it happens turns on basically nothing. In that world two people develop from Z. Z is my origin. So both of them are me — in that world I am two people.
(5) Then someone in the group who does phil of bio brought up chimerism. Sometimes two zygotes — two seperate fertilizations, so four gametes — fuse into one embryo, and you end up with a single person who has two cell lines, sometimes two different sets of DNA in different tissues. That person has two origins. So by (2) they're two people, or else there's no fact of the matter about who they are.

I can't accept (5)'s conclusion — a chimera is one person with one brain and one life, not two people sharing a body — and (4) is barely better: at most one of those twins is me, and honestly neither of them looks like me in the way that's supposed to matter. So something in there has to go. Which step is it, why did it look fine when he said it, and what am I paying once I cut it?

Concretely: after I cut the bad step, what do I now have to say about the twin and about the chimera? And does any of that come back around and cost me (3)? If it does, I'd rather find some other way to block (4) and (5) than give up (3). Also — is (4) really a separate step from (2), or is it just (2) read a certain way? Because if the twinning move is already sitting inside (2), then my problem is with the principle and not with his inference, and I'd want to know how to state (2) so it stops doing that.
