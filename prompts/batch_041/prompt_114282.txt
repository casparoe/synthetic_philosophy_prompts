I chair the selection committee for our foundation's annual prize for poetry in translation, and I need the written opinion I'll circulate before we vote. Our rules are spare: the prize goes to the best book of poems published in English translation during the previous calendar year; the committee's decision is final; a prize must be awarded every year. They say nothing about what the committee must be able to read, or whether we are honouring the poet or the translator. That silence has become the whole problem.

The shortlist holds three books. Two are translated from French and Arabic, and at least one of us reads each of those languages. The third is translated from a language no remaining member of the committee reads: our one reader of it recused herself in March, having studied with the poet, and our conflict-of-interest rules bar her replacement. We may not add a juror once the shortlist is fixed, and the printer needs the winner's name in three weeks. Nor do the other two books offer much of an escape — both are respectable, but neither has anything like the reputation of the third, which is by common report the outstanding book of the year.

So the third book reaches us as three documents. There is the English book itself, which most of us find remarkable, though we cannot say how much of that is the translator's doing. There is the translator's note on the original, which we solicit from every shortlisted translator: he calls it the finest book of poems he has read in twenty years, and he also insists, at length, that its effects depend on features English cannot carry, so that his version should be read as a new poem rather than as a rendering of the old one. He stands to gain if we award it. And there is an assessment we commissioned from a lecturer in the language, who describes the original's argument, diction and form in detail, then declines to judge its quality and volunteers only that it strikes her as cold.

Two of my colleagues have dug in. One says we have a book of English poems in front of us, we have read it, and we should judge what we read. The other says that if the translator is right, we have never encountered the book the prize names, and that awarding it would be to certify a poem none of us has read — a counterfeit. Underneath the procedural question is one I keep failing to settle: whether a person can know that a poem is good on another person's say-so, and what a prize asserts publicly when it says so.

Please draft the opinion I will put before the committee. It should recommend a specific course of action, with a date, and if the recommendation is anything other than awarding the prize to the third book, say plainly what we should do instead. Give the reasoning we could defend at the ceremony without embarrassment. Take seriously the strongest version of the position you are arguing against — both of my colleagues are serious people, and I have quoted them fairly. And tell me whether your analysis would change if the lecturer, instead of finding the book cold, had agreed with the translator that it is a masterpiece, or if the translator's note had come from someone with nothing to gain. I don't want a survey of what other prizes do or of what critics have written about translation; I want the argument.
