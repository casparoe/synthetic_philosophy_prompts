ok, I'm in a philosophy of psychiatry reading group and we keep circling the same fight: are the diagnostic categories real kinds or just useful fictions, and does that question even make sense if the categories change the people they get applied to. I want to step back and get the spec first, before we argue about any particular view. So write me the desiderata that any theory of psychiatric classification has to meet. Meaning: if someone hands me a theory -- a realist one, a pragmatic/instrumentalist one, a looping-effects one, whatever -- what does it have to be able to deliver before I take it seriously.

I want each item on the list with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and I want the following in there:

- the phenomena it has to explain. Not just that categories are fuzzy and overlapping, but the specific stuff: comorbidity rates that are absurd if the categories are tracking distinct diseases, the catch-all residual bin at the end of each chapter, the fact that the same person gets different labels from different clinicians in different countries, remission and relapse that dont line up with the category boundaries, prevalence figures for some categories moving by an order of magnitude in a few decades with no obvious change in the underlying population.
- the looping thing. If applying a label changes how people behave, how they describe themselves, what they report, and sometimes whether the symptoms exist in that form at all, the theory has to say what that does to the idea of getting the category right. Does the target move when you aim at it? And if it does, can a category be valid in the way the theory wants it to be? This is the part I most want pushed on, so dont be gentle with it.
- the distinctions it cant collapse: reliable vs valid, useful vs true, individual variation vs category membership, and natural kind vs interactive kind -- if that last distinction is doing real work, say what work.
- what it cant contradict on the empirical side. Heritability numbers for some of these conditions, the messy partial overlap between who responds to drugs and who responds to talk, cross-cultural variation in what gets reported at all, the way some categories look continuous with normal variation and some look like a distinct cluster.
- the pragmatic constraints. Here's the extra thing I want folded in, because I think it's what makes the whole problem hard: the same classification has to serve three or four audiences at once. Researchers want homogeneous categories they can build a biology on. Clinicians want categories that pick out something treatable and that wont wreck the patient. Patients and their advocates want categories that name a real thing they have without reducing them to it, and that get them access to services. Insurers and courts want a decision procedure. A theory that satisfies one audience tends to fail another. I want a requirement on the list about this, stated properly -- something like, the classification has to be able to be one and the same object for all of them, or else the theory has to explain why it cant be and what follows from that.

Then flag any pair (or triple) of the requirements you think cant be satisfied at the same time, and say which one you'd give up and why. And tell me which single requirement does the most work -- the one that, if you take it seriously, kills the most otherwise popular theories.
