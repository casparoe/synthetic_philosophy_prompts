I teach the last meeting of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on evolution and culture: fourteen students enrolled, eleven or twelve usually in the room, ninety minutes, movable chairs, a whiteboard, no projector. By this point in the term they can recite the ingredients of natural selection — variation, heritable transmission, differential reproduction — and they are altogether too comfortable applying them to ideas; several have already told me, with a confidence I find suspicious, that culture obviously evolves in the same way. I would like the final session to make that confidence cost them something. Please design a game for the session whose rules, rather than my commentary, do the teaching. By the end they should have felt in their own choices that what wins in a population of competing ideas is whatever is cheapest to carry, easiest to remember, and most inclined to make whoever holds it pass it on — and that this can be an idea that is a genuine burden on the person who holds it. Keep the framing out of the rules: they should not be told in advance what the exercise is about. And please do not build me a demonstration of how messages get garbled in transmission; drift and distortion should be a byproduct of play, not the point of the exercise.

Two structural demands, because I want the game to reach the parts of the analogy that are hard rather than the parts that are easy. First, the ideas should compete inside people and not only between them: whatever a participant can retain, remember, and act on should be limited, so that taking on one idea costs them another, and at least one of the items in circulation — slogans, habits, tunes, small rules, whichever is easiest to stage — should be costly to its carrier while still out-competing a cheaper and more obviously useful rival, with the participants able to reconstruct afterwards why it did. Second, arrange at least one moment in which a participant ends up holding something close to an idea they never received from anyone, so that in the debrief we can ask whether what looked like inheritance might have been convergence, and whether the game can tell the difference. Materials should be ordinary: index cards, tokens, a timer; no money, no phones, nothing that needs printing in colour.

Give me the rules, the starting materials, and a round structure with timings that fit the ninety minutes, then a debrief that maps particular moments of the game onto the corresponding parts of the evolutionary analogy and names the places where the analogy strains. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I am especially worried about a round in which everyone has converged on the same idea and the whole thing goes flat — and what I should do when it does. Be frank about which parts of the picture the game cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply from the front of the room, and say what to do about the three or four students who will treat the exercise as a joke about the internet.
