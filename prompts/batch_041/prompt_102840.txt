I have drafted a short essay and would welcome detailed comments on it. I am less interested in encouragement than in objections: I would rather be told that a distinction collapses than that it is promising. Please write your comments in British English.

The essay:

**Obfuscation leverage and the limits of debate**

Debate as a safety technique rests on an asymmetry. Two agents are asked to argue opposing answers to a question, and a weaker judge decides which is right. The hope is that this converts a hard problem — working out the answer — into an easier one, since the judge need only recognise the better argument when it is placed before them. This is usually justified by analogy with proof: finding a proof may be hard, but checking one is easy, so an agent that knows the truth can expose an agent that does not.

I want to argue that the operative asymmetry is not proof-checking but something I shall call obfuscation leverage. Define the leverage of an argument as the ratio between the cost of constructing it and the cost of locating its defect. An argument has high leverage when it can be produced cheaply and can be dismantled only at great expense. Where leverage is high, debate may be worse than a single model answering directly, because debate does not merely permit such arguments; it rewards them. The dishonest debater need only find one cheap construction that survives inspection, while the honest debater must locate the specific defect in whatever has been offered. The judge, meanwhile, may mistake the presence of an opponent for genuine scrutiny, and so discount the residual doubt that would otherwise attach to an unexamined answer.

It is useful to separate two forms of obfuscation. Local obfuscation buries a defect in a single step: a false lemma, a suppressed premise, a leap that the argument needs and cannot supply. Local obfuscation is vulnerable to the standard remedy of asking for more detail, because the detail must eventually expose the step. Global obfuscation is different. Here no individual step is false; the defect lies in a definitional or framing choice, so that the conclusion follows from the premises only by stipulation, or the question is answered by being re-described. Global obfuscation is immune to the request for more detail, because more detail is precisely what it produces most cheaply. The honest debater who tries to refute it is drawn into a dispute about the meaning of terms in which the dishonest debater has already chosen the terms.

If this is right, then the natural remedy is not better arguments but better commitments. I propose what I shall call a commitment ledger: before the exchange begins, each debater must state at least one falsifiable commitment — a claim whose negation the judge could check in bounded time — on which their answer depends. A debater who cannot state such a commitment is not defeated but is recorded as having given no answer at all. The effect is to shift the contest from who can persuade to who can be pinned down. A globally obfuscated argument, on this rule, cannot win, because it can be neither pinned down nor shown to follow.

Two worries press against this. First, in domains where the truth is a matter of degree, an honest debater may be unable to offer a crisp falsifiable commitment, and the rule may therefore penalise candour and reward the confident simplification. Second, the distribution of obfuscations is adversarial and non-stationary: a judge trained on the obfuscations it has seen will be met with obfuscations of a kind it has not. The commitment ledger may buy time rather than safety.

There is also a tempting optimism I do not share. One might hold that leverage is bounded in practice because constructing a convincing obfuscation is expensive for any agent. But leverage is a ratio, and the cost of locating a defect can be raised independently of the cost of producing the argument. If both debaters are optimised against the judge, there is no reason to expect the ratio to remain favourable.

The test I would want, then, is comparative rather than absolute: take a judge of fixed strength, and measure its error rate on a question when it receives (a) a single answer, (b) two answers without an adversarial exchange, and (c) a full debate. If debate does not reduce the judge's error rate relative to the alternatives, it has failed on its own terms, whatever the transcripts look like.

4. Does the commitment ledger help, or does it merely relocate the difficulty into the judge's capacity to check commitments — a capacity the weaker judge was assumed not to have?

6. Finally, suggest one thing to add and one thing to cut.
