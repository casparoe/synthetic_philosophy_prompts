I'm a science and ideas journalist, and next month I'm presenting a long feature of mine at a departmental seminar in classics and ancient philosophy. My thesis is that the Stoic revival's organising idea — sorting experience into what is and isn't within our control — is not a simplification of the ancient doctrine but a quiet inversion of it: in the texts, the distinction marks out where responsibility lies, in our judgements and acts of assent, whereas the modern version uses it to mark out where serenity lies, and in the process drops both the claim that externals are indifferent by nature and the providential cosmology that made that claim intelligible. I open the piece with the first line of the Enchiridion as most readers meet it — 'Some things are in our control and others not' — and build the argument on the distance between that word and what I'm told the Greek, something like 'what is up to us,' actually marks. The room will be: two Hellenistic specialists, one working on Epictetus and one on Stoic determinism and the compatibilist account of assent; a Roman historian who suspects the popular revival is really a reading of Marcus Aurelius's private notebook and Seneca's political career; two ethicists who are sympathetic to the revival; a dozen graduate students; and, at the back, the woman who runs the Stoicism reading group that served as my case study. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I'll actually get, in the order they'll come and in each objector's own vocabulary, each flagged for how much damage it does to the thesis if I fumble it; what I should concede in the first five minutes to take the wind out of the predictable ones; a two-minute reply to each, with an honest note on where the reply is soft; and the single question I'm hoping nobody asks. Assume I have no Greek and can check nothing on the spot: give me arguments, not a reading list, and tell me plainly which two or three of my claims I should cut from the talk before they are used against me. Keep the whole sheet short enough to hold in one hand at the podium.
