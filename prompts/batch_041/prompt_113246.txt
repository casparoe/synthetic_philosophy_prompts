I sit on a small applied machine-learning team. We are building an assistant that assumes control of a user's calendar: after roughly six weeks of passively observing which invitations she accepted, declined, or moved, and how she used the gaps left over, the assistant fits a model of what she prefers and then proposes — and, under a blanket opt-in she granted at the start, books — the following week's schedule. Two of my colleagues, Dana and Marcus, spent most of a planning meeting arguing about what that fitted model actually is. I stayed out of it, and I genuinely cannot tell who had the better of it, so what I want is an adjudication of their exchange rather than a general essay on preference learning.

The system, concretely: it sees accept/decline/move decisions and calendar gaps, it fits a single scalar model over schedule features (how many meetings, when, with whom, how much contiguous free time, how many context switches), it assumes she usually takes the option she likes better but with some randomness, and it then maximizes the fitted score subject to her hard constraints.

1. Dana: "The fit does not identify her preferences and cannot. For any set of choices, there is a preference model under which those choices were the best available ones — that is nearly what fitting means. So whatever tells you which model to pick is not the behavior. It is the prior you smuggled in, or the simplicity penalty, or the assumption that she is mostly rational with a little noise. Her behavior is consistent with a great many stories — she treasures mornings alone, she hates the friction of declining, she is protecting a reputation for being available — and the data cannot separate them. You are not recovering her preferences. You are selecting one story and calling it hers."

2. Marcus: "Every empirical inference is underdetermined by its data. If 'more than one model fits' were fatal, it would be fatal to psychology, to economics, and to the part of physics that infers forces from motion. The question is not whether the fit is unique but whether it predicts her choices in situations we have not seen. That is testable, and it is the only thing we could ever have wanted."

3. Dana: "It is not only about unseen situations. She is choosing inside machinery we built. If she declines 8 a.m. meetings, part of the reason may be that the assistant has been absorbing the consequences of her declining. We are reading a signal our own system has already shaped. And then the model gets used to maximize. A model that predicts her behavior is not the thing she wants; it is a proxy, and if we optimize it hard we will find every gap between the proxy and the target. Fit 'she dislikes declining' and it stops offering her anything. Fit 'she values unscheduled time' and it clears the calendar of the meetings that are her job. Predicting well and serving well are different objectives, and the second is the one we are graded on."

4. Marcus: "Then keep her in the loop. The model proposes, she vetoes, and the vetoes are data. That is not circular; it is how delegation works. If the proxy is off, we learn before it does damage and we correct. And refusing to model her preferences does not avoid the problem — it hands her calendar to whatever defaults we hard-coded, which are our preferences wearing a lab coat."

5. Marcus, later: "Or do not optimize at all. Imitate. If she would have declined this, decline it; if she would have booked it, book it. Imitation cannot run away from her the way an optimizer can, because it is trying to match her rather than to beat her."

6. Dana: "Imitate what — her policy, or her goals? If it is her policy, you reproduce her mistakes, including the ones our assistant caused, and the assistant can never do anything she would not have done, which is most of the point of having one. If it is her goals, you need a model of her goals to know what to imitate, which is the model you were trying to avoid, and the moment you use it to choose among options she never faced you are maximizing a proxy again. Imitation relocates the problem; it does not solve it."

7. Dana, on the veto: "And the veto is not a clean oracle. What she endorses depends on what she knows and on how we framed the option, and she can be wrong about what she will want next month. So either your target is 'what she would endorse,' in which case the assistant has an incentive to manage her endorsements — to show her the schedule in the frame that gets the yes, to let her grow used to the arrangement — or your target is 'what she would endorse if she were fully informed and thinking clearly,' which is unobservable and is a substantive claim about what is good for her, not a fact we can read off her. You have smuggled in a normative ideal and called it a training signal."

8. Marcus: "I will take that. It is a normative choice. So is the alternative. Saying 'whatever she says now is authoritative, even if she is confused' is also a normative choice, just an unexamined one. My complaint is that you treat the presence of a value judgment as a defect in my proposal when it is a feature of every proposal, including yours. Better to make it explicit and let her set it than to hide it in a default."

9. Dana: "Fine — but that is an argument for transparency, not for the claim that behavior tells us what she wants. And notice what your fix costs. If the assistant must ask before anything consequential, it is not doing the job; the entire value was that she would not have to think about it. So the veto is in tension with the function, and neither of us has said how to trade them off. The loop is worse than a nuisance, too. Because her choices are partly responses to the assistant, the data we learn from is partly our own output, and a model trained on that loop can settle into a schedule that is stable — she stops resisting it — without being anything she would have chosen from scratch. That is not noise around a fixed target. The target is moving."

10. Marcus: "The loop is the strongest thing you have said, and I would answer it with data we did not generate: hold out a period, run a small randomized experiment, or simply ask her about outcomes we did not cause. But the identifiability complaint still does no work. Nobody promised a unique model. The promise was a useful one, under a stated prior, with a human able to correct it. 'It is underdetermined' is true of everything and therefore rules out nothing."

Please adjudicate this. I want a verdict on the moves, not a summary. Specifically:

- Sort the disputes into empirical, conceptual, and normative ones, and say which apparent disagreements dissolve once they are sorted that way.

- Give me a verdict on the central question, which I take to be this: in a delegation system of this kind, is it coherent to describe what the assistant learns as the user's preferences, and if not, what should we say it is learning?
- One narrow question I want answered directly: Dana says the assumption that the user is mostly rational, with some randomness, does the epistemic work. Is that right, and does the choice of how much randomness to allow change what the assistant is entitled to conclude about her, or only how confident it should be?
- If their disagreement is really several disagreements bundled together, pull them apart and tell me who wins each one, and who has the better of it overall.
