Write me a short story about a craft tradition where nobody's allowed to invent new designs — everything has to be a variation on one inherited design, and all the creativity lives in the knobs.

So the setup: there's a workshop lineage (you pick the craft, just make it physical and specific — boat hulls, violins, loaves, I dont care, but I want to be able to feel the material) with a founding spec that's treated as fixed forever. Makers are allowed to adjust a small set of named parameters — thickness here, rise there, whatever the craft gives you — and nothing else. On top of that, each maker gets a lifetime deviation budget: a ledger of how far they're allowed to drift from the ancestral spec in total, and unused units can be gifted or bequeathed to apprentices. So the whole tradition is people spending their budget extremely carefully, and arguing about what counts as spending it.

The story should turn on the protagonist discovering something about the space of knobs itself. Use whichever of these actually generates the best story, or something better: a parameter everyone treated as adjustable turns out to be pinned by something deeper; a quantity everyone treated as fixed turns out to have been a knob all along; the same design survives an enormous twiddle; or it falls apart from a twiddle so small that nobody can see why. And the sting should be that the founding spec was itself somebody's variation on an earlier spec, which nobody ever counted against anybody's budget.

I want it to take seriously what makes a variation a variation of *this* thing rather than a new thing, and whether the maker's sense of how far you can push it is something they discover or something they're upholding by convention. Dont end with a fable-moral spelled out. Let the craft and the counting do the work. 1500 words or so.
