Fourteen months ago I became one of five trustees of a small family foundation, and we make grants across three areas that our staff score on a single impact-per-dollar scale: global health, animal welfare, and work on long-term risks from emerging technology. The scale is convenient, and I have come to believe that it conceals the whole problem. I hold substantial but less than full credence in two broad moral outlooks, and they do not merely disagree about numbers. One treats animal suffering as a cost of the same kind as human suffering; the other gives human beings a weight that no animal interest can outweigh; and neither supplies a scale on which the other's reasons can be expressed. Our staff's comparisons are therefore not measurements but the artefact of a choice that nobody has defended, and our next allocation round is in the spring.

So I went looking for what the literature on decision-making under moral uncertainty says, and found a dispute that has run for two decades without much movement. One camp treats moral theories as sources of cardinal stakes and asks what minimal sort of comparison is needed to run expected-value reasoning across them, distinguishing comparisons that are structural or definitional from ones that are substantive and contested. A second holds that many theories supply only ordinal rankings, so expected value is undefined and we need a decision rule that uses less information, at the price of looking either absurdly cautious or absurdly insensitive. A third says the framing is a mistake: what to do when you do not know which moral view is correct is itself a first-order moral question, and the decision-theoretic apparatus is an imported formalism that presupposes the commensurability it is supposed to deliver. A fourth worries that any such procedure will be swamped, because a minute credence in a theory with enormous or infinite stakes will dominate everything else. Running underneath are unresolved disputes about how to individuate theories and distribute credence over an open-ended family of variants of each, and about whether credence is even the right attitude to take toward a moral outlook, as opposed to some non-doxastic commitment of a different kind.

I would like a diagnosis, not a verdict, and I am not a philosopher by training, so I will add the citations myself. Which of these disagreements is substantive, which is verbal, and which are two groups answering different questions under one heading, one about the theory of rational choice and one about the nature of moral commitment? My own hunch, offered as a hypothesis, is that the formalists and the deflationists are not really contradicting each other but answering different questions, and that this is why the exchanges feel inconclusive. Is the impasse instead a hidden formal problem, an aggregation problem of the kind that tends to come with an impossibility result? Or is it a disagreement about whether there is any fact of the matter about the magnitude of moral value apart from a theory? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a representation theorem fixing which decision rules survive on ordinal information alone, a distinction that dissolves the puzzle cases, a demonstration that the question is ill-posed, or nothing at all? Characterize each position by what it actually claims, and defend the diagnosis against the most obvious objection to it.
