I write a column on science and ideas, and I am at work on a piece about a question that can sound either trivial or unanswerable depending on how it is phrased: when a thought leads to another thought, or a belief to an action, is the thought doing any causal work, or is all the pushing happening at a lower level? I want to build the piece around a thought experiment Douglas Hofstadter develops in I Am a Strange Loop, the careenium, because it lets the question be posed in a setting where nothing is at stake except the description.

The careenium is a vast flat arena containing an enormous number of tiny magnetic balls — the simms — that careen around, collide, and occasionally stick together into clusters. The clusters form, dissolve, and re-form. The individual simms are chaotic in their details, but from a distance you can see stable higher-order formations, the simmballs, that seem to track features of the world outside the arena and to interact with one another in regular ways. The walls of the arena alter their bounciness in response to outside conditions, so information from the environment enters the system through the walls. Hofstadter maps the simms onto neurons, the simmballs onto symbols or concepts, and the arena onto the cranium, and then asks whether the simmball-level patterns are doing any of the pushing, or whether only the simms push and the simmballs are a useful summary.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind who disagree about this, and who are both good at what they do. One holds that the simmball level is causally real: when a pattern of clumping is what explains and predicts what happens next, and when intervening on the pattern is the only tractable way to change the outcome, calling the pattern a cause is not a figure of speech. The other holds that this cannot be right: every simmball-level regularity is fully realized by simm collisions, and if the simms already determine what happens, then citing the simmball adds a description without adding a cause; the pattern may be indispensable to us, but indispensability is epistemic, not causal.

Neither should be a strawman. The realist should not claim that simmballs are spooky forces; the exclusivist should not deny that simmball-level predictions are useful or that we often cannot do better. They should press each other on what pushing is supposed to mean, on whether the debate is substantive or merely verbal, on what sort of evidence could tell in favor of one side, on whether the simm-level details are even in principle trackable, and on whether the simmball level might be the only level at which intervention is possible. I would like the dialogue to contain genuine back-and-forth: each should respond to the other's actual formulations, concede what seems right, sharpen where the other has gone wrong, and land at least one point the other cannot easily dismiss. If they end without agreement, that is fine; I would rather they end with a clearer sense of what the disagreement is than with a forced resolution.

Set the dialogue on a long train ride after a workshop, or over a late dinner; I do not need much scene-setting. Aim for about 1,000 to 1,200 words. Keep the tone philosophical but readable — the column's audience is educated general readers, not specialists. Please do not have either character cite the literature by name; I want the arguments to stand on their own.
