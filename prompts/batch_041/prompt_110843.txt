I'm putting together a reading group on echo chambers and epistemic bubbles for next term, and I want to get the ground rules straight before we start, because every paper I read seems to be solving a slightly different problem. Before I wade through any more of them, write me the criteria list: what does any theory in this area actually have to be able to do before I should take it seriously?

Put each requirement on the list with the case or consideration behind it. Roughly what I want covered, though you should add what I've missed:

- the cases it has to get right. Mine, off the top of my head: (a) the person who's never heard the other side purely by accident and would change their mind tomorrow if they did; (b) the person whose community has taught them outsiders are liars, so counter-evidence gets discounted before it arrives; (c) the person who reads widely but whose sources all trace back to one origin, so the breadth is an illusion; (d) the person who's isolated with a false belief, knows it, and is miserable and hunting for better information; (e) a minority holding a true view against a hostile mainstream, which your theory had better not file under 'bubble'; (f) the same formation whether it's self-selected or imposed from outside.

- the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse: mechanism (no exposure vs. active discrediting) as against outcome (holding a false belief vs. having a closed way of forming beliefs); how it looks from the inside vs. from the outside; individual-level vs. structural; harm to what you believe vs. harm to how you go about believing.

- anything it has to stay consistent with, and anything it can't just assert.
