I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've ended up on the policy side of content moderation for a platform that operates in both the US and the EU. I keep running into the same disagreement with colleagues: the Americans say a rule against hate speech is unworkable because it turns on the content of what someone said, while the Europeans say their law turns on the circumstances and effects of the utterance, not the opinion itself. I suspect both sides are working from a picture of the free-speech tradition that neither has actually read closely, and I want to check that suspicion against a specific passage rather than against slogans.

Here is the passage, from John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, chapter 3:

"even opinions lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act. An opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor, or that private property is robbery, ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer, or when handed about among the same mob in the form of a placard."

Help me understand what is actually doing the work here. My questions, in order of how much they bother me:

First, walk me through the argument of the passage as Mill seems to intend it, defining the terms that carry weight — "immunity," "positive instigation," "mischievous act" — and say which of them is load-bearing.

Second, is this a content restriction in disguise? The proposition about corn-dealers is identical in the press case and the placard case, so the restriction seems to attach to the mode and audience of delivery rather than to the idea. But if the state may punish a true proposition because of what an excited audience will do with it, I can't tell whether that's a principled distinction or a "heckler's veto" with better manners. Does Mill have a way to keep those apart, and does the distinction survive if the audience is not excited but merely receptive?

Third, and this is the part I most need: can this passage carry the weight that European-style hate speech law puts on it? My understanding is that much of that law targets expression that does not incite violence or any immediate act at all — it attacks the standing or dignity of a group. Mill's example seems to require a downstream act and an audience already primed for it. If the harm being legislated against is the degradation itself rather than the riot, is Mill's line even the right tool, or am I importing a framework that answers a different question?

I'd like a close reading, not a survey of the debate, and please flag where you're reconstructing Mill's intent rather than reading it off the text.
