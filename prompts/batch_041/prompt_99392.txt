Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I'm a novelist — three books, none of them philosophy — and a friend who runs an upper-level undergrad seminar on ethics has roped me into writing one of the exam questions, because she says her students can recite Nozick's experience machine in their sleep and she wants something they can't have seen before. So what I need from you is: the question itself, a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme that actually separates the A's from the B's by naming the moves each one has to make. It's a 45-minute question, worth 25 marks.

Here's the thing I've been chewing on (it's in the novel, which is why it's on my mind). Everyone trots out the tank, everyone says they wouldn't get in, everyone concludes hedonism is dead. But I don't buy that the tank isolates what people are actually recoiling from, because it bundles three separate things together: you're being decieved (you'd have false beliefs about your own life), you're not really doing anything (no real contact with the world), and there's nobody else in there with you (no real relationships). Three different complaints wearing one coat. So I want the question built around a case that pulls them apart. Let me write the case out so you don't have to guess at it:

Call it the solo world. You're offered a machine that puts you into a world that is real, not simulated — you actually walk on real ground, you actually build real things, your hands really move, everything you make is really made and everything you learn is true, nothing is faked anywhere. But you're the only person in it. There is no deception and no unreality, just solitude, and you are happier there than you have ever been. (There's a harder version where the world is full of other people who are behaviorally perfect but have no inner life at all, but let's leave that to one side and keep it to the solo world.)

What I want the students to do is tell me which of those three bundled features the anti-hedonist intuition is really tracking, and whether the hedonist can answer. Specifically:

(1) Separate the three things the tank fuses — truth of belief, real agency, and other people — and say exactly which one the solo world isolates, and whether the intuition still fires when the other two are gone.
(2) Say what the hedonist has to claim about the solo world to keep hedonism standing, and whether that claim is independently plausible or just a patch bolted on to save the theory.
(3) Deal with the objection that the whole intuition is status quo bias — that people refuse the tank because it's unfamiliar and because we've been taught that the word real is a magic word, not because they've discovered anything true about welfare.
(4) Say what a desire-satisfaction theorist and an objective-list theorist each owe here, and whether either of them gets off any easier than the hedonist does.

Please write the question in the register the students will actually see, so not the rambling way I've just laid it out, then the model answer, then the marking scheme. In the marking scheme be specific: name the move that earns a solid B, name the extra move that turns it into an A, and name the particular mistake that should cost marks — I'd rather it be brutal than vague. Marks per move should add up to the 25. And one hard constraint: no reading list, no citations, no titles. It's a closed-book exam and I don't want them guessing at names.
