I'm a novelist — four books in, literary fiction, mostly close third and first person — and I've got a craft problem that I'm fairly sure is a philosophy problem wearing a hat, so I want to try handing it to you in the only vocabulary I'm actually fluent in and see what survives the trip.

The book I'm drafting has a house in it. Not a haunted one, a smart-ish one: a thermostat that learns, a fridge that nags, lights that anticipate. The narrator is a woman clearing out her dead sister's place, and she talks about the house like a difficult relative — it wants the windows shut, it thinks she's asleep, it prefers the other sister, it's sulking. Some of that is deliberate free indirect discourse, so the reader genuinely can't tell whose words those are: hers, the house's, or mine. That ambiguity is the whole engine of the book.

Here's the philosophy I keep circling. Dennett's vending-machine case — the one about whether a machine that takes coins can be said to believe the thing in its slot is a quarter — and his line that the marks on a shopping list only mean something because of the person who wrote the list. Versus Searle, who says no, hold on, there's a real difference between intentionality that comes from a mind that has one and intentionality that's borrowed from somebody who uses the thing. Original versus derived. I've got the shape of the disagreement, but I've never read either of them properly and my working vocabulary is entirely author / narrator / character / reader, plus POV, free indirect, unreliable narrator, dramatic irony, the implied author, and the old chestnut about a character who takes over.

So: restate that whole fight, as faithfully as you can, in my vocabulary. Author, narrator, character, reader. Where does the intentional stance land — is it just the reader's willingness to take up a character as a person? Is my narrator's mind the original intentionality in the room and the character's the derived stuff? Does the shopping-list point mean my sentences are the list and I'm the list-writer? I suspect the narrator is the interesting empty slot here and that neither of them has a word for her, but I might be flattering myself.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want: what carried over cleanly, what you had to fudge to make it fit, and what in their argument has no counterpart at all in author/narrator/character/reader — and is that because my vocabulary is missing something, or because their disagreement is thinner than it looks.

Two honest-answer requests. One: don't tell me the translation is elegant and leave it there. If it only works by smuggling in something from my side — like a quiet assumption that the author is God, which I don't think I hold but probably do — say so. Two: if my hunch about the narrator being a fourth kind of mind is just me mistaking a literary device for a metaphysical category, tell me straight, and tell me what I'm getting wrong. I'd definately rather hear that the question is confused than get a tidy answer that flatters it. And it bugs me alot, so if the whole thing can't be done well in my vocabulary, say that instead of doing a polite bad version of it.
