I'm a TA for a small AI safety seminar and I've been handed the take-home grading this term, which I did not exactly volenteer for, so I'd like a second opinion before I put a number on this one. The unit we just wrapped was on control — the stuff where you take seriously that the model might be actively working against you and ask what you can still guarantee anyway. The handout I wrote framed it like this: a control protocol is a wrapper around a possibly-adversarial model (an untrusted model U does the work, a trusted model T watches it, a human gets pulled in when suspicion crosses a threshold); a threat model is the list of things the protocol is allowed to take for granted; and a safety case is the argument that runs from the threat model to "no catastrophic action." The one thing I kept hammering in lecture is that the threat model is a decision about what you refuse to assume, not an observation about the model — you don't get to quietly slip in "U is basically fine" and then present the result as a control result.

The question was: "Protocol P: U drafts each action; T scores its suspiciousness; anything over threshold goes to a human who can reject it or send it back for a rewrite; the team has human budget for four escalations per hundred actions; U knows P in full, since we don't do security through obscurity. Threat model: U is misaligned and deceptive, U can spend as much of its own inference as it wants planning a single action, and one successful catastrophic action is enough for U to get what it wants. A red team given U and P tried two hundred attack policies; none of them produced a catastrophic action at matched usefulness, and the best one landed just under the threshold. Assess the safety case for P against this threat model, say what the red-team result does and does not establish, and say whether a clean result here would be evidence that U is aligned." The student wrote about a page, and stripped down to its claims it runs like this: control and alignment are different enterprises and control doesn't have to wait for anyone to solve alignment; the red-team number only means anything at matched usefulness, so a protocol that buys safety by tanking usefulness has shown nothing; the escalation budget caps the damage, because with four escalations per hundred actions at most four percent of the actions can be the bad kind, which bounds the failure probability; the remaining gap in the safety case is closed by T being trusted, since T went through the same alignment training as U and passed the same evals, so we know it isn't scheming; and if P survives a red team drawn from the strongest available models, that's decent evidence U isn't scheming, though she grants it isn't proof.
