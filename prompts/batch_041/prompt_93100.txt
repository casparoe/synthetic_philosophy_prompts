I'm a PhD student in cog sci, and I've been trying to figure out what to make of the alignment folks' HCH / factored cognition setup. The rough idea: you define an idealized deliberator recursively — it takes a hard question, breaks it into easier subquestions, sends each to a copy of itself, and aggregates the results — and HCH is the fixed point. I get why that's a convenient toy model for scalable oversight. But I keep wondering whether it's an idealization of deliberation at all, or whether it's an idealization of something else (a bureaucracy, a market of subagents, a proof-checking pipeline) that happens to borrow the word. Two things bug me. First, real deliberation isn't just resource-unbounded; it's also non-decomposable in ways that matter — I reframe the question, I notice the subquestions were the wrong ones, I hold the whole thing in view and that's where the insight comes from. Second, HCH assumes the decomposition is answer-preserving and that checking is easier than generating, and those aren't neutral assumptions — they're doing a lot of the work. Write an essay on whether HCH/factored cognition is a defensible idealizaton of deliberation. Weigh the case that it captures something genuine (thinking as recursive decomposition, with verification as the load-bearing primitive) against the case that it idealizes away the phenomenon. Reach a conclusion and defend it — don't just say it depends on what you want it for. If you do think the idealization is legitimate for some purposes and not others, commit to which purposes those are and why the line falls where it does.
