I teach a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar on ethics, sixteen students, and I want to run a single-session activity that makes them feel the full force of the challenge Plato stages in the Republic through the story of Gyges -- the shepherd who discovers a ring that makes him invisible, seduces the queen, kills the king, and takes the throne -- before they ever hear it called a challenge. The usual classroom treatment flattens this into a single question about whether you would still be good if no one could see you. I want the activity to carry the two halves of the actual problem and the way they interlock: first, that when conduct becomes unobservable, the sanction that supposedly keeps us just is removed, so anyone who stays just seems to be doing it for some other reason; and second, and more uncomfortably, that reputation can come apart from desert in both directions, so the person who is just but widely taken to be unjust is treated worse than the person who is unjust but widely taken to be just. My students reliably nod along at the first half and miss the second, so the mechanics should force them through both. The philosophical pressure I care about is the difference between justice being good for its own sake and justice being good only through its consequences -- and I want them to feel how quickly a system that rewards the appearance of justice rewards appearance alone.

Please design the activity as something runnable, not a lecture with a game bolted on: rules, roles or teams, a way to represent conduct and reputation separately, a payoff scheme that uses points or tokens rather than real money, a round structure that fits comfortably inside 90 minutes with time to spare, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the two halves of the problem. The design principle I care most about is that the idea should show up in what they do rather than in what I say: arrange the incentives or roles so that they encounter the temptation, the divergence between what they did and what they are taken to have done, and the pull of buying reputation outright, and only then name the machinery. In the debrief I want you to tell me where in the sequence each of those moments landed, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it (I am especially worried that students will simply behave well because they know a professor is watching, which would gut the whole thing), and, frankly, which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself -- I suspect a game scored in points cannot show whether anyone would be just with no reward at all, but I would like your judgment rather than my guess. Assume no special equipment beyond paper, envelopes, a whiteboard, and whatever tokens you specify.
