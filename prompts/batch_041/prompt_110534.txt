I spent thirty-five years running clinical trials before I retired, and I have come to moral and political philosophy late. I have been reading my way into the literature on paternalism and nudging — choice architecture, defaults, opt-out schemes, the label libertarian paternalism — and I have the strong impression that the participants argue about verdicts in particular cases before anyone has said what a theory in this area would have to accomplish. I would like the specification first.

Please write me the desiderata for a theory of justified paternalistic interference. Not a defence of any position, but the list of things any adequate account must deliver — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each item. Treat the standard as one that has to be good enough to settle contested policy, not merely to organize intuitions.

I expect the list to cover at least the following territory, though I would rather you tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are the prejudices of a particular camp: the difference between helping an agent secure the ends he already holds and substituting the intervener's ends for his; interventions that operate on an agent's beliefs or deliberations rather than on his actions; the boundary between acting for a person's own good and acting because his conduct is thought offensive or immoral, or because it harms others; the competent adult who knowingly and repeatedly chooses what is bad for him, set beside the adult who says he wishes he could stop; those for whom the presumption of competence fails outright, and the continuum between them and the rest of us; self-paternalism, where there is no second party and the agent binds his own future self; the state as nudger against the shopkeeper who rearranges the shelves; and the requirement, if it is one, that a justification be statable openly to the person it governs — including whether a measure that would stop working once disclosed is thereby discredited.

Three things I especially want. First, mark which requirements are negotiable and which are not, and identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate theories. Second, say whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable; I suspect that a demand for disclosure and a demand for effectiveness will pull against each other, and I want to know whether that is a real tension or an artefact of how the requirements were stated. Third, and closest to my own experience: say what a theory here must be consistent with empirically. I have watched a great many interventions that looked decisive in a pilot turn out to be small, heterogeneous, and short-lived. If a theory's verdicts depend on the size and durability of such effects, that dependence should be made explicit rather than assumed away.

Skip the survey of the literature. I can read the primary sources; what I cannot find is the standard they are all implicitly being held to.
