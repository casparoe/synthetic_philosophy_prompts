I'm in a small reading group that has been working through popular writing on behaviour genetics, and one member has settled on an analogy I keep hearing elsewhere as well: asking how much of a trait is genetic and how much environmental is like asking whether a rectangle's area owes more to its length or to its width. Both inputs are indispensable, he says, neither can be varied without changing the area, and so the question is ill-posed -- and anyone pressing it is either confused or has an agenda. I've noticed that in practice he uses the picture to license considerably more than that. On one occasion it was the claim that asking whether a trait is influenced by genes at all is pseudo-scientific; on another, that a high heritability estimate tells you nothing whatsoever about whether an intervention would work. I'd like a proper audit rather than a ruling on the politics. Set out the correspondence explicitly -- what plays the role of the length, the width, the area, and the rule that area is the product of the two -- and then say where the mapping fails. I'm especially interested in features of the geometric case that do no work in the analogy's home argument but get quietly imported along with it: that length and width are single fixed quantities rather than distributions, that they can be varied independently of one another, and that any area can be reached by adjusting either dimension alone.

Then tell me which of my friend's three claims actually follows -- the modest one, that asking which factor contributes more is ill-posed; the strong one, that the very question of genetic influence is pseudo-scientific; and the practical one, that heritability is useless for predicting whether an intervention will work -- and which of them was being carried by the picture's unstated features rather than by the argument. If the rectangle is a bad fit, I'd like a better analogy, and I'd rather you compared candidates than simply proposed one. A colleague has offered a replacement drawn from industrial statistics: a factory's output varies from batch to batch, and you can decompose that variation into a share attributable to the machines and a share attributable to the workers, so that heritability becomes a variance share in a particular run of production. Does that do better? Say what corresponds to what, whether it repairs the rectangle's problems or merely relocates them, and whether it can underwrite the modest claim without also underwriting the strong one. If neither survives, say what a genuinely illuminating analogy would have to preserve -- in particular how it would have to represent the fact that heritability is a property of a population at a given time and place rather than of a trait or an individual, and that the two inputs are not independent of one another, since an organism's environment is in part a consequence of its own traits.

Be explicit about where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong, and finish by saying what would change your mind. If you conclude that the modest claim survives the audit, tell me what you would have to be shown -- about heritability estimates, or about the analogy itself -- to decide that even that claim is an artefact of the picture rather than a result about the world. I'd rather have a calibrated answer that flags its own weak points than a tidy one that doesn't.
