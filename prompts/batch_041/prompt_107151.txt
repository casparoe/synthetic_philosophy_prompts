Okay so I'm in this seminar on realism vs. anti-realism and we burned a whole session on the knowability paradox — the one where "every truth could in principle be known," plus a couple of innocent-looking principles about knowledge (that whatever is known is true, and that if you know a conjunction you know each of its parts), seems to force the conclusion that every truth actually is known. The whole room treated the only interesting question as whether you restrict the knowability principle, and nobody seemed bothered by whether the restriction is legit. I've ended up landing on a view and I want you to attack it. My view: the restriction isn't a patch, it's principled. The paradox only ever gets going because we let knowability range over claims that secretly smuggle in the very ignorance we're supposed to be explaining away — things like "p, and nobody has ever known that p." Anti-realism was never claiming those could be known. What it should say is: any truth that doesn't already presuppose ignorance of itself could be known by someone. That seems right to me and it seems like anti-realism comes out completely untouched. Tell me where that's wrong.

What I want is the case against it, sorted by how decisive each piece would be. In particular: (a) are there natural, independently-motivated ways to restrict the principle that just fail — either because the paradox climbs back in anyway, or because they're so strong the thesis goes trivial and stops distinguishing anti-realism from plain realism? (b) which structural feature of knowledge is actually doing the work here — is it the factivity, or the closure of knowledge over conjunction, or something about how "known" interacts with possibility — and does dropping any one of them look like a legitimate move or like cheating? I have a friend who insists you can simply deny that knowing a conjunction gives you knowledge of each conjunct, and I honestly can't tell if that's a real position or a dodge. (c) a specific case I keep flip-flopping on: take some fact about a particular person's private life that in fact never gets discovered. Is "that fact was never known" itself something that could be known? If I say yes, does the paradox come back through the back door; if I say no, what's the principled difference between that claim and the general claim that there are truths nobody will ever know? And (d) be honest with me — has the restrictionist move been qualified so much that nothing could count against it anymore, and if so is that fatal or is it just the price of doing anti-realism at all? Also tell me what my restricted thesis predicts that its rivals don't, and which of these defeaters a committed restrictionist would themselves admit to rather than just wave off. Rank them from "this kills the view" to "awkward but survivable."
