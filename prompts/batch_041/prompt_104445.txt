I am presenting next month at a joint session of our logic and computation group and a philosophy of mathematics reading group, with a few quantum-information people attending because the title mentions the physical version of the Church–Turing thesis. I have twenty minutes to speak and twenty for questions, and I want a Q&A prep sheet built for this room rather than for the general reader.

The paper defends a deflationary reading. My claim is that the thesis is two claims that are almost never prised apart. On the catalogue reading, it says that everything we would call an effectively computable function falls inside the class the familiar formalisms pick out. On the constitutive reading, it says that being effectively computable just is being computable by one of those formalisms. The equivalence proofs among the competing formalisms settle a theorem about formal systems and nothing further; they are equally well explained by the hypothesis that all those formalisms were built to hit the same pre-theoretic target, so they cannot support the catalogue reading without assuming what they were meant to establish. The catalogue reading is a universal generalization over an open-ended class — supportable, never provable, not refutable by any single case — which puts its epistemic status somewhere between a theorem and an empirical law without being either. The constitutive reading is a stipulation, and stipulations are fruitful or sterile rather than true or false. What the thesis actually does, I argue, is license us to stop arguing: to say of a proposed procedure that it is not a computation and have the matter closed. The interesting question is what makes that licence defensible. The physical version is a separate claim, hostage to physics, and I argue that it survives only by idealizing away resource bounds — which is precisely what makes it unfalsifiable.

This room will not tolerate hand-waving about the intuitive notion. Three computability theorists treat the thesis as a definition and will want to know what is left to dispute. Two philosophers in the later-Wittgenstein vein deny that there is a determinate pre-theoretic notion for the catalogue reading to be about, and will turn my stipulation/claim distinction against me. A constructive mathematician will say that effective already has a precise sense and that my reading of it is the revisionist one. The quantum people will ask whether continuous-state or supertask-style models of computation, or oracles, break the physical claim, and whether my unfalsifiability charge boomerangs onto the catalogue reading. A graduate student will ask whether I am simply a conventionalist about mathematics, and whether that is coherent.

Prepare the sheet: eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each would do; for each, a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft; what I should concede at the outset; which objections are really about my framing rather than my thesis; the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive; and the question I am hoping nobody asks. I know the literature — do not give me citations, reading lists, or attributions; argue with me instead.
