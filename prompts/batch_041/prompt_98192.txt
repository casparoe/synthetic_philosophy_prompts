I recently staked the central argument of a paper on the assumption that the probability of an indicative conditional — 'if A, then B' — is simply the conditional probability of B given A, and the referee report I just received attacks that assumption with the triviality results: the family of proofs showing that the equation cannot hold unrestrictedly, so that something has to give. Either indicative conditionals are not ordinary truth-conditional propositions at all, or the equation survives only for simple, unembedded conditionals, or only relative to probability functions that already accept the antecedent, or only for special classes of probability functions, or only in a semantics where conditionals do not denote anything.
