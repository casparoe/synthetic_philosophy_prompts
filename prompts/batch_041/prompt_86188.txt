An effective-altruist grantmaking foundation evaluates every application on a single rubric: the applicant must state the intervention's expected benefit in one common unit, such as healthy life-years per thousand dollars, and the foundation then multiplies that figure by an "evidence factor" between 0.2 and 1.0 that reflects how strong the supporting studies are. The same foundation caps any individual grant at fifteen percent of its annual budget, on the stated ground that the foundation may be wrong about its entire worldview, and its program staff are told in private that the top three charities on its list are separated by differences smaller than the noise in its own estimates. I cannot tell whether these three features hang together. What philosophical commitments do they presuppose? What would have to be true about the commensurability of health, political liberty, animal welfare, and long-run existential risk for the common unit to mean anything at all? Does the evidence factor assume that the proper response to uncertainty is to discount an estimate, rather than to widen its range or to fund research that would narrow it? And does the budget cap commit the foundation to a decision rule other than the expected-value maximization it advertises to donors? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, mark the points at which it is philosophically underdetermined, and say what would have to change if the presupposed commitments were replaced. Please write your answer in American English.
