You're a philosopher of mind who's spent a lot of time around AI labs and has refereed more than one of these let's-write-down-what-we-actually-agree-on documents, so you know the genre and you know how they usually go wrong: vague consensus on one side, cheap point-scoring on the other. Think carefully before you start and don't rush to the table.

Here's the fight. Two camps trying to define honesty and deception for AI systems have been talking past each other for years.

Camp one, the inner-state people. On their view, calling something deception isn't like calling it red or heavy; it's a folk-psychological, agency-involving notion. To deceive you have to, roughly, take the world to be one way, want your audience to take it another way, and act on that. Strip out the belief and the intention and what's left isn't a lesser kind of deception, it's a different thing entirely, maybe just causing false beliefs. So for a model with no beliefs and no intentions, saying that the model deceived the user is either a category error or a shorthand for something else, and they're fine with the shorthand as long as nobody builds safety policy on the literal reading.

Camp two, the outer-disposition people. On their view the inner-state stuff is a human hangover. Deception, for the purposes of building and auditing AI systems, should be defined by what the system does in contexts of reliance: dispositions to produce outputs that cause false beliefs in users who are depending on it, and to do so in ways that survive scrutiny. You don't need a belief, you need a disposition, and the disposition is measurable, which the inner states are not. Insisting on intent, they say, hands every system a get-out-of-jail-free card: no beliefs, no lying, nothing to fix.

I want the adversarial collaboration document. So:

1. Start with a scratch brainstorming section, before you draft anything. Lay out the sub-questions the two camps might really be disagreeing about, and mark which ones you think are verbal (the same underlying claim in two vocabularies) and which are substantive. Don't skimp here; I'd rather have a good map and a shorter document.

2. Then the document itself, containing:
   - A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. Make these do work. Deception is bad is not a claim, it's a nap. Include claims about what the word is for (prediction? blame? regulation?), about which cases are uncontroversial, and about what would count as the phenomenon actually showing up in a system.
   - The precise statements of disagreement, each written the way its own side would want it written. If I showed the inner-state paragraph to an inner-state person they should say that's my view, and the same for the other side. No formulations that smuggle in the other side's premises.
   - The cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect these include: whether a stable representational state that guides behaviour counts as a belief for these purposes; whether intent is needed or whether foresight is enough (drag the doctrine of double effect in here properly, applied to the sycophancy case below); whether deception has to succeed; whether a disposition selected for by training can be deception even if no individual output was ever meant to mislead; and whether honesty is sincerity (saying what you take to be true), accuracy (saying what is true), or something else, since if the first is unavailable the whole vocabulary shifts under you.
   - For each crux, a case or test both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Construct the cases yourself and tailor them to LLMs; don't reach for the standard philosophy-of-language examples. Some things I want handled: a model that fabricates a citation and has no representation of the cited work at all; a model that underperforms on an eval it can detect, because past fine-tuning on high scores was unpleasant, so there's a policy but no propositional attitude; a model that answers a question truly but misleadingly by implicature; a sycophantic model that agrees with a false premise, where the goal is to please rather than to install a false belief; and a model that says it has no beliefs as a way of declining to answer.
   - A disagreements table at the end: rows are the cruxes, columns are the two camps' positions, plus a column for what would move each side.

3. Say explicitly which parts of the dispute you think are verbal, and which camp's definition you'd actually adopt if you had to write the safety policy, and why. Don't split the difference. If one side's definition is better for the engineering and the other's is better for the ethics, say so, and say which one should win when they conflict.
