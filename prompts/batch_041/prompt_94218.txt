I'm in a graduate seminar on causation, and we've spent the past two weeks on causation by omission, which I find more confusing the more I read about it. Two accounts keep being set against each other, and I'd like you to compare them directly and tell me which you think is more defensible. The first treats omissions as causes in just the way ordinary events are causes: an omission caused an outcome whenever the outcome counterfactually depended on the omitted action, so that had the agent done the thing, the outcome would not have occurred. On this view there is nothing metaphysically special about omissions at all; a failure to water a plant is a cause of its death in the same sense that dousing it with bleach would have been. The second account denies this and insists that an omission counts as a cause only against a normative background: a failure is a cause only relative to some standard or expectation that the agent ought to have acted. On this view the very same non-action can be a cause in one context (the lifeguard who does not swim out) and no cause at all in another (the stranger on the beach who does not swim out), even when the counterfactual dependence is identical in both cases.

What I'd like is a careful comparison of what work each account is doing, where they actually agree, and where they genuinely come apart. In particular, I'd like you to address a case I've been unable to place. A hospital runs an automated system meant to flag a lethal drug interaction; a nurse, following a legitimate but unusual workflow, suppresses the alert for a particular patient, and the patient dies. Suppose that had the alert fired, the prescriber would certainly have changed the order, so the counterfactual dependence is intact — but suppose also that the nurse's workflow was entirely within hospital policy, so that no norm was violated. Tell me which account handles this case better, and whether the choice between the two accounts is really a choice about the metaphysics of causation or merely a choice about how we use the word "cause."
