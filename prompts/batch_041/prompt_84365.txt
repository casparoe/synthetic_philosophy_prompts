Here's the pattern I keep running into. Whenever the Church-Turing thesis comes up, the case for it goes roughly like this: look at all the different ways people have tried to formalize "effective procedure" — Turing machines, the lambda calculus, recursive functions, register machines, string-rewriting systems — and they all carve out exactly the same class of functions. What are the odds they'd all land on the same class by accident? So we must be onto something real. I've made this argument myself and I still think it has force, but its started to feel like it gets something for free. Nobody copied anybody (some of these were worked out independently), but the people who built them were all chasing the same informal idea, testing against the same little stock of examples, and it wouldn't shock me if any formalism with enough expressive power ends up with the same closure properties for structural reasons. So the agreement seems less like several witnesses separately describing the same stranger and more like several rulers agreeing because they were all cut from the same bar of metal. I've seen the same shape of argument elsewhere too — a bunch of models or tests agree, and the agreement gets treated as the strongest evidence there is, even though they share most of their assumptions.
