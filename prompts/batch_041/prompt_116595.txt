I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on the Historikerstreit and its afterlife, and I want from you a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not a restatement or critique of my own position. Here is what I will be defending. The argument is that the long-running dispute over whether the Holocaust is unique and the parallel dispute over whether it is modern have been run together for decades, and that the confusion persists because the participants never drew a distinction explicitly: the distinction between uniqueness of the event and uniqueness of its explanation. My claim is that the Holocaust can be singular at the level of its mechanism -- the particular administrative machinery, the particular ideological target, the particular sequence of decisions and their timing -- while remaining an instance of a quite generic class at the level of its enabling conditions: bureaucratic rationalization, industrial and logistical capacity, a modern state's monopoly on the legitimate use of violence, and a majority population's willingness to remain indifferent. I argue that once these two levels are separated, most of the apparent contradiction between the camp that treats the event as a rupture and the camp that treats it as an outgrowth of modern processes dissolves, and what remains is a much narrower disagreement -- about which level should bear the moral and the historical weight, and about whether an explanation that cites generic conditions can ever be an explanation of this event in particular. I do not want you to adjudicate that; I want you to tell me what I will be hit with.

The audience matters more than the thesis, so here it is precisely. Nine or ten people. Two are German-trained historians of the Third Reich, one archival and one from the everyday-history tradition; both are allergic to theory, both have heard the modernity thesis before and regard it as an alibi in sociological clothing, and both will reach for the word relativization early. One is a sociologist working squarely in the tradition that treats the Holocaust as a test of modernity rather than an aberration; she will think I am conceding far too much by keeping the event-level singularity, and that I have quietly restored the aberration view I claim to reject. One is a philosopher of history who works on explanation and narrative and who will go straight for the distinction itself, arguing that event and explanation cannot be prised apart this cleanly, since what counts as the event is already fixed by the explanatory interests one brings. One is a scholar of survivor testimony who will ask what work my two levels are doing for the memory of the people involved, and whether the analytic tidiness costs something there. The rest are graduate students, one of them working on comparative genocide, who will press me on whether my framework licenses or forecloses comparisons with other mass atrocities. Assume I know the literature; assume you do not need to cite it and that I will not have it in front of me when the questions come.

What I need, in order: the objections I will actually get from this room, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them -- in their vocabulary, not in mine, and not the objections I would get from a conference of analytic metaphysicians or from a general audience. Order them by how much damage they would do if I fumbled them, taking likelihood and severity together, and say which of the two historians will speak first and why. For each: the two-minute reply I should give, a note on where that reply is soft and what a sharp follow-up would be, and whether I should concede the point up front, hold it, or deflect it to a level of the argument where I can win it. Tell me explicitly what I should concede before anyone asks, since I would rather give ground early than be seen to be forced. Then give me the one question I am hoping nobody in that room asks, and be honest about why it is the dangerous one. Finally, identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session at all.

Throughout, do not give me readings, bibliographies, or citations; I need to be able to answer from the argument as I have stated it. State how confident you are in each of your predictions about what this audience will raise, and single out the one point in the whole prep sheet -- a predicted objection, a reply, or a concession -- that you are least sure of, and say why.
