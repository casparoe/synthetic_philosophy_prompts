I'm in Year 12 at a large comprehensive and I run the school's philosophy society, which meets in a geography classroom on Thursdays at lunchtime for fifty minutes; we get twelve to fifteen people, mostly Year 12 with two or three keen Year 10s who come because a friend dragged them along, and there is no budget at all, so everything we use has to be printable on the school printer or made out of scrap paper and old envelopes. We've spent this term on personal identity and modality — the usual brain-transplant cases, then the fission case with the two hemispheres — and last week somebody asked, half joking, whether they could have come from a different sperm and egg, and the room immediately split into two camps who spent the remaining twenty minutes asserting their intuitions more and more loudly without anyone being able to say what was doing the work. I've read a summary of the doctrine that a person's origin is essential to them, which I gather is usually credited to Kripke, and I can see that it is a real position with real arguments behind it, but I don't want to open by announcing it, because I've noticed that the moment I name a view first everyone spends the rest of the session defending whatever they already said in order to save face.

What I want is a runnable activity — a game with rules and a scoring scheme, a role-play, a simulated bureaucracy, a staged set of forced choices, whatever you think carries it best — that makes them feel the pull of the origin-essentialist claim from the inside and then feel it give way somewhere, so that the idea shows up in what happens rather than in what I say afterwards. I'd like the mechanics to force them through the case I think is genuinely awkward and that nobody in the room thought of: that a single fertilised egg can divide and become two people, so if the origin is the fertilised egg then my twin and I share an origin, and origin cannot be the thing that makes me me; and, mirroring it, the worry that if the origin is the particular sperm and the particular egg, then identity turns on an event that looks like a lottery, which makes it seem to rest on something arbitrary. I want them to have to commit to a verdict under some kind of pressure rather than merely discussing — a rule that they must certify or refuse to certify identities across possible worlds, a scoring scheme with a cost to inconsistency, a registrar who can reject a filing. One picture I keep half-imagining is a sort of trans-world registry office, where each participant is a person filing a claim about which worlds they exist in and a registrar has to accept or reject it on stated grounds; I'm not attached to that, and if you have a better vehicle, use it, but I'd like whatever you choose to make the cost of each ruling visible to the person making it.

Please give me the rules, the materials and how to make them cheaply, a minute-by-minute timeline for the fifty minutes, the scoring or payoff scheme (no real money), the exact debrief and how it should map the moments of the activity onto the parts of the doctrine, an account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and an honest note on which feature of the position the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief. Please write it in British English, and finish with a final line beginning 'Verdict:' that states in one sentence the feature of the doctrine the activity cannot reproduce — the thing I have to add by hand.
