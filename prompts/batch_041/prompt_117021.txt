I'm a novelist — my last book was set in the twenty-four hours after the Mytilene vote, told from the deck of the second trireme, mostly from the rowers' point of view, because I got weirdly fixated on the fact that the whole thing comes down to a boat not being quite fast enough. Anyway, a classicist who blurbed the book has invited me to give a paper at her department's colloquium on Athenian democracy next month, and I've made the mistake of agreeing to give an actual argument instead of just reading a scene and taking questions about my prose. The argument, roughly: the three standard exhibits of democratic failure — Mytilene, Sicily, the trial of Socrates — usually get explained by the crowd being ignorant, or fickle, or led around by demagogues, and I don't buy it. What actually links them, I want to say, is an asymmetry between how cheap it is for the assembly to decide something and how dear it is to un-decide it. The decree against Mytilene cost an afternoon; undoing it cost a night of rowing and a crew, and it barely landed. The Sicilian expedition couldn't be recalled because by the time anyone had second thoughts the fleet was already gone. And a jury verdict is the one decision a city makes that has no second ship at all — there's something about the amnesty after the Thirty making the impiety charge the only way to reach him, but I don't trust my memory there so don't build anything on it. I've been calling the general thing reversal cost in my notes, and I have a second mechanism I'm much less sure of: Nicias trying to kill the Sicilian proposal by demanding an absurdly large force and then getting the force, so that the deliberative move designed as a veto becomes the mandate. Also, I'm working from Thucydides and Plato as I remember them from twenty years ago, so if I've got a detail wrong, flag it rather than quietly writing around it.

The room will be about a dozen people and I know roughly who: two or three Thucydides people, an epigrapher who works on Athenian decrees and thinks literary sources are a menace, a couple of political theorists who read the Greeks for sortition and direct democracy, and one philosopher who teaches the Apology and has probably heard every amateur theory of the trial there is. Prep me for the Q&A. I want the objections I'll actually get from this room, not the ones I'd get from a general audience or a book club, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them — the epigrapher and the Thucydides person will not object in the same words even when they're making the same point. Order them by how much damage they'd do to the paper, not by how likely they are; a rare question that guts me matters more than a common one I can shrug off. For each: a reply I could actually deliver in two minutes, and a candid note on where the reply is soft or where I'd be bluffing. Tell me what I should concede up front, in the first five minutes, so I'm not spending the whole hour on ground I never needed. And tell me the one question I'm dreading and haven't prepared for — I'd rather see it coming.
