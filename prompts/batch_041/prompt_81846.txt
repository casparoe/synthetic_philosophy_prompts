Write a dialogue between two philosophers of logic about the problem of logical omniscience. Aim for roughly 1,500–2,000 words. The dialogue must be self-contained: no citations, no references to published work, no names of authors. Everything either speaker needs must be stated or argued inside the dialogue itself.

The two speakers:

The Modeler works in the standard semantics in which an agent knows a proposition just in case that proposition is true in every world the agent treats as possible, and in which the agent's knowledge is closed under logical consequence. They regard this as an idealization of the same kind as a frictionless plane or a market with zero transaction costs, and they hold that a formal semantics is in the business of representing what an agent's information rules out, not of representing the psychological episodes by which the agent comes to appreciate it.

The Critic studies reasoning as it is actually performed and holds that closure is not a harmless smoothing of the target phenomenon but an erasure of it: the very thing we wanted a theory of — drawing conclusions, failing to draw them, noticing that two beliefs conflict — is what the model makes impossible. The Critic is not a skeptic about formal methods and must be given the strongest version of their position, including a positive proposal of their own.

Requirements for the exchange:

1. The Critic opens with the concrete failure: in the standard semantics, an agent knows every logical truth and every consequence of anything it knows. The Critic should make this vivid with a case — an agent who knows the axioms of a small system and has not derived a theorem, or who knows each of several premises without knowing their conjunction. The Critic must preempt the "mere idealization" reply rather than wait for it.

2. The Modeler replies by drawing at least two distinctions and must commit to which one does the work: (a) closure under the rules the agent has actually adopted versus closure under all validities; (b) the agent's information ruling out a possibility versus the agent's access to that information. The Modeler should argue that the semantics models information, not access, and should say what, on their view, we lose if we give up closure — ideally with a case where dropping closure lets the model represent something incoherent as a genuine epistemic state.

3. The Critic presses: if the Modeler retreats to "information, not access," either the truth condition on knowledge is being weakened or knowledge has become a dispositional or behavioral notion. The Critic should force the Modeler to say which, and show the cost of each. The Critic should also raise factivity directly: an agent who has not yet derived a truth that is in fact a consequence of what it knows — does the model say it knows it, and if not, what does it say instead?

4. Each of the standard repairs should be raised by one speaker and its price exposed by the other: (i) adding impossible worlds, at which the semantics is not closed under the logic being modeled, so that an account of what is true at such worlds is needed and the threat of explosion returns; (ii) relativizing knowledge to an agent's awareness or to a restricted vocabulary, at the cost of making awareness an unanalyzed primitive or knowledge non-factive; (iii) treating knowledge as derivation within a resource bound, at the cost of turning epistemology into complexity theory and making "knows" depend on a chosen notion of cheapness. Let the Modeler defend one of these as the least bad rather than dismissing all three.

5. At least once, one speaker should construct a small explicit model — two or three worlds and two atomic propositions is enough — and walk through what the semantics predicts about what the agent knows, then modify it to display the point at issue. The other speaker should respond to that specific model, not to models in general.

6. Both speakers should address whether the problem is a problem for a definition of knowledge or only for the use of that definition to model agents. One of them should propose a criterion for when an idealization is legitimate — for instance, that it is legitimate when the phenomena of interest are invariant under removing it — and the two should disagree about whether closure passes that test, with at least one concrete comparison to an idealization drawn from another science. The other should either accept the criterion and fight over its application, or argue that the criterion is rigged or vacuous.

7. Each speaker must concede at least one point and then show that the concession does not rescue the other's position. No strawmen, no talking past each other: every reply must engage the specific claim just made. The dialogue should not end in agreement or in a polite stalemate; each speaker should close with a positive statement of their position, and it should be left as an open, precisely stated question which of them has the better account of the case from requirement 1.
