You are a formal epistemologist and game theorist who has also worked on the philosophy of disagreement, and I would like your help setting a research agenda rather than compiling a survey. I have just begun a three-year fellowship with one funded postdoc, roughly a day and a half of my own week, and a small travel budget. My training is in Bayesian confirmation theory, so I am at home with probability, information partitions, and elementary fixed-point arguments, but I am not an economist and there is no modeler on the team. I have decided to spend the fellowship on the formal machinery underneath Aumann's agreement theorem rather than on the crowded normative debate about what the theorem implies for the steadfast view in peer disagreement; my working hunch is that whatever is still live is upstream of that debate, in the modeling assumptions themselves. Please brainstorm the space of open problems first, before you commit to any ordering, and be careful to distinguish questions that are genuinely open from questions that merely happen not to have been addressed in the literature you happen to remember. Also distinguish open questions from questions that are open only because they are intractable or ill-posed; I would rather be told that a thread is a dead end than be handed a promising-sounding agenda that dissolves on contact.

Now prioritize. Which open questions or lines of work should we actually take up, in what order, and why? For each, tell me what makes it upstream of other work in the area, how tractable it is for two people with my background using pen-and-paper tools, how neglected it currently is, and what specific development over the next year would move it up or down my list. Please flag anything you think is overrated. Three threads keep surfacing in my own notes and I cannot decide how to weight them. The first is the partitional scaffolding: in the standard proof, the region where the two posteriors differ cannot be common knowledge, because any proposition that is common knowledge must be a union of cells of the finest partition that is coarser than both agents' information partitions, so each agent's posterior is pinned to the common prior conditional on one and the same cell. That looks to me like a fact about the model rather than a fact about disagreement, and I want to know how much of the theorem's bite survives if the scaffolding is weakened — say, if only the occurrence of the announcement is common knowledge and not its content, or if common knowledge is replaced by common belief with probability one. The second is the agent who cannot even conceptualize some of the possibilities the other is entertaining, so that what she has is not a partition of a fixed space at all; I want to know whether agreement remains a theorem in that setting, becomes an artifact of a modeling choice, or simply fails to be well-posed. The third is constructivity: the theorem appears to tell me that agreement is a fixed point without telling me how to reach it, and the iterative results I know that do reach it seem to lean on the state space being finite. Which of these three, if any, is the red herring?

Finally, I want the ordering defended on stated criteria — importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other questions depend on the answer — with the trade-offs between those criteria made explicit, and I want to know what evidence or result would reshuffle the ranking. For the top two items, sketch the shape of the result I would be aiming at, so that I can judge whether it is a real target or a mirage, and tell me honestly which of them is more likely to be settled in three years and which is the kind of question that swallows a career. Do not give me a bibliography or a list of names to look up; I want the argument for the ordering, the criteria you used, and where you would expect the strongest objection to your ranking to come from.
