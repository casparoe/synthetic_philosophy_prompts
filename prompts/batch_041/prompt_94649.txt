I have no training in philosophy, but for several months I have been arguing with a friend about what we are actually claiming when we say that a thing has a disposition — that this glass is fragile, that this sugar is soluble, that this wood is flammable — and I have reached the point where I want the specification before I look at any more proposals. So please write out, as carefully as you can, the desiderata for a theory of dispositions: the full list of things any adequate account must be able to do — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it has to respect, the findings it cannot contradict — with the particular case or consideration that makes each entry a requirement rather than a mere preference, and with your own judgment about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of one camp that another camp could reasonably reject. I am not after a metaphysics for its own sake; I want an account good enough that I can tell, in ordinary cases and in strange ones, whether a disposition claim is true. I care most about the list and the reasoning behind each item, so if you close with a short scorecard of how the main families of answers fare — the view that a disposition is just a counterfactual about what the object would do, the view that a disposition is really its physical basis under another name, the view that dispositions are irreducible and fundamental, and any others you think deserve a line — please keep it brief, because the list is the thing I want to sit with.

The reason I am stuck is that the obvious account, on which being disposed to do something is just a matter of the thing's doing it under the right trigger, seems to fail on cases that are easy to describe and hard to accommodate. A glass can be fragile while a guardian angel has arranged that the instant it is struck it will be made unbreakable and then restored, so that it never breaks although nothing about its fragility has changed; I have heard that kind of case called a fink. A glass can be fragile and actually struck while swaddled in foam, so that it does not break, and the fragility seems untouched; that one, I gather, is a mask. And a perfectly sturdy glass can be rigged with a firecracker that goes off when it is struck, so that it breaks when struck while not being fragile at all. Beyond those, I have invented three cases of my own that I have not seen discussed and that I suspect are harder. First, a pane of glass whose internal structure is exactly that of ordinary glass but which has, woven through it, a network that stiffens for the duration of any impact and then relaxes, so that it never breaks when struck: is it fragile, and does it matter that the network is part of the glass rather than a wrapper around it, and that the network is itself disposed to stiffen? Second, a board that is not fragile now but is disposed to become fragile if left in the sun, which will dry and crack its fibers: is a disposition to acquire a disposition the same kind of thing as the disposition it will acquire, and what does a theory owe us about how the two levels relate? Third, a crowd that is disposed to stampede when a particular rumor spreads, even though no single member of the crowd is disposed to stampede on hearing the rumor, each running only once others run: is the crowd's disposition a real disposition, and of what, and what must a theory say to earn the right to attribute dispositions to anything other than individual parts?

For every requirement you list, tell me which case forces it and why a theory that failed it would be unacceptable rather than merely unfashionable. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating the popular answers, and — this is the part I most want — whether any two of your requirements cannot be satisfied at the same time. If some pair is jointly unsatisfiable, I would rather be told that the disagreement is really about which requirement to abandon than be handed a tidy theory that quietly drops one of them.
