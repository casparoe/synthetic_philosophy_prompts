Two colleagues in my department fell into an argument after a graduate seminar on the ethics of human extinction, and they have asked me to referee it. Both are careful philosophers and neither was making an obvious mistake, which is why I have not been able to settle the matter on my own. I set out their exchange below in the order it occurred, and I have tried to state each move in its strongest form rather than as it was first put. The question at issue is whether the extinction of humanity would be bad for some reason beyond the deaths it involves.

(1) Lena began with this: the activities that give our lives their point — medicine, scholarship, the pursuit of justice, the care of a craft, the raising of children — are valued by us under the description of being worth doing, not merely worth doing by us, here and now. When I take up such a practice I do not think of myself as its last practitioner; I understand its value as something that does not depend on my continuing. Extinction would make that understanding false. Medicine would turn out to have been an episode with no successor, and the point I find in it now would be a point borrowed against a future that never arrives. So extinction takes something from our present lives, and what it takes is not anyone's life.

(2) Marcus replied that the psychological claim is not merely false but false in a way that damages the argument. There are people who know that what they value will not outlive them: the last member of a religious order that is dying out, a scholar in a field that will not outlast her, a woman restoring a house that is scheduled for demolition, a parent who knows that the family line ends with her child. Such people do not value their work less; they often value it more, and they do not experience it as borrowed. Continuation, then, is not a condition of valuing something. What the argument needs is not a description of how valuing works but a claim about how it ought to work — and that is a different and much more demanding claim.

(3) Lena answered that those cases do not show what Marcus thinks they show. In each of them the practice ends inside a world that goes on. The dying order is remembered and mourned; the scholar's work is read by people who do something else; the demolition is witnessed; the child is loved by someone who will survive her. What these people rely on is not that their practice continues but that there remains a human world in which value can be registered at all. That is precisely what extinction removes. The condition she needs is therefore weaker than the one Marcus attacked, and the examples satisfy it.

(4) Marcus said that if the condition is only that someone be left to register the value, then the thesis has quietly become a claim about being witnessed, and the harm of extinction has become the harm of being unwitnessed. That is either a harm to us — we will be forgotten — or a harm to nothing at all. On the first reading the value of a cure depends on the cure's being remembered, which is false: a cure that works is worth having whether or not anyone recalls it. On the second reading the claim is not a harm claim at all. He added a further worry. If the whole thing is a fact about what we can bear to contemplate, it may explain why we recoil from the prospect of extinction without supplying a single reason to prevent it. Facts about our needs and incapacities are not reasons of the right kind.

(5) Lena said that the direction of dependence had been reversed. It is not that a practice is valuable because it is remembered; it is that the value of a practice is not confined to the episode in which it is exercised. That a cure works is a good thing, full stop, and such goods are not annihilated by being unknown. But their being carried on is a good of the same order: a world in which a valuable practice continues is better than one in which it stops, and better independently of how anyone in either world feels about it. The loss in extinction is therefore an impersonal loss, one that accrues to the world rather than to any individual, which is exactly why counting deaths fails to capture it. She added that the loss is also, in a sense, retroactive: extinction would make of our lives a project that came to nothing, and we ordinarily allow that the fate of what one cares about can bear on the value of having cared.

(6) Marcus granted the retroactivity for relational facts — being someone's ancestor, having one's work completed by another — but said that this concession costs Lena the distinctiveness of her thesis. If what matters is the continuation of valuable practices, then the collapse of a civilization that leaves human beings alive but destroys medicine, scholarship, and law is the same catastrophe, and it need not involve an extinction at all; and if humanity were extinguished but immediately succeeded by beings who carried on every one of our practices, the harm would be largely cancelled, which is a strange implication for a thesis about extinction to carry. Worse, the requirement of continuation has no natural stopping place. If a practice's value depends on its going on, why should one further generation suffice, or a thousand? Either the requirement is arbitrary or it is without limit, and a requirement without limit makes every finite world defective and extinction no worse than the ordinary condition of things.

Please adjudicate this exchange for me, move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible but overstated, and give a brief justification; where a move turns on a premise that the other party has not yet challenged, say what that premise is. Then tell me which single point of disagreement is doing the most work in the exchange, and which of the two is more nearly right overall. Finally, say what both of them are missing — in particular, whether either has drawn, or failed to draw, a distinction that would dissolve the standoff. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want a verdict on the arguments as they were actually made.
