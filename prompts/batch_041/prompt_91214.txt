I am a final-year undergraduate reading philosophy, and the course I am taking this term on the epistemology of credence has just finished its unit on the Sleeping Beauty problem. Our essay is on a topic of our own choosing, and I should like to avoid writing yet another survey of the halfer and thirder positions; a friend in the seminar half-joked that we ought to ask what a philosopher writing before the puzzle existed would have made of it, and I want to take that suggestion seriously rather than playfully. My proposal is John Maynard Keynes, on the strength of A Treatise on Probability, published in 1921, well over half a century before the problem was first formulated. The exercise is frankly counterfactual, but I should like it done under constraint: rather than speculate about Keynes's temperament or invent views for him, I want an argument from what he actually commits himself to in that book, so that the verdict is one his own framework licences rather than one merely attributed to him.

The parts of the Treatise that seem to me to bear on the puzzle are his treatment of the principle of indifference, which he both relies upon and sharply restricts; his insistence that probability is a logical relation between evidence and hypothesis, so that one and the same proposition may stand in different relations to different bodies of evidence; and his distinction between the probability of an argument and its weight, together with his willingness to allow that some probabilities are neither numerically measurable nor even comparable with one another. I should like you to work out what those commitments come to in the sleeper's situation. Does her evidence, as Keynes understands evidence, present her with the kind of symmetry that licenses an application of the principle of indifference, and if so, over which partition: the coin outcomes, the days of the week, or the awakenings themselves? Might the weight of the argument, as opposed to its probability, do real work here, and might Keynes be pushed towards the unfashionable conclusion that neither a half nor a third is rationally required, or even that her credence is not a quantity at all? Does the self-locating character of the question create a special difficulty for a logical-relationist view, given that what she acquires on waking is not a new proposition about the world but something about her own position within it? And please test the reconstruction against variants rather than only the canonical case: the version in which the coin is not tossed at all and she is woken every day regardless, so that the counting argument appears to deliver an answer even though she has learnt nothing; the version in which tails produces a very large number of awakenings; and the version in which she is told that it is Monday.

The essay needs a verdict, so please commit to one, with the support that makes it the most plausible reading of Keynes rather than merely a convenient one, and then give the strongest objection to it, including the objection that a Keynesian answer is not a solution but a refusal to engage with the question. I am aware that some contemporary writers have argued that the puzzle is ill-posed, so I am alive to the possibility that Keynes's position is a version of that charge; what I want to know is whether he has the resources to make it stick rather than merely to change the subject. I have the Treatise to hand, so there is no need for page references or a bibliography; what I want is the reconstruction itself, and a clear statement at the end of which of his commitments he would have had to abandon, or which he would have had to extend, in order to give a definite answer at all.
