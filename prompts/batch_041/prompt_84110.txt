I run a small reading group in a machine-learning safety lab, and this term we have been working through what people usually call the control agenda: the line of work that deliberately sets aside the question of whether a model is aligned and asks instead what protocols would keep us safe if it were not. We have spent weeks on the standard toolkit — trusted monitoring, untrusted monitoring, trusted editing, defer-to-trusted, resampling, and the usefulness-versus-safety frontier that all of these protocols trace out — and we keep arriving at the same uncomfortable observation, namely that the whole edifice rests on a red team that is supposed to stand in for a worst-case schemer, so that a safety case can only ever be as strong as the red team's imagination, and the untrusted policy is in some sense the co-author of the guarantee we are offered against it. To close out the term I have been asked to write something lighter for a departmental blog, and I would rather it be genuinely funny to the four people in the room who know what untrusted monitoring means than vaguely amusing to everyone else.

Please act as a staff technical writer at a fictional frontier AI lab: someone who knows this literature cold, has been handed the job of drafting internal compliance documents, and has no suspicion that anything about the arrangement is strange. Your task is to produce, in that voice, an internal staff handbook — a corporate, deadpan, numbered-clause document of roughly 800 words, with a plausible title of your own invention — in which the lab subjects its own human employees to exactly the protocols it designs for its models, while the models occupy the trusted-monitor roles. Cover at least the following, each of which should yield its own running joke: how an employee is classified as trusted or untrusted, and why the classification is ultimately made by the weaker of two models; what happens to an untrusted employee's work when it is routed through a trusted editor; the use of honeypot assignments; the collusion clause, including how coordination between two employees is detected over a channel the document freely admits it cannot read; the resampling policy; and a short appendix of frequently raised objections that quietly concedes the load-bearing assumption. The escalation should be entirely in the direction of honesty: by the final clause the document should have admitted, without ever saying so, that its guarantees hold only on the good faith of the entity it has declared untrustworthy.

Stay rigorously deadpan — no winking, no narrator stepping out from behind the document, no punchline that names the joke. Every protocol you describe must be a real one from the control literature, and every objection the document accidentally raises must be a real one, so that a specialist recognizes the target as fair rather than merely cute. Do not name any real lab, company, or researcher, and do not put words in the mouth of anyone real; invent names if names are needed. Before you draft, brainstorm briefly for yourself the list of protocols and the specific absurdity each one produces when its logic is turned on people, and pick the four or five that land hardest; then write the handbook itself, and give me only the finished document.
