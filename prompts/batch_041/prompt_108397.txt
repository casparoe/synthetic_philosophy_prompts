I belong to a small discussion group that meets twice a month — a couple of philosophy graduate students, a veterinarian, a retired schoolteacher, and me (I write software for a living). Last session we spent most of the evening on the question of how much the sheer number of insects should weigh in how we treat them, and two members, whom I'll call Ruth and Hal, ended up in an argument that the rest of us could not referee. I had no stake in it and I'm reconstructing it from memory, so please treat the wording as approximate. Ruth's opening was this: insects are probably not sentient, but 'probably' isn't 'certainly'; if there is even a one-in-a-hundred chance that a farmed insect feels pain, and we breed and kill something on the order of a trillion of them a year, then the expected suffering in play is enormous — comparable to, or larger than, everything the animal-welfare movement has ever worried about; so if you accept expected-value reasoning anywhere, you have to accept it here, and insect welfare becomes the largest welfare problem there is. Hal's reply was that you cannot multiply a huge number by a credence and announce the product as a quantity of suffering: that credence is not a physical chance of something happening, it is your uncertainty about which theory of mind is correct, and Pascal-style fanatics use precisely this move with tiny probabilities of huge or infinite payoffs, which nobody thinks is binding.

Ruth came back that fanaticism is a problem about infinite values, and nothing here is infinite — the numbers are large but finite, and finite quantities should be treated as finite; refuse to act on small probabilities of harm and you will ignore every risk you cannot see. Hal said the disanalogy is that for the animals we already protect the probability is not small at all, and that the real trouble is where the argument stops: the same structure tells you to worry about nematodes, about fish larvae, about any tissue that might be doing something we don't understand, and eventually about things nobody believes are conscious — a principle that commits you to treating every grain of sand as a possible moral patient is broken, not profound. Ruth answered that if the argument's conclusion is unwelcome, that is a fact about our intuitions, not about the argument, and that the stakes are asymmetric anyway: if she is right and he is wrong we are complicit in something of astronomical moral significance, whereas if he is right and she is wrong she has merely wasted some effort. Hal said that asymmetry proves far too much, since it can be run for any conceivable entity, and that she had quietly traded 'probable' for 'possible.' Ruth then pressed him: name a credence below which a being's suffering stops counting — if you can't name one that isn't arbitrary, weight by credence and let the numbers do the rest. Hal said that is the trap: there is no threshold in the arithmetic and none in the epistemology, which is exactly why it looks like a dilemma, but what we need is not a number for the probability of a cricket's pain — we don't have one — and 'we don't know' is not a quantity you can multiply by. Ruth said that amounts to giving up on acting under uncertainty at all; Hal said no, it amounts to giving up the fiction that this particular uncertainty has a numerical value, and that when the uncertainty is shaped like a disagreement between whole theories, the response is to look at mechanisms, evolutionary history, neuroanatomy — that numbers are the last step, not the first.

What I want from you is a step-by-step adjudication: take the exchange move by move, in the order it happened, and say for each one whether it lands and why, distinguishing the moves that are clearly right from the ones that are clearly mistaken and the ones that are merely a standoff between different starting points. Then tell me who, if anyone, is more right overall, and — this is the part I most want — what both of them are missing, including whether the way they have framed the dispute is itself the problem. I would rather you tell me honestly where a step depends on an empirical claim about insect nervous systems or on a contested decision about how to reason under deep uncertainty, and say so, than manufacture a tidy verdict; if the disagreement dissolves once the positions are stated precisely, or if I have misdescribed one of them badly enough that the question as I've put it can't be answered as posed, say that plainly rather than working around it. Don't split the difference to be agreeable, and don't pretend to settle the empirical questions from the armchair.
