I'm a tort lawyer — personal injury and clinical negligence, so I spend my life on duty, breach, causation and who ends up holding the loss. A philosopher friend keeps pressing Singer's drowning child on me: the shallow pond, the child, and roughly the principle that if you can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance then you ought to do it, and the distance between you and the child is not in itself morally relevant. I can follow it fine as philosophy. What I want is to see it put through my vocabulary instead.

So: restate the argument as faithfully as you can in the concepts I actually work with — duty of care, acts versus omissions, foreseeability and proximity, the but-for test and intervening causes, remoteness, the reasonable person, a duty owed to a determinate claimant, contribution between joint wrongdoers, and the remedial tail (damages, insurance, who bears the loss in the end). Keep it honest: don't smuggle the law's assumptions in and then call it a translation.

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually want. What carries over cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — and for each bit of residue, whether it's my framework being too narrow (the law is a partial-compliance machine, it isn't in the business of maximal demands) or his argument being less determinate then it looks.

Three things I'd like you to press on.

First, the crowd. The pond in my world is never empty — there's a crowd on the bank, plus an insurer, a regulator and a budget. Singer's version seems to need exactly one person in a position to help. Does anything in the legal kit — joint and several liability, contribution, something like market-share reasoning, the cheapest cost avoider idea — give a principled way of splitting a rescue duty across many potential rescuers? And does his principle say anything at all about that case, or does it quietly assume nobody else is standing there?

Second, the cap. Legal duties are mostly ones somebody can be made to perform, and that caps them: no court makes you give up everything above marginal utility, and the reasonable person is not a hero. Is that cap a defect of the legal vocabulary, or a real constraint on any morality that has to be run by institutions — i.e. a morality that can't be institutionalised isn't worth much?

Third, quickly: where rescue is already a service somebody provides for money — ambulance, coastguard, insurers — does the duty migrate from the passer-by to whoever funds the service? Or does framing it as an individual's duty only make sense in a world with no institutions in it at all?

And be straight with me about what you're guessing at. I keep repeating the line that the common law imposes no general duty to rescue — is that even right any more, and where does it actually stand? Seperate out what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know the area, don't hand me a tidy consensus that isn't there, and say so plainly where you're unsure or where I've got the law wrong.

No reading lists, no case citations, no footnotes — I can't check them and I'd rather have your reasoning. Plain English, please.
