You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist who also writes literary fiction. Please write a short story — roughly 1,500 words — that turns on a genuine, unresolved dispute within the language-of-thought hypothesis: whether the format of thought is sentence-like (discrete constituents, predicate–argument structure, recursive syntax, explicit negation and disjunction) or map-like (an analog, geometric format that represents a whole array of facts at once, but has no obvious way to express negation, disjunction, or arbitrary logical combinations). Begin by brainstorming: identify one concrete situation in which the two hypotheses make different predictions — a creature or system that can represent that one object is to the left of another but apparently cannot represent the converse, say, or that can represent a state of affairs but not its denial — and build the story around that divergence. The philosophical dispute should be the engine of the plot rather than a topic the characters merely discuss: let one decisive scene, and the characters' competing interpretations of it, carry the weight, so that a careful reader could reconstruct the disagreement from the story alone, without being told it. Do not cite or name real researchers, books, or papers; invent any scientists the story requires.
