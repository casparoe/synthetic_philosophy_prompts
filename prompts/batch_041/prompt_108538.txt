So I'm a novelist — three books, a fourth that's definately going to be late — and every spring I get guilted into covering for a friend who runs the intro course Minds, Selves and Stories at the local college while he's off on a residency somewhere. Normally it's fine: I talk about how characters are made out of memory and desire, everybody nods, I go home. But this year he's left me a stack of final exams and a marking rubric I only half understand, and I've got alot of scripts due back to the registrar by Friday, so I'm outsourcing my uncertainty to you. The last unit of the course was Parfit on personal identity — the teletransporter, the scanner that destroys you and rebuilds you on Mars, all that — and the exam question was:

Explain Parfit's teletransporter case and what Parfit thinks it shows about (a) whether the person who steps out of the machine on Mars is you, (b) what matters in survival, and (c) how the variants in which the machine makes two copies, or leaves the original alive, bear on his argument. Be explicit about what Parfit is and is not claiming.

Here's the script I'm staring at. Second-year, usually pretty sharp, clearly wrote it in a hurry:

The teletransporter is Parfit's case from Reasons and Persons. You step into a machine on Earth that scans your body and brain down to the atoms, then destroys you and beams the information to Mars at the speed of light, where a new body is assembled out of local matter in exactly the same arrangement, with all your memories and traits intact. The obvious worry is that this is just a fancy way of dying and being replaced by a stranger who thinks he's you.

Parfit's answer is that the machine is a way of travelling. He's a reductionist, which means he thinks there's no such thing as the self — no ghost in the machine, just a bundle of experiences — so the question of whether the Mars person is really you isn't a deep question with a hidden answer. There are no further facts beyond the physical and psychological ones, so once we know the Mars person has your memories, your plans and your personality, there's nothing left over to argue about. What matters in survival is what Parfit calls Relation R, which is memory continuity: the Mars person remembers your life from the inside, and that's all that survival could ever amount to. Identity in the strict sense is not what matters.

The two-copy version is what really drives the point home. If the machine leaves you alive on Earth and also makes a copy on Mars, or makes two copies, then we can't say both copies are you, because identity is one-to-one and you can't be two people. But R holds to both of them, and it holds just as strongly as it would in the ordinary case, so if R is what matters then the branching doesn't take anything away from you — it's not a disaster, it just shows that the question of which one is really me has no answer and doesn't need one. That's why Parfit says identity is not what matters.

I don't want to just slap a number on this and move on, because I'm honestly not sure how much of it is load-bearing and how much is just confident-sounding prose. What I need is a grade out of 20, and then the line-by-line comments I can hand back to him: which parts earn the marks and which don't, sentence by sentence, so that when I write in the margin I sound like I know what I'm talking about. If a paragraph is basically fine, say so. If a sentence quietly gets something wrong, tell me which sentence and why. I'm paranoid about being fooled by good writing, which is a thing I know about myself, so don't just give me a general impression of the whole thing.
