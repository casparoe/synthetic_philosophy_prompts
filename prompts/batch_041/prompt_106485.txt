I'm a first-year law student who has just started a summer placement in a state agency's office of general counsel, where most of my work is helping to draft and revise statutory language, and the question of how a court will read the words we write has stopped being academic for me. The course I'm taking this term is organized around the textualism/purposivism divide, and I've been assigned a case analysis of Bostock v. Clayton County, decided in 2020, as the test case. The trouble is that the case does not behave like the clean illustration the syllabus promised, and I'd like you to do the analysis properly. Work only from the facts as I give them here, and don't worry about citations; I need the analysis, not a bibliography. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 makes it unlawful for an employer to discriminate against an individual because of sex, and the statute was enacted in 1964 by a Congress that, by all accounts, was not thinking about gay or transgender employees and said nothing about them either way. In 2020 the Supreme Court held 6-3, in an opinion written by Justice Gorsuch and joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan, that firing an employee for being gay or transgender is necessarily discrimination because of sex, on the reasoning that the employer is penalizing a trait or an act it would not have questioned in an employee of the other sex; the ruling disposed of three cases, including one brought by a transgender woman who had been fired by a funeral home. Justice Alito, joined by Justice Thomas, and Justice Kavanaugh each dissented, Alito on the ground that the majority was not applying the ordinary meaning the words had at enactment and was in effect legislating, Kavanaugh on the ground that the ordinary meaning of the phrase does not reach sexual orientation even though sex figures in the causal story.

Treat the two theories as the lens and run the analysis in both directions. First, what does each theory say the Court actually faced here, and what would each have required of it, being precise about the object of interpretation each is committed to -- the enacted words, the enactment as an act, the legislature's intentions, the statute's purpose -- and about whether ordinary meaning is fixed at the time of enactment or at the time of decision. Second, which features of the episode does each theory explain, and which does it misdescribe? In particular I want you to take seriously the possibility that the majority's textualism and the dissents' textualism are both genuinely textualism, and to say what that does to the familiar claim that this decision vindicates textualism as a method. Third, what does the episode show about the theories themselves: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case they never anticipated? Where a theory is simply silent, say so rather than making it speak.

Here is the part I actually want your help with, and where I have a hunch I'd like tested rather than assumed. I keep trying to separate three things that the opinions seem to slide between: the semantic content of the sentence Congress enacted; the communicative content of the enactment, in the sense in which a speaker means something by an utterance, so that the 1964 Congress is treated as having asserted something to us; and the applications that Congress would have agreed to if it had been asked about them. My hunch is that Gorsuch's opinion is best reconstructed as being about the first, that Alito's dissent is really about the second or about the third dressed up as the first, and that purposivism is a theory about the third that keeps borrowing the authority of the first. Test that hunch: tell me where it holds, where it distorts what the opinions actually do, and whether, once the three are sorted, textualism and purposivism remain distinct positions or turn out to be two names for the same underlying disagreement about which of the three is the object of interpretation. Then run the counterfactual: suppose the identical words had been enacted in 2020 rather than 1964, with the same silence in the legislative record. Does either theory's answer change, and if it does, what does that tell us about whether these theories are theories about language at all? I'd rather have a disciplined account that marks the limits of each theory than a survey, and please flag anything in my framing of the facts that you think is doing illicit work.
