In the United States and a number of other countries, the rules governing the paid transfer of human biological material form a pattern that is easier to observe than to justify. Plasma collected for the manufacture of medical products is routinely bought from donors, who are paid for each donation and may donate many times a month; blood collected for transfusion is obtained almost entirely from unpaid volunteers, and the suggestion that donors should be paid for it is treated as a corruption of a gift rather than as a service. Gametes may be purchased, though the payments are invariably described as compensation for time, risk, and inconvenience rather than as a price. A kidney may not be bought, and offering to buy one is a criminal offence. Yet a healthy volunteer may be paid to swallow an experimental drug or to undergo an invasive procedure in a clinical trial, and the same jurisdictions permit people to sell their labour in occupations considerably more dangerous than plasma donation.

Reconstruct the theory of commodification that this pattern presupposes. What is the operative distinction the law and the surrounding norms are tracking — between parts that are integral to the person and parts that are detachable or renewable; between selling a good and selling labour, risk, or inconvenience; between markets that crowd out altruism and markets that merely coexist with it; between transactions that exploit the poor and transactions that merely offend taste? State the implicit view as precisely as you can, then separate what the pattern strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where several incompatible theories would license the same set of rules. Identify any point at which the rules are genuinely inconsistent with one another rather than merely surprising, and explain why the plasma case is the hardest one for the most obvious unifying principle. Finally, say what would have to change in law and practice if that principle were adopted cleanly, and whether the result would be more defensible or less.
