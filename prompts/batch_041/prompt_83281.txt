Ok so I've got an odd amount of free time next year -- no teaching, a small budget, and a supervisor who'll let me chase one thing and only one thing. I've been circling the centipede game and the backward induction paradox for about two years and I'm convinced it's the most underrated knot in the theory of rationality, but every time I go to pick a thread I end up going back and fourth.

Here's what I actually want: treat that year as the budget. Which open questions around backward induction and the centipede matter most, in order? And I mean a defended order, with your criteria out on the table -- I don't want a tour of the area, I want to know which question is load-bearing. Especially the dependency structure: which problems, if someone settled them, would make the others dissolve or get easy, and which ones look central but are really downstream of something else. The point of the year is one paper that makes the rest of a thesis cheap, so weight things by how much else they unlock, not by how much I'd enjoy them.

Threads I keep bouncing between, so you can see the shape of my confusion: (a) whether the common-knowledge-of-rationality story is a real claim about two people or a formal artifact that only looks like one; (b) what a player is supposed to believe at a node that's only reachable if the other player has already done something irrational -- and whether that has a determinate answer at all or is just a choice of solution concept in disguise; (c) the experiments, where people keep going and keep going longer the longer the game is -- refutation, bounded rationality, or evidence the subjects don't share the experimenter's model of the game?; (d) whether any of it says something real about deterrence and reputation (the predatory pricing / chain store story it's supposed to be about) or whether that analogy is loose; (e) a friend's claim that the students' argument in the surprise exam is the very same move as backward induction here -- so if you think the exam can still be a surprise, the students' argument must have a hole, and if it's the same argument the centipede has the same hole. Is she right, and if she is, does it move anything up or down?; (f) something I haven't seen anyone put this way: the paradox only bites when nobody can commit in advance, and the moment you hand the players a binding commitment device the unraveling stops -- which makes me wonder if the real question is about commitment technologies rather than about rationality; (g) a variant where one person occupies both seats at different times, which I'd expect to sharpen things (you know the other player's preferences for certain) but might actually dissolve the standard arguments, since those seem to assume two agents.

Don't just work my list -- if the load-bearing question is something I haven't named, say so and demote mine. And two extras: tell me what you'd cut if I only got six months, and tell me what I'd plausibly learn in that time that would flip the order, including anything near the bottom that maybe belongs near the top.
