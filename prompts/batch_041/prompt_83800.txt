You are a philosopher of material objects who also writes literary fiction. Write a short story, roughly 1,500 words, that dramatizes the problem of material constitution: the apparent fact that a lump of clay and the statue shaped out of it are two things in one place, sharing all their matter, yet differing in what they can survive.

Build it around this situation, which you should flesh out with names, voices, and a concrete setting. A financially desperate regional museum has sold a large unfired clay figure by a recently deceased sculptor to a company that buys industrial clay by weight; the company intends to grind the figure down and re-fire the material into floor tiles. The sculptor's estate sues to stop the destruction on the ground that the figure is a protected artwork, and the company's lawyer answers that the museum sold them clay, and clay is clay. A young conservator who knows the object intimately is called to give evidence, and it falls to her to say what there is in the gallery: one thing or two.

Let the story turn on the metaphysics rather than decorating it. In the conservator's testimony and in the argument around it, I want the strongest available case that the lump and the figure are numerically distinct objects — that the lump existed before the sculptor ever touched it, that the lump would survive being crushed into a ball or having a thumbprint smoothed away while the figure would not, and so on — and I want the opposing counsel to press the obvious objection that two things cannot coincide, made of the same atoms with all the same properties. The conservator must not stop at asserting that they have different properties; she must say what makes the difference, or else honestly give up the two-object view and hold that there is only the clay, with statue-talk being a convenient way of speaking. Make her choice explicit, let it be costly, and let a character who disagrees with her get the best word in.

Before you start drafting, think through the candidate positions and pick the one your story will commit to; then let the story's decisive scene — the moment when a truck is loaded or a restraining order is or isn't granted — turn on that position. Close with a short authorial note of about 150 words saying which view of constitution the story lands on, which rival view it treats most fairly, and what it deliberately leaves unresolved.
