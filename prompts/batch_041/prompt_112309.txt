I run a graduate reading group on classical Chinese thought, and last week one of the students reconstructed the butterfly passage at the end of Zhuangzi's second chapter as a freestanding skeptical argument — not, as I had always taught it, a lyrical epilogue about the transformation of things, but a machine for manufacturing doubt. He put it on the board roughly as follows, and I confess I could not immediately say where it fails.

(1) Any experience I am now having could be a dream: dreams can be as vivid, coherent, and emotionally continuous with the rest of my life as waking experience. (2) So to know that I am awake rather than dreaming, I would need some mark or criterion available to me from the inside that dreams cannot counterfeit. (3) There is no such mark: whatever I might cite — the coherence of the scene, the sense that it is too real to be a dream, the ability to read text, the pinching test — has occurred, or could occur, within a dream. (4) Therefore I cannot know that I am awake, and never could. (5) But nothing in (1)–(3) is specific to dreams. Any hypothesis compatible with my total experience and indistinguishable from the ordinary case by any mark available to me yields the same result. (6) So no belief I have about the world is knowledge. (7) And the symmetry cuts the other way too: my belief that I am the dreamer rather than the dreamed is no better off — which is precisely what the passage seems to concede when it leaves the man and the butterfly unable to say which of them is now dreaming the other. (8) Hence the waking/dreaming distinction, and with it the distinction between dreamer and dreamed, cannot be drawn from the inside at all; it is not a distinction anyone is in a position to apply. (9) If so, there is no one awake to assert the argument, and no premise that is known rather than dreamt.

I find this compelling step by step and absurd as a whole, and I suspect the student has smuggled in an assumption that proves far too much. My instinct is to save (1), which seems plainly true of dreams, and to save the passage's own closing note about transformation rather than skepticism — that still strikes me as the more faithful reading — and to give up whatever else must go. Tell me which step or steps to reject, and why. Then price the alternatives for me. If the fault lies in the demand for an inner mark in (2), say what it costs to drop that demand, and whether doing so leaves (3) untouched. If instead the trouble is the slide from the bare possibility of dreaming in (1) to the sweeping claim in (5)–(6), tell me exactly where the quantifier or the modality turns illegitimate, and whether a view that rejects the closure or sensitivity assumptions can still honor whatever insight the original passage contained. And if the argument really is valid and the absurdity in (9) is simply the price of the premises, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and what a defensible version of the passage would then look like.
