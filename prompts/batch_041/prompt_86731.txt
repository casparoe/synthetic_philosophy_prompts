I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in an invented aristocratic republic, Valdania, between roughly 1835 and 1875. In my fictional world — as in several real ones — a formal code of honor obliges any gentleman who is publicly insulted to issue or accept a challenge to a duel; to decline is to forfeit standing altogether, and the practice is widely regarded as both necessary and, in some unexamined way, admirable. Over the course of the book, the practice dies. I have come to believe the novel is really about why such things die, and I want a scene that tests the question rather than settles it. Two features of my invented history matter. First, the collapse is abrupt — a decade, not a century. Second, it happens first among the oldest families, the ones with the most honor to lose, and only afterward spreads down to the lesser gentry. Please write that scene for me.

Take the role of a philosopher of history and moral psychology who also happens to write dialogue well, and work carefully. Before you draft anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming: identify the strongest version of each position, name the crux you intend the conversation to reach, and note the temptations you mean to avoid — in particular, letting one character be refuted by the other's rhetoric, or letting the scene end in a bland both-factors-mattered truce.

The scene is a single sustained conversation between two equally intelligent characters.

The first, Aurelia, is a visiting philosopher. She holds that the practice ended through honor itself: no one was ever refuted, but the content of honor changed, so that continuing to duel became the dishonorable thing and refusing became the honorable one. On her view, moral argument worked not by defeating honor but by recruiting it.

The second, Balthasar, is a political economist and historian. He holds that the practice ended because the interests and the standing incentives that sustained it shifted underneath everyone's feet — the state's growing monopoly on violence, the declining cost of a damaged reputation in a commercial society, the rising cost of maintaining the code — and that the moral arguments were at best a respectable surface over these currents.

Requirements:
- Neither character may be a strawman, and neither may win by fiat. Each must answer the other's actual points, concede what is genuinely strong in them, and press where they are weak.
- Both characters must confront the two features I described — the speed of the collapse and the fact that it began at the top — and each must show why the puzzle is either a problem or an asset for their own view.
- The conversation must reach the real crux: whether these are rival explanations at all, or two descriptions of a single mechanism, and if the latter, which part of the mechanism is doing the causal work.
- Along the way, the characters must engage, by name or by clear description, at least these distinctions: the difference between a change in what people believe is right and a change in what honor demands of them; whether a reason can be causally efficacious without being the efficient cause of the change; honor as a competitive, positional good rather than a private belief; and the awkward fact that the reformers were insiders who had themselves lived under the code.
- Let the characters notice when one of them is merely restating the phenomenon — for instance, that it ended because it became dishonorable to continue may be an explanation or may be a tautology — and make them fight over it.
- End with each character naming the single piece of evidence that would most change their mind. Do not force a reconciliation.

Write in American English, in the register of an intelligent novel rather than a journal article. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to scholarly works; the characters should reason from the situation, not from literature. Aim for roughly 1,500 words.
