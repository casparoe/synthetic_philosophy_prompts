My reading group has spent a term testing Leibniz's ethics against cases, and we have arrived at four verdicts we take to be his, though we have reconstructed them by argument rather than found them stated in these terms.

(1) A man of ample means who declines to rescue a stranger from ruin when the rescue would cost him almost nothing does wrong, though no law is broken and no court could compel him. (2) A man who gives away everything he owns to relieve the destitute, leaving his own household destitute and his creditors unpaid, acts from real compassion but not justly: benevolence must be guided by wisdom, and a wise benevolence would have honoured his prior obligations. (3) A magistrate who is scrupulous in his dealings with his own citizens and wrongs none of them, but who acquiesces in the ruin of a foreign people when it is convenient and lawful to do so, is not just, because the object of benevolence is minds as such and not merely one's own community. (4) A trustee who quietly diverts part of an estate he holds, on the reasoning that the heir will never miss it, in order to relieve the destitute, does wrong.

The fourth verdict is the one that has us stuck, because it admits of more than one reconstruction. On one, Leibniz treats certain claims as a floor that no sum of good consequences may breach. On another, he treats the good of the community of minds as the criterion, and holds that the arithmetic of consequences is always wrong in cases of this shape because it omits the goods of trust, of general security, of example and of the agent's own integrity. We suspect a third reading as well, on which justice is for Leibniz a habit of the will — benevolence ordered by wisdom — so that the verdict turns on the quality of the agent's will rather than on the form of the act or the total of its effects.

The new case is this. A trustee can, once, secretly, with no possibility of discovery or repetition, and with no harm to the heir that she could ever notice, divert a modest sum from a large estate to fund a measure that will certainly save many lives. He acts from genuine universal benevolence, on accurate information about every consequence, and the act will leave no trace in his own character or in anyone's expectations. What would Leibniz say, and what should we say if we want our judgement to cohere with the four verdicts above?

Give his verdict as best you can; set out the candidate rules that fit the earlier four and what each predicts here; say which reconstruction you would back, and why. Then name the further case that would best discriminate between the surviving rules, and state what each predicts about it. Finally, keep the answer honest about its own standing: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among those who know Leibniz's ethics well, do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and tell us if any of our four verdicts looks to you like a misrepresentation of him.
