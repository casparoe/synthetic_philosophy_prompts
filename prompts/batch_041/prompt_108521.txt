You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of modal metaphysics, personal identity, and the philosophy of biology. Think carefully before you answer, and begin by silently brainstorming the strongest version of each position and the most difficult implications of the case below; do not include that brainstorming in your final response.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers. The first defends origin essentialism: the thesis that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg, because the particular gametes that actually united at your conception are part of your essence. The second defends what we may call the pattern view: the thesis that your identity is fixed by the organized biological information your life realizes, not by the particular gametes that supplied the initial matter, so a numerically different sperm-egg pair could in principle have produced you if it realized the same pattern. Neither philosopher should be a strawman; each must engage the other's strongest argument.

The dialogue should turn on a hypothetical technology called gametic reconstitution. The technology takes ordinary somatic cells, reprograms them into gamete-like cells, and combines them into an embryo. Suppose scientists take cells from Alice, correct them so that the resulting embryo's genome and epigenome exactly match those of the zygote from which Alice actually developed, and bring that embryo to term. The origin essentialist says the resulting person cannot be Alice, because Alice's actual gametes are essential to her. The pattern view says the resulting person could be Alice, because the pattern is the same. Let the dialogue explore whether the case exposes a defect in origin essentialism, whether the pattern view can distinguish a person from a mere copy, and whether either side can explain why gametic origin matters if the information does not.

Present the dialogue as a numbered list of exactly five exchanges. Each exchange must contain both speakers' contributions, and each speaker may speak for no more than two sentences per exchange. Do not include an introduction, a conclusion, any citations, or any quotations from existing texts. Write in American English.
