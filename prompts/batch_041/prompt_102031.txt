I have no formal training in philosophy, demography, or economics, but I have been thinking on my own about whether a larger population is better. I keep meeting arguments for pronatalism, against population growth, and about whether adding happy people makes the world better. I want a theory good enough to judge both a government’s pronatalist policy and a family’s decision to have another child, and before I take sides I want the specification: what must any theory of whether a larger population is better be able to do to count as adequate? Please write the desiderata.

I want a list of requirements, with the case or consideration behind each. What phenomena must the theory explain? Which cases must it get right? Which distinctions must it respect? Which findings from biology, economics, or demography must it not contradict? Which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp? Flag any pair or subset of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. Then, if you can, say which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular theories. I care more about the criteria than about scoring named views, so keep the weight there.

To make this concrete, here are the cases I have been turning over. First, a technology can create simulated minds who are happy, have rich inner lives, never learn that they are simulated, and can be created in any number without affecting anyone outside. Second, a variant where those minds are happy but their happiness depends on a false belief that they are real. Third, a variant where their lives are barely worth living. Fourth, a variant where creating them uses computational resources that would otherwise create a smaller number of much happier simulated minds. Fifth, a policy that increases population by extending existing lives rather than by creating new people. Sixth, a policy that increases population by immigration rather than by births. Seventh, a policy that adds people who are happy but whose existence changes who else is born, so that the added people replace others who would have existed. Eighth, a policy that adds people who are happy but makes existing people slightly worse off. Ninth, a policy that adds people who are miserable but whose existence prevents a larger number of people from being even more miserable. Tenth, a world where population size increases while average happiness decreases, and a world where the reverse happens.

For each requirement you list, say which of these cases motivates it, or which general consideration does. I especially want the theory to be able to compare worlds that differ both in how many people exist and in how well off those people are, and worlds where the identities of the people differ. It should distinguish making an existing person better off from making an additional person exist. It should not treat people as interchangeable units or as mere means. It should handle uncertainty about whether future lives will be worth living. It should distinguish population size from population density, from total life-years, and from the number of minds. It should respect the thought that adding a happy person is not automatically bad, while also respecting the thought that a huge population of barely happy people is not automatically better than a smaller population of very happy people.

Tell me which of these requirements are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of one camp. Which are jointly unsatisfiable? I suspect the real fault line is between the requirement that adding a happy person cannot make a world worse and the requirement that a world of many barely happy lives cannot be better than a world of few very happy lives, but I want to know whether that is the deepest tension or whether there is another. If the list itself yields a result, say so.

Do not give me citations, reading lists, or names of papers. I cannot look them up, and I want the requirements in plain terms. If you must refer to a position, describe it rather than naming it. A table with columns for the requirement, the motivating case, whether it is non-negotiable or a camp preference, and which kind of theory it excludes would be helpful.
