As a doctoral student in formal epistemology, I keep running into a difficulty with the standard Dutch book argument and would like a careful reconstruction of what a familiar practice presupposes. The argument, in its usual form, holds that an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability calculus is committed to a set of bets each of which she regards as fair or favourable but which jointly guarantee her a loss, and that this vulnerability is what makes incoherent credences irrational. Now consider the ordinary practice of a sports bookmaker. The prices posted across a mutually exclusive and exhaustive field of outcomes sum to implied probabilities well above one — the overround — so that a bettor staking proportionally across the whole field loses no matter what happens. By the Dutch book criterion, that is a sure-loss book. Yet we do not regard the bookmaker as irrational; we treat the margin as a fee, and bookmakers commonly describe their posted prices not as their own degrees of belief but as instruments for balancing their liabilities, adjusting the line in response to money wagered rather than to evidence. A bettor who plays the whole field, by contrast, is exactly the sort of agent the argument is meant to indict.

What I would like is an account of what this practice presupposes. Does the Dutch book argument require coherence as a norm on credence states, or on the prices one is prepared to act on, and does the bookmaker's practice presuppose the first reading while quietly excluding the second? Does it presuppose that the sure-loss requirement is agent-relative — binding only on the agent who would bear the loss, so that profiting from another's incoherence is not itself a defect? And does it presuppose a substantive view about what odds reveal, namely that a posted price is a report of belief rather than a market instrument? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as the material allows, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, identifying which rival views about credence, preference, and price it leaves open, and saying what would have to be revised — in the practice or in the argument — if the presupposition I have attributed to it turned out to be false.
