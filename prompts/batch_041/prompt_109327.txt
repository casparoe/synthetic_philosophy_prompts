ok so I've been going down a Keynes rabbit hole, not the economics stuff but the "what's it all for" stuff, and I want a short story out of it.

Two pieces of his I keep chewing on. First, the essay from around 1930 where he predicts that in a hundred years the economic problem will basically be solved, we'll all be on something like a fifteen hour week, and the real crisis won't be scarcity but what to do with the time. He calls the answer the art of living, and he's weirdly pessimistic about it -- we've been trained for so long to strive and scramble that we won't know how to enjoy purposeless time, we'll panic at it. He also makes this distinction I like: absolute needs (food, shelter, the stuff that can in principle be satisfied) versus relative needs (the ones about feeling superior to the next person, which never get satisfied no matter how rich everyone gets). Second, the memoir he wrote near the end about his early beliefs, where he admits the Cambridge crowd he grew up in took their ethics from Moore and decided the good life was entirely a matter of certain states of mind -- the contemplation of love and beauty and truth, things he called timeless -- and that they basically didn't give a damn about consequences or duty or doing anything, which he later half-regrets as a kind of irresponsibilty.

Write me a short story, 900 to 1300 words, set maybe twenty years out in a mid-sized city where the fifteen hour week has actually landed. I want a protagonist stuck in the seam between those two Keynes's -- the world where absolute needs are met and almost all remaining striving is relative (status, followers, who has the better garden), and the demand to fill the rest of the time with something that's supposed to be intrinsically good rather than instrumental. Maybe they used to have a job that was all about optimizing other people's time and now they have to optimize their own. I don't want a utopia or a dystopia, just the texture of it -- the small daily problem of the art of living, the temptation to convert leisure back into work, the way relative needs sneak back in even when you know they're hollow. And I want the story to take the Moore/Keynes states-of-mind idea seriously enough to test it: is a life of contemplation of love and beauty actually available to an ordinary person, or does it presuppose the very striving it's supposed to replace? Don't name-drop or cite anything, just write the story.
