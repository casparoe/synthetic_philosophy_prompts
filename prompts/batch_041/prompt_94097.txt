My opening move is the one that always seems to sink you lot. Fine — the pain doesn't cause my belief that I'm in pain, the brain state causes both. But then look at what my belief is supposedly doing: it's *about* the pain, it's right about the pain over and over, in absurd detail, and it got there without the pain ever touching it. That's not a knower, that's an extremely lucky commentator. And it gets worse, because on your view that's true of every thought I've ever had about experience, including the thought that epiphenomenalism is true. Some people call this the paradox of phenomenal judgment, whatever — here's my question: how is your theory about experience rather than just humming along beside it? Doesn't the view eat its own tail?

And here's the part I actually want you to chew on, because I think it makes things worse. Suppose someone builds a box that reads the relevant brain state and talks — it produces full, accurate first-person reports about what I'm feeling, in my voice, while I sit there mute. Your view seems to say: sure, fine, the box is doing exactly what my speech centres do anyway, the experience is irrelevant to both. Which would mean the difference between me reporting my pain and a machine reporting my pain is just which wire runs to the mouth. Do you accept that? If you do, in what sense is my report a report *of the experience* rather than a report of the brain state with the experience standing next to it doing nothing? If you don't accept it, tell me which step you're giving up, because it looks like it falls straight out of the view.
