I'm a fifth-year in the history department here, and the chair has asked me to give the student remarks at our centennial dinner next month -- six minutes, right after the dean and right before the fundraising pitch, in a room that'll be about a third emeritus faculty (people who've taught the historiography seminar for forty years and will know exactly what I'm doing up there) and two thirds alumni, donors, and spouses who came for the free wine. The organizing committee circulated a draft of the "official" narrative and it's a lot. It opens with the department's founding in 1926 in two borrowed seminar rooms and ends with us as "a world-leading centre for historical scholarship," and every paragraph in between is a rung on that ladder. The awkward part is that the founding really is embarrasing: the money came from a bequest by a local industrialist whose fortune was built on the mills, and the first chair's inaugural lecture basically argued that our job was to train men for imperial administration. The committee's fix is to make that the "humble beginnings" act of the same three-act play, so the arc becomes bad origins, brave reckoning, enlightened present. Which is precisely the move I've been trained to flinch at, and I keep catching myself writing it anyway -- I drafted a paragraph last night that literally said "we've come a long way since," in the draft where I'm supposedly refusing the ladder. I want something warm and genuinely celebratory, I love this place, I'm not trying to do a takedown, but I don't want to hand the room another ladder.

Here's the stuff I'd like the speech to actually work with, not just name-check. There's the old point (Butterfield, that 1931 essay everybody cites and nobody rereads) that the sin isn't optimism about progress, it's a method: abridgement, pulling out of the past only what looks significant from here, so that earlier people get sorted into forerunners or obstacles, and the ones who lost become foils instead of people who were doing something intelligible on their own terms. That method also pushes the historian toward a morality play -- Butterfield's crack about the whig historian feeling "inconclusive unless he can give a verdict" -- and toward modernizing the dead, so that even after you've corrected the impression you keep sliding back into treating them as us in slightly funnier clothes. The part that interests me most is the meta-level: the story of how historians got over whig history is itself usually told as a progress narrative, we were naive, then came the critique, now we know better, which means the whole thing eats its own tail unless you're very careful. And there's the counter-punch from people who think that "the past for its own sake" is a way of ducking the fact that we always arrive at the archive with present questions, so that the anti-whig pose can become its own kind of quietism. I don't need you to resolve that for me. I need the speech to be honest about living inside it.

So: write me the six-minute speech, addressed to that mixed room, that honors a centennial without building a ladder. It has to be speakable out loud, it should get at least one real laugh (the dean will have already made a joke about "the journey," so something that plays off that is fair game), and it should land on one concrete thing about the founding generation that is neither forerunner nor villain -- somebody doing something that didn't lead anywhere, that we can't recruit to our story. Don't name-drop historiography at the audience; the emeriti should get it from the shape of the thing and the donors shouldn't feel like they wandered into a seminar. And please don't let it curdle into "well, everything is complicated and nothing can be celebrated," which is its own smug move.
