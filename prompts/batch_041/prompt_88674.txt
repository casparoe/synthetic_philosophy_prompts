I'm in a decision-theory reading group and a friend has an argument I cant climb out of. Take the role of a philosopher of probability whos heard every objection to this kind of thing, and brainstorm a little before you commit to a diagnosis.

Heres the argument. (1) A rational agent's degrees of belief have to be coherent: there should be no set of bets she'd accept one at a time that together guarantee she ends up poorer. (2) If her degrees of belief break the probability rules, a bookie can always pull that off — a set of bets, each fair by her own lights, that jointly guarantee her a net loss. Like this: she's 60% sure it'll rain and 60% sure it won't, so the bookie sells her a $1-if-rain ticket for 60c and a $1-if-dry ticket for 60c; she pays $1.20 and gets handed back exactly $1, and each ticket was fair by her own numbers. (3) So her degrees of belief must obey the probability rules. (4) But notice how (2) works: it gets her to take each bet because each one is fair by her own lights. So the argument assumes a rational agent takes any bet that's fair by her lights. (5) Yet a rational person can wave off a bet that's fair by her lights — if the stakes are big enough that losing would wreck her, or if she simply doesn't care to gamble, refusing isnt a flaw. (6) So the argument proves too much: taken strictly, it makes refusing a fair bet irrational, which makes risk-aversion and plain non-gambling irrational. (7) That cant be right.

So which step goes? I'd love to keep (1) and (3) — the conclusion is one I believe.
