I am a novelist, and for three years I have chaired the grants panel of a small hardship fund for writers: one-off payments of a few hundred pounds to authors in acute financial difficulty, subject only to the trustees' rubber stamp. Our charter says the fund exists for misfortune and not for the consequences of a writer's own decisions — a clause now carrying more weight than it can bear. We sit monthly, we read twenty applications in an afternoon, and we decide by intuition; which is to say, by whichever of the four of us is most fluent that day. I have been reading around luck egalitarianism, the view that what a person may claim from others is compensation for the disadvantages she did not choose and nothing for the risks she took on — the distinction sometimes drawn as brute luck against option luck. Please turn that into a procedure we can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure ends in a refusal, and where it must hand the decision to the panel because the question is one of value rather than of fact.

It has to keep two things that the slogans tend to lose. The first is the difference between holding someone answerable for having made a choice and holding her answerable for how it turned out: a writer may choose to leave a salaried post and yet not have chosen the illness that made the gamble unrecoverable, and I want the procedure to say which of those two things the panel is actually judging. The second is that our applications are essays, and our decisions track how well an applicant writes about her own life; a novelist cannot help noticing that we are therefore distributing money according to an unchosen talent for self-narration. Say what a procedure that takes luck seriously should do about that, even if the honest answer is that we can do very little.

Then run it on three cases from last month: a poet whose teaching post ended when her depression became disabling; a novelist who resigned a secure job to finish a book that did not sell and who cannot now make his rent; and a translator whose flat was flooded after she let the contents insurance lapse to save money. Tell me which way the procedure points in each, and be explicit where it returns an answer the panel will hate.

Four writers have ninety minutes and no economist. Mark the points where judgement cannot be eliminated, and end with a single line beginning "Verdict:" giving, in a short phrase, the default ruling we should adopt when the tests leave a case unsettled.
