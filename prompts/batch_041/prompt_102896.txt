I am a philosopher whose work sits at the intersection of ethics and formal methods, and for the last several years I have been preoccupied with the inefficacy objection — the claim that because a single individual's contribution to a large-scale collective harm (a marginal vote, one serving of meat, a few tonnes of carbon) makes no detectable difference to the outcome, that individual has no reason, or at least no duty, to refrain. I have grown impatient with how the debate is conducted in my own field, where the decisive moves are made by appealing to intuitions about thresholds, expected value, complicity, and the distinction between doing and allowing, and I want to see the whole thing rebuilt in the vocabulary I actually trust when I am doing formal work: causal attribution. Concretely, I want you to take the inefficacy objection and its principal replies and restate them in the machinery of causal inference and cooperative game theory — interventions and the do-operator, marginal versus average causal contributions, counterfactuals evaluated with different things held fixed, Shapley values, and variance- or risk-based decompositions of a collective outcome — treating the total harm as a payoff function over the vector of individual actions.

I am reasonably fluent in this vocabulary; I have used do-calculus and Shapley values in my own modelling and can follow a derivation, though I am not a specialist, so please err on the side of spelling out the formal moves rather than gesturing at them. One of my doctoral students recently proposed that we simply define each person's contribution as their Shapley value in the game whose payoff is the harm, and I want to know what that buys us and what it quietly assumes. As you translate, keep the translation honest: do not help yourself to the formal framework's own normative assumptions — that the relevant unit of assessment is the individual, that effects are the currency of moral importance, that a zero marginal effect is a zero — and then present the result as though it were a faithful rendering of what the ethicists meant. Then give me the residue report, which is really the point of the exercise: which claims in the debate survive translation cleanly, which survive only in a distorted form, and which have no counterpart at all in the formal vocabulary — and, for that last category, tell me whether the gap reflects a genuine limitation of causal-attribution talk or whether it is evidence that the original claim was doing less work than it appeared to.

Run the translation at least twice, on two structurally different cases: a threshold case, such as a vote in an election decided by a single ballot, and a roughly linear case, such as the demand that an individual stop eating meat. I want to know whether the two end up looking like the same problem in the formal vocabulary or like two different problems that the informal debate has blurred together. Please write this as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic for emphasis, no tables; the way one would write a long letter to a colleague.
