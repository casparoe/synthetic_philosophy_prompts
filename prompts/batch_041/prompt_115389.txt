I am a novelist — six books, no philosophical training worth mentioning, and a private suspicion that I have been bluffing about this subject for years — and in a fortnight I must lead a seventy-five-minute session at a writers' residency. My audience is fourteen novelists and poets: no science background, impatient with abstraction, and several of them convinced in advance that the whole topic is a fad. What I have been asked to teach is the pairing that philosophers treat as a single move — the strange inversion of reasoning that Darwin and Turing are each supposed to have performed, the claim that competence does not require comprehension. A blind process, or a mechanism, reliably produces results indistinguishable from those of an understanding producer; and the honest response is not to hunt harder for the hidden understander but to see that the question was malformed. I have a whiteboard, no slides, and perhaps forty minutes of my own talking in me.

Do not explain the idea to me; I can read. Plan how I should teach it. Specifically:

- The order the pieces must go in, and what depends on what. In particular: must I first make them feel the full force of the intuition I intend to invert — that competence has to be traceable to a competent cause, that a design implies a designer — or can I open with the inversion and work backwards? Say what I lose if I choose wrongly, and identify the point of no return in the sequence.
- The one case that should carry the whole session. I keep reaching for the thermostat, and I keep reaching for the eye. Tell me which, if either, and what the carrier must have and must lack: it has to make them feel the pull of the opposing intuition before it dissolves it, and it must be something they can hold in mind for seventy minutes without a diagram.
- The misconception map. What they will arrive believing, what they will form as I go, and the precise point in the sequence at which each must be headed off. My own guesses: that Darwin's story makes evolution a matter of luck; that Turing's test is about a machine tricking a judge; that the two inversions together amount to a proof that machines think; that I have merely licensed calling a thermostat a believer; and that the pairing is a loose analogy between biology and computing that dissolves on inspection. Tell me which misconception is the fatal one — the one that, once formed, cannot be removed later — and what I must say early to prevent it.
- The literary parallel I cannot stop reaching for. My audience are writers, so I want to invoke the mid-century critical tradition that cut the author off from the work — the intentional fallacy, the death of the author. Tell me whether that parallel earns its keep or whether it collapses the two inversions into a slogan; and if it does earn its keep, exactly where it belongs and what it must not be allowed to imply.
- The objections they will raise, in the order they will raise them. I expect the mimicry objection, the Chinese room, and the complaint that if this is right then nothing is really anything. Tell me which I should absorb where they stand, which I should park explicitly, and which I should refuse to settle at this level — and say so out loud.
- What to leave out at this level, and how to announce the omission without appearing to dodge.
- One question to put to the room at the end that will show me whether the idea landed rather than merely impressed them.

Give me the plan as a sequence of stages I can follow at the board, with the reasoning for the order made explicit. No readings. End with a single final line, and nothing after it, in exactly this form: Load-bearing idea: <the one thing the whole sequence exists to establish, in under twenty words>.
