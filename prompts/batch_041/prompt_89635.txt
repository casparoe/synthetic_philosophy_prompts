A disagreement broke out at a dinner I attended, between a vision scientist and a philosopher, and I ended up as the bystander they spent the evening trying to recruit.

The scientist opened with what she took to be obvious. Perceptual experience, she said, is delivered by dedicated sensory systems that run off the information reaching the sense organs, and what a person believes or desires does not get to alter what she actually sees, hears, or tastes. If you know the wine in front of you cost two hundred dollars, you may describe it differently, attend to it differently, or draw different conclusions about it, but the taste itself is the same either way; the change is downstream of perception, in judgment or description, not in experience.

The philosopher resisted. He said this only looks like a clean line because she is quietly defining perception as whatever is left over once everything cognition might touch has been subtracted, which guarantees her conclusion. He noted that what a person knows can change where her attention goes and how features are grouped, and that if the resulting experience differs -- if the same face now looks hostile, if the same radiograph now pops out with a tumor that a novice cannot see -- then something cognitive has reached into experience itself. Experts, he insisted, do not merely interpret the same raw sensory data differently; the very features that present themselves change with training.

She replied that the expert cases show the opposite: they are cases of perceptual learning, where repeated practice gradually rewires the sensory system, so the difference in experience is a lasting change in the machinery rather than a belief reaching down into a particular act of perceiving. A belief that arrives this afternoon and instantly recolors this afternoon's experience would be the interesting phenomenon, and the wine case is confounded by attention, memory, and expectation, none of which establishes that the sensory system itself has been penetrated. He answered that this reasoning would let her dismiss every candidate case in advance, and that unless she can say what would count as a belief changing an experience rather than merely redirecting attention, her encapsulation claim is a promissory note rather than a finding. She said that cuts both ways: he cannot simply point at a difference in experience and call it penetration, since the difference might lie entirely in how the person conceptualizes what she senses, and introspection will never tell them apart.

I would like you to referee this exchange rather than take a side wholesale. For each move they make, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right, and briefly explain why. Then say what each of them is missing and where the genuinely open question lies. Please write this as continuous prose -- no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables; the way you would set it out in a letter.
