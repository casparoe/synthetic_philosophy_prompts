I've been covering the science-and-ideas beat for a while, but this year I keep getting dragged into court stories — the AI imagery prosecutions, the e-bike fights, that whole genre — and I've realized the part I actually keep screwing up is statutory interpretation. A ruling comes down, the majority says the text is plain, the dissent says the majority invented a purpose the legislature never voted on, and both of them are reading the same twenty words. I have maybe ninety minutes to file and my editor reads three grafs and then calls me, so what I want from you is a procedure I can actually run, in order, on a statute I've never seen before. I do have a JD but I never practiced, so I know "plain meaning" and "legislative purpose" as slogans and not much past that. Things I'd want the procedure to get its hands around: how to tell when the text genuinely runs out and a judge is really constructing law rather than interpreting it (I'm pretty sure there's a distinction like that kicking around in the originalist literature, interpretation vs. construction); how much weight to give the whole-act context, the rule against reading a provision as dead weight, obvious scrivener's errors, and the current vogue among some judeges for running word searches in giant text databases to prove what ordinary people would have understood; and the difference between the purpose of the particular legislature that voted it through and the purpose of the statute as a thing that just keeps existing while the world mutates underneath it. And I want it to say, at the end, when I should stop pretending this is a text question and just write that the outcome rests on a value judgment the court made — that's the sentence I'm always too scared to type. So: where does the procedure stop with a no, and where does it hand the call to a human?

Then run it on the two things sitting in my notebook, please. First, a state statute that makes it a crime to knowingly transmit an image of an identifiable person who's nude or having sex, without consent — and the live question is whether a photorealistic AI-generated image of a real person who never posed for anything counts. Second, a county ordinance barring "motorized vehicles" from the Greenway Trail, and a guy on a Class 1 pedal-assist e-bike who got a ticket; the county says motor means motor, he says everyone knows what a bike is (some states have a whole class system that treats these things as not-motor-vehicles, which is part of why it's messy). One of these I have a gut feeling about, and I'd rather you not tell me what I want to hear — actually no, tell me straight, and if the procedure lands somewhere that makes the peice I was planning look dumb, say so.

And don't hedge your way out of it. If the honest answer is that textualists would split, fine, but then tell me which side you'd bet on, why, and what single fact would flip it — I'd rather have a procedure that's wrong in a way I can see than one that's so balanced it can't be wrong. Keep in mind I can't look anything up on deadline: if a step requires legislative history I can't get in ten minutes, say what I should do instead, and give me the two-sentence version for when the editor asks.
