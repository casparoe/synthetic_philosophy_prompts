My type-designer friend and I have been going around in circles for like a year about what makes an 'a' an 'a', and I want to actually settle it by first getting clear on what a theory of it would even have to do. Her line is that an 'a' is its skeleton -- the underlying stroke structure, bowl plus stem, the way the strokes meet -- and that everything else (weight, contrast, serifs, slant, whether the bowl is round or square or a teardrop, single-story vs. double-story) is style, i.e. stuff layered on top of the skeleton. My friend who does vision science thinks that's backwards: there's no skeleton, just a family of shapes that all get called 'a' because each is close enough to some prototype or shares enough features with the others, and the skeleton is something we project back onto them afterward. And a third friend who works on handwriting recognition says we're both wrong, an 'a' is a motor program, a stroke sequence, and the shapes are just what different hands and pens do when they run that program. We keep sliding between "what is the letter a" and "what is a typeface" and "how do people actually read" without noticing, so I'm starting to think the question as we've been asking it is ill-posed. Before we go another round I want the actual spec.

So write me the desiderata for a theory of letter identity -- what any adequate account of "this shape is an a" has to deliver, with the case behind each requirement. It obviously has to get the standard stuff right (single-story a as in Futura, double-story a as in Times, the a in blackletter, the a in italic and in cursive, a kid's a, an a that's been through a bad photocopier, an a with the counter filled in). But I want the harder cases on the list too, and I want to know what work each one is doing: a mirrored a, an a rotated 180 degrees, a Cyrillic 'а' that looks exactly like ours, a schwa, a glyph that's an 'a' only because of the letters around it, an 'a' that's been stretched into a logo and barely reads as a letter anymore. And I want the distinctions it can't collapse -- between "this is an a" and "this is a good a," between letter identity and typeface identity, between identity and legibility, between being an a and merely being similar to an a. One more requirement I care about alot, and I genuinely don't know whether it belongs on the same list or needs a separate theory: the account should also say something about style, because the interesting fact is that if you show someone three letters from an unfamiliar font, they can usually tell you what the rest of the alphabet would look like, and they can tell when a fourth letter doesn't belong. That's the thing Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project was trying to model in Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies (McGraw was his collaborator on it, I think), and it seems like it's definately the same question as letter identity from the other direction: if 'a' is just a family of shapes, where does the constraint come from that makes one particular a go with one particular b?

Then tell me which of these requirements are real and which are just prejudices of one camp, and flag any pair I should expect to be jointly unsatisfiable -- my guess is that "invariant across every historical and handwritten a you'll ever see" is going to fight with "must reject the mirrored and upside-down ones," and maybe "learnable from a handful of examples" fights with "covers the whole historical range." Which single requirement does the most to rule out candidates? And then, briefly, how template matching, a feature list, prototype theory, plain family resemblance, the skeleton/role idea, and a motor-program account do against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria -- I'll do the scoring argument with my friends myself.
