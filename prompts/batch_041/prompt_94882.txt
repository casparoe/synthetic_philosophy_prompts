I keep ending up in the same circular argument about Dennett's real patterns and the three stances, and I want to know whether its the kind of fight that could ever be settled. The setup as I understand it: you can take a physical stance on a system (talk about its parts), a design stance (talk about what it's for), or an intentional stance (talk about what it believes and wants), and Dennett says the intentional stance's posits are real when they're the best compression of a system's behaviour -- real the way a center of gravity is real. His critics say that's just instrumentalism with extra steps: either the belief is there or it isn't, and useful isn't the same as true. I've watched smart people hold both lines for years without either budging. So is this a genuine disagreement about what exists, a verbal one about the word 'real', or two camps answering different questions under one heading -- one about prediction and compression, the other about what's actually in the head? Use this case, which keeps tripping me up: a chess engine's evaluation score. It's a compressed summary of the position, it predicts the engine's next move better than any transistor-level story, and it feels like a design-stance object through and through -- yet nobody would say the engine literally believes its score of +1.3. Does that help the Dennett side, or does it show that 'real' has quietly stopped meaning anything? Diagnose the stalemate rather than surveying it, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here.
