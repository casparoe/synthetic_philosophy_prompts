You are an expert in the philosophy of mind and in applied ethics, with a special interest in the moral status of invertebrates and in how one should decide under severe empirical uncertainty about whether a creature has a mind. Please think this through carefully and at length, and begin by laying out your reasoning before you commit to a conclusion.

I am a retired entomologist — my career was in insect physiology and population ecology — who has spent the years since retirement reading moral philosophy. I have been struck by the argument, made by some philosophers, that because insects are so staggeringly numerous, even a very low probability that any one of them is sentient might yield an expected number of sentient beings large enough to dominate our moral priorities. I cannot decide whether I find this compelling or whether it is a trap, and I would like your help with a related, more concrete task.

A close friend, an applied ethicist, sits on the grant committee of a foundation that funds animal-welfare work. Over several years I have watched her vote on a series of proposals, and for each I noted the two quantities that seemed to bear on her decision: how many individual animals the intervention would affect, and her own stated credence that animals of that kind are sentient. Here is the record as I recall it:

1. Reform of broiler-chicken welfare: roughly 10^10 birds affected; credence in sentience near certainty. Funded; she called it a top priority.
2. Welfare of farmed salmon: roughly 10^9 fish; credence about 0.9. Funded; top priority.
3. Welfare of octopuses in aquaculture: roughly 10^7 animals; credence about 0.7. Funded.
4. Welfare of farmed shrimp: roughly 10^12 animals; credence about 0.35. Funded.
5. Improved conditions for a few thousand fish in a laboratory: roughly 10^4 animals; credence about 0.9. Not funded; she said the number of animals was too small for the foundation to take on.

Now a new proposal is before the committee. It would fund a program to reduce the suffering of a widespread agricultural pest insect, reaching on the order of 10^15 individuals a year. My friend has told me, in conversation but never in a vote, that she puts the chance that an insect of this kind is sentient at about one in a thousand. The committee has room for only one new program this year; the alternative is an expansion of the shrimp program, reaching about 10^12 shrimp at the same credence as before.

I would like you to do four things, in order. First, brainstorm the candidate decision rules that might be driving her votes — rules of the form "fund exactly when ___," taking the number affected and the credence in sentience as their inputs — and state them as precisely as you can; I would like at least three. Second, say what each rule predicts for the insect proposal, and check each against all five earlier votes, noting any rule the record already rules out. Third, give your best guess as to how she will vote and why, identifying the rule you think she is most likely running and your reasons for preferring it. Fourth, describe the single further case you would put to her that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, and say what each rule predicts about it and how her answer would discriminate among them. Be explicit about your assumptions and about how confident you are at each step.
