Two years ago the question of how a state should stand toward religion was, for me, a purely abstract one; then I moved to Germany for a postdoc and discovered, while registering my address, that I was expected to declare a religious affiliation, that a declaration would add a surcharge to my income tax, and that the machinery for collecting it ran through the ordinary tax office. I have been trying ever since to work out how the arrangements I thought I knew—the English, the German, the French, and the American—actually differ, and this term I am also a teaching assistant in an undergraduate course on religion and politics. The following comes from the midterm.

The question I set was this: Established churches, church taxes, and secularism are frequently treated as so many versions of a single thing. Distinguish the church–state arrangements of England, Germany, France, and the United States. For each, say what the state gives to religion, what it takes, and what it refuses; and answer directly whether a state-collected church tax is a form of religious establishment.

One student's answer, transcribed:

At bottom the four cases are variations on one question: how far the state will go in recognizing religion. England keeps a formal establishment—the monarch is Supreme Governor of the Church of England, its bishops sit in the House of Lords, and Parliament has a hand in the church's governance. Germany has no single established church, but it does have the church tax, which the state collects on behalf of the major religious bodies and hands over to them; the charge falls on members of those bodies, and one can escape it by formally leaving. Germany is therefore best called a cooperative model rather than an establishment: the state is not the church's head, but it does act as its tax collector. Because the tax falls only on members, and members are free to leave, the arrangement is really no more than a bureaucratic convenience the state provides—it is not support for religion, and it is not entanglement. France went the other way: the 1905 law separated church and state and installed laïcité as the governing principle, which is simply the French name for the separation of church and state—the same idea the Americans wrote into the First Amendment. The United States has no establishment; the First Amendment bars Congress from legislating on the subject of religious establishment, and the Supreme Court has read it to require a wall of separation, a phrase that appears in the Constitution itself. The result is a spectrum: England at one end with a genuine establishment; Germany in the middle, funding religion without establishing it; and France and the United States at the other end, both committed to separation—France more aggressively, since laïcité keeps religion out of public institutions, and the United States more permissively, since it leaves religion to private life.

I would like you to grade this answer out of 100 and to give me the comments that justify the grade, point by point rather than as a general impression. For each substantive claim: say whether it is right, wrong, or right but overstated; where it is wrong, state precisely what the error is and what the correct account would be; and where it is right but reached by a step that does not follow, say so. I care most about the conceptual distinctions here, so be explicit about which distinctions the answer preserves and which it blurs. Tell me which errors are venial and which should cost real credit, and end with the grade and a one-sentence summary the student could act on. Do not let the confidence of the prose raise the mark.
