I want to stress-test a position about sortition that I take to be the strongest reconstruction of the lottocratic case, though I suspect it does less work than its defenders assume. Here it is, in my own words.

The sampling view: a randomly selected citizens' body derives its authority from being a statistical sample of the polity, not from descriptive resemblance (looking like the population) and not from deliberating better than an elected chamber. The body's decision is licensed because a random sample is an unbiased estimator of what the whole polity would judge; legitimacy is transmitted from the polity to the body by the sampling procedure, much as a well-drawn poll inherits the standing of the population it measures. Deliberation inside the body is then either a refinement of the estimate — bringing participants closer to the judgment they would reach with better information — or a detail that carries no normative weight.

Tell me what would count against this. I do not want a general essay on sortition's merits, and I do not want the usual list of objections to lottocracy as such; every entry has to engage the sampling premise in particular. Specifically:

- Which assumptions the view rests on, and for each, what the world or the argument would have to be like for it to fail. Distinguish assumptions that could fail on their own from ones that fail only together.
- The defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a committed defender of the sampling view would concede that it counts against them or would dismiss it, and give the defender's best reply rather than the weakest.
- Whether deliberation refines the estimate or destroys it: if the body's post-deliberation judgment is not an estimate of anything the wider polity holds or would hold under any specifiable condition, say what remains of the sampling premise.
- What the sampling view predicts that a purely procedural account of legitimacy, and a straightforward epistemic-defense account, do not — and whether any of those predictions has already failed.
- Whether the view collapses into a rival. If the sampling logic works, why convene a sample rather than simply poll one; if it does not, say whether anything distinctively lottocratic survives.
- Whether the view has been insulated by qualification, and whether that insulation is a vice in this case.

Format: begin with a table with these columns — defeater (short name); the condition that would make it obtain; the step of the argument it targets (the sampling premise, the representativeness premise, the move from sample to decision, the transmission of legitimacy, or the assumptions about deliberation); severity (fatal / serious / awkward / cosmetic) with a one-line justification; whether the defender concedes or dismisses it, plus the best reply; and what would settle it (an argument, a formal result, or an observation). Order the rows from most to least damaging. After the table, add three to five sentences of verdict on whether the position is well insulated and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Close with a JSON object with the fields "most_exposed_step" (string), "most_decisive_defeater" (string), "collapse_risk" (the rival it most nearly collapses into, or null), "insulation_verdict" (one of "well insulated", "moderately insulated", "poorly insulated"), and "insulation_reason" (one sentence). Assume you cannot search: no citations, no reading lists, no names of scholars; argue from the structure of the view. If you think a different reconstruction of the lottocratic case is stronger, say so in one sentence at the very end, but evaluate the sampling view regardless.
