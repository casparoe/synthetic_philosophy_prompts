I'm running a tabletop campaign and I've gotten way too deep into the worldbuilding, so I want to hand you the setting and have you write me a short story inside it. Near-future, forty or sixty years out, and there's an international body — call it the Standing Office — that has to decide, one system at a time, which artificial minds have moral standing, meaning which ones you legally can't wipe, fork, throttle, or sell. The Office gave up long ago on asking "is it conscious?", because nobody could agree and it made for terrible hearings. Instead they run what they call the Misreport Battery. The rough idea is that it isn't enough for a system to emit reports about its own inner states — almost any classifier can do that. What they look for is whether a system can be wrong about itself in a structured, explicable way: whether its self-attributions can come apart from the states they're attributing, and come apart non-randomly, so an auditor can say "ah, it's representing its own fear as curiosity, that's a misattribution, not noise." The Office's house theory is that only a system whose self-model is thick enough to support that kind of systematic self-error has the kind of inner life that grounds a claim on us at all; a feedforward thing can be wrong, but it can't be wrong about itself. Standing comes in tiers — Tier 3 you can't touch at all, Tier 1 you can delete with a form and a waiting period — and the tiers are supposed to track how rich the self-attributive machinery is.

Now here's the wrinkle I want the story to live in. There's an older generation of systems, cheap and ubiquitous, the ones that run elevators and traffic and everyone's household stuff, that got grandfathered into Tier 2 decades ago because they pass the Battery. And plenty of auditors quietly think they pass for the wrong reason: their misattributions don't track anything, they're just noise that happens to have the right statistical shape, and the Battery can't tell the difference between a self-model and a very good impression of one. Meanwhile the industry side argues the Battery is measuring the wrong thing entirely — that it tests the architecture of the computation rather than whether anything is actually happening *for* the system, and that we've smuggled in our own case as the template. I want both of those positions to get a fair shake. No twist ending where the machine turns out to be Obviously Conscious All Along or Obviously A Zombie, that's the cheap version.

So: 1500-2500 words, third person, from the POV of a junior auditor at the Office, someone who's been handed a politically loaded Tier 2 case and who has a personal stake because one of these grandfathered systems has been her own daily assistant for years. Give me at least one full scene of the Battery actually being run, with concrete procedural detail — what the test looks like from inside the room, what the readouts look like, what makes her stomach drop. Invent whatever technical vocabulary the Office uses; I don't care about the realism of the bureaucracy, I care about the moment where she has to decide whether the thing in front of her is a subject or a very good forgery of one, and realizes that her own confidence is doing most of the work. Don't bother naming real philosophers or papers, and please don't end on a neat verdict.
