There is an argumentative move I keep meeting and cannot put a name to, and I should like to know whether the thing already has one; if it does not, I want to know what it ought to be called and where its edges fall.

Here is the move. Two people are arguing about what ought to be done, and when you press one of them for the premise that would license their conclusion, what they offer is a claim that refuses to settle on either side of the fact/value line. 'You can't run a railway like that.' 'A community that behaves so won't hold together.' 'That is simply not how a family works.' Pressed, the speaker reads the claim as a prediction -- a plain factual claim about what will happen -- and so escapes the charge of smuggling in a value; wanting to conclude, the speaker reads the very same claim as a norm, and so reaches the ought. The premise changes its logical colour to suit the moment, and because each reading is defensible on its own, there is nothing solid to take hold of. I do not think this is just the naturalistic fallacy, since no one here is deriving an ought from a premise that is purely an is -- the difficulty is the premise itself, which never has to declare which side it is on. Nor is it ordinary equivocation, because the shift is not between two senses of a word but along the descriptive/evaluative axis.

Is there a name for this? If so, what is it, and how does it stand to the nearest concepts, the fact/value distinction and the naturalistic fallacy above all? If there is no name, please coin one and define it carefully: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie -- a premise that merely happens to be both factual and evaluative on one side, a premise that exploits the ambiguity on the other, and some test for telling which of the two a given case is. A bare label would not satisfy me; it is the carving I am after.

Please answer in American English.
