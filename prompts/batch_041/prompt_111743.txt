My friend read one of those pop-sociology books about honor cultures, dignity cultures and victimhood cultures, and he's been explaining it to everyone we know. I want to know how much of his version I should actually beleive. Here's his summary:

'There are three kinds of culture and they form a historical sequence. Honor cultures are the premodern, clan-based ones: your worth is whatever other people think it is, so an insult has to be answered with violence, and a woman's chastity is really the family's property. Dignity culture is the modern Enlightenment version: your worth is intrinsic and unconditional — this is basically Kant's idea that persons are ends in themselves — so conflicts get handed to courts and the state instead of settled by dueling, and nobody's standing depends on what anyone says about them. Victimhood culture is the thing thats been growing on campuses in the last decade or so: it's dignity culture gone soft, where people compete to be the most oppressed, and slights that a dignity culture would shrug off become moral emergencies that require administrators to step in. And since dignity culture treats people as ends in themselves while the other two use people as means to reputation or status, dignity culture is obviously morally superior, so the whole sequence is basically moral progress.'

I already half-agree with him that these are ideal types and that real cultures mix all three, and I know 'victimhood culture' is a politically loaded label. What I can't tell is where his summary stops tracking the actual analysis and starts being his own editorializing. Can you go through it more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who works on this stuff would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong — and then whether his moral-superiority conclusion survives even if the descriptive part is fine? If there's a word in there doing double duty, tell me which one and why it matters. And one more thing: is three even the right number of types, or is he leaving one out?
