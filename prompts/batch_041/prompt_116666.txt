I've been roped into giving the commencement address at my old department's graduation this June — it's the philosophy and history-and-philosophy-of-science faculty, the science students, and a gym-full of parents who are mostly there for the photos. The dean's one intruction was "make it about how science actually works," which everyone in the room will hear as the standard stuff: question everything, follow the evidence, be willing to be wrong. I teach this stuff, and half the front row has taught the demarcation problem for thirty years, so if I stand up and say 'science is whatever is falsifiable' they'll smile at their shoes — and they'd be right, because we all know the counterexamples cut both ways. But the parents and the undergrads don't need a lecture on why the problem is supposedly dead; they need something they can actually hold onto.

Here's the idea I want the whole thing built around, so please don't let it slide into being decoration: I think we've been pointing demarcation at the wrong kind of thing. We keep asking whether a claim or a theory is scientific, and then we drown in counterexamples — unfalsifable bits of good physics, falsifiable bits of nonsense. What if the property belongs to a community or an institution instead? The question isn't whether some single paper could be refuted, it's whether the group has a live, working procedure for losing an argument it doesn't want to lose. Astrology and homeopathy come out badly not because no observation could count against them but because in practice nothing ever does; and plenty of respectable science — string theory, chunks of cosmology — sits somewhere on that same axis in a way that has nothing to do with whether any one paper is falsifiable. That's the spine. Everything in the speech should either carry it or get out of its way.

Two things I'm scared of: sounding like I'm sneaking in the opposite cliché ('well, science is just one way of knowing, all equally valid'), which I don't believe, and saying anything the faculty would consider flatly false. So it has to be honest enough for the experts and warm enough that nobody's grandmother feels scolded. About eight minutes, spoken, warm, a little funny if you can manage it — not a seminar. Give me one moment the faculty can chew on and one that lands for the parents. Write me the speech.
