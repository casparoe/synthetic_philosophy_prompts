American plasma donors are paid and whole-blood donors are not; you can be compensated for an egg but not for a kidney; you can raise the price of a generator in July and not in the week after a hurricane; and you cannot sell your vote at any price. I have never been able to read one theory off that pattern, and I would like you to try.

Start with the asymmetry I find most loaded: donation is permitted and praised wherever sale is banned. If the objection were to a good's circulating at all, the gift should trouble us as much as the sale, so the practice seems to presuppose that the wrong lies in the money, or in the standing offer that money makes possible, rather than in any particular transfer. Is that reading right, and is the position stable? Does the pattern presuppose that some goods are inalienable, others merely non-market, and others market-inalienable, with the line drawn by the good's meaning rather than by the seller's need or the buyer's power? Does the ban on vote-selling rest on the same premise as the ban on kidneys, or does it quietly import a separate claim about what a vote is for?

Then sort the candidate accounts -- corruption of meaning, exploitation of the seller, incommensurability, expressive damage -- into those the practice rules out and those it merely fits. Where the pattern is plainly unprincipled, tell me whether that is a defect or evidence of a theory being back-filled, and what would have to change under a straightforwardly commodification-permitting view, or under a straightforwardly blocked-exchange one.
