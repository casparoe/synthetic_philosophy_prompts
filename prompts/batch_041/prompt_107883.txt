I chair the monthly mortality and morbidity review at a mid-sized district general hospital — I'm a consultant paediatrician by background but the panel looks at adult deaths too, and every month we sit down with eight or ten cases where somebody thinks the care fell short. The question we keep getting stuck on is the one that decides everything downstream: did something that didn't happen cause the death? A dose not given, a round not done, a call not made, a referral not sent. We're all clinicians, nobody in the room has read any philosophy since medical school, and at the moment we resolve it by whoever feels strongest about the case, which means the night registrar who breached our sepsis protocol gets written up as having 'caused' a death even when the consultant who was actually on thinks the woman was going to die anyway. I did a philosophy module about fifteen years ago and I remember the but-for test, and something called an INUS condition (a necessary part of a set of conditions that was sufficient but not necessary for the outcome, or possibly the other way round, I'd have to check), and the point that an omission isn't an event at all, so calling one a cause is partly a decision about who to blame dressed up as a finding about the world. I'd like the proceedure to take that last point seriously instead of hiding it. The output goes into a report I have to sign, which goes to the trust board and occasionally to the coroner, so it needs to be defensible to people who weren't in the room.

So: please write me a procedure the panel can actually run, in the room, in under an hour, on a case where the facts are already known and nobody is going to do more investigation. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns a flat no, and where it has to hand the decision back to the panel because the thing being asked is a value judgement rather than a fact — I'd rather it say 'this step is a judgement call' then pretend to derive it. It also has to keep three things apart that we currently mash together: whether the omission caused the death, whether anyone was at fault, and whether a death like this is preventable by us in future, because I suspect we're letting the third one drive the first.

Then run it on three of our cases. One: a woman with sepsis whose first antibiotic was five hours late after the trigger, breaching our own protocol; she died about a day and a half later. Two: a man whose overnight observations were missed for four hours, on a ward whose escalation pathway had been shut for two nights running because of staffing; he arrested at six and died after resuscitation. Three, and this is the one where I suspect the procedure is going to tell us something the panel won't want to hear: an inpatient whose deterioration alert never fired because of a configuration error made two years ago by an engineer who has since left, and who died; the panel has already decided in the corridor that this is 'human error' and wants a name attached to it. I want the procedure to be able to say that nothing caused the death, or that something did but nobody was at fault, or that we can't tell from the notes — and to be clear about which of those is a finding and which is a confession of ignorance. And tell me where it will misfire and what it can't do. Please write it as plain continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered steps, no bold, no tables — the way you'd write it up in an email to a colleague, since I'll be pasting it into our report template anyway and the template has its own formatting.
