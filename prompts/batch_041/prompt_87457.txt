I'm about 60k words into a novel — small Midwestern town, a woman who got out and then came back after the company she co-founded went public, and the town's reaction to her — and I keep hitting the same conceptual wall, so I'm asking you instead of my friends at the residency, who have all given me different answers and one of them has stopped speaking to me about it. Here's the wall. The Jante rules run "You're not to think you are anything special," and the eleventh one is basically a threat that they know something about you — and what strikes me is that these aren't really about envy of your stuff, they're about the humiliation of being seen as having set yourself above, or of being seen at all. Tall poppy syndrome gets described as egalitarianism doing its thing, but the American Dream is also an egalitarian thing, just inverted: it says the tallest poppy is the one who deserved to grow, and if you didn't grow it's on you. So the town in my book could be doing at least four different things when it cuts her down, and they're not the same thing morally: (a) plain envy of what she has, (b) resentment of the norms that decided she'd be the one to have it, (c) a group protecting its own self-image, which is closer to cowardice than to justice, or (d) something I actually think is legitimate — a demand that she not treat the people she left as scenery. I'd add a fifth: maybe the American Dream and Jantelagen aren't opposites at all but two faces of the same demand, that you justify your height or you don't get to have it, and the difference is only who's holding the scissors.

What I want from you is a prioritization, not a survey. Of the open questions in this cluster — the envy/resentment/recognition distinction, whether levelling down is oppression or a form of resistance, whether the Dream is a promise or a threat, whether there's any coherent sense in which a community's disapproval can be *right* — which two or three are most worth the next year of my thinking, and in what order? Defend the order: which questions are upstream of which, which are actually tractable for a novelist who can't run experiments and mostly has other people's arguments and his own ear, and which sound deep but wouldn't change a single scene if I settled them. Tell me what would move a question up or down that list, and please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I've got a shelf of books I haven't read and no time; I just want to know where to point my head.
