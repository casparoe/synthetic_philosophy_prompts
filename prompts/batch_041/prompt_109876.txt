I have been trying to work out, for myself, what it means to say that two possibilities are equally likely when I have no information favouring either, and I keep going in a circle. The thing that set me off was an old puzzle about drawing a chord at random in a circle. I can describe three procedures that each seem to deserve the name "picking a chord at random": choose two points independently on the circumference and join them; choose a radius, then choose a point along that radius with no preference for any position on it, and take the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius; or choose a point anywhere in the disc with no preference for any region, and take it as the chord's midpoint. If I then ask how likely the chord is to come out longer than the side of the largest equilateral triangle that fits in the circle, the three procedures answer a third, a half, and a quarter. Each of them is unbiased in the only sense I can make clear to myself — it does not favour any part of the range of the quantity being chosen over any other part of the same size — and yet they disagree, so at least one of my notions of "no preference" must be doing something I have not noticed.

I have tried to break the deadlock with cases of my own. First, a machine in a workshop drills a hole in a circular plate; the operator sets one dial for the angle and another for the distance from the centre, and each dial is, in the ordinary sense, as likely to stop anywhere in its range as anywhere else of the same width. I am confident the holes are not spread evenly over the plate's area but crowd towards the middle, and I am equally confident this is not a paradox, only a fact about the machine. Second, suppose I am told that some positive quantity — the size of a town, the length of a river, the income of a household — is "as likely to be one value as another" over a range spanning several orders of magnitude; I notice that being unbiased in the value itself and being unbiased in the logarithm of the value give very different answers, and that only the second of them survives my switching from miles to kilometres, which by itself should never move my credences. Third, a coin comes from a mint I know nothing about: half of me insists the chance of heads must be a half, since what else could it be, and the other half notices that nothing tells me to favour "double-headed" over "not double-headed" either, so that by the same reasoning there would be a half chance the coin is double-headed, which cannot be right. Fourth, I cut a rope at a point I have chosen with no preference at all, and here I feel no puzzle whatever, because what I am choosing is a position along the rope, and stretches of equal length are the obvious units.

What I want is an essay that settles whether the principle of indifference — the rule that says that when you have no reason to prefer one possibility to another you should treat them as equally likely — ought to be abandoned, kept as it stands, or kept only in a repaired and restricted form, and if the last, precisely what the repair is and how I am to apply it. I do not want to be told flatly that the chord problem shows the principle to be incoherent; I want the strongest case for it set out fairly before it is judged, and then a verdict, even if the verdict is that only a shadow of the principle survives. Please organise the essay in the following sections. "The puzzle, stated plainly" — one paragraph, restating the chord problem and the three answers, so that the rest can refer back to it. "What the three procedures actually disagree about" — two or three paragraphs. "The case against indifference" — three or four paragraphs, including an account of why my rope case feels untroubled while the coin case does not. "The case for a repaired version" — three or four paragraphs, saying exactly what the repair is and what it does and does not deliver. "My verdict" — two paragraphs, taking a side, with the verdict compressed into a single sentence I could write on an index card. "What the verdict says about my four cases" — one or two paragraphs, going through the drilling machine, the town sizes, the mystery coin, and the rope in turn, and admitting plainly which of my reactions the verdict cannot save.
