I'm writing an essay on commitment devices and want to close it with a villanelle — one where the form carries the argument rather than merely illustrating it.

Here is the thought I want the poem to hold. On the standard two-selves picture of a Ulysses contract, a self at one time binds a self at a later time, and the pact works only because the later self cannot cheaply renegotiate it. That makes the device rigid in its letter and perpetually tested in its spirit: the bound self rarely breaks the pact outright — it reinterprets it, finds the loophole, argues that these circumstances are the exception the binder never contemplated. And the very faculty that lets you write the pact, the ability to bind your future self, is the faculty that lets you dissolve it, so the device has to be at least partly out of your hands: a friend holding the money, a mechanism you cannot reach, a majority that would have to vote to amend. This is why the personal case and the constitutional case have the same shape and the same legitimacy worry, and why I keep arguing about it in the corners of the internet I read.

So: a villanelle, nineteen lines, in which the two refrains are the voice of the self that binds and the voice of the self that is bound. The tercets are the interval — successive moments in which the bound self tries to reinterpret, and the refrain returns unchanged, because that is the whole point of a device: it cannot be argued with. The closing quatrain brings the two refrains together, and I want it left genuinely undecided whether they are one voice or two. The poem should not settle whether precommitment is something a person does to himself or a contract between two people wearing one name.

Two constraints here are doing real work, so please honor them rather than treating them as ornament. First, keep the rhymes strict and the meter slack — what can be precommitted to is what can be cheaply checked, so the binding constraint on the poem should be the one a stranger can verify at a glance. Second, the refrains must mean something different each time they return while their words do not change at all; the gap between what the binder meant and what the bound self can be held to is where the whole thing lives.

Write it for a reader who has thought hard about self-binding and also for one who hasn't. The first should recognize the two-selves problem and the enforcement problem without either being named; the second should simply find it a good poem. Don't let it resolve into a moral at the end.
