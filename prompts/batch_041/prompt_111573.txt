A colleague of mine who teaches contract law has a line he produces whenever the conversation turns to ethics: a promise, he says, is simply a contract that no court will enforce. He does not mean it as a joke, and he takes the analogy to be doing real work. His claim is that the two are the same kind of thing — one party invites another to rely on a commitment, the other accepts, and the promisor thereby takes on a liability — and that the only difference between them is institutional: in the one case there is a tribunal with a remedy, in the other there is not. From this he draws two conclusions that he thinks any honest person must accept. First, that the wrong of breaking a promise is entirely a wrong done to the promisee, so that a promise no one has relied upon and no one will miss is only nominally binding. Second, that because a contract breaker is generally permitted to pay damages rather than perform, there is no deep difference between keeping a promise and compensating the person to whom it was made.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I would rather locate the precise points of failure than trade intuitions with him. Please do the following.

1. Set out the correspondence in full: what answers to offer and acceptance, to consideration, to breach and remedy, to the doctrines that excuse or defeat performance (duress, mistake, impossibility, the pre-existing duty rule, promissory estoppel), and to the availability of specific performance rather than damages. A table of correspondences is welcome.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for his two conclusions from the ones that are merely incidental to the comparison.

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports from the contract case that the promise case may lack — a third-party enforcer, a substitutional remedy, parties conceived as strangers bargaining at arm's length — and, just as importantly, what the promise case contains that the contract case has no place for: promises to oneself, promises to the dead, the thought that the promisor is bound to perform rather than merely to pay, and the possibility that breaking a promise wrongs the promisor as well as the promisee.

4. Deliver a verdict on each of his two conclusions: does either survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if one survives, does it survive for the reason he gives or for some independent reason?

5. If a better analogy for the bindingness of promises is available, give it and treat it the same way: what maps onto what, and where it in turn fails.

I am not looking for a survey of what others have written, and I do not need case names or a reading list; I want the comparison audited and the argument made on its structural merits.
