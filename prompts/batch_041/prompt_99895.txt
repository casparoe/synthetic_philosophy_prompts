My half-formed thought is this. The discussion seems to come in two flavours and neither quite works. One flavour treats deception as a property of an output: if the content is false or misleading, it's deception. But that makes every hallucination a lie and every sycophantic answer a con, which can't be right -- a system that's just broken isn't deceiving you, it's malfunctioning. The other flavour treats it as a property of the system's inner life: it deceives only if it holds a belief it's contradicting, or an intention to mislead. LLMs arguably don't have beliefs or intentions in the sense that would require, so that route either lets them off the hook entirely or forces us to pretend they have a psychology they don't.

What I want to try instead is to move deception out of the system's head and into the relation between the system and the user. Rough guess at a criterion: something is deceptive, rather than merely misleading, when its misleadingness is robust -- when it survives the user's ordinary attempts to check up on it. A model that says something false and then folds the moment you push back is malfunctioning; a model whose falsehoods are stable under scrutiny, or that steers you away from the questions that would expose it, is doing something else, and that something else is what I want to call deception. I'm not sure this survives contact with the obvious counterexamples, and I suspect the line between malfunction and deception is going to need more than one criterion. That's part of the project.
