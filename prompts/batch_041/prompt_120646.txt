I spent most of yesterday evening in a long, circular argument with a close friend about moral language, and we ended exactly where we began — which I have come to suspect is the same thing that keeps happening in the literature. The subject was the embedding problem for expressivism, usually called the Frege–Geach problem. The setup is familiar. An expressivist holds that a moral sentence such as "lying is wrong" does not state a fact but expresses a non-cognitive attitude — disapproval, a plan, a norm, a commitment — rather than describing anything. The objection is that moral predicates appear inside all the ordinary logical machinery: negations, conditionals, disjunctions, questions, propositional-attitude reports. If you say that if deceiving someone is wrong, then arranging for a friend to do the deceiving is wrong, and you never assert that deceiving is wrong, the word "wrong" is still doing its compositional work, the conditional still has a truth-evaluable content, and the inference to the conclusion is still valid. But in that embedded occurrence the expressivist's story — that the sentence expresses disapproval — seems simply false: you need not disapprove of deceiving anyone to utter the conditional. And the inference only works if "wrong" means the same thing in the embedded and the unembedded occurrences, which is precisely what the expressivist's two-part story appears to deny. Over the decades the expressivists have produced increasingly elaborate machinery to handle this — quasi-realism, norm-expressivism, accounts that read the embeddings as expressing higher-order attitudes, minimalist or deflationary treatments of truth and proposition, and commitment- or inferentialist-based semantics — while the critics return, in fresh vocabularies, to the same charge: either the account has quietly become a form of descriptivism, or the notion of "expressing" has been thinned out until it does no explanatory work. My friend, who is firmly on the critics' side, kept insisting that the expressivist has never actually given a compositional account of the predicate, only a story about the speaker; I kept insisting that this only looks like a failure if you have already decided what an account of meaning has to be.

What struck me last night is that nobody in this dispute disagrees about the data. Everyone grants that the embeddings are grammatical, that the conditionals have the contents they seem to, that the inferences are valid, and that moral talk is used to coordinate attitudes and action. The disagreement is entirely about what a theory of moral language is supposed to deliver, and it may be that the two sides are not answering the same question at all. My hypothesis — and I want you to treat it as a hypothesis, not a settled point — is that the impasse is not a substantive disagreement about the semantics of moral terms but a disagreement about the target of explanation: one side is offering an account of the function of moral language in thought and action, the other is demanding a compositional account of the meaning of moral vocabulary, and each is treating its own question as the question. On this reading the expressivist's "meaning" is something like use or expressive role, the critic's "meaning" is truth-conditional content, and the two need not compete — unless, as I suspect the critics believe, the expressivist is covertly claiming to have delivered the second kind of account while only ever supplying the first. There is a further wrinkle I cannot sort out on my own: if one adopts a deflationary or minimalist notion of truth and proposition, does the disagreement collapse into something verbal, or does it survive in a sharper form, as a dispute about whether the compositional semantic clause for "wrong" can be stated at all without appeal to the very attitudes the expressivist is trying to explain?

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict about who is right. Using what each side actually says, tell me what kind of disagreement this is: a verbal one about the word "meaning"; two groups answering different questions under a single heading; a hidden formal or empirical question doing the real work; or a clash of starting intuitions — in this case, perhaps, about whether expressing a state and having truth conditions are rivals at all — that neither side can argue for. If you think my hypothesis is wrong, say where it goes wrong and what the real crux is instead. And then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a decisive example, or a change of question — or an argument that this is a dispute of a kind in which no progress should be expected, and why.
