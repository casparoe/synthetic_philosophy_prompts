I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I am preparing the handout for a session of a graduate seminar on practical reason. The session concerns cases in which an agent faces two or more options and, on the best account she can give, has no reason to prefer any of them. What I want from you is the handout's centrepiece: a decision tree that takes a reader from one starting commitment to a definite position on what an agent in that situation may do, must do, or cannot do.

The starting commitment, phrased in my own words and not anyone's: two options can be, so far as the agent can tell, equally worth choosing. Assume the reader grants this; the tree is not to argue her into it, and no node may simply re-open it.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Get the dividing questions right first, and locate the positions at the leaves afterwards. Every branch node must be a question the reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and it should be a question about a concrete case, or about a claim concerning one — never a question of the form "do you accept the rationalist or the voluntarist picture?" A reader who has read none of the literature should be able to walk the tree.

These distinctions must survive somewhere in the tree, at whatever level they belong:

1. A tie in value (the options are equally good) as against a tie in reason (the considerations for each are equally strong, though the options need not be equal in value) as against mere motivational indifference (the agent does not care either way).
2. Three different ways a comparison can fail to favour either option: the options are equal; the options cannot be compared at all; the options can be compared but neither is better and they are not equal — some writers call this last case parity, and you may use the word, but do not spend the tree on its history.
3. Whether the tie is a fact about the options or an artefact of the description under which the agent is considering them, and whether a claim that there is a tie can be mistaken at all, and by what standard.
4. The difference between what the agent may do, what she must do, and what she may do but only with an excuse.
5. The difference between a tie-breaking rule adopted in advance and a tie-breaker that is a feature of the options themselves.
6. Whether the act of choosing can supply the reason that was missing.

Mark each node as empirical or normative: empirical if it turns on what agents can in fact do (whether a will can move without a reason, whether anyone is ever genuinely indifferent), normative if it turns on what is permitted. Where a branch needs both, split it into two nodes.

The tree has to handle the following cases; each must appear as the discriminating case at some node, and at least one leaf must be reachable only through a node that uses it.

(a) The case conventionally called Buridan's ass: an animal equidistant between two identical bales. I do not care what the historical Buridan argued and do not want the tree to turn on it. I want to see which positions make the animal's death inevitable, which make it irrational, and which make it impossible.
(b) Two job offers from the same employer, in two cities the agent likes equally and for no further reason. She must answer by Friday. Does it matter whether she tosses a coin, or simply accepts the one she happened to be looking at when the deadline arrived?
(c) Two strangers in the water, equidistant from the boat, with time to reach only one. Here the tie is not between two of the agent's own goods but between two people's claims on her.
(d) A small hospital's pharmacy must stock one of two drugs with identical expected outcomes. The decision will set the standing practice of the hospital, so this agent's arbitrary pick will be repeated by people who never face the tie themselves.
(e) A tie between two options whose worth will be settled by the choice itself: the agent will be in a position to judge what each was worth only from inside one of them, so "are they equal?" cannot be answered from a standpoint outside the choice.
(f) A tie between two options, where before the tie arose the agent had casually promised a friend to take one of them. The promise is no reason at all for some positions in the space and a decisive reason for others.

For each leaf, give the position's name if it has one, or "unnamed" if it does not, then one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one sentence on what it must say about the case that leads to it.

Then, in three short sections after the tree:

- the answer-combinations that no one occupies, with a diagnosis of each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored;
- the pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, and what further case, if any, would separate them;
- where the tree is likely to misfire, and which of its nodes are doing real work as opposed to decorating a distinction the leaves do not need.

No citations, no reading list, no quotations, and no guessing at who held what. If a position is usually attached to a named author and I have not supplied the name, leave the leaf unnamed rather than risk a wrong attribution.

Size: ten to fourteen branch nodes, eight to twelve leaves. The handout will be photocopied, so keep each leaf to two sentences.
