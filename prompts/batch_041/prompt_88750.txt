The essay, provisionally titled "The Error Profile":

The usual way to state the behavioral criterion for mind runs through success. An interlocutor who cannot tell the machine from the person on the basis of the conversation, the thought goes, has no grounds for denying the machine thought. I want to argue that this puts the evidential weight in the wrong place. What carries the burden in the imitation game is not the machine's profile of correct answers but its profile of errors. Competence is cheap: a system can be coached, retrieved into, or fine-tuned toward it, and once competence is reached it looks much the same across very different architectures. Errors do not. Which distinctions a speaker blurs, which questions they over-answer, when they hedge and when they bluff, how they recover from a slip, what they forget and in what order — these are stamped by the machinery underneath, and they are harder to counterfeit than correct answers, because you cannot produce the right error without the right mechanism producing it.

Call this the error-profile criterion: two systems are behaviorally equivalent, in the sense the imitation game cares about, only if they are indistinguishable not just in what they get right but in the shape of what they get wrong.

The criterion needs a distinction to survive. I separate errors of capacity — slips traceable to limited memory, attention, or time, which a patient machine could avoid outright or reproduce at will — from errors of constitution, mistakes that arise from being the kind of thing that is embedded in a body and a lifespan, and so bounded in ways it cannot survey from outside. Only constitutional errors, I suspect, resist imitation. But I can feel this distinction wobbling: it may just be the capacity/constitution line redrawn by whatever we happen not to have engineered yet.

And then the regress. A system could be built expressly to reproduce human error patterns — the same fatigue effects, the same slips of attention, the same characteristic overconfidence. Once that is possible, the error profile becomes one more output to imitate, and the criterion collapses back into ordinary indistinguishability with no added evidential force. So either the error profile is a temporary engineering advantage that will be erased, or there is something about human error that cannot be reproduced without reproducing the mechanism — and I do not know which, or how I would tell.
