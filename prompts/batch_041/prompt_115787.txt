Professional licensing bodies in many countries set the pass mark for their entry examination by a procedure they publish in full. They convene a panel of experienced practitioners and ask each panellist, question by question, to estimate the probability that a candidate who is just barely competent to practise would answer that question correctly. The estimates are averaged across the panel, the averages are summed, and the total is the pass mark; candidates who fall below it are refused a licence, and the examiners have no discretion to pass anyone on the strength of the overall impression they made.

Now consider a legal system built on hard numbers. A driver is over the alcohol limit at one specified concentration in the blood and under it at any concentration below that, however much either driver has drunk and however impaired either appears; petty theft below a stated value is handled by an administrative penalty and above it by prosecution in the criminal courts; the age at which a person may consent, vote, or be tried as an adult is a whole number of years and not a day less. The same system also insists, as a constitutional matter, that a criminal prohibition is invalid if it is so vague that a person of ordinary intelligence cannot tell in advance what it forbids, or if it gives the police no guidance and so invites arbitrary enforcement. Yet in practice the police do not stop drivers a fraction over the limit, prosecutors decline marginal cases, and sentencers treat the person who went a hair over the line as morally on all fours with the person who stayed a hair under it. Alongside the hard numbers, the system runs on standards that are deliberately vague — what a reasonable person would have done, what a statement materially misleads a consumer into believing, what degree of force is excessive in an arrest — and expects juries to apply them with no numbers at all. And where a video-review system adjudicates line calls in sport, the written rule requires the ball to have touched the line, but the review protocol leaves the original decision standing whenever the tracking technology places the ball within a few centimetres of it, on the ground that the technology's margin of error is wider than that.

What theory of vagueness does this package of arrangements presuppose? Does the drawing of sharp lines commit it to epistemicism — that there is a determinate fact of the matter about whether this driver is over the limit or this candidate is competent, with the statutory number and the panel's aggregate merely our best guesses at a boundary that exists independently of us? Or do the tolerance, the prosecutorial discretion, and the margin-of-error protocol commit it to supervaluationism — that at the margins there is no fact of the matter, that the statutory number and the summed estimates are admissible precisifications rather than discoveries, and that choosing among them is a policy decision dressed up as measurement? Or is there a third reading on which these lines are not precisifications of a vague antecedent predicate at all but fresh stipulative ones, so that the vagueness problem never arises — and if so, does that dissolve the difficulty or merely relocate it? Is the package internally consistent, or does the void-for-vagueness doctrine presuppose the very determinacy that the enforcement tolerance denies? Distinguish what the arrangements strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with, say where they are underdetermined between rival theories, and tell me what would concretely have to change — in the licensing procedure, in what a plea that one was only just over the line amounts to, and in the constitutional doctrine — if the system adopted a thoroughgoing supervaluationism, and again if it adopted a thoroughgoing epistemicism. I am also curious whether averaging panellists' judgements about a barely competent candidate is doing anything structurally like quantifying over admissible precisifications.
