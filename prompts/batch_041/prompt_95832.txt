I'm writing a paper on the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda for a comparative philosophy seminar, and what I need from you is not an exposition of the doctrine but a piece of reasoning about a case I have not seen it asked to handle. Please work from the verdicts I set out below rather than from general impressions of what "many-sidedness" is supposed to mean, and don't worry about references — I don't need citations or a reading list, only the argument.

The track record. Our seminar has been treating the doctrine as a package with a record of decisions, and these are the verdicts we reconstructed as standard within the tradition:

(i) Asked whether the self is permanent or impermanent, it answers: both, though not in the same respect — permanent in respect of the substance that endures, impermanent in respect of the modes successively assumed.

(ii) Asked whether a substance is identical with its qualities or distinct from them, it answers: neither without qualification — one in one respect, two in another.

(iii) Asked whether a pot exists, it answers: it exists as itself and does not exist as anything else — the same qualified both, with the respect again drawn from the object.

(iv) Asked about the sweeping theses that everything is permanent and that nothing is permanent, it answers that each is false as stated, and true only once a respect is named.

The pattern we extracted is that every assertion is to be prefixed with syāt, "in some respect," and that once the respect is named, predications that would otherwise contradict one another can be asserted together. (I have also seen syāt rendered "perhaps." I take that to be a translator's convenience rather than part of the doctrine, but tell me if I am wrong to.)

The new case. Two colleagues are quarrelling over whether a certain nineteenth-century reformer — the particulars don't matter, only the shape — betrayed the movement he inherited or rescued it. Each has hold of something real: the first can point to the doctrines he softened, the patrons he accepted, the demands he let drop; the second to the schools he founded, the legal gains he won, the community that outlived him. Neither will settle for "he yielded here and held firm there." Each asserts a single unqualified verdict — he betrayed it; he rescued it — and each takes the other to be simply mistaken. A third colleague proposes to close the matter in the Jain manner: he betrayed it and rescued it, in different respects; so both disputants are right, and the minute should say so.

What I need:

1. Whether the record above licenses that resolution, and what the tradition's own verdicts would actually yield here.

2. The record is compatible with more than one rule governing when a "both, in a respect" verdict is available. Set out the candidate rules you can see — I have been working with two, one on which the respect must come from a distinction in the object itself, and one on which the respect is simply the standpoint of whoever is speaking — and say what each predicts about the new case. Tell me which of the four earlier verdicts best support which rule, and whether some further rule fits them at least as well.

3. Your best guess at the verdict, with the reasons for preferring it.

4. A further case, specified concretely enough to be decided, that would separate the readings you keep.

5. If the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say which principle the tradition would be adopting with each of the competing resolutions.

Throughout, keep what is settled among people who know this material apart from what is genuinely disputed among them, and don't let a contested reading pass as the consensus. Where you are offering a reading rather than a fact about the tradition, mark it as such.
