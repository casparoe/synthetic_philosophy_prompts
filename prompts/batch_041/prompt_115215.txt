In my epistemology seminar we have been working on the Meno problem — the question of why knowledge should be worth more than a true belief held with the same confidence — and our session on Socrates' answer turned on his image of the statues of Daedalus, which in the dialogue run away and escape unless they are tied down, and stay put once they are bound; the binding is supplied by working out the reason why, and a true opinion that has been tied down in that way counts as knowledge. My instructor used the image to close the question: knowledge is better than true belief because it stays with you, and what stays with you can guide action again and again, whereas an unsecured true belief may slip away at any moment.

I would like to audit the image as an argument rather than admire it. Please set out the correspondences explicitly — what answers to the statue, to the chain, to the statue's tendency to wander off, and to whatever makes a chained statue worth more than an unchained one — then say where the mapping breaks, which features of the statue case are being imported into the epistemology without warrant, and whether the conclusion my instructor drew survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Two things trouble me in particular. First, the image appears to explain why knowledge lasts longer, not why it is better; if the extra value is just reliability over time, has the Meno problem been given what it asked for? Second, if what ties a belief down is an account of the reason why, and an account is itself something one believes, why does the tether end a regress rather than start one? If the image cannot carry the weight it is being asked to carry, tell me what picture would do better, or whether the question admits no such picture at all.
