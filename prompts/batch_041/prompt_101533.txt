You are a philosopher working on social choice theory and the formal analysis of rights. Before you begin writing, spend a short while planning: set out in rough notes what the position below is actually committed to, which parts of the standard framing it must reject, and how you will make the case. Then produce a sustained, rigorous argument.

Your task is to argue for a specific position on Sen's liberal paradox. In the standard setup, a liberal right is modeled as pairwise decisiveness: individual i is decisive over the ordered pair (x, y) when, if i strictly prefers x to y, society must strictly prefer x to y. Minimal liberalism requires that at least two individuals each be decisive over at least one pair; weak Pareto requires that unanimous strict preference be preserved by society; and once there are three or more alternatives, the two conditions cannot both hold. The familiar responses are to weaken the liberalism condition by confining it to personal or private choices, to weaken Pareto, or to declare that rights have been misformalized and abandon the project.

The position I want you to defend takes the third route, but in a specific form. It holds that rights should not be built into the aggregation rule at all, and should instead be modeled as restrictions on the agenda. On this reading, each individual is assigned one or more alternatives over which they hold a removal right: they may strike those alternatives from the feasible set before any aggregation occurs. Only afterward does a social welfare function satisfying weak Pareto and independence operate over the alternatives that remain. Argue that this agenda-restriction reading does three things: it dissolves the impossibility rather than trading one axiom against another; it reconstructs the idea of a right more faithfully than pairwise decisiveness, because it locates the right in control over which options are on the table rather than in the ranking of them; and the problems it leaves behind — how removal rights are allocated, what happens when two individuals' removal rights bear on the same alternative, whether removal rights can be waived, traded, or exercised strategically — are the substantive and tractable problems, whereas the original paradox is not.

Do not merely assert this. Anticipate the strongest objections and answer them. In particular: the charge that agenda-restriction is ad hoc, saving the appearance of a result by changing the subject; the charge that if removal rights are generous enough to be recognizably liberal, the framework collapses into dictatorship or vacuity; and the charge that the paradox is precisely a proof that no formalization can honor both liberal rights and Pareto at once, so any reconstruction must be conceding a real loss. Finally, state what would count against your position, and what a defender would have to concede if the agenda-restriction reading turned out to reproduce the same impossibility under a different name.

Be concrete where you can: work through a small example with three alternatives and two individuals, and show where the original paradox bites and where your reconstruction stops it. Aim for the texture of a careful paper section, not a survey.
