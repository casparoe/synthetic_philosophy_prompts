I have no academic training, and I would rather you reasoned things out than sent me to a reading list, so please do not bother with references or suggestions for further reading.

By way of background: I am a keen amateur birder. I have spent years reading field guides, checklists and the introductions to them, so I can follow an argument about clades and grades, grouping by ancestry as against grouping by resemblance, monothetic and polythetic definitions, diagnostic characters and diagnosability, lumping and splitting, typological thinking as against population thinking, and the standing quarrel over whether a taxon is discovered or decided. I am not a biologist, so gloss anything I would be unlikely to know. I have also spent years arguing with friends about whether generations are real: baby boomers, millennials, Gen Z, and so on.

What I want is a translation, as faithful as you can make it, of the debate about generations into that taxonomic vocabulary. Take the positions one at a time: first the claim that a generation is a real social kind, that a birth window together with a shared historical situation produces a distinctive outlook its members carry with them; then the sceptical claim that generations are labels applied after the fact by journalists, marketers and people selling advertising, and that the apparent differences between cohorts are really differences of age or of period. For each, say what carries over into taxonomic terms cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what cannot be expressed there at all. Please do not let taxonomic assumptions quietly do work that the generational claims were never making.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about. Three things I suspect do not survive the translation, and I would like to be told whether I am right and what it shows if I am. First, a generation is not a lineage: its members are contemporaries, not ancestors and descendants, so the clade analogy may be doing no work, and a generation may be nearer to a grade, which I have the impression has fallen out of favour as a real kind. Second, generation-talk is meant to explain the outlook of the people in it, whereas a taxonomic category does not explain the traits of its members; if that is so, then wanting generations to be real kinds in the taxonomic sense may be wanting the wrong thing. Third, people know which generation they are said to belong to and speak about themselves accordingly, and I cannot think of anything in taxonomy that answers to that. I would also like your view on a related muddle: whether the ordinary arguments slide between people born in a particular window, people who were young during a particular period, and people of a given age now, and whether anything follows from that.

Finally, where the translation fails, say whether the failure is a defect of taxonomy, something a framework built on descent and resemblance cannot express, or a sign that the generation claim was never as clear as it sounded, and whether the residue tells us anything about which side of the debate has the better of it. Please write the answer in British English.
