I run the mediation-support desk at a regional organization: every draft ceasefire or settlement text that goes to our lead mediator crosses my desk two days before it goes to the parties, and my unit's written assessment is what the mediator uses to decide whether to present a document as a basis for a durable settlement or only as a truce. I want a procedure I can actually run on a draft, built on Kant's 1795 essay on perpetual peace rather than on standard conflict-resolution doctrine: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and returns "truce, not peace," and the points where the text passes every test and the remaining question is political, so it goes back to the mediator marked as a judgment call rather than a finding. Note that Kant's preliminary requirements are not all of a kind — some tolerate gradual implementation while others do not — so the procedure needs a way to sort failures into disqualifying now and carried as conditions. Kant wrote for a Europe of sovereign states and a law of nations; my files contain a government, an armed group that holds territory and levies taxes, and two external sponsors with basing rights, so say where his conditions carry over, where they must be restated before they can bite at all, and where they have nothing to say.

Then run the procedure on two drafts. First: a twenty-four-month cessation of hostilities in which the government keeps its full standing army and may resume operations if the armed group misses two benchmarks, the group's conversion into a party is deferred to a later stage, both sponsors guarantee the text, and an amnesty covers, among other things, the poisoning of a village and the killing of the previous mediator. Second: a settlement the mediator's political masters are already briefing as historic, in which two states end a border dispute by dividing a small buffer state's territory between them, paying compensation, and agreeing to move populations.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and which of the two verdicts I am not going to like.
