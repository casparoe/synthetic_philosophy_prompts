I'm a third-year PhD student on the AI-safety side of things and I've been defending a view I'd like you to try and break. The view: you can define "low-impact" behaviour — avoiding side effects — without ever specifying what's valuable. My version says the agent should just minimize how far it pushes the world off its default trajectory, where the default is the course things would have taken had the agent not acted, and that default is fixed by the world's own causal structure rather than by any ranking of outcomes. What would count against this? Rank the defeaters, and give me the ones a sympathizer would concede, not just the cheap "any metric smuggles in values" line — I want to see where the value actually creeps in. Concrete cases please: an agent that stays perfectly on-trajectory while doing something monstrous; two impact measures that disagree about the same case and exactly what the disagreement traces to; whether "default trajectory" is even well-defined once other agents are acting too; and what happens when the metric is coarse enough to count as value-neutral — does it then license catastrophe? If the honest verdict is that the definition only survives by getting vague enough to be unfalsifiable, say so, and say whether that's fatal or just a cost. I'd be most moved by a case where it gives the wrong answer for reasons that have nothing to do with how fine- or coarse-grained the metric is.
