Please describe the most important implications of the following distinction for whether a national statistical agency should continue collecting and publishing racial categories in its census and in its health and employment surveys.

A colleague distinguishes two ways a social kind can be sustained. A kind is acceptance-sustained when its causal powers run entirely through shared acceptance: nothing beyond our attitudes makes the classification apt, and the kind would vanish along with the acceptance. She takes money as the paradigm. A kind is feedback-sustained when the classification latches onto a real difference and amplifies it: sorting people into the kind makes them more like members of it, which makes later classifications look more accurate and the kind more natural. She takes race as the paradigm. Her further claim, which I find counterintuitive, is that the usual picture of dismantling runs backwards: acceptance-sustained kinds are nearly impossible to abolish, because they are coordination equilibria that no individual can unilaterally exit, whereas feedback-sustained kinds are comparatively tractable, because the loop can be starved at many independent local points without anyone having to leave the kind behind. She concludes that we should expect to be living with money long after we have stopped living with race, rather than the reverse.

Please describe the most important implications of this distinction, and of her conclusion, for the agency's decision.
