I'm a journo who writes about science and ideas, and for our year-end issue I want to run a piece of short fiction — something that does for a philosophical puzzle what the best sci-fi does for AI, i.e. lets you feel the idea instead of reading a lecture about it. So: write me a short story built around the surprise exam paradox, but please don't set it in a classroom with a teacher and a chalkboard, thats the version everyone's already seen. Put it somewhere it could actually matter — a regulator who announces that every firm will be audited once this year and that nobody will see it coming, a no-notice inspection regime, an unannounced readiness test, a hospital's random audit, whatever you can make live.

The things I want the story to really be about, not just decorate: that the whole thing turns on the deadline, and that drop the fixed horizon and the reasoning falls apart; that the load-bearing step in that reasoning is trusting the announcement on the last possible day, which is exactly the thing the reasoning itself is busy destroying; and that this makes it self-defeating — the clever ones conclude nothing is coming, stop worrying, and get ambushed on a Wednesday. I'd love one character who never runs the argument at all and so can't be surprised-or-not the way the clever ones are, and someone who insists the announcement isn't even a claim anyone could know in advance, since it's about what they'll believe later. And I want the person who made the announcement to be genuinely stuck: do it on the last possible day and it isn't a surprise, and she knows it, so the announcement has quietly turned into a promise she can only break.

Don't end with anyone explaining the paradox to the reader. No headings, bullets, bold, or tables — continuous prose only, like a letter. American English. Around 1,200 words is fine.
