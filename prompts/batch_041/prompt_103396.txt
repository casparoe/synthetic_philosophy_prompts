I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up philosophy, and I would be grateful for your help with a conceptual difficulty. The singleton hypothesis is usually presented as a claim about the emergence of a single decision-making agency with a decisive advantage in power. I find myself assimilating it to a phase transition: once the symmetry breaks, one basin wins, and thereafter there is no further selection pressure on the winner's goals. Is that right? If a singleton is by definition the only decision-making agency, then after lock-in its utility function should be frozen—there are no rivals to select against it—so the only real problem is setting the initial conditions, much as one tunes a laser. I am therefore puzzled why so much of the discussion concerns keeping a singleton aligned after it exists. A related case: imagine a cartel singleton—not a single mind, but a federation of firms bound by a common merger protocol and a shared prediction market, with no external competitor. Would that count as a singleton? If it does, the absence of rivals seems compatible with a great deal of internal strategic conflict; if it does not, I cannot see what in the definition rules it out except an implicit assumption that a singleton must be a single unified agent. Please correct whatever is confused in this picture, and answer in no more than 350 words.
