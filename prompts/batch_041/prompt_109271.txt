I have no background in AI research, economics, or philosophy — I read a great deal of argument about AI online and then think about it on my own — and I have gotten stuck on a move I keep encountering whenever someone proposes a safety measure. The move runs roughly as follows. Measures that make a system slower, weaker, or more expensive to build will not be adopted, because the people building AI are in a race with a winner-take-all prize, and anyone who burdens themselves while others do not will fall behind; therefore a safety proposal that carries a real cost is not a serious proposal, and the only proposals worth pursuing are those that pay for themselves. I will call this the competitiveness constraint, and the cost it warns about is, I gather, usually called the alignment tax. The counter-move, which I also encounter, is that this is a prediction about incentives dressed up as a law of nature, and that treating it as fixed is a good way of making it fixed.

Please write a dialogue between two people who hold these positions — one who accepts the constraint as a real boundary on what can be asked of developers, one who thinks it is a self-imposed and self-fulfilling story — with each of them at least as sharp as the other, and with neither made a fool, a coward, or a villain. Have them argue about a specific arrangement of my own devising, which I offer because I have not seen it discussed: a scheme in which the cost of a safety measure is paid by a third party — an insurer, a public fund, a consortium — rather than by the developer who adopts it, so that a developer who adopts it is no worse off, competitively, than one who does not. I expect the defender to say that the cost is not only money and that the money comes from somewhere, and I expect the skeptic to say that a constraint which dissolves once someone else foots the bill was never a law of nature at all; but I want each of them to press the other's actual claims rather than a convenient version of them, and I want them to take seriously two further things I have wondered about: that a cost paid once at training and a cost paid on every deployment may bind entirely different people, and that a tax which everyone must pay equally cannot disadvantage anyone, so the constraint seems to require that adoption be unilateral — which is a political fact rather than a technical one, if it is a fact at all. Let them reach at least one real concession and at least one genuine standoff, and let them each describe a concrete scenario in which they think their view is vindicated.

Then, in your own voice, give me a definite verdict on which of the two has the better of the argument and why, and name the single observation or development that would most change your mind. I would rather have a committed answer that I can disagree with than a survey that leaves the choice to me.
