I have drafted the following essay on Dennett's "Where Am I?" and I would like you to comment on it. I am especially interested in whether the central argument is sound, whether I have misrepresented Dennett's position, and whether the conclusion is coherent. Here is the draft:

Dennett's "Where Am I?" is usually read as a defense of the view that the self is a "center of narrative gravity" — a useful fiction, not a real entity. On this reading, the story's lesson is that the question "where am I?" has no determinate answer, because the self is not the kind of thing that has a location. I want to argue that this reading is too quick. The story's central device — the radio link between brain and body — does not support the narrative view; it reveals a tension in it.

The tension is this. Dennett wants to say that the self is a narrative construction. But the story is told in the first person. The "I" in the story is not a character in a narrative; it is the subject of experience. Even when Dennett's brain is in a vat in Houston and his body is in Tulsa, there is something it is like to be him. That "something it is like" is not located in the brain, but it is also not a fiction. It is the first-person perspective, and it cannot be reduced to a third-person narrative.

The "two Dennett" scenario makes this vivid. In the story, a second body is created, also controlled by the same brain. Now there are two bodies, each behaving as if it were Dennett. If the self were a narrative construction, there would be two narratives, and hence two selves. But there is only one brain, and presumably only one stream of consciousness. The first-person perspective is singular, even when the third-person narratives are plural. This shows that the self cannot be reduced to a narrative.

The radio link is best understood as an interface. In a virtual reality system, the user is not located at the computer or at the avatar; the user is located at the interface, the point of interaction. Similarly, Dennett is not located at his brain or at his body; he is located at the radio link, the point of interaction between brain and body. But the interface is not a spatial location. It is a functional location. The self is a functional location, not a spatial one.

I conclude that Dennett's story is best read not as a defense of the "no-self" view, but as an illustration of its limits. The self is not a thing in the world, but it is not nothing either. It is a perspective, and perspectives are not located in space. The question "where am I?" is not unanswerable because the self is a fiction; it is unanswerable because the self is a perspective, and perspectives do not have locations.

Please provide detailed comments. I am particularly interested in (1) whether the distinction between the narrative self and the first-person perspective is defensible, or whether it collapses; (2) whether Dennett has a response to the objection that his view cannot account for the first-person perspective; and (3) whether the "interface" analogy is helpful or whether it introduces new problems. I am not looking for citations; I want conceptual feedback.
