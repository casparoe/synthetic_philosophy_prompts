Hi, so I'm a junior and I run the history club at my school, and we got roped into doing the exhibit for our town's 150th birthday at the public library — four panels, like 150 words of text each plus pictures, and the historical society board has to approve every word before it goes on the wall. Here's my problem. Basically everything in the town archive is the record of six families: the mill owners, the bank guy, the guy who donated the land for the park. There's a whole shelf of the mill's ledgers, which list every worker by name with their pay and their fines, and honestly that's the most detail we have on like four hundred people. Meanwhile the stuff that's actually interesting — what people ate, what they fought about, who married who — only exists in two diaries and a shoebox of letters from one woman whose family kept everything, and one court case. So my friends and I keep arguing about what we're allowed to say on the panels. My teacher keeps saying we should be doing history from below instead of just writing about the important guys, and she gave this whole talk about microhistory where you zoom all the way in on one person, ideally a slightly weird one, and use their life as a window onto how the entire society worked — the odd case is what shows you the rules everyone else followed without ever noticing them. Which I get and it's cool, but then how do I know when one person's diary is allowed to stand in for everybody? And how am I supposed to use a ledger written by the boss to say anything true about the workers, when the boss is the one who decided what got written down in the first place? And here's the thing that's really bugging me: the only ordinary people who end up in an archive at all seem to be the ones who got in trouble or fell on hard times, so the people I *can* actually write about are exactly the ones who weren't typical. If poor people barely show up in the records, is that a fact about them or a fact about the records?

So what I want is a decision procedure I can actually run, panel by panel, while we're deciding what goes on the wall — not a general essay about being careful with sources. I need the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, the point where it just stops and says no, you can't claim that, and the point where it has to hand the decision off to a person because it's a values call and not a fact question. It should be honest about which steps are just judgment dressed up as method. Then run it on three things we're seriously considering: (1) a panel built around the mill ledgers — can we say anything real about the workers from them, and if so what, and what's off limits; (2) a panel around that one woman's diary, which is genuinely the best material we have, where the whole question is whether we can present her as a voice of the town's women or whether she's just one unusual person and we have to say so; and (3) the family who only appear in the records because of a lawsuit, where I have a bad feeling your procedure is going to tell me something I don't want to hear, and I'd rather know now so I can plan around it instead of finding out in front of the board. Please don't hand me a reading list or tell me to go find books about this, I can't, and don't just say to be careful — I need the actual steps in order. Also the board is touchy, three of them are decended from the mill family, so tell me which of our claims are actually strong enough to defend and which ones I should just drop. And please tell me what the procedure can't do, because I'd rather know the limits up front than discover them at the meeting.
