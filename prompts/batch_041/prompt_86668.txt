I run a biweekly discussion group for graduate students in philosophy and computer science. Most of them are comfortable with basic game theory, expected utility, and the prisoner's dilemma, but almost none of them have thought about decision theory under correlated or logical uncertainty, and none have encountered acausal reasoning. Next month I want to spend a single ninety-minute session on surrogate goals as a defense against acausal threats: the idea that an agent facing a threat aimed at something it genuinely values might deliberately adopt an additional, otherwise arbitrary goal — say, preventing the destruction of some unremarkable object — so that the threatener's leverage is diverted onto the surrogate. Don't explain the idea to me; I already find it plausible and I understand the basic move. What I need is a plan for teaching it.

Lay out the order in which the components have to be introduced for the session to feel like an argument rather than a curiosity. I take it that the following need to be in place in some order, but I want your judgment on the dependency structure and on what I have wrongly included or omitted: the difference between a threat's target and the threatener's cost of following through, including how that cost depends on third parties and reputation; why ordinary precommitment and reputation-based deterrence are not yet the phenomenon; what has to be true about the threatener's beliefs and about the agent's own decision theory for a surrogate to do any work at all; and the possibility that adopting a surrogate can make the agent worse off when the threatener holds private information about how cheaply they can attack the surrogate rather than the original target. Then choose the one concrete scenario that should carry the whole session — something like a hostage negotiation, a bargainer facing an adversary who can only reach them through prediction rather than causation, or a two-agent bargaining problem with an observer — and tell me what that example must have and must not have, and why the obvious alternatives are worse pedagogically.

Finally, map the misconceptions I should expect and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. My guesses: that a surrogate goal is just self-deception or pretending; that it is identical to a commitment device or to burning one's bridges; that it can only work against an irrational or fanatical threatener; that the surrogate has to be something absurd and worthless, like a platinum sphere of an oddly specific diameter, or else the threatener will not take it seriously; and that the whole maneuver is pointless unless the threatener is causally reachable. Tell me which of these are real misconceptions, which are half-truths worth correcting only in passing, and which I should not raise at all because raising them would plant them. End with a single question or exercise I can put to the group in the last ten minutes that would show whether they have understood the mechanism rather than merely acquired the vocabulary, and say what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level — formal decision-theoretic treatments, the safe-Pareto-improvement literature, implementation in actual AI systems — so that I do not leave them thinking the session was a survey.
