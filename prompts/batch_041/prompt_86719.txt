I took early retirement from the bench (chemistry, and for the last decade or so of it I was making extra money as an expert witness in groundwater contamination cases) and I've spent the last few years reading philosophy, which has been humbling. Here's the thing that keeps nagging at me.

The law's test for causation is the but-for test: the defendent caused the harm only if, but for what they did, the harm wouldn't have happened. Simple counterfactual dependence. But then the same law turns around and does two other things. First, when two sources each put out enough of the same contaminant to cause the harm on its own, but-for says neither one caused it, and nobody accepts that answer -- so we get the "substantial factor" idea, or formulas about being a necessary element of a set of conditions that was together sufficient. Second, in the toxic cases, the story people tell is that a doubling of the risk is enough to let the plaintiff win, even though for any individual plaintiff the exposure still wasn't a but-for condition -- most exposed people don't get the disease, so but-for that exposure, nothing about them changes.

So: what theory of causation does that whole package presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed -- which account of what it is for A to cause B the practice only makes sense under, which rival accounts it quietly rules out, whether the three parts presuppose one thing or are two incompatible theories stitched together (but-for dependence in one room, some notion of a real productive connection in the next), and what would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with a strict counterfactual account on one side or an interventionist account on the other, where you ask what would happen if you reached in and set the exposure yourself rather than just deleting it. And please say where the practice is actually underdetermined -- where several theories would license exactly the same verdicts, so the law isn't committing itself to anything.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no summing up at the end.
