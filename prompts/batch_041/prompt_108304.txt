I chair the review panel of a small foundation that funds high-risk work in the foundations of computing, and I have to circulate our decision memo before we meet next Thursday. One of the three proposals on the docket comes from a group that wants to build a tabletop device whose ideal mathematical description — a physical system with a continuous, real-valued state, plus a measurement procedure that reads off a real number — they say computes a function that no Turing machine computes; in their telling it would decide, for an arbitrary program, whether that program halts. Their abstract asserts that a successful demonstration would establish that the class of physically computable functions properly exceeds the class of Turing-computable ones, and they present the work as an empirical refutation of the Church–Turing thesis. A second proposal, which we will see next cycle, is in the same family but takes a different route: a machine that completes infinitely many discrete steps in a finite stretch of time, by halving the interval between successive steps. Our funding guidelines require every project to specify a success criterion that a hostile reviewer could check, and the panel must decide whether to fund the first proposal outright, fund it with conditions, or decline — and then whether to adopt a standing test for proposals of this kind, since we are told to expect more of them.

Please draft that memo. It should say which version of the thesis our decision actually turns on: the reading on which the thesis is a claim about what an ideal agent can do when following a finite, mechanical procedure, in which case no physical gadget could refute it and a working device would at most be grounds for revising our concept of computation; versus the reading on which it is a claim about the limits of any physical process whatsoever, in which case a working device would refute it. State plainly which reading the panel should adopt, why, and what follows for the money. The memo also has to confront the objection that no finite experiment could distinguish a device that genuinely computes an uncomputable function from one that merely has extraordinary precision and is being idealized in the mathematical model — say whether that objection is fatal, and whether some weaker success criterion survives it. And it should cover whether the two routes (the continuous-state device and the infinite-steps-in-finite-time machine) raise the same issue or different ones, since our standing test has to be written to handle both.

I do not want a memo that simply waves the proposal away; the group's strongest point is that a refusal to fund is itself a substantive empirical bet about the world, and if we are treating the thesis as unrevisable we owe an account of why that is not just dogmatism about a claim we have quietly decided to treat as definitional. So the memo needs a clear recommendation with a time frame, the reasoning behind it, the criterion (if any) under which we could fund work of this kind, and an explicit statement of what evidence would actually change the panel's mind — as well as a fair statement of the case for the group, since two of my panel members are inclined to fund them precisely because they think we are protecting a dogma.
