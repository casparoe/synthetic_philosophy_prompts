I teach a grad seminar on distributive justice, and for the last few months I've been keeping a running list of my gut verdicts about inheritence, mainly to see whether I even have a consistent view or just a pile of reflexes. Could you tell me what principle, if any, actually generates the list, and where it forces me to drop something? All the cases are a parent or grandparent handing something down. (1) A widow leaves the family house, maybe $600k, split between two kids: fine, and I don't care that it passes untaxed. (2) A hedge-fund guy sets up one of those dynasty trusts — as I understand it, a few states have scrapped the old rule against perpetuities so it can pay out to descendants for centuries, and the unrealized gains get their basis stepped up at each death so nobody ever pays capital gains on it: that one strikes me as close to indefensible, and I'd tax it down to almost nothing. (3) A baker leaves the shop to the daughter who actually worked there for twelve years and gives the other two kids cash: not just fine, positively good. (4) Parents spend forty grand a year on private school, tutors, and an SAT bootcamp: fine. (5) A dad gives ten million to a university and the kid gets in: not fine, and the not-fine-ness seems to be about the admission, not the money. (6) A grandfather buys his grandson a $2M apartment in a city where nobody that age can afford one: here I genuinely wobble and have no verdict. (7) Parents pay for med school so the kid graduates debt-free: fine. (8) A family hires lawyers to keep the kid's DUI off the record so it never touches his applications: not fine. (9) Parents read to the kid every night and drag him to museums: fine, obviously, and I don't want any policy touching it.

The principle I keep reaching for is that the objectionable ones are positional — they buy a better spot in a queue for something scarce — and the innocent ones are merely absolute, making the kid's life better without shoving anyone back; I've been calling them positional bequests versus capability bequests. But then (4) should be objectionable, since tutors plainly buy position against other kids, and I don't think it is; and (2) should be innocent, since a trust doesn't obviously shove anyone back, and I think it's the worst thing on the list. So either I bolt on extra clauses — something about whether an institution is being captured, something about scale or the dynastic time-horizon, something about whether the advantage is contestable — until the principle saves all nine verdicts, or I keep it clean and pay the price of overruling one of them. Tell me which, and which verdict has to go; say what your preferred principle predicts for (6); and give me one more case I haven't listed that would be a hard test of it. I already know the literature here, so skip the citations and the reading list — I want the argument, not the bibliography. Also flag how confident you are in your main claim, and tell me the single step where you're least sure of yourself. American spelling, please.
