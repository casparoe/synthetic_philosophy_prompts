Suppose David Hume were restored to life and handed a set of mid-twentieth-century papers on the relation between descriptive and normative discourse — no commentary, no summaries of his own work, only the arguments as their authors set them out. I would like you to reconstruct, as carefully as you can, how he would have reacted to two of them in particular.

The first attempts to derive an 'ought' from an 'is' by way of institutional facts. In outline: a man utters a sentence by which he promises to pay another man five dollars; promising is, under the constitutive rules of the institution, the undertaking of an obligation; to have undertaken an obligation is to be bound to discharge it; therefore the man ought to pay the five dollars. The author's contention is that every premise is either an empirical report of what was said and done or an unpacking of the rules that constitute the practice, and that the conclusion is nonetheless normative. The usual reply is that the step from 'he has undertaken an obligation' to 'he ought to discharge it' is itself normative, or else that the conclusion is only an institutional 'ought', binding relative to rules the agent remains free to reject, so that nothing categorical has been derived.

The second argument is purely formal: from any descriptive premise one may validly infer a disjunction one of whose disjuncts is normative, so a valid argument with an entirely descriptive premise set can have a conclusion in which 'ought' appears; but such a conclusion is merely weaker than its premise and carries no guidance. Defenders of the gap reply that the thesis must therefore be stated with more care — as a claim about non-vacuous or detachable entailment, or about arguments whose entire vocabulary is descriptive, or about what can guide action — rather than as the bare assertion that no 'ought' ever follows from an 'is'. A third strand of the same literature goes further and treats the naturalistic fallacy as though it were simply another name for the gap, although the fallacy was originally aimed at definitions of good rather than at derivations.

My question is what Hume would have said about all of this. Would he regard the promise argument as a refutation of his thesis, or as precisely the kind of explanation he had demanded, followed by a demonstration that the normative force enters through the practice rather than through the facts? Would he see the formal trick as leaving his point untouched, or as showing that his point was never a logical thesis at all but rather the psychological claim that reason alone cannot move us to act? Which of the two arguments do you think he would have found more threatening, and what would he have identified as the real gap that survives them?

Please argue from Hume's own commitments rather than summarizing what commentators have said about him; you need not cite any literature. Finish with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of three labels: UNTOUCHED, if the arguments leave his position as he himself understood it; RECAST, if he would concede that the doctrine needs restating while insisting that the substantive insight survives; or REFUTED, if he would have to abandon the claim that normative conclusions cannot be derived from descriptive premises.
