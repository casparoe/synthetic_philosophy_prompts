Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors about whether the shift from foraging to farming was a mistake. Stipulate that they agree on the empirical picture: per-capita nutrition, stature, and leisure decline after the transition, population rises enough that total welfare increases on any measure either of them accepts, and storable surplus makes a new kind of durable, heritable inequality possible. They disagree not about the facts but about what category the episode belongs to. One insists it was a mistake — a bad choice for which someone was answerable. The other insists that "mistake" is the wrong word, since there was no choosing subject, the pre-agricultural baseline is not a stable thing to compare against, and the honest options are "misfortune" (bad, but unchosen) or "trap" (bad, self-reinforcing, and chosen by no one).

Have them press each other on four points: (1) whether a bad outcome no one chose, and no one could have avoided, can still be a mistake, and what the word would then be doing; (2) whether "affluent" in the claim about hunter-gatherers describes a level of welfare or only the narrowness of the wants being measured, so that the comparison may be between a full larder and a small appetite; (3) whether irreversibility matters, since once a population exceeds what the landscape supports without cultivation the option of returning is gone, and the question becomes what the first generation owed the tenth; (4) whether the population-ethics point cuts for or against the verdict, given that the additional people farming fed would otherwise never have existed, so a per-capita loss and a total gain can both be true. Each should put the strongest version of the other's case and concede what genuinely needs conceding, rather than winning on a technicality.

After the dialogue, add a brief note in your own voice: which of the two you find has the better case, which single consideration would change your verdict if it went the other way, and where the dialogue leans on a stipulation or a contested premise rather than on argument.
