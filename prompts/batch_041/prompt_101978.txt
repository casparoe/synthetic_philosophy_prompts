I'm a novelist — three books, none of them bestsellers — and through a series of bad decisions I've ended up on the grievance committee of my writers' guild. We meet Thursday, and I have to circulate a written opinion before then. That's the thing I need you to draft.

Here's the case. A member, call her R., spent two years as the uncredited ghostwriter on a memoir that's now enormous. She signed a work-for-hire contract with an NDA, and our own rules say a ghostwriter who's waived authorship in writing can't come back and claim it. She isn't asking us to void the contract. She's asking the guild to recognize her role publicly, and to change the rule going forward so the person who actually wrote the book gets named.

The obvious move is "credit her," and that's exactly why I can't just write that. Two things are eating me.

First, R. says she doesn't want credit handed to her by the guild. A recognition that exists because a rule says so isn't the thing she's missing. She wants the client to acknowledge her, and the client — through his lawyer — has offered a compromise: he'll record her contribution in an archive that stays sealed until he dies or consents, so the fact is preserved but the acknowledgment is deferred. She says that's worse than nothing, a promise of recognition from a man who'll be dead. Meanwhile someone on the committee has floated what she calls a polite alternative: credit the role instead of the person — a line in the front matter saying the book was written with an uncredited collaborator, no name attached. She says recognizing a category isn't recognizing her, and I half think she's right.

Second, we have a mirror case in the same packet: a writer who WAS credited and hates it, says the credit reads like a leash and follows her into every negotiation.

A friend of mine keeps saying this is just the Hegel master-slave thing, but it looks backwards to me. In Hegel the one who does the work ends up with something real and the master ends up hollow and dependent on a recognition that doesn't count. Here the one who did the work is invisible and the guy who did nothing gets all the recognition, and the recognition seems to be working fine for him. So either Hegel's wrong, or I'm holding it upside down, or the analogy breaks somewhere I can't see.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate. I need a concrete recommendation we can actually vote on Thursday — change the rule, issue a statement, do nothing, whatever you conclude — with a timeframe, the reasoning, and a genuinely fair hearing for whichever side you land against. Don't bother with citations or reading lists, nobody's checking. Two pages max, it goes in the packet.
