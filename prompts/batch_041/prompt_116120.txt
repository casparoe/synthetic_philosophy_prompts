I have been arguing with a friend about Thomson's lamp, and I have talked myself into a position I cannot yet defend. My claim is that the lamp is a poor ambassador for the family of puzzles it is supposed to represent: its apparent paradox is manufactured by the fact that the description never settles what happens at the accumulation time, so the standard reply — that the case simply has no fact of the matter to discover, and therefore no contradiction to expose — is available far too cheaply. If that reply always works, supertasks look like a mere bookkeeping problem about infinite sequences, when I suspect the real action lies in the idealizations (point masses, instantaneous impulses, exact real-valued event times) that we smuggle in without noticing. I would like you to build me a case that closes off that cheap reply. Specifically: construct a supertask in ordinary classical mechanics in which the state of the system at the accumulation time is fully determined by the mechanics, and in which the determined state is not the state you obtain by taking the limit of the states immediately before the accumulation time — so that a reader cannot say the description is silent, because the description actively rules the limiting state out. I want the case honest enough to be read as a small theorem rather than a stipulation: it should show that there exist supertasks whose accumulation state is forced, and it should leave me with a general condition under which such forcing occurs.

Please work to the following specifications. First, it must be a supertask in the strict sense — countably many discrete events, each of finite duration, all of the same physical kind (collisions, contacts, emissions), completed by a finite time — and not a hypertask with uncountably many steps. No lamps, switches, dials, or other devices whose changes of state are decreed rather than caused. Second, the physics must be thoroughly ordinary: classical point masses or rigid bodies, elastic or impulsive collisions, finite speeds, finite total energy, no exotic spacetime, no time travel, no infinite forces, and no appeal to an infinite collection of independent agents, machines, or clockwork helpers. Third, you must write down the initial data explicitly and derive every pre-accumulation state from the equations of motion; nothing about the accumulation time itself may be stipulated. Fourth, the state at the accumulation time must be discontinuous from the limit of the earlier states, and that earlier limit state must be excluded by the mechanics rather than merely unattained. Fifth, the verdict should not be an artifact of the topology in which the limit is taken: state which topology you are using and whether the verdict survives a change of topology; if it does not survive, say exactly where it breaks and what that shows.

After the scenario, do the checks. Write out the initial data and the sequence of interaction times, and show that the events really do have an accumulation point and that the relevant conserved quantities are tracked through every individual interaction. Identify the state at the accumulation time and name the precise principle that forces it, distinguishing that principle from mere continuity of the trajectory. Show that the limit of the pre-accumulation states is a different state (or fails to exist) and explain why that does not amount to underdescription. Then run the case backwards: is the time-reversed evolution also a solution of the same equations? If it is not, say what that does to the case and whether it can be repaired. Finally, tell me which single feature of the construction is doing the work, what becomes of the verdict if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the no-fact-of-the-matter line would attack the case. The purpose of the exercise is to support the argument described above, so a case that assumes the verdict it is supposed to deliver — or that reaches it only through an idealization so extreme that no one would accept it — is of no use to me.
