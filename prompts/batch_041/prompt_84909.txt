I'm putting together a three-year project on precommitment — me, one postdoc, one PhD student, starting next fall — and the thing we're supposed to produce at the end of it is a book on when it's legitimate for an agent or a polity to tie its own hands. I've been circling the same handful of questions for a year and getting nowhere, so what I want from you is an actual agenda, not a survey.

Here's the framing I keep coming back to; tell me if its a dead end. I think there are three quite different structures hiding under the single heading of precommitment. (1) The Ulysses case: one agent, at t1, binding herself against her own anticipated weakness at t2. Binder and bindee are the same person, and the whole point is that she doesn't trust herself. (2) The constitutional case: a present majority entrenching something against future majorities, who never agreed to it and may not be able to identify with the entrenching coalition at all. (3) The contract case: the binding is done by someone else — the crew lashing Ulysses to the mast, the doctor, the bank — so enforcement comes from outside, which raises the awkward question of what the second party's standing is and why the agent can't simply untie herself.

My hunch is that a lot of the hand-waving in the constitutional literature comes from sliding between (1) and (2), and that (2) needs a seperate justification — something like: the arrangement has to be one the bound parties could recognise as protecting them too, plus some kind of trigger that the bound generation can pull without the binders' consent. And I suspect (3) is doing more work in the real world than either of the others.

So which questions should we actually work on first? Give me three, maybe four, in order, and defend the order. I care about which ones other questions depend on, which are tractable for people whose toolkit is philosophy and legal theory rather than empirical political science, which are being neglected because they look settled, and what would move a question up or down the list. And don't give me a reading list — I want the arguements.
