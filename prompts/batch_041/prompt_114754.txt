On that view math is basically a formal game played with marks on paper, and a statement like 2+2=4 isn't about anything real, its just a legal move in the game, so the nominalist has to say mathematical statements are neither true nor false. Fictionalism is the same idea in a fancier outfit: math is a useful story, like a novel, and 2+2=4 is true the way "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" is true.

Platonism used to be the default, but it got hit hard by what's called the Benacerraf dilemma. Benacerraf pointed out that if numbers are these abstract objects then we could never have any contact with them - you can't see a number, touch a number, or bump into one in a lab. So how could we ever know anything about them? And since we obviously do know a huge amount about them, the platonist picture has to be wrong. The dilemma part is that if you give up the abstract objects you lose the idea that math is objectively true, and if you keep them you can't explain how we know about them. Either way platonism is dead.

Platonists have one big comeback, the Quine-Putnam indispensability argument: our best scientific theories can't even be stated without mathematics, and we're supposed to believe in whatever our best theories say exists, so we should believe in numbers the same way we believe in electrons.

So in the end the whole thing comes down to taste. The math is the same either way, nothing in actual practice depends on it, and you can pick whichever picture you find comforting.'
