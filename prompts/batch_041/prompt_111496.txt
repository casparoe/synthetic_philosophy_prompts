I teach a final-year seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and this term I want to use Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) as the set piece for the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas. What I need from you is a reading of the film through that theory, faithful in both directions: the Nyāya apparatus must do real work on the film's structure, and the film must be allowed to push back where it does not fit. Please do not give me a general essay on Eastern philosophy and the elusiveness of truth, and please do not retell the plot; I know the film and my students will have watched it the week before. I want the kind of piece I can circulate as a handout — specific, argumentative, and honest about where the reading fails.

Set out the apparatus as you go, so that students can follow it, and make it answer to the following characterisation rather than to a textbook summary. Nyāya admits four means of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison or analogy, and verbal testimony — and it insists that the last of these is a source in its own right and not a disguised inference, provided the speaker is a reliable authority and the matter is one the hearer's own senses and inferences could not otherwise reach. Perception it defines as cognition arising from contact between a sense faculty and an object, untainted by verbal association, not deviating from its object, and determinate, while still distinguishing an earlier, inchoate stage of sensory givenness from the structured cognition that follows. Memory, doubt, hypothetical reasoning and error it refuses to count as pramāṇas at all, and error it explains as misattribution — the placing of a memory-image onto what is actually before one. Against Mīmāṃsā and Advaita, which treat a cognition's validity as intrinsic and which add postulation and non-apprehension to the list of sources, Nyāya holds that validity is extrinsic: a cognition is innocent until challenged, confirmed after the fact by further cognition and by successful practical activity, and liable to be cancelled later by a cognition that displaces it.

Now the film. It gives you four reports of a single death — the bandit's, the wife's, the dead husband's delivered through a medium, and the woodcutter's — together with a frame in which a priest, the woodcutter and a passing commoner shelter at the ruined gate and try to make sense of what they have heard. Work through the following, and keep the film's own images in view rather than reducing it to an illustration of a doctrine.

- Whose cognition is each report? Take the medium seriously. If testimony is knowledge derived from a reliable speaker's cognition, what is the status of a report uttered through a body that was not present at the event, on behalf of a speaker who is dead? Say whether Nyāya's conditions could be met there at all, and what the failure shows.
- The woodcutter perceives, and then withholds. That is not error but concealment, and it may not even be self-deception. Does Nyāya's account of error through the defects of passion and aversion stretch to cover it, or does the film expose a gap between being wrong and refusing to say? The dagger is the test case.
- The commoner reasons from a general cynicism about human nature to conclusions about these particular people. Reconstruct that as a Nyāya inference, with the five steps of thesis, reason, example, application and conclusion, and say which of the fallacies of the reason it commits.
- Nyāya is a realist school, and on its account at most one of the four reports can be veridical; the film's reputation is for a relativism that would deny there is any fact to be had. Say where the film genuinely commits to that relativism, where it merely withholds, and where the apparent relativism is an artefact of the frame. Then say plainly where the film resists the Nyāya reading and you cannot force the fit.
- Note the medium of the film itself: the accounts are shown to us as flashbacks, so that we seem to perceive what is in fact narrated to us. What does the Nyāya insistence that testimony is a distinct source imply about our position as audience, and about the common critical claim that the film makes us witnesses?
- The ending turns on the priest's recovered trust and the woodcutter's taking up the abandoned child. Is that a resolution of doubt of the kind Nyāya's extrinsic-validity thesis would licence — validity settled by what one then does — or is the film quietly substituting moral consolation for epistemic settlement, and is that a confusion or a correction?

Close with what the film contributes to the Nyāya debate that a philosophical example could not: in particular, whether the school's concentration on the reliability of the speaker leaves it unable to say anything about the reliability of a speaker's own self-conception, and whether the four accounts are best read as competing pramāṇas at all, or as four failures of the same one.

No quotations from the film or from any text, no citations and no reading list — I want the argument only.
