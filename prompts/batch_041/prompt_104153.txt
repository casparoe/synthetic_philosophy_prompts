I sit on the judicial conduct commission of a country that came out of a long authoritarian period about a decade ago — call it the Republic of Marne, the real name doesn't matter for you — and we're hearing a case against a sitting appellate judge that I have to turn into a draft opinion before Thursday's closed session. The facts: back in 2011, under an emergency decree issued by the old regime's interior ministry, the judge ordered a newspaper editor held in indefinite "administrative detention" without charge. The decree was formally impeccable — properly promulgated, within the powers the old constitution gave the ministry, and the high court of the day upheld it twice. The judge says he had no discretion; the statute said "shall," and any judge who balked was himself removable. The editor was held four years and died in custody of an untreated illness. The judge has had a clean record since, and three former detainees have written in supporting him, saying he treated them decently.

Here's the knot. Our enabling statute tells us, in its operative section, to decide every case "according to the law in force at the time of the conduct," and then in its preamble says the commission exists "to vindicate the rule of law and the dignity of persons." Half the commission reads the preamble as pure rhetoric and wants to apply the old decree as law — which means the judge acted lawfully and there's nothing to punish. The other half wants to say the decree was never really law at all, that a command so unjust forfeits its legal character, so the judge was violating the law he swore to uphold. I keep thinking there's a third door, but every time I open one it turns into one of the other two. I've been rereading that old Hart–Fuller exchange and it strikes me neither of them ever had to actually run a commission.

Please draft the opinion I'll circulate: a concrete recommendation (remove from the bench, censure and retain, or decline jurisdiction, with a time frame for any sanction), the reasoning, and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Two things I specifically want you to take a position on, because I think that's where the real action is: first, whether the commission can consistently treat the old decree as not-law while still leaning on old-regime legal acts all over the place — the judge's own commission, the property titles, the marriages, the pardons — since if we say the regime's commands weren't law, its not obvious why its appointment of this judge was valid, and if we say they were law, it's not obvious what the preamble is even for; and second, whether the right place to put the "intolerable injustice" line is at the level of the decree's validity, the judge's legal duty, or his moral blameworthiness, because I suspect the two camps are each parking it somewhere different and talking past each other. Don't just recite the famous positions — I need something I can read aloud to six commissioners who disagree with each other. Write it in American English, tell me how confident you are in the recommendation you land on, and flag the single step in your reasoning you're least sure about; I'd alot rather know where the soft spot is now than be surprised by it Thursday.
