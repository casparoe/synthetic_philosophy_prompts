I'm doing a feature for a science mag about where the line for animal consciousness should be drawn, and next week I've got a philosopher on the phone who's spent years defending what she calls the wiring-speed criterion: a creature counts as conscious only if it has neural tissue that can hold a state long enough to broadcast it across distant regions — a few hundred milliseconds of the stuff, not fast reflex loops. On her line octopuses and crabs are in, fish and insects are out. Can you draft me 8-10 interview questions that get past her just restating it? I want at least one on the size problem (a fruit fly's whole brain is roughly 140,000 neurons — does that rule flies out, or does it just show the criterion is a size proxy in disguise?), one on wether slow wiring is really a fact about consciousness or only about the kind of memory you can test in a lab, and one on convergence (if consciousness earns its keep, why would evolution only bother building it in the lineages that happen to have slow wiring?). For each of those, give me the follow-up that still works once she gives her standard reply. Then work in a few others: whether a threshold criterion even makes sense for a trait that looks continuous; what she does with crows, who cache and plan and seem to keep score without anything like the wiring she's asking for; what would actually change her mind, and please don't let the answer be more data; and one on the practical fallout — her line spares the octopus and lets us boil shrimp by the ton, so how does she square a switch with the idea that what's at stake is a degree of moral risk? One warning: she's a good sport but she rolls her eyes at the heckle that you can't get consciousness from behaviour, so if you want to press the evidence problem, do it through her own criterion rather than from outside it.
