I've been going around in circles for months with a friend from my decision theory seminar about the common prior assumption, and it's definately driving me nuts because we agree on every single theorem and still never move. She's in the Harsanyi/Aumann camp: if two rational agents share a common prior and then just update on their private information, they can't agree to disagree — so persistent, sincere disagreement between people who each respect the other's rationality has to be some kind of rational failure, and the common prior is basically what "rational" means for a group. I'm on the other side: I think the common prior is an empirical assumption, usually false, and that two agents can be perfectly rational, share all the evidence they'll ever get, and still rationally disagree because their priors just differ — Aumann's theorem, as I see it, assumes the thing in dispute and then reports that you can't dispute it. We keep re-deriving the same math at each other and it settles nothing.

What really wedges us is an example she invented, which she calls "the twins": two agents wired at birth with different priors, who then live through the identical evidence stream and end up believing different things. She says the twins can't both be rational, because rationality plus common knowledge of rationality plus a common prior rules it out, and the common prior is precisely what "the same information" has to mean for a group. I say that just shows the theorem is about an idealized species that starts from a shared point, not about what an actual agent ought to believe, and that "rational" is doing two different jobs in her mouth — sometimes it means internally coherent, sometimes it means would-agree-with-an-ideal-observer. So: is this a substantive disagreement, or a verbal one where we mean different things by "rational," or are we quietly answering different questions under one heading (her a descriptive one about a formal model, me a normative one about real agents)? Or is there a hidden empirical/formal question underneath — like whether a common prior can be justified at all rather than merely stipulated — that neither of us is actually addressing? Diagnose what kind of disagreement this is, grounded in what each side really claims, and then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, or honestly nothing, and why.

And be upfront about how confident you are in the diagnosis — flag the one part of it you're least sure about.
