I'm a novelist — I'm about 200 pages into a book set in a consciousness research lab, the kind of place where two people have been having the same fight since the mid-90s, and I've hit a wall: I can't write the big blow-up scene until I actually know what these two agree on. My two are (a) a heterophenomenologist, the Dennett type — the subject's reports are data about what the subject believes and says, you treat them like the text of a novel, you never get to peek behind them at the real experience; and (b) a neurophenomenologist, the first-person-methods, Varela type — you train subjects for weeks in a disciplined way of reporting, and those reports are supposed to constrain the neural theory, not just get interpreted away.

What I need is the joint statement those two could both sign after a long good-faith collaboration — the thing my characters would tape to the lab wall. So:

- the claims both can put their names to: what the data actually is, what counts as a report, what taking reports seriously does and doesn't commit you to, and what neither of them is claiming (I'm pretty sure each has a strawman of the other and I want the strawmen formally retired in writing)
- where they still genuinely split, with each side's position in wording that side would accept — no caricature, and no mush that means nothing
- the cruxes: the sub-questions that, if they got settled, would actually move at least one of them
- for each crux, something both would agree bears on it — a case, a study design, a result — with each side's prediction written down in advance, so I know which way the scene tips

Some specific things I want handled, because they're what the argument scene is really about: whether you can tell a trained report from an untrained one without already assuming the phenomenological theory that says training matters; whether the it's-just-a-fiction move is a piece of method or a metaphysical claim smuggled in dressed as method (and if it's the latter, is the fight partly verbal — say so, and name which part); whether the interviewer-guided second-person way of eliciting reports is contamination or the only route to the pre-reflective stuff at all; and whether first-person methods ever make a prediction that could come out wrong, or whether they only ever redescribe what the neuroscience found. Those four are the cruxes as far as I can tell, but use your judgement.

Format it as a numbered list of agreed claims, then a table of the disagreements with a crux column and a predictions column. Casual register is fine, my characters talk like real people. And if the honest verdict is that the two of them are using the same word for different things, fine, say that too — just be seperate and specific about which word.
