For three years our state health plan's coverage council has applied a statute that imposes a surcharge on treatment for any condition "attributable to the member's own conduct," without ever defining the phrase, and I have been asked to write the staff recommendation for next month's meeting. Behind the statutory language, I take it, sits some version of the thought that disadvantages members bring on themselves should be theirs to bear while those that fall on them through circumstance should be shared — but the council has never said which version, and its rulings have not obviously followed a single test. I want a recommendation that is consistent with what the council has actually done rather than with what I happen to think is fair.

The record, in brief. No surcharge was imposed for a congenital heart defect, for injuries from a crash caused by another driver, or for lung cancer in a lifelong non-smoker — in none of these was there conduct of the member's to point to. No surcharge was imposed, either, for a respiratory disease in a member who took a second job in a dusty plant because it was the only work available, and who was never warned of the specific hazard: a voluntary act, but one taken without any real alternative and without notice of the risk. A surcharge was imposed for complications of elective cosmetic surgery, for a knee reconstruction after a recreational skiing fall, and for a wound infection following a refusal of a tetanus booster that three clinicians had recommended — in each of these a voluntary act, an available alternative, and a risk the member could have foreseen.

Next month's case: a member with a serious but treatable heart condition that developed from a dietary pattern the member knowingly maintained despite repeated advice from clinicians about precisely this risk. The member also carries a diagnosis of binge-eating disorder with a documented neurobiological basis, and was raised in a household where that diet was effectively the only food available. The member did not change the pattern.

What decision best coheres with the record? Which precedent is the closest analog? Lay out the candidate rules the record is consistent with — I can see at least one keyed to whether the member had a reasonable alternative to the risky conduct, and one keyed to whether the member knew or could reasonably have foreseen the specific risk — state what each predicts here, and identify which cruder reading the record already rules out. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, tell me what principle the council would be adopting with each choice, and what further case would separate the surviving rules. Finally, tell me which of the familiar objections to responsibility-tracking in health care — that it abandons the imprudent, that it is unduly harsh, that it makes members answer for conditions they never chose — the council has already walked into, and whether any of them surfaces as an inconsistency in its own record.
