For the past several months I have been helping to assemble a reference work that sorts political movements, past and present, into a left–right taxonomy, and the task has turned out to be far less clerical than I expected. The entries that trouble me are precisely the ones in which the economic description and the cultural description pull in opposite directions; working on them has made me doubt that I know what, if anything, holds a side together. This matters to me beyond the job, because I have always described myself as belonging to one side, and I am no longer confident the description survives contact with cases like these. Before I submit my own classifications, I need to be able to anticipate the rulings of the project's lead editor, since her calls are the working standard for the rest of us.

Her rulings so far, as recorded in the project files:

1. A federation of industrial trade unions demanding the nationalization of heavy industry, a statutory living wage, and universal pensions. Left.
2. An association of manufacturers and financiers seeking lower corporate taxes, weaker labor protections, and lighter environmental regulation. Right.
3. A movement of students and artists against censorship, established religion, and paternalistic morality laws, whose economic program is vague and largely confined to public funding for the arts. Left.
4. A technocratic movement pressing for central planning, steep wealth taxes, and the nationalization of banking, which takes no clear position on social or cultural questions. Left.
5. A religious-traditionalist movement for moral order, confessional schooling, and the defense of the national culture, economically friendly to large employers. Right.
6. A national-sovereignty movement demanding strict borders, cultural homogeneity, and the restoration of traditional authority, alongside generous pensions and tariffs to shelter domestic workers. Right.

The entry now on my desk combines an emphatically egalitarian economic program — breaking up large firms, heavy taxes on inherited wealth, worker cooperatives, a generous welfare state — with a cultural and political program that is traditionalist and nationalist: it wants to restrict immigration, promote the majority religion, uphold traditional family forms, and is openly impatient with liberal procedural norms and with the claims of minorities. Its rhetoric is populist, pitting a virtuous people against a corrupt elite; much of its rank-and-file support has drifted over from what used to be the industrial left, while its intellectuals, its funders, and the established parties that court it come from the right.

I am not asking you to tell me what this movement really is. I want your best prediction of how she will classify it, together with the reasoning that gets you there. Please give me: (a) the several distinct rules that would each fit her six prior rulings, and what each of those rules predicts for the new entry; (b) your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals; and (c) the single further case, were it to turn up in the archive, that would most cleanly force a choice among the leading rules, and why.

Be explicit about how much of your prediction rests on genuine evidence from her record as opposed to your own prior about how movements of this kind are usually classified; I am less interested in a confident answer than in an honest map of the uncertainty. And tell me what would change your mind — which item in her record, or which consideration about the nature of the left–right divide, would lead you to predict the opposite verdict, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to break down.
