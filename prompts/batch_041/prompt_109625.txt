I've been going back and forth with a couple of friends about the behavioural insights team our city hired, and I can't get the shape of the disagreement straight. The stuff they do is all of a piece: they flipped the organ donor register to opt-out, they got the pension scheme set up so you're enrolled automatically and have to go out of your way to tick the box and leave, they put the "most of your neighbours use less electricity than you" line on the bill, they moved the fruit nearer the till. And what they're proudest of is that they never tell anybody anything. The pitch is: we don't lecture, we don't fine, we don't ban — we just rearrange the furniture so the thing we want is the path of least resistance. But it's the same council that's running the blunt "you ought to give up smoking" ads on the side of the buses and handing out fines for leaving your engine running outside the school. So what does the nudge half of it actually presuppose about reasons for action?

Specifically: if you design a policy so that it can only ever work by hooking into motivations people already have, are you presupposing that a reason for someone to do something has to be connected to something they already care about — that if you're genuinely indifferent to having money in old age, there's no reason for you to save, only a reason-for-the-council or a reason-you'd-have-if-you-wanted-something-else? Or is that just a liberal political constraint they've adopted (don't coerce, don't be paternalistic) that's perfectly compatible with believing there are reasons that apply to you whether or not you care? And here's the tangle I can't undo. Half the justification for these nudges is that people's choices are warped by present bias, anchoring, whatever — so what they pick isn't what's actually good for them. That sounds like it needs some standard of your good that isn't just whatever you happen to want, which looks like the opposite of the other thing. Can one practice coherantly presuppose both? Reconstruct the view of reasons it commits to, and be careful to separate what it strictly has to presuppose from what it merely fits nicely with — I suspect a lot of rival views would license exactly the same policies, but I want to know which ones get ruled out.

For contrast: the neighbouring borough just cancelled its whole nudge programme and spent the money on free evening classes in basic stats and household budgeting instead, on the theory that you don't steer people at all, you teach them the skill and then they decide for themselves. Does that really commit to something different about people and reasons, or is that an illusion too? And please tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong and what would actually change your mind about it — what you'd have to hear the nudgers say about their own work, or what argument, to conclude that the practice presupposes external reasons after all, or that it doesn't presuppose any particular view.
