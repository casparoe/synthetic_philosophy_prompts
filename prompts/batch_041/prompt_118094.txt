I am a novelist at work on a short book set in a magistrate's court in a fishing town, and I have lately been reading Paul Grice on meaning and conversation, which has left me with a story whose shape I can see but cannot yet write. My narrator is the court interpreter — the only person in the room who commands both the defendant's dialect and the formal register of the bench. The dialect obliges a speaker to mark, grammatically, how she came by what she is asserting: whether she saw it herself, heard it from another, or is only surmising. Nothing can be said in it without also saying how one knows. The prosecution's case rests on a single sentence of testimony, and the interpreter must render it either as a bare assertion, which would convict the defendant, or in a way that carries the witness's uncertainty, which would not. I want the story to turn not on where her sympathies lie but on the theory of meaning: on what a speaker says as against what she merely implicates, and on whether that distinction survives being carried across a language boundary.

I would like the story to dramatise three things without ever lecturing about them. First, the way an implicature can be taken back — a scene in which someone tries, after the fact, to cancel what her words conveyed, and finds that she can do so without contradicting herself, or cannot. Second, the way certain meanings cling to a particular word rather than to the shared expectations of conversation, so that they cannot be paraphrased away and cannot be retracted; that is the interpreter's predicament in miniature. Third, and most important to me, the reflexive structure of meaning: that to mean something by an utterance is to intend one's hearer to recognise one's intention, and to intend that the recognition itself do the work. The defendant and the interpreter have a private understanding built almost entirely out of such recognitions, and I want the climax to be a moment in which something is unmistakably conveyed and can never be proved — the one thing the court cannot enter into the record.

I am also curious about the sort of suggestion that seems to travel with the mere shape of a sentence rather than with any particular context, a hint that no hearer can plausibly pretend not to hear, and about whether such a hint belongs to what the words mean or only to what a speaker can be presumed to have meant. Keep the interpreter's professional ethics in the background rather than the foreground; the interest lies in the grammar of what was meant. I would rather have the concepts embodied in the dialogue and in the silences than explained by the narrator, and I would rather the ending be exact than consoling.
