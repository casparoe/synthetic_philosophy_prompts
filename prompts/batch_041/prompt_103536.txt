My colleague Nadia works on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness, and I want to get my prediction in before she weighs in on a case our lab is about to write up. Her verdicts so far, all of them answers to the same question -- how many streams of experience are there here:

- full commissurotomy in an adult, the classic disconnection picture: two. She's unmoved by the fact that the patient reports feeling like one person and gets through the day fine.
- congenital absence of the corpus callosum, where the person never had one and shows little or nothing on the usual disconnection battery: one.
- alien hand after a stroke, callosum completely intact: one -- just one person having a very strange experience of her own hand.
- dissociative identity disorder, intact brain, alters who can't get at each others' memories and describe themselves differently: one, since at any given moment there's one stream running.
- hemispherectomy: one, obviously.

Now the new case. Two healthy adults, each with an ordinary intact brain, are hooked up by a two-way neural link our collaborators built. It isn't the usualy one-bit-per-trial demo: continuous visual and somatosensory traffic both ways, latency around 30 ms, and after training each volunteer can steer their own hand using the other's visual field and reports feeling touches delivered to the other's hand, faint but real. Everything else untouched -- two bodies, two autobiographies, two inner voices, two brains sharing no tissue.
