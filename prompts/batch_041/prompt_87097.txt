I've been argueing with a friend about whether the past actually constrains the present or whether "we got locked in" is just a story people tell after the fact, and we agreed to settle it on one concrete case instead of trading abstractions. The case I want to use is Japan's power grid, which is split between two frequencies -- 50 Hz in the east (Tokyo and up through Tohoku and Hokkaido) and 60 Hz in the west (Osaka, Nagoya, Kyushu and so on). As I understand it the split goes back to the 1890s, when the eastern and western utilities bought their generating equipment from different manufacturers, German kit in one case and American kit in the other, and nobody coordinated; the boundary then got baked in along the regional utility territories, which is why it runs through places like Shizuoka and Niigata rather than following any river or mountain range. The two halves are tied together by a few back-to-back frequency converter stations (the first at Sakuma, I think in the 60s) whose combined capacity is a small fraction of what either grid can generate, which is why after the 2011 earthquake and the nuclear shutdown the east couldn't just buy power from the west even when the west had plenty to spare. Don't sweat exact dates or megawatts, I'm hazy on those and they aren't the point.

What I want is this run as a case of path dependence in the economists' sense -- the Paul David / Brian Arthur picture where small early events get amplified by increasing returns and you end up stuck with something you'd never pick on the merits, QWERTY as the poster child, and alot of sceptics who think that whole literature is folklore. So map it properly. What's the actual mechanism here: increasing returns in the technical sense (learning-by-doing, network effects, everyone coordinating on a single standard), or just sunk capital and switching costs, and does the distinction matter for whether the theory applies at all? Was the original choice genuinely arbitrary in the relevant way, given that neither frequency was obviously better and both worked fine for lighting and motors? And would the same outcome have arrived anyway -- is the interesting fact that Japan ended up with two frequencies rather than that it ended up with those two, and does a national split look contingent when Europe converged on 50 and North America on 60? I've also read that for decades many regions ran both frequencies side by side, which if true makes the lock-in less clean than the QWERTY version, so say something about that if it's right.

Then the part I actually care about: where the path-dependence story breaks or overreaches on this case. If the split survives mostly because converting is expensive rather than because the system is dynamically self-reinforcing, that seems like trouble for the stronger claims; and if modern power electronics and HVDC mean the split could be papered over at a price nobody wants to pay, I want to know whether that confirms the theory's pessimism or shows the persistence has a boring explanation with nothing to do with lock-in. And what does the case show back at the theory -- a clean confirmation, an anomaly, or a locked-in inefficiency that's smaller than the cost of ever fixing it, so that the theory's favorite kind of example turns out to be doing less work than advertised. Where you're unsure, say so rather than picking a side.
