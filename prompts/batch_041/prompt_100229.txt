I am research counsel to a standing law reform commission in a common-law jurisdiction. Every year the commission receives a handful of references asking whether conduct whose principal alleged wrong is that it is immoral — not harmful in any way we can readily point to — should be made a criminal offence, should remain one, or should be decriminalized. Current references include consensual incest between adult siblings, sexual conduct between a person and an animal, and the public desecration of religious symbols. Our recommendations go to the Attorney-General, who treats them as presumptive; the commission is five members, one of whom holds that any conduct the community finds vile should stay on the books; we get roughly six weeks per reference; and we have no budget for original empirical work beyond what the statistics agency already publishes. I need a decision procedure a lawyer with no philosophy training can run in a day, and I need it to be honest about where it is doing philosophy rather than applying a test.

Build it from the Devlin–Hart controversy over the enforcement of morals. Use Devlin's thesis that a shared morality is part of the social bond and may be enforced to prevent social disintegration, his standard of the ordinary person's intolerance, indignation, and disgust, and his concession that there remains a realm of private morality the law should leave alone; use Hart's reply that the disintegration claim is an empirical hypothesis rather than a premise, that a society can survive moral pluralism, and that we must distinguish the positive morality a community happens to hold from the critical morality we are entitled to defend. Supplement this with the later harm-and-offence distinction, so the procedure can separate conduct that wrongs identifiable persons, conduct that merely offends, and conduct that is condemned without either. Keep the procedure sensitive to the fact that the community's morality may be divided rather than shared, and give it a way to treat near-unanimity, bare majority, and genuine controversy differently. Note that our Supreme Court has struck down two morality-based offences in the last decade on privacy grounds, so the procedure must also flag constitutional vulnerability and say when that alone decides the matter.

Deliver the procedure as ordered questions with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which it stops with a no, and the points at which it must hand the decision to the commissioners as a question of value rather than fact. Specify the output: a recommendation, a confidence level, and the triggers that require a written dissent.

Then run it on all three references, including at least one where it reaches a conclusion the commission will not want. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire, which distinctions it had to flatten to be usable, and what it simply cannot settle.
