I want a credence, not a survey of positions. My own number is close to 0.5; a colleague puts it near 0.85, and I am trying to learn whether the gap is in our framing or in our facts.

The proposition, which I will call the Asymmetry Thesis: the force of the retroactivity objection to the Nuremberg and Tokyo convictions derives overwhelmingly from the fact that the tribunals were constituted by the victorious belligerents, not from the retroactivity of the law they applied. Had a tribunal with identical jurisdiction, identical substantive law, and identical defendants been convened by a body that was not a party to the conflict, the objection would have lost more than half its force. If you reject that operationalization — the more-than-half clause in particular — substitute your own, state it explicitly before answering, and hold to it throughout.

Decompose the question. I expect at least the following terms, each with a probability, one or two lines of justification, and a stated combination rule:

Norm-retroactivity. Was aggressive war already prohibited as between states before the tribunals sat, so that what the charters created was liability rather than the norm? And if the prohibition bound states only, how far does extending it to individuals amount to creating a crime?

Liability-retroactivity. Granting that individual liability for aggression was new, is the maxim that no crime exists without pre-existing law a moral requirement or a system-relative rule of recognition? Separate the two readings — I believe they pull in opposite directions, and that the thesis is only interesting under one of them.

Forum-retroactivity. Is the composition of the court — who appointed the judges, who selected the defendants, whose conduct went unexamined — a defect of legality or merely of fairness? The thesis needs it to be the latter; say what that costs you.

Ratification. Do the later codification and broad acceptance of these norms answer the ex ante question, or is that a confusion of legitimacy with subsequent approval?

The Tokyo variation. Tokyo differed from Nuremberg in the charges actually pressed, in the treatment of the head of state, and in the separate opinions. Does that variation raise or lower your credence, and does it reveal which of the sub-questions is doing the real work?

Then, in this order: (a) name the sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and give the number you would hold if you flipped your answer on it; (b) say what would move your number most — an argument, a historical finding, or a conceptual distinction I have not supplied; (c) say whether the thesis is well-formed at all, or whether retroactivity and victors' justice are two names for a single defect, in which case the thesis is either trivial or incoherent, and tell me which; and (d) briefly, say what your decomposition implies for a tribunal convened tomorrow by a coalition to try the leaders of a state it has just defeated, on a charge the coalition's own conduct arguably instantiates — give a number there too, and identify which of your sub-questions that case tests.
