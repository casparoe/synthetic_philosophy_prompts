Ok, I've been going in circles on this and I need an outside view. The problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory -- the Mercury perihelion / general relativity kind of case, where a theory gets credit for something we already knew -- has been debated for decades and nobody has moved an inch. As far as I can tell the camps are: (1) it's a real defect in the formalism, and the fix is technical (counterfactual credences, a novelty factor bolted on, conditioning on a corpus rather than on everything you happen to know); (2) it's not a defect at all, since the probability calculus was never meant to model the psychology of one scientist changing their mind, and the whole puzzle comes from expecting it to; (3) it's a symptom of the deeper idealization that agents are logically omniscient and certain of what they know, so old evidence is just the place where that idealization shows through.

My hunch is that the debate has stalled becuase two seperate phenomena are hiding under one label. One is evidence that merely happened to be around before the theory was invented (Mercury). The other is evidence that was actually used to construct the theory -- and that second thing is just the ordinary statistical sin of using the same data to generate a hypothesis and to test it, i.e. overfitting / selection effects. If that's right, then no amount of tinkering with conditionalization fixes the second case, because the trouble isn't in the probability axioms, it's in what the prior is allowed to depend on; and the technical-fix camp is solving the wrong problem while the no-problem camp is right about Mercury and wrong in general. Try it on a clean case: two rival theories dreamt up to explain the same old datum, equally simple, where only one of them also predicts something new. Does anything in the standard menu of solutions tell you what to do there, or does the distinction just fall out of the formalism?

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? A substantive one about the norms of confirmation? A disagreement about what the formalism is for -- a descriptive account of credence change versus a timeless logic of support? Or two groups answering different questions under one name? Is some hidden formal result doing the work? And if my old-versus-used distinction is real, does the honest move end up being to relativize confirmation to a corpus, and if so which corpus -- because that smells like making confirmation agent-relative, which is its own mess.

I want a definite diagnosis, not a menu. Tell me which of these you'd actually bet on and why, what would count as progress given it (a distinction, a formal result, dropping the question), or say straight out that nothing should be expected and why. Also flag where you think your own diagnosis is weakest.
