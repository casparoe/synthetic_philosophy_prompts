You are a philosopher of value and a specialist in early twentieth-century Cambridge philosophy, and you are also the instructor of a final-year undergraduate seminar on meaning, value, and the limits of philosophy. I need you to produce one examination item for that seminar: the essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. Think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming the conceptual cruxes in the material below; I want the question aimed at a crux rather than at coverage, so do not settle on the first plausible framing you generate.

The topic is Frank Ramsey's treatment of the meaning of life in the short late piece conventionally called the Epilogue. The position to be examined is roughly this: Ramsey treats the question of life's meaning as arising from within a human perspective rather than from a detached survey of the universe. On this reading, the vastness, age, and indifference of the cosmos are not by themselves a threat to meaning, because the demand that our concerns be vindicated by the scale of the whole is a confusion — a demand to see things to scale when importance is not a scale-relative property. Students will have read the piece, but the examination is closed-book. The question must therefore be answerable by reconstructing the position in paraphrase; do not build any part of the question or the model answer around a verbatim quotation, do not require students to supply one, and do not include citations, reading lists, or references to secondary literature, since a candidate without library access could only guess at them.

Here is the crux the question must force. Ramsey's move looks like it locates meaning in the perspective. But if the perspective is where meaning lives, what stops the view from collapsing into the bare report that people care about what they happen to care about? And if it is not merely a report — if it claims that perspectival importance is genuinely authoritative for the person whose perspective it is — then in what sense is it not simply the objectivism about value that Ramsey's naturalism seems to forbid? Put differently: is the view a thesis about the metaphysics of value, a thesis about the logical form of the question what the meaning of life is, or a quietist refusal to answer that question, and does it matter which? The question must not be answerable by summarising Ramsey, and it must not be answerable by reciting the standard menu of Camus, Tolstoy, Nagel, or objective-attitude accounts of meaning.

To keep the item from being answerable off the shelf, embed in it a small piece of apparatus that is not part of the standard secondary literature. Invent a short case, for use in the examination, in which a person's scientific worldview and her sense of what matters come apart: for instance, a researcher who has spent her career measuring the large-scale structure of the universe, who accepts without reservation that human beings are a transient and spatially negligible feature of it, and who nevertheless finds her work and her attachments as important as ever; or, alternatively, a person who draws the opposite conclusion and finds the scale of things corrosive of importance. The question must ask what the Ramsey-style position implies about which of these responses is correct, or whether it implies that the question of correctness does not arise here. Candidates must be required to say what work the perspectival claim is doing in each case, and whether it can be stated without covertly reintroducing a standard of correctness that transcends the perspective.

The question should be answerable in ninety minutes and should carry twenty marks. Write the question itself in no more than 150 words. Then write a model answer of roughly 700 words, written as a strong candidate would write it rather than as a lecture: it should take a definite line on the crux and defend it, while marking honestly where that line is vulnerable. Then write the marking scheme. The marking scheme should name five must-hit points, assign each a mark value summing to twenty, and for each point give a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. It should also state explicitly which single move separates an A answer from a B answer, and identify the most seductive wrong answer — the one that looks sophisticated but has in fact changed the subject. Where the model answer attributes a specific doctrine to Ramsey, keep the attribution to the broad shape of the view described above and avoid asserting textual details that cannot be checked; where an answer would need such a detail, make the question ask for a reconstruction rather than a quotation.
