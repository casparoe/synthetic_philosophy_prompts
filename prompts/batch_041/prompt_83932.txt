Write a dialogue between two philosophers who both accept panpsychism — that experience, or something proto-experiential, is a fundamental feature of reality — but who draw opposite ethical conclusions from it. The first takes pan-sentience as a serious moral thesis: if experience goes all the way down, there is no non-arbitrary place to stop counting moral patients, and the ordinary business of fundamental physics — colliding particles at high energy, erasing quantum information, letting superpositions decohere — is therefore not ethically neutral. She wants to know what we owe whatever bears these micro-experiences, and whether experiments ought to be designed to minimize their disruption. The second accepts the metaphysics but thinks the ethical inference is a category error: what commands moral concern is suffering, not experience as such, and suffering requires a unified, valence-bearing subject that scattered micro-experiences cannot constitute; he suspects her view turns every measurement into a massacre and can guide no actual decision.

Have them argue rather than trade monologues. The first should press him on where he draws his line and why valence is any less arbitrary a cutoff than complexity, and should confront the problem of how countless micro-experiences could ever add up to a single subject that suffers. The second should press her on the counting problem — how many micro-subjects a given experiment disrupts, and how one of them would be weighed against a person — and on whether her position can recommend any research program rather than merely paralyze one. Neither should win cheaply: let each concede a point, and leave the exchange unresolved. Around 800 words. No appeal to specific papers or results is needed; the disagreement is conceptual, and neither party should invent experimental findings to settle it.
