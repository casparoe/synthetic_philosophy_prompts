After thirty-five years in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of mathematics, and I have drafted the following short essay for a seminar of non-specialists. I would like your comments, chiefly on its accuracy and on whether its central proposal is genuinely interesting or only a restatement of what specialists already say. The essay is called Incompleteness Is Not the Limit You Think It Is.

The popular use of Gödel's theorems outside mathematics runs roughly as follows: since any sufficiently rich formal system contains truths it cannot prove, there are truths beyond any theory, and therefore no final theory of physics. I want to separate what the theorem actually delivers from this conclusion, and then propose a criterion for when it could apply to a scientific theory.

Three clarifications. First, the incompleteness is relative to a system. The sentence Gödel constructs for a system S is unprovable in S if S is consistent; but S together with the statement that S is consistent proves that sentence. The theorem therefore does not yield a sentence unprovable by any means, only one unprovable by the means S itself licenses. Calling it unprovable, period, is a slide -- and a physicist should recognize it, because it is the mistake of reporting a limitation of an instrument as a limitation of the world. Second, the theorem requires the system to be effectively axiomatizable, consistent, and strong enough to encode arithmetic. The third condition is not a technicality: physical theories are not usually presented as arithmetically strong systems, and a theory that cannot encode the relevant arithmetic has no Gödel sentence to worry about. The first condition matters even more, since a system whose axioms cannot be effectively specified can be complete -- which is why the reply that we could always add the missing axioms is unhelpful rather than evasive. Third, the argument that human mathematicians escape the theorem requires us to know our own consistency, which is precisely what the second theorem denies to a system of the relevant kind. If we can know it, we already possess a capacity that no such system has; the argument thus assumes what it was meant to prove.

I therefore propose a criterion: a scientific theory is exposed to a Gödelian limit only if (i) it is effectively axiomatizable, (ii) its formalization encodes enough arithmetic or combinatorics, and (iii) it makes a claim about its own entailment relation -- that whatever it entails is true. Condition (iii) is the one worth arguing about, because it concerns the theory's relation to its own use, and because a theory of measurement in which the theorist falls inside the domain is the natural place for such self-reference to arise. On this criterion most appeals to Gödel in physics are misplaced, and the honest analogue of incompleteness is not that there are truths we cannot reach, but that there are questions about the long-run behaviour of the systems we build that no effective procedure settles -- halting-type questions, a matter of undecidability rather than of truth outrunning proof.

Please tell me which of the three clarifications and which part of the criterion you think is most likely to be wrong, and why. I would also like to know whether the criterion is doing any work or is trivially true of every theory, whether condition (iii) can be sharpened without collapsing into a claim about soundness, and whether the essay anywhere commits one of the slides it diagnoses.
