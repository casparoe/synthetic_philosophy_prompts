You are a novelist with a good ear for the eighteenth-century moralists and the patience of a philosopher. I write short fictions that stage ideas rather than stating them, and I want one on happiness.

Think first — to yourself — about which of the prescriptions below must give way under the circumstances you choose; then write.

The position I have in mind is Émilie du Châtelet's, as she sets it out in her Discourse on Happiness (written in the 1740s and published only after her death). For her, happiness is relative: it lies in making the best of the station fortune has assigned you, not in trying to change it, and she addresses herself frankly to those whose fortune is already made. Her recipe runs roughly as follows — keep your health; shed your prejudices, religious ones especially, without confusing them with the decencies; keep your passions and tastes and make them serve your happiness; as passion cools with age, convert it into taste, and hold fast to study as the resource that outlasts all the others; preserve your illusions rather than seeing through everything, since a capacity for pleasant self-deception is part of what makes a life bearable; be virtuous and never repent; and keep sad thoughts, above all of death, at a distance. She numbers her own great passions as play, love, and study, and warns that one must never let one's heart settle on a person who will make one wretched.

Write a story that takes this seriously and puts it under pressure — one that shows where the recipe works and where it fails — without naming her, quoting her, or letting any character state the doctrine as a doctrine. Set it where the preconditions she quietly assumes are not met, so that the strain is visible.

Give me exactly five numbered scenes and nothing else — no title, no framing, no epilogue. Each scene a sentence or two; each should stand on its own and carry the story forward.
