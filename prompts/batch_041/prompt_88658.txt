I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, and I'm opening the next session of our reading group on Pascal. We've spent three weeks on the Pensées, mostly on the fragments where he worries away at the relation between justice and force, and I want to begin with a poem rather than a summary — the fragments are notes rather than a system, and a summary flattens precisely what makes them worth the three weeks. I've tried to write this myself and it keeps collapsing into either a slogan or a sermon, so I'm handing it over.

Here is the shape I want. Two voices, JUSTICE and FORCE, and twenty-five lines: twelve in which justice speaks, twelve in which force answers, and a single hinge line in the middle that belongs to both and could be spoken by neither alone. The second half should run the first half's images back in reverse with the roles swapped, so the poem is a mirror hinged on that one line — the form should be the argument rather than a costume for it, and the reversal should feel like something the argument forces, not a trick. What I want the poem to carry: that each of the two is helpless on its own and monstrous when it acts alone; that they are disputed unequally, since force is never really contested and justice always is, and that asymmetry is where the damage starts; the moment where, because we cannot make justice strong, we quietly settle for making strength just; and the appeal to custom and established law as the thing obedience actually rests on, along with the warning that it is dangerous to let the people see that the laws are not just. It should end without a resolution — no "therefore might is right," and no theodicy that relocates the missing justice somewhere else and calls the circle closed. Keep it plain and aphoristic, close to Pascal's own unadorned prose; no archaism, no "thee" or "thou," no Latin, and please don't name him in the poem.

After the poem, add a short note of a few sentences telling me where the poem takes liberties or leans on a contested reading: what the fragments clearly commit Pascal to, and what is genuinely disputed — for instance whether the move about making strength just is his own view or a position he reports in order to turn it, and whether the appeal to custom leaves room for a true justice he thinks we cannot reach in this life. Separate the settled from the contested, and don't present one side of a live scholarly disagreement as the consensus. I don't need citations or editions.
