I've been trying to write down where I actually stand on diachronic rationality, and I'd like an audit of the package before I commit it to paper. Here it is.

(1) I accept the money-pump argument in its standard form. If my preferences cycle — I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — then there is a sequence of trades, each of which I would accept at the moment it is offered, that leaves me holding something I rank below what I started with. I take that to show the cyclic preferences themselves are irrational, so a rational agent's preferences must be acyclic.

(2) What is fundamentally wrong with the cyclic agent, I think, is exploitability: a trader who knows her preferences can reliably extract money from her. So I treat the no-money-pump condition — being the kind of agent a bookmaker cannot systematically profit from — as the basic constraint on practical rationality, and the acyclicity in (1) as a consequence of it.

(3) I think preferences are time-indexed and may rationally change. What I want at thirty need not be what I want at sixty, and neither is the privileged one. When I choose, the ranking that governs is the one I hold at the moment of choosing, and I don't think there is any further, time-transcendent fact about what is "really" good for me that could adjudicate between my successive selves.

(4) I think some options are genuinely incommensurable — neither better nor worse nor equal — so a rational preference structure can be incomplete. And I don't think it is irrational to decline a trade when the options are incommensurable: one is not obliged to have a price for everything.

(5) When these arguments say that an agent ends up "worse off," I read that as worse off by the agent's own ranking, not by any objective measure of welfare.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Tell me which of these commitments actually pull against each other and which only appear to, then order the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine one, say what would have to give: which commitment is doing the damage, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs elsewhere in the package. I would rather have one or two tensions diagnosed sharply than five flagged as equally worrying, so please discriminate — if a pair looks like a conflict only because I have stated one member ambiguously, give the reading under which they are compatible and then say whether that reading is actually available to me given the rest. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the ranking you produce: which argument or consideration, if it turned out to go the other way, would move a commitment up or down your list, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
