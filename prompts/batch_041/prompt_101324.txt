I'm workshopping a short paper for a reading group on Dennett's real patterns, and I want a sharp pair of eyes on it before I send it round. Take the role of a philosopher of mind who knows the three-stances stuff cold but is not a Dennett cheerleader -- I want someone who'll tell me where the arguement actually breaks, not someone who'll tell me it's a nice contribution. Think it through carefully before you start; I'd rather you spend the first bit of your answer getting the target of the paper straight than rushing to react.

Here's the gist. Dennett says a pattern is real when it's the cheapest way to describe or predict the data -- the pattern earns its keep by compressing. And the three stances are basically three compression schemes: physical (predict from microphysics), design (predict from function), intentional (predict from beliefs and desires). My claim is that Dennett is running two different criteria together and only one of them is doing any metaphysical work. Compressibility is a fact about the data (up to the usual additive-constant caveat from the invariance theorem, so, ok, roughly a fact about the data). Usefulness-for-an-agent is a fact about the data plus the agent's interests, its time and memory budget, and what it already knows. Dennett needs the second to make patterns "real for us," but he needs the first to make them "real, period." I want to argue you can't have both at once without the whole thing collapsing into either plain observer-relative pragmatism or a kind of Platonism about descriptions.

The bit I'm least sure about, and the bit I most want you to attack: I bring in logical depth. Roughly, a string can have a very short generating program but still take an astronomically long time to actually produce from that program -- picture a simple rule iterated for a huge number of steps; the final configuration has a tiny description (the rule plus the start state) but the only way to get it is to run the thing for that long. Kolmogorov-style compressibility alone would say that final configuration is a real pattern (short description!). But no bounded agent can ever unpack it in reasonable time, so it's a pattern with no stance that can actually use it. So I say real patterns need compressibility *and* a depth cap, where the cap is set relative to the agents in question. But then the cap is agent-relative again, which is exactly the problem I started with. I half-suspect this is a circle and I should just embrace it -- real patterns are relative to a class of agents, full stop, and Dennett's mistake is wanting the word "real" to do more than that. But maybe there's a way out I'm missing: maybe the pattern is real iff it's compressible relative to *some* physically possible agent, which makes it mind-independent enough (no actual observer required) while still not being Platonist.

A few secondary things I want checked: (1) whether I've got the physical stance right -- I say it's not really a *different* stance so much as the stance of refusing to compress, and I'm not sure Dennett would accept that framing. (2) whether my iterated-rule example is doing what I think it's doing, or whether there's a cleaner case. (3) whether the whole "depth cap" move is already sitting in the literature under a different name -- I have a nagging feeling someone's done this and I'll get dinged for it.

So: read it as if you're the respondent at the reading group. Start by telling me the one thing that, if true, sinks the paper. Then go through and mark where I'm being sloppy versus where I'm actually wrong. Tell me if the central distinction (compressibility vs usefulness) is a real distinction or just a verbal one. And tell me honestly whether the "some physically possible agent" escape hatch works or whether it's cheap. Don't be polite about it -- I'd rather find out now.
