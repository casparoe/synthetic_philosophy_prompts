Write a short story — 1500-2500 words is fine — about a jury or an expert panel where the Condorcet jury theorem is quietly doing all the work. The setup I want: every member is individually a bit better than a coin flip on the question they have to settle, so the naive math says more members means more accuracy, and someone in the story leans on exactly that ("nine of us, we're basically certain"). Then I want it to come apart, and I want the mechanism shown through the plot rather than explained by a character who's read the literature: their mistakes share a source. Same expert witness, same forensic test, same rumour, same urge to agree with the room — the point being that the deliberation is what correlates them, so twelve jurors end up carrying maybe two independent signals. The sting should land on the one dissenter nobody in the room can stand, the only genuinely independent error present, who turns out to be worth more than the eight who agree. Keep the probability honest in both directions: with common errors, or with members a shade worse than chance on this particular question, a bigger panel is worse, not better — I want someone who's actually read Condorcet to nod, not wince. Don't let it turn into a lecture; no footnotes, no "as the theorem states."
