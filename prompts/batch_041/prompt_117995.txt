I chair the grants committee of a small international foundation that funds adolescent health programs in about a dozen countries. Three years ago, after a bruising fight over an earlier grant, our board adopted a Local Standards Provision: a program may be renewed only if the partner can show that its design is validated by the standards of the community it serves. The Provision is silent on what to do when the community does not speak with one voice, and that silence is now in front of us.

Our longest-running partner runs an adolescent and maternal health program in a rural district. It works through mothers' groups and, as a matter of method, leaves decisions about a girl's care to her household -- in practice, her father and his brothers. The partner says this is the only way the program is trusted, and that two earlier attempts to work around the household failed and left the district without services for years. A faction in the district -- mostly girls who have finished secondary school and a few returned migrants -- has written to us saying that this arrangement is exactly what they want changed, that the partner's consultations never reach them, and that our money entrenches the people who decide for them. The elders' council replies that the dissidents do not speak for the district. Renewal is on the agenda in three weeks.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before that meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- renew, renew with conditions, suspend pending a review, or something else you specify -- with a time frame and a statement of what we would need to see to revisit it, and it should close with the rule the board is adopting for future cases and what that rule does not cover.

Reason it through for an audience of board members who are not philosophers and who do not agree among themselves about whether moral standards are relative at all, so the opinion cannot depend on settling that question. In particular: say which party we are treating as authorized to state the community's standards, and defend that choice against the obvious objection that the dissidents are also part of the community, so that the community is not a single thing we can defer to; consider whether the fact that their standards differ from ours is by itself any reason for us to defer, given that our standards are also ours; and be candid that in choosing whom to consult we are exercising power rather than escaping it. Treat the strongest case against your recommendation at full strength, not as a formality.

No citations are needed; I want the argument, not the literature.
