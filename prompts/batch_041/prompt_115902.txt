You are to act as a philosopher of social science and a satirist with a specialist's command of the strategic-studies literature on credibility, commitment, and brinkmanship. I use pieces of this kind in a graduate seminar on the ethics of war, so the document must be accurate enough to teach with: the humour has to come from the doctrine, not from invention. Begin by spending a few paragraphs thinking on the page — set out the paradox you intend to dramatise and satisfy yourself that you have it right — and only then write the piece.

The paradox. There is a family of arguments in the strategy literature that runs roughly as follows. A threat is useful only if it is credible, and it is credible only if the adversary believes you would carry it out. But the threats worth making are threats to do what you would not want to do, so the threatener's problem is not resolve but commitment: making it the case that, at the moment of decision, carrying out the threat is no longer a matter of choice. Hence the familiar doctrines — burn the bridges behind you, make the response automatic, delegate the decision, arrange things so that the outcome is left partly to chance rather than to anyone's will, since a choice can be reconsidered and a chance cannot. The sting is that the policy becomes more effective the less it lies under the policy-maker's control, so that the ideal deterrent is one that nobody, including its owner, can call off. Bring in the further wrinkles as they earn their place: the difference between a threat and a warning; why compellence — getting someone to act — is so much harder than deterrence; the fact that a deterrent nobody can use is useless and a deterrent somebody can use is dangerous; the way two adversaries each fearing a surprise attack may produce the attack they fear; and the consequence that a state can deter itself.

The task. Write the complete text of a public-information booklet issued by a fictional government to its own citizens — the issuing body might be called the Directorate of Strategic Reassurance — explaining and defending a national policy of deliberate loss of control. The booklet announces, in the calm register of a civil-defence pamphlet, that the authority to order retaliation has been partly transferred to an automatic process that the government itself cannot override: a lottery, a machine, a sealed envelope, whatever the booklet prefers. This transfer is presented throughout as the crowning achievement of a rational security policy. The register is deadpan bureaucratic reassurance from the first line to the last: no winking, no narrator stepping outside the document, no sentence that concedes the document is a joke, and no preface or afterword addressed to me.

What I want in it. Roughly 700–900 words, with a title, a few headed sections, and at least one question-and-answer section in which anxious citizens raise objections and the booklet replies. Those replies must be the best answers available on behalf of the doctrine, not strawmen. The objections a reader will feel — that the policy stakes the country on a coin toss; that no one can be answerable for a machine's decision; that a threat whose execution is uncertain is exactly the sort of threat that invites the other side to gamble; that the doctrine cannot be tested, since it explains both war and peace — should all be present in the text without ever being labelled as objections; let the booklet's own reassurances expose them. Include at least one paragraph that is a genuinely good argument, so that a reader who knows this literature finds the case made better than expected and only then notices what has been conceded. End with a paragraph of reassurance in the pamphlet's voice. Avoid the field's catchphrases; the doctrine should emerge from the mechanics of the policy, not from its slogans. And do not resolve the paradox: the piece succeeds only if the paradox is still standing at the end, visible through the composure of the prose.

Two further constraints. Do not cite, quote, or name any real person, state, treaty, or text — invent the institutional furniture instead. And do not include anything that comments on the piece from outside it.
