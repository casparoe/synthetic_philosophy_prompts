I'm a hospital chaplain, not a philosopher, but I sat on our ethics committee for a year and I've been chewing on an argument one of the residents made about advance directives. He thought we shouldn't honor them at all, and I could never tell if he was being clever or if there's a hole in it. Here's how he put it.

(1) What makes a treatment decision mine to make is that it reflects what I actually want right now -- my current, competent preferences about how my life goes.
(2) An advance directive, by the time it takes effect, is the voice of somebody who's gone. The woman who wrote "no tube" is not the woman in the bed. Different values, different life, and in the sense that matters she isnt there anymore.
(3) So when we follow the directive we let a person who no longer exists boss around the person who does.
(4) Overriding a competent person's expressed wishes in favor of somebody else's wishes is exactly what we call paternalism, and its wrong.
(5) Therefore we shouldn't honor advance directives, except maybe as one bit of evidence about what the patient would have wanted.

The thing is, (4) feels airtight and I basically believe it, and (5) still feels monstrous to me. And not only in this kind of case. If the earlier self really has no standing over the later self, I can't see whats left of promises, contracts, or saving for retirement. A guy who takes out a 30-year mortgage at 30 and wants out at 50 is the same setup, and nobody thinks he just gets to stop paying. So I'm worried the argument proves way more than the resident wanted it to.

So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd rather keep (4) than (5), but I want to know what I'm paying if I do. And the middle steps bother me somehow -- that phrase "in the sense that matters" especially -- but I can't put my finger on why, and I'm not sure my unease is even pointing at the real problem.
