For the past six years I have been a career law clerk in the same chambers, writing the statutory-interpretation memos that the judge reads before deciding a hard case, and for rather longer than that I have read the rationalist blogs closely enough to have acquired a specific allergy: procedures that appear to decide a question but are legible only once you already know the answer, and arguments that launder a contestable value choice as a finding about language. I am now trying to build the judge a reusable procedure, and I would rather it announce that it is stuck than manufacture a verdict.

Context and constraints. We get perhaps two genuinely hard interpretation cases a month; the routine ones never reach me. The memo decides which way the chambers leans, so the stakes are real — a wrong call changes what people may lawfully do — but nothing is on fire, and I have a day, occasionally two, per case. The judge signs whatever eventually issues; I do not. The judge is a committed textualist by temperament and by stated method, while I have never managed to talk myself out of the purposivist's founding intuition that a legislature's point bears on what its words mean. A procedure that only one of us can run is useless; it has to be one I can hand to the judge with a straight face.

What I want. Not an essay on textualism versus purposivism — I know the slogans, and reciting them is the very failure mode I am trying to escape. I want a decision procedure, in order, that a clerk can actually run: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure returns a determinate answer and stops, and the points where it must hand the question back as a contestable value choice rather than a fact about language. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure; I would rather see them labeled than disguised.

Three commitments I have reached on my own and want the procedure to take seriously, because they are where the slogans break. First, there is a seam, somewhere, between finding a text's meaning and giving it legal effect; the interesting question is not whether the seam exists but where it falls, and whether the procedure can locate it without appealing to the very intention it was meant to discipline. Second, a legislature is not a mind: actual intent is frequently unavailable, and the stand-in — what it would have wanted — is a counterfactual that we, not they, get to specify, and I want the procedure to say plainly when it is doing this. Third, "just read the words" is not an alternative to interpretation but a decision to fix one context among several admissible ones, since the same sentence carries different content depending on which context you hold fixed; the procedure should make that choice explicit rather than pretend it away.

Then run the procedure on three cases, at least one of which returns an answer that cuts against my purposivist sympathies — do not soften it. The first is an invented municipal ordinance: "No animal may be brought into the municipal library, except a guide dog accompanying a blind patron," with a preamble invoking protection of the collection and quiet, clean reading rooms. Apply it to a patron carrying a sealed goldfish bowl, to a trained service dog for a patron whose disability is not blindness, and to a patron carrying a taxidermied dog. The second is an invented industrial-safety scheme: "Every operator of a steam boiler in a factory shall hold a certificate from the board of examiners, and the board shall inspect each boiler annually," now confronting a factory that runs an electric induction furnace and a pressurized gas line and contains no boiler at all. The third is an invented governance provision: "A member of the board who misses three consecutive meetings shall be deemed to have resigned," applied to a member who missed three consecutive meetings, two of which were cancelled for want of a quorum and so never occurred. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I know the literature well enough to be dangerous, and I want the procedure.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the procedure as a whole and in each of its steps, and single out the one step you are least sure of and why.
