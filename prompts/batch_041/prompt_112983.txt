Okay, so I've been arguing with a friend about the whole wild-animal-suffering thing — the line that nature is mostly a charnel house and that if you take suffering seriously you can't just assume wild animals have lives worth living. I want to actually work through a real case with you, not a hypothetical. The case is the Aral Sea. You probably know the shape of it: the Soviet cotton-irrigation diversions off the Amu Darya and Syr Darya shrank it over decades until it broke into pieces and mostly dried up, the native fish got wiped out as salinity climbed, the fishery that employed a lot of people collapsed, and a big chunk of the old seabed is now salt desert where basically nothing lives. I don't need the history, I've got that. I want the welfare analysis.

So take the "net welfare of ecosystems" framework and actually run it on this, and don't skate. A few things I want you to really grapple with. First the weird implication you have to face: if the sea died, the total amount of animal life in that region fell off a cliff — and if you're suffering-focused that sounds like it might be good? Fewer lives, less suffering, assuming those lives were net negative to begin with. Is that the right conclusion, or does the whole thing just hang on an unsettled empirical premise about whether wild animals' lives are worth living? Second, replacement. The deltas, the irrigation canals, the exposed seabed with its rodents and insects, the birds that use the remnants — does the accounting change if the destruction didn't so much subtract life as swap one set of suffering creatures for a different set? Third, and this is the one I actually care about: scale it down. Compare the dead sea to a hectare of the cotton monoculture that killed it, which is itself a habitat. Which one supports more sentient life, and which one supports more suffering, and are those the same question or not? And if they come apart, what does that do to the framework — is "net welfare" even well-defined when you're adding up insect-years against fish-years, or is the whole thing quietly smuggling in a common currency it never justifies?

Then flip it around: what does the case show about the theory? Does the Aral Sea confirm the suffering-focused picture, or is it an anomaly, or a case the picture never really anticipated because it was built for farm animals and lab animals and got stretched to cover an entire dying sea? And what about the humans in the story — the fishing towns, the health effects — do they just drop out of a net-welfare-of-ecosystems analysis, or does a serious version of the view have to put them back in? I want an honest answer about where the framework goes silent instead of a tidy one that pretends it covers everything.
