Last term our department's governance working group began piloting a futarchy-style procedure on a decision we genuinely care about — whether to keep the weekly seminar hybrid or return it to in-person only — and the chair keeps deploying Robin Hanson's futarchy slogan, 'vote on values, bet on beliefs', to close down objections. The objections are not to prediction markets as such; they concern the metric. The pilot fixes a single measurable objective (seminar attendance), and several of us suspect that the choice of objective has already settled, in advance, the very value question the vote is supposed to answer. When we press him, the chair replies that the metric is precisely what is being voted on, and that the market's only task is to tell us which format best achieves it.

I want a disambiguation rather than a defence. Lay out the distinct, precise claims the slogan could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the radical, and give a verdict on each: which are platitudes nobody would deny, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or self-undermining. Along the way, separate the several things 'voted on' might mean — a single scalar objective, a weighting over several objectives, a decision rule, or the metric itself — and the several things 'bet on' might mean: unconditional forecasts of outcomes, conditional forecasts of an outcome given a policy, or a market in the metric itself. Say whether the conditional structure is essential to any interesting reading or merely an implementation detail, and whether the slogan is offered as a description of how political disagreement actually divides, a prescription for how it ought to be resolved, or a feasibility claim about mechanism design. I am most interested in two readings that pull in opposite directions: one on which the metric is a value the vote has already fixed, so the market must take it as given and the chair is entitled to dismiss us; and one on which the choice of metric is itself a belief about what would serve the underlying values, so a market could in principle price the metric too, and the chair's move is question-begging. Tell me which reading the slogan's users most plausibly intend, which reading would actually license the chair's move, and what further step in our pilot would tell the competing readings apart.

Open with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate the question.
