I am writing the final examination for a 400-level course in formal epistemology, and I need one essay question on Dutch book arguments, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. My students can already recite the standard line: an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability calculus is irrational, because a bookie can assemble a set of bets that loses her money in every state of the world. I want a question that this recitation cannot answer, one that forces students to locate exactly where the argument's normative force comes from and where it runs out. The unit has covered the betting interpretation of credence and a reformulation that replaces money-payouts with payouts defined in terms of the agent's own credences, so the question may draw on both, but it should not depend on students recalling particular authors or titles.

The question should have four parts. First, students must distinguish what the Dutch book theorem establishes as a mathematical result from the further, non-mathematical premise that turns it into an argument that the agent is irrational, and must say precisely what that bridge premise is and why it cannot be dropped. Second, they must engage a concrete objection: suppose a bookie exploits an agent whose credences are non-additive only because she has not noticed that two of the propositions she is betting on are logically equivalent. Students should say whether the agent is irrational in the relevant sense, and what that case reveals about whether the argument presupposes logical omniscience. Third, they must assess the claim that the argument is merely prudential — that a guaranteed monetary loss counts against an agent's rationality only if she cares about money — and evaluate whether the credence-denominated reformulation genuinely earns the bridge premise or merely relocates it. Fourth, they must draw a conclusion about the strongest claim a Dutch book argument can defensibly support, and identify one thing it cannot establish.

For the model answer, aim for roughly 700 to 800 words, at the level of a strong graduate student. For the marking scheme, build it around five must-hit points, give each a point value summing to 30, and for each point add a one-line note on how a student typically misses it — the scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain, not by awarding marks for mentioning the theorem. Begin the response directly with the exam question itself, with no preamble and no restating of these instructions, and write the model answer so that it opens with its bottom line in the first sentence and then gives the reasoning. Write in American English.
