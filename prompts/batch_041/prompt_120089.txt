ok, so I'm a 2nd-year PhD student in bioethics (my background is pop ethics, which I'm sure is why I keep dragging non-identity into everything), and I'm stuck on a chapter about choosing which child to bring into existence. Before I let any theory bully me into a position, I want to just write down my gut verdicts and see what, if anything, they add up to. Could you play the role of a philosopher who actually knows the non-identity stuff and the person-affecting/impersonal distinction, think carefully before answering, and start by brainstorming a few candidate principles in a rough scratchpad sort of way before you commit to one? Be blunt — I'd much rather find out one of my verdicts has to go than get a mush answer. And please don't just tell me what the literature says, I've read it; I want to know what fits *my* calls.

Setup for all of these: I've got several embryos and I pick one to implant; the others are never born. Assume the traits are probabilistic rather than certain, and assume each resulting life is either worth living or not — I'm not asking you to relitigate that part.

My calls:

(i) Embryo A will likely have a short, painful, degeneratve condition; B won't. Picking A is wrong, not merely unwise. High confidence.

(ii) A vs C, where C will have something mild and lifelong — moderate eczema, a bit of hearing loss — but by C's own lights will have a long, good life. Picking C over A feels fine, or at least not wrong. Lower confidence; I might just be being lazy.

(iii) A deaf couple wants the deaf embryo, because they want a kid who shares their language and their community. I don't think that's wrong, and I get irritated when people call it abuse. Now here's the bit that bothers me: if a *hearing* couple asked for the deaf embryo because they thought deaf culture was cute, I'd say no. Same embryo, same predicted life, different parents, different verdict. Whatever is doing the work there, it isn't the child's welfare.

(iv) Selecting against a serious disease: not just allowed, I think you're required to do it (assuming no cost to anyone else). Selecting for a modest advantage — say the embryo that'll likely be a bit smarter or a bit taller — I think is allowed but not required, and honestly it gives me the ick. If "best expected life" is the rule, why is one a duty and the other just permitted, when the advantage might be worth more welfare than dodging the disease? This one keeps me up. I guess this is the liberal-eugenics question in a nutshell — if selection is allowed at all, on what grounds do you ever say no?

(v) The savior-sibling case: I pick the embryo that's the best tissue match for my sick existing kid. I think that's allowed, but what's doing the work doesn't seem to be the new child's welfare at all.

(vi) Two embryos with the same expected welfare: one is a near-certain decent life, the other a coin-flip between a great life and a bad one. I'd pick the safe one. That isn't expected-value maximising, so something else is going on and I want to know what it is.

(vii) The one I keep flip-flopping on: I can pick the embryo likely to grow into a warm, happy, not-particularly-useful person, or the one likely to grow into someone who'll do a lot of good for others but be somewhat less happy themselves. No gut call at all. Tell me what your principle says here, and whether that comes out as a reductio.

The half-baked principle I've been toying with: pick the child you'd be willing to have been, if you'd been told you were going to be one of the possible children — or maybe not exist at all, that's the part I can't get right. Does that get (i)-(vi)? Where does it break?

What I want back: (a) the principle, or the small set of principles, that gets as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could code it up; (b) which clause is motivated by which case; (c) its verdicts on a couple of cases I haven't mentioned; and (d) an honest list of which of my calls it can't accommodate, and which single one you'd tell me to give up. If the honest answer is "your verdicts are inconsistent, so choose between a messy principle that saves them all and a clean one that kills (iv) or (iii)", just say so.
