I keep running into the maxim usually called Chesterton's fence, and I have now seen it do opposite kinds of work in two arguments, which makes me suspect I do not know what the maxim actually claims. The first argument concerned a hospital rule requiring two clinicians to independently verify a particular high-risk dosage. The rule was introduced after an incident that no one can now document; the only surviving trace of its rationale is a line in an old manual. A colleague argued that we cannot drop the rule until we know why it was put there, and since the records are gone we cannot know, so we cannot drop it. The second argument concerned a municipal bylaw requiring a fixed gap between buildings. Its original fire-spread rationale is obsolete, but the bylaw now produces effects nobody intended, preserving a certain streetscape and slowing redevelopment. The same colleague argued that the bylaw must stay because it plainly has a function, even though its original purpose has lapsed. In the first case, the absence of a recoverable historical reason was treated as a reason to keep the rule; in the second, the presence of a present function was treated as a reason to keep it despite the lapse of the historical reason.

I would like a menu of the precise claims this maxim could be making, from the nearly trivial to the strongly conservative, with a verdict on each. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical or normative thesis, or something false or empty; say what kind of inquiry it demands (recovering the original intention, identifying present function, estimating counterfactual effects, locating authority or reliance, or something else); and say which reading my colleague needed in each of the two arguments. If no single reading supports both uses, say so, and identify which use the maxim cannot sustain without being repaired.

Please also address the following. What does the maxim imply when the original reason is discovered and is discreditable, for example that the bylaw was passed to exclude a group or that the double-check was a workaround for a supplier's packaging defect since corrected? Does knowing why the fence was put there count for or against keeping it? What does the maxim imply when there is no deliberate origin at all, because the rule is a frozen accident, an emergent convention, or the residue of a long-dead conflict? Does it apply to a fence whose origin is unknowable in principle rather than merely undocumented, and if so does it become a permanent veto? How does the strongest reading relate to the wider Burkean case for inherited institutions: is the fence maxim a weaker, more empirical cousin of that case, or does it depend on the Burkean premise that individual reason cannot reconstruct what accumulated experience has deposited?

I have a tentative taxonomy I would like tested. Some inherited arrangements are fences, deliberately erected for a purpose; some are scars, left by an injury or conflict and serving no current purpose; some are load-bearing walls, whose removal would bring down something we value even though no one designed them to bear that load. The maxim seems to assume the first category, but most of the hard cases seem to fall into the second and third. Does the taxonomy help, and which readings of the maxim survive it?

Finally, give me a repaired statement of the maxim: one that preserves whatever is defensible in it, distinguishes the historical question from the functional one, and tells me when I may remove a fence without knowing why it was put there. Lay the readings out as a menu rather than settling on one and explaining it, and state plainly which of my colleague's two verdicts the best reading cannot accommodate.
