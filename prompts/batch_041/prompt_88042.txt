I am writing a novel whose central chapter is an impossible tribunal, and I need you to predict how Thomas Robert Malthus would have answered the questions put to him there.

The premise, which I will make plausible in the fiction: Malthus — the clergyman and political economist of the Essay on the Principle of Population, the antagonist of Godwin's and Condorcet's perfectibility, the critic of the Poor Laws — sits at a table with two centuries of hindsight, having been handed a briefing dossier that I have written and that you should treat as stipulated fact. It tells him that fertility in the rich world has fallen below replacement while world population has multiplied many times over; that the escape from subsistence agriculture came through fossil energy and industrial nitrogen rather than through moral restraint; that contraception is cheap, lawful, and nearly universal; that modern states transfer large sums to the poor as a matter of course; and one item of my own invention, so that you cannot coast on anything you have already read — a reversible fertility suppressant that is delivered once, at no cost and without any question of consent, and holds for a generation unless reversed.

Five interlocutors question him. A demographic historian who holds that the escape from the trap came from coal and the transition, and who regards the principle as refuted in the long run. An economist who thinks Malthus was right about the pre-industrial world and wrong to call that a law, and who holds that a prediction either fails or is not a prediction, so that right but early is not a verdict but a failure to specify. A moral philosopher who wants to conscript him as an ancestor of contemporary population ethics and asks him whether a much larger population of lives barely worth living is preferable to a smaller and happier one, and how numbers are to be weighed against quality of life — a question that did not exist in his lifetime and cannot be answered from his texts without invention. A historian of empire who charges that his principle supplied a scientific alibi for famine, for laissez-faire cruelty, and for colonial policy, and who will not let him retreat into theodicy. And a neo-Malthusian environmentalist who wants to make him the patron of limits to growth and of birth control, and who will be disappointed.

For each of the posthumous issues the dossier raises — artificial contraception and its public provision, sub-replacement fertility, the welfare state and cash transfers, fossil fuels and synthetic fertilizer, that population question, and my invented suppressant — give me his most probable position, the confidence you attach to it, the argument he would make in his own idiom, and the specific commitment of his own that the position rests on. Where he would have to abandon or quietly revise something he had staked himself to, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.

Then the question I care most about, which is the spine of the chapter. Press him: was his principle refuted, vindicated, or right but early? Make him confront the rescue maneuvers one at a time — restricting the theory's domain, redefining the quantity it predicts, extending the horizon, or conceding that no finite run of data could refute it — and say which he would take and which he would refuse as beneath him. Then ask him to distinguish, in his own words, a theory that is merely early from one that is false, and to name the predictions of his own that he would now retract.

Keep him in character rather than improved: I want his register, his rhetoric, the moralism about restraint, the providential frame, the places where he would be evasive or self-serving, and the places where he would find a modern question not wrong but unintelligible.

End with a short scene note: the first thing he says, and the single question that would leave him genuinely without an answer. Give me a working memo, not an essay. Do not supply citations, page references, or reading lists; I cannot check them and will not use them. Where you are extrapolating past anything he wrote, mark it as invention rather than let it pass as his.
