I am a graduate student in political theory, and I keep running into the following sentence from Orwell's "Politics and the English Language": "the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts." In a recent seminar it was cited three times, each time to do different work: once to argue that the phrase "enhanced interrogation techniques" does not merely disguise a practice but helps cause people to accept it; once to argue that teaching students to prefer active verbs will make them more resistant to authoritarian politics; and once to defend a campus speech code that bans certain slurs on the grounds that the words themselves make certain thoughts available and others unthinkable. I want a disambiguation, not a summary of Orwell. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the trivial to the radical. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical hypothesis, a conceptual truth, or false, and whether it actually supports the use it was put to in the seminar. In doing so, distinguish at least: (i) causal claims about language shaping thought from constitutive claims about thought requiring language; (ii) claims about individual psychology from claims about social practices and institutions; (iii) semantic claims about what can be said from pragmatic claims about what is salient or easy to say; and (iv) claims about the direction of fit between thought and language, including the possibility of a feedback loop. Say which reading Orwell himself appears to hold and whether it is consistent with the political aims of the essay. If the request is confused—if the sentence cannot bear the weight these uses put on it, or if the readings I have listed are not all readings of the same claim—say so in your first sentence rather than forcing a unified answer. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
