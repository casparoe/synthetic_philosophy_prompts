I'm the data steward for a small hospital network's master patient index, and I keep running into a problem that I think is actualy the identity of indiscernibles wearing a lab coat. We get pairs of records that match on every field we have — same name, same date of birth, same address, same phone, same insurance ID, even the same typo in the street name — and the default rule from the vendor is: if they're indistinguishable, merge them. But I've been reading about Leibniz's principle and that old two-spheres thought experiment (Black's, I think), and I'm now worried the rule is exactly backwards: two things can be qualitatively identical and still be two things, and in our case merging two patients is a lot worse than leaving one patient split. I need a procedure my team can actually run on a pair of records, not a philosophy essay. We handle maybe 15-20 of these a week, each decision has to be made in under 20 minutes by a staffer who has no philosophy training, and anything that changes a patient's identity has to be signed off by the data governance lead. The procedure should be a list of questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with merge or keep separate, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's a values call — for example, how much evidence of distinctness we require before we accept the cost of a split record. I also want it to be honest about the cases it can't settle, the ones where the records are symmetric in every way we can observe and we have no independent causal history to break the tie.

Please run it on three cases: (1) two records that match on every field and have the same creation timestamp, but one has a note from a clinic visit that the other doesn't; (2) two records that match on every field but have different audit trails going back to two different registration desks on the same day; (3) two records that are perfect duplicates in every field, including a hash of all the data, with no audit trail at all because they came from a legacy system. For each, tell me what the procedure says and where I should be suspicious of it. And I want the case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like — probably the one where it tells me to keep two records seperate and eat the cost, or merge two records and accept a risk — spelled out, not softened. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do, and whether there's a version of the indiscernibility principle that would actually justify the vendor's default rule, because if there is I want to know what I'd have to believe to accept it.
