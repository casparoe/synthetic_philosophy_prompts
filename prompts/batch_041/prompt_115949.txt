I teach a final-year seminar on social choice, and every year the same thing happens: the week we do Gibbard–Satterthwaite, someone at the back asks why nobody just builds a voting app that fixes it, and all I've got in reply is a twenty-minute lecture. The version of the theorem I put on the board is deliberately plain: if there are at least three options and your rule always produces a winner, and no voter can ever do better by misreporting their preferences, then the rule is dictatorial — one voter's ranking fixes the result whatever the rest of you say. So I'd like something satirical to hand out with the problem sheet, and I'd rather not write it myself, as my attempts keep collapsing into explainers.

Write me the FAQ page for a fictional startup — call it 'Sincerely Yours' — that sells a supposedly strategy-proof voting platform to universities, housing co-ops and parish councils, with a flagship product called 'SincereBallot' and a 'PeakFinder' mode. The humour has to come from the maths being right, so every escape from the theorem should get it's own cheerful entry: restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences and taking the median voter's favourite; replacing a winner with a lottery in which each voter is the dictator with equal probability; quietly deleting one of the options; insisting that the system 'only counts sincere ballots', which of course it cannot detect; and handing the decision to a single trusted expert, which is just the dictatorial case in a nicer jumper. Don't wink at the reader — the company should be entirely pleased with itself — and by the end the reader should feel that the theorem isn't a design flaw in our voting rules so much as a description of what a rule is. Invented names only, no real companies or living people, and no explaining the theorem at the reader: every technical point should arrive as a boast or a disclaimer.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered entries, each a question followed by a two- or three-sentence answer, with no preamble and no closing note.
