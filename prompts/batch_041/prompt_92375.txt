I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I'm in a seminar on determinism and predictability that has spent the last three weeks on chaos. I've been trying to write a short paper on the relationship between determinism and predictability, and I keep returning to a passage in the preface to Francis Maitland's translation of Poincaré's Science and Method. The passage reads: "The conception of the 'working hypothesis,' provisional, approximate, and merely useful, has more and more pushed aside the comfortable eighteenth century conception of 'laws of nature.'" A few sentences later, the preface quotes Poincaré as saying: "It is a mistake to believe," he said, "that the love of truth is indistinguishable from the love of certainty;" and again: "To doubt everything or to believe everything are two equally convenient solutions; both dispense with the necessity of reflection." I think I understand the general rhetorical point—that scientific theorizing is more provisional than the eighteenth century supposed—but I can't get clear on what it would mean for the concept of a "law of nature" to be "pushed aside" by the concept of a "working hypothesis," and why Poincaré would connect that shift to the virtue of doubt.

My seminar has been focused on the technical result that deterministic systems can have sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and I've been assuming that the interesting question is whether determinism is true. But this passage makes me suspect Poincaré is after something else: maybe that "determinism" and "law" are features of our descriptions rather than of the world, or that the real issue is the attitude we take toward our models. I've also been reading about the idea that determinism is a property of a theory's models rather than of the world, and I wonder if that is what Poincaré is gesturing at. I have a friend in the seminar who keeps saying that chaotic systems are "indeterministic" because we can't predict them, and I want to be able to say precisely what is wrong with that without just repeating the standard distinction between determinism and predictability. Could you help me understand the passage? Specifically: what is the force of "pushed aside" here—is it a claim about scientific practice, about metaphysics, or about the meaning of "law"? How does the "working hypothesis" conception change what we are asserting when we say that a system is governed by a law? And does Poincaré's emphasis on doubt give us a way of thinking about determinism that is neither the eighteenth-century "laws of nature" picture nor the naive "unpredictable therefore indeterministic" picture? I'd also like to know what the passage does not say—whether I'm reading too much into it by connecting it to the chaos literature, which of course came much later. Please give me a close reading of the passage and then connect it to the determinism/predictability issue in a way that I could use in a seminar discussion, without assuming I've read the rest of Poincaré's book.
