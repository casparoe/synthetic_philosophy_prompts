I host a small philosophy podcast, and my next guest works on the foundations of probability. I don't want her to simply restate the Principal Principle on air; I want a set of questions that press on the specific position she defends, which I'll summarize here so you can aim at it. She accepts the Principal Principle — the thesis that a rational agent's credence in a proposition A, conditional on the objective chance of A being x together with admissible evidence, should equal x — but she denies that it is fundamental. On her view it is a special case of a more general principle of deference to ideal experts: a rational agent should defer to the credences of an agent she takes to be ideally informed and rational, and the Principal Principle drops out once one identifies the objective chances with the credences of such an expert. She also holds that the undermining-futures problem — the difficulty that, on a best-systems or Humean account of chance, there can be a future that is consistent with the present chances yet would have made those chances different, apparently forcing a retreat to the so-called New Principle — is an artifact of a badly stated admissibility clause. Her proposed fix is to define admissibility in terms of screening-off: a piece of evidence is admissible just in case it screens the outcome off from the chance.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each with a one- or two-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, organized so that between them they cover at least these pressure points: (1) whether the general deference-to-experts principle actually entails the Principal Principle or only a weaker surrogate, especially where the ideal expert's credences are undefined or where the agent is uncertain which candidate expert is ideal; (2) whether the screening-off definition of admissibility is circular, or can be stated without already presupposing the chance-credence link it is meant to underwrite; (3) whether the exclusion of self-undermining chance propositions is independently motivated or is simply built in to save the principle; and (4) a case she is likely to find uncomfortable — a reliable crystal ball that discloses the outcome of a chance event before it occurs, which is the standard illustration of inadmissible evidence but which a screening-off account may well classify as admissible. Keep the questions pointed and technical, and leave out anything biographical or of the "how did you come to work on this" variety.
