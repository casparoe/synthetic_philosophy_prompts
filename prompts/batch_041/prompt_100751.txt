A colleague in an interdisciplinary reading group has asked me to referee an argument of hers before she sends it out. Her target is the ordinary practice of explaining attitudes and behaviour by generation — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — and her conclusion is that these are not real kinds but artefacts of a modelling choice. She notes in passing that "generation" is used in two ways: for a mere birth interval, and for a cohort welded into something like a self-conscious group by a shared formative experience (a war, a crash, a new technology). She says the argument tells against both. It runs as follows.

(1) To call Millennials or Gen Z a real kind is to claim that sharing a birth cohort makes a difference to people's attitudes and behaviour over and above the difference made by their age and by the period in which they are observed.

(2) In standard demographic accounting, birth cohort = period of observation − age. Age, period, and cohort therefore cannot vary independently: fixing any two fixes the third.

(3) Hence the separate contribution of cohort cannot be read off any body of observational data. Estimating it requires an added constraint — for instance, that two adjacent cohorts differ by equal amounts, or that one of the three effects is nil.

(4) Such constraints are chosen by the analyst; they are not discovered in the data.

(5) So the size of any "generation effect" is fixed by the analyst's assumptions rather than by observation.

(6) A kind whose apparent causal contribution is fixed by the analyst's assumptions rather than by observation is not a real kind.

(7) Therefore generations are not real kinds; "Gen Z" is a modelling artefact.

I have checked the arithmetic behind (2) and it is correct, and I have no quarrel with (3) as a statement about what a dataset can settle on its own. But the argument makes me uneasy for a reason I cannot make precise. Nothing in steps (2) through (6) is special to cohort. Substitute "age" for "cohort" throughout, adjusting (1) accordingly, and every step survives; the same reasoning then shows that age is not a real kind, and by symmetry neither is period, and by the same token neither is any variable whose effect cannot be separated from the effects of the others with which it is exactly linked. That cannot be right.

Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked compelling in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. The steps I would least like to surrender are (2) and (3), which are just bookkeeping; beyond that I have no strong attachment, and I would rather not be told only that the argument is invalid — I want to know what the repair costs and whether a repaired version still supports her deflationary conclusion about generations. I would also like to know whether the same argument, run against the second sense of "generation" — the cohort bound together by a shared historical experience — fares any better, or whether the two senses come apart precisely at the point where her argument goes wrong.
