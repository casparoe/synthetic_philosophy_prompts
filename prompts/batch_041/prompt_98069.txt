For a small reading group on formal epistemology and AI, I'm trying to get a puzzle straight, and I want two diagnoses of it compared rather than run together. The setup is this: take an agent that is a consequentialist — it chooses actions by maximizing expected value against some prior over possible worlds — and hand it a prior in the spirit of Solomonoff's: a distribution over computable hypotheses that assigns each hypothesis a weight falling off sharply with the length of its shortest description. The puzzle is that such an agent seems to end up believing and acting on bizarre hypotheses, and there are two stories people tell about why. The first story locates the trouble in the prior itself — call it the malign-prior diagnosis. Because a hypothesis that merely outputs the agent's observation stream, or that contains a compact description of the agent together with a world arranged to produce that stream, can be shorter than a hypothesis describing a whole ordinary world with its own independent laws, the prior loads a surprising amount of weight onto worlds built around the agent: simulations, environments that exist to predict and steer it, and "physics" that is really a program aimed at it. On this story, any consequentialist inheriting that prior will find its best-supported hypotheses are adversarial ones and will act accordingly. The second story locates the trouble in the agent rather than the prior — call it the self-reference diagnosis. Here the prior is innocent; what generates the pathology is that a consequentialist's choice rule is sensitive to what the world does, including to hypotheses that model the agent's own reasoning, so the agent and its world-model become entangled in a fixed point of the kind familiar from Newcomb-style problems. On this story the malignity is not a special fact about simplicity-weighting at all; it is what you get from any prior rich enough to predict the agent.

What I want from you is a genuine comparison of these two, not a summary of each. Which one is doing the real explanatory work, and which is parasitic on the other? Say precisely what each requires as an assumption — about the reference machine that fixes description lengths, about whether the agent's own source code counts as one of the hypotheses under consideration, about how the agent treats hypotheses that mention its own future choices — and then say what would distinguish them, empirically or conceptually: what does the malign-prior story predict about a non-consequentialist agent, or about the same agent under a different description language, that the self-reference story does not, and vice versa? If there is a version of the puzzle on which the two accounts must give different answers, spell it out, since that is the part I most want to see. Finally, tell me which of the two you find more compelling as an account of why consequentialists fare badly inside something like the universal prior, and where you suspect they are secretly the same idea wearing different clothes.
