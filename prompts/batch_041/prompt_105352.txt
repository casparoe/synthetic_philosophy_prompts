I've got two freinds who've been arguing for like a year: one is a pessimist about aesthetic testimony (thinks you can't really get aesthetic knowledge or justified aesthetic belief from someone else's say-so, or at least that you shouldn't), the other thinks that's way too strong (testimony can transmit aesthetic justification, or at least aesthetic reasons, and the pessimist is confusing a bunch of different things). They've finally agreed to do an adverserial collaboration. Write the joint statement they could both sign. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who's right -- the document itself, in their two voices but with one set of claims they both endorse.

I want it to be in the weeds, so please build in these distinctions and don't collapse them:
- testimony about non-aesthetic properties (this painting is from 1630, it depicts a skull), testimony about thin aesthetic verdicts (it's beautiful, it's good), testimony about thick aesthetic characterizations (it's melancholic, it's kitsch, it's unified), and testimony about one's own experience (it made me cry, I found it boring).
- testimony as evidence (you infer from their say-so) vs. testimony as transmission (you just take their word) vs. testimony as recommendation (they're telling you to go see it).
- the pessimist thesis can be about impossibility (you can't come to know it this way), illegitimacy (you can know it but believing it this way is somehow wrong or bad), or inauthenticity (even if you believe it, it isn't really your aesthetic judgment). Make the optimist say which of those they accept and which they reject, and vice versa.
- the acquaintance principle (aesthetic knowledge requires first-hand experience) vs. the autonomy of aesthetic judgment (you should judge for yourself) vs. the causal dependence of aesthetic experience on first-hand attention. These can come apart.

Then the deliverable:
1. Numbered agreed claims -- things both can sign, including about what the debate is and isn't about, what the cases show, and what neither side needs to deny.
2. The precise disagreements, each phrased in a way that the side holding it would accept as its own view, not a strawman.
3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case, argument, or thought experiment both sides agree would bear on it, and write down what each side predicts about it. Try to include at least one crux about the gallery guide case, one about the blind tasting / expert connoisseur case, one about the spoiler case, and one about a pill that makes you believe a painting is beautiful without ever seeing it.
4. A short section on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, if any, and why.
5. At the end, for each side, say what argument or consideration would actually change its mind, and add a note on which agreed claim is most likely to be a fudge or a slanted formulation, and what would break the agreement.

Keep it concrete and don't lean on names of philosophers or specific books -- I want the reasoning to stand on its own. And yeah, casual is fine, but the final document should be tight.
