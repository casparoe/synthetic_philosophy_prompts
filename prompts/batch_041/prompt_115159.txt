I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, bioethics-adjacent (my disseration is on the ethics of cognitive enhancement), and our department newsletter does a joke issue every spring. I got asked for a piece this year and I'd like to do better than the usual look-the-line-is-blurry haha.

What I want is a parody of an insurance company's internal medical-necessity policy bulletin — the kind of document that tells claims adjusters what counts as treatment and what doesn't. Deadpan and fully in character, no winking, no narrator stepping out to elbow the reader. Around 700 words, laid out as numbered provisions with a short billing-code appendix at the end. Invent the company; don't name or quote any real people, papers, or actual policy documents.

What I'm satirizing is the therapy/enhancement distinction as it actually gets operationalized — the move of making it tractable by defining medical necessity as restoring species-typical functioning, and then letting an actuarial table decide what typical means. So the humor has to come out of the content, not out of silly names or exclamation marks. Someone who knows the enhancement literature should be able to tell that the writer knows exactly where the bodies are buried, and someone who doesn't should still be able to tell what's being mocked by the last provision.

The document should cheerfully commit to all the consequences of that definition. Some of what I'd want it to handle, though find your own way in:

- normal functioning is a rolling statistical baseline, so the bulletin needs a procedure for reclassifying last decade's enhancements as this decade's treatments, and last decade's treatments as this decade's deficits;
- the prevention loophole: something done for a condition you don't have yet is reimbursable as prophylaxis if the actuarial projection puts you near the bottom of the distribution within ten years — which is, of course, itself a moving target;
- the people who decline and stay put get reclassified as impaired without having done anything, and the bulletin treats this as a bookkeeping event rather than a harm;
- an appeals process that is scrupulously fair on paper and accomplishes nothing;
- a clawback: if a procedure is later reclassified downward, the patient owes the difference, plus interest.

Three or four fake billing codes in the appendix would be a nice touch, each one catching a different version of the objection — the vanity case, the prevention case, the everyone-else-is-doing-it case, the I-just-want-to-keep-up case.

Keep it dry. I definately think the funniest version of this document is one that never once admits it's funny.
