I have no background in history or economics, but I've spent the past few months reading about famines and arguing about them, and I've gotten stuck on what I think is a genuine question rather than a semantic one. The argument began over dinner with my brother-in-law, who insists the Irish famine was a crime — that the blight was weather but the deaths were policy — and it then spread to the Bengal famine of 1943 and to the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward in China. His view, roughly, is that calling a famine a natural disaster is always a way of laundering a decision: the harvest shocks are real, but so is the fact that people with power chose to keep exporting grain, or to keep requisitioning it, or to let relief sit unmoved, and those choices are what actually killed people. I've also come across the older idea — the entitlement approach I associate with Amartya Sen — that people starve not because food vanishes but because they lose the ability to command it through wages, prices, and legal claims on it, which seems to make the whole business even less natural. My problem is that I cannot tell how much of the disagreement between us is empirical and how much is definitional, and I want a number to argue with rather than a lecture.

To make the dispute tractable, I've been trying to split the phrase political crime into three separate claims. (A) The famine, or its actual scale, was preventable by some policy that was genuinely feasible at the time, given the information then available. (B) The people who could have prevented it knew, or had strong reason to know, that their choices would produce mass death. (C) The failure was a choice rather than a limit — indifference, ideology, or the pursuit of other goals — and not mere incapacity. My hunch is that (A) is true of nearly every large famine, (B) is true of most, and (C) is true of only some, and that the phrase political crime is really a bet on (C) while almost all of the historical evidence we actually have bears on (A) and (B). I'd like you to tell me whether that diagnosis is right or whether I've stacked the deck.

So: give me your probability that the following proposition is true, stated so that it could turn out false — that for each of these three famines, the one in Ireland in the 1840s, the one in Bengal in 1943, and the one in China after the Great Leap Forward, the famine or its actual scale was the foreseeable consequence of choices made by identifiable people who had feasible alternatives, and that under some such alternative the deaths would have been drastically fewer. Then decompose it. I want your probability for (A), (B), and (C) separately for each of the three cases; your probability that the natural shock alone, with no policy error, would still have produced comparable mortality; and your probability that crime should require intent rather than culpable negligence, since I suspect that one conceptual choice moves the final number more than any historical fact does. Tell me how the terms combine into the overall figure, which single sub-question the result is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would shift it most. My own probability is about 0.6 and my brother-in-law's is about 0.95, so please also say where our disagreement most likely lives — in the facts, in the counterfactuals, or in what we each mean by crime.
