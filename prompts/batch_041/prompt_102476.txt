I want a short story, maybe 1000-1500 words, that makes Aumann's agreement theorem feel like something that could actually happen to two people instead of a result you prove on a blackboard. Setup I'm imagining: two characters who are both strict Bayesians, who start from the exact same prior, and where each one knows the other is a strict Bayesian starting from that same prior. They disagree, stubbornly and over a long stretch of time, about some ordinary probability — whether it'll rain on the wedding day, whether a coworker is lying, whether a mushroom is safe — and neither one is being irrational, biased, or hiding anything. I want the reader to feel the squeeze of the theorem the whole way through: if their beliefs ever really became common knowledge, one of them would have to cave, so how the hell are they still going at it? Then I want a resolution that doesn't cheat. No one gets tired and drops it, no one turns out to be secretly irrational, no deus ex machina. The way out has to be honest: either they keep knowing each other's verdicts but never each other's evidence and reasoning, so the posteriors never actually become common knowledge, or the common-prior assumption quietly breaks somewhere and the story shows us exactly where. I want someone to finish it and think "oh, so that's the loophole."
