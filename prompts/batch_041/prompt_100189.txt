Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between epistemicists and supervaluationists about vagueness. I have read too many comment threads in which these two camps restate their positions at each other, and I want the document they could both actually sign: not a dialogue, not a verdict, and not an explanation of why the debate is stuck, but the constructive text that a genuine adversarial collaboration would produce.

Set it out as follows.

First, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign. I want these to be substantive rather than platitudinous, and I would rather they were surprising than safe. Include, if you can get them past both parties: that both reject degree-theoretic treatments of vagueness; that both preserve classical propositional logic and classical consequence for the object language; that both hold sorites arguments unsound, and agree on which premise does the damage; that both hold the sharp-cutoff existential — the claim that some number of grains is the last that makes a heap — to be true; that both accept that no empirical procedure, no exercise of linguistic competence and no introspection will deliver the location of the cutoff; and that both therefore owe an account of why ordinary speakers say there is no fact of the matter, rather than taking that remark at face value. Be precise about scope: say which of these hold for vague predicates generally and which only for sorites-susceptible ones. Where a claim would be vacuous as stated, tighten it until it is not.

Second, the disagreements, as a table. One row per question, with a column giving the epistemicist's answer and a column giving the supervaluationist's answer, each phrased in terms that side would accept as its own — neither caricatured nor pre-conceded. Further columns: the crux the row turns on; a concrete case, argument or test that both sides agree would bear on it; and each side's prediction for that test, recorded in advance. Make sure the table includes the status of borderline sentences (truth-valueless versus bivalent but unknowable), the behaviour of the truth predicate itself, and what becomes of the individual sorites conditionals taken one at a time. For the row on the truth predicate, the test I have in mind is the pair of sentences 'P or not P' and 'P is true or not-P is true': the two camps should diverge there, and I want that divergence written out.

Third, a short section on cruxes. For each, say which side it would move and in which direction, and what each side would count as evidence against its own view. I expect the load-bearing ones to be whether the in-principle unavailability of the cutoff is evidence of ignorance or of the absence of a fact, and whether higher-order vagueness is handled better by one camp's machinery than the other's; but tell me if I have the cruxes wrong.

Fourth, say plainly which parts of the dispute are terminological, if any, and why — I suspect at least the quarrel over the word 'true' is partly verbal, and I want that separated from the residue that is not.

Write for someone who can reason about this but cannot look anything up: do not name theorists, cite works, or lean on any particular author's formulation, and do not presuppose that the reader has memorised a canonical statement of either view. Use 'epistemicist' and 'supervaluationist' purely as labels for the positions. British English. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a label of no more than a dozen words naming the single crux on which, if it were settled, the disagreement would most nearly disappear.
