I'm in a seminar on the ethics of cities (I'm a 4th year, the course is half philosophy half urban planning students) and next week I have to run the whole 80-minute session myself, about 18 people at round tables. The prof wants us to stop reading about NIMBYism and actually feel it, and I want the thing to land without me lecturing at them first. Can you design the activity?

What I want them to walk out feeling: that whether the corner building gets built is never a separate question from who gets to decide, and that the who-gets-to-decide question has no neutral answer -- every procedure for answering it is already a distribution of rights. Also that the NIMBY isn't being a hypocrite, she's in a stable spot.

Here's the rough shape I had, fix it or replace it: three parallel neighborhood tables of six. Each table has a block map with an empty corner lot and six roles -- four owner-occupiers, one renter, one small local developer whose financing is contingent on a deadline. Two votes, in this order. First, before any discussion and by secret ballot, they pick the decision rule for the second vote (unanimous, two-thirds, simple majority, or abutter veto). Then they vote on whether to approve a four-story twelve-unit building that needs upzoning. Rule that you may lie about your preferences but not about the facts on your role card. No real money, stakes are just your character's interest -- quiet, view, rent, the ability to stay, profit, deadline.

Then the part I care most about: after the vote, I deal each table one shock card -- the renter's lease ends, an owner gets a job offer, a family with two kids wants to move in -- and they have to re-run the rule vote and the project vote. And during the second round I drop in two or three absent-resident cards: people who are affected but aren't in the room (the night-shift workers who can't make a 7pm meeting, the people who'd move in, the ones who'd be pushed out two neighborhoods over). Nobody plays them, they just sit on the table.

What I need from you: full rules and setup, the role and shock and absent-resident cards written out so I can print them, a timeline for the 80 minutes, what to say and not say while it's running, and then a debrief that maps each moment onto the actual ideas -- procedural vs substantive rights, use value vs exchange value, why the secret rule vote is the whole game, why the shock card makes positions look like principles until you move the person. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time (I'm worried the developer just gets dogpiled, or that they all approve unanimously and nothing interesting happens) and how to handle it. And be straight with me about what this format can't reproduce -- I already suspect it flattens the real power asymmetry, since in the real thing the landlord has a lawyer and the tenants don't, and no card can carry decades of displacement.
