I will state the setup in full so that nothing in your answer turns on recalling a specific author. The causal exclusion argument, in the form I care about, rests on three assumptions. First, supervenience: mental properties are fixed by physical properties, so there can be no mental difference without a physical difference. Second, causal closure: every physical effect that has a cause at all has a sufficient physical cause. Third, exclusion: a single effect does not have two distinct and independently sufficient causes unless we are prepared to call the case genuine overdetermination, and genuine overdetermination is unavailable here because the mental and the physical are not independent of one another. The familiar conclusion is that a mental property is either identical to the physical property that realizes it or is causally inert.

Now take seriously a wrinkle that I think reshapes the problem. Suppose content externalism is true: what a mental state represents depends partly on features outside the head, so the supervenience base of a mental property is not confined to the brain. The physical condition that is supposed to pre-empt the mental cause is then not simply a neural event but the neural event together with a scattering of environmental facts, distributed across space and time. Consider what this does to each response below.

Evaluate these responses to the argument:

(a) Type identity: mental properties simply are physical properties, so there is no second candidate cause and nothing to exclude.
(b) Closure denial: mental properties are additional causes over and above the physical ones, so interactionism is true.
(c) Overdetermination acceptance: the mental and the physical are both sufficient causes of the same effect, and systematic overdetermination is tolerable after all.
(d) Variable-relativity: causation is relative to a chosen causal model, and once mental variables are included, the mental property is a difference-maker; the appearance of exclusion is an artifact of having chosen the wrong variables.
(e) Grounding: the physical grounds the mental, grounding is a non-causal dependence relation, and so grounding and causing never compete for the same effect.
(f) Inherited efficacy: mental properties are causally efficacious, but only derivatively, inheriting whatever efficacy they have from their physical realizers, so there is mental causation without any additional causal work.
(g) Eliminativism: there is no mental causation to save, and the felt need to save it is a confusion.
