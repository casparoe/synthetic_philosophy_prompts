I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar session on the two-envelope problem, and the discussion reliably collapses into the same handful of canned reactions. I want a diagnostic instrument instead of a lecture: a decision tree that takes a reader from the one commitment everyone in the room shares to the resolution that reader actually holds.

Every path should begin at the same commitment: one envelope holds twice what the other holds, you have opened yours and seen the amount, and you are weighing the argument that the other envelope is equally likely to hold half or double that amount, hence is worth 1.25 times as much, hence should be taken. From that starting point, build the tree as a numbered sequence of questions with named endpoints, so that it can be printed as a single page.

Each internal node must be a concrete case or claim that a reader can answer without knowing which resolution it leads to. Do not use nodes of the form 'do you think the expectation is well defined?'; use the case that forces the issue. Each leaf should name the resolution in plain prose and add one sentence stating the commitment that leaf has the hardest time defending. Do not attribute positions to authors, and do not include citations, a reading list, or references to the literature; describe each position in your own words.

The tree must reach at least one leaf in each of the four places where a resolution can locate the trouble: in the description of the case itself; in the arithmetic that generates the 1.25 factor; in the decision rule that says take the option with the higher expected value; and in the conclusion, that is, the reasoning is sound and one should switch. Six to eight leaves in total.

These cases must appear on the tree, since they are what pulls the leaves apart:

- the bare version, with nothing said about how the envelopes were filled;
- a version where the filling rule is known and the amounts are bounded: a fair coin chooses between the pair of ten dollars and twenty dollars and the pair of twenty dollars and forty dollars, and you are handed one member of the chosen pair at random;
- a version where the amounts come from a known distribution that is a perfectly ordinary probability distribution yet has no finite average, the chances of very large amounts falling off just slowly enough that the average never settles;
- a version where your utility for money is bounded, say logarithmic or capped, with an ordinary known distribution behind the amounts;
- a version in which, after you switch, you are offered the chance to switch back, and so on.

After the tree, add two short sections. In the first, name any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate and say which case would separate them if one existed. In the second, list the answer combinations that no leaf occupies and say of each whether the gap is there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it.

Write in American English.
