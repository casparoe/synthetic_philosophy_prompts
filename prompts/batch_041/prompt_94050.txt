I'm running a session of a film-and-philosophy seminar on Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men, and I want to use it to teach the epistemology of testimony — the dispute between reductionists, who hold that we need independent, non-testimonial grounds before we may believe what someone tells us, and anti-reductionists, who hold that we are entitled to accept testimony by default, with the burden falling on whoever wants to doubt it. On its face the film looks like a straight vindication of the reductionist: Juror 8 refuses to accept any witness's word until it can be independently corroborated, and the whole drama consists of his dismantling, piece by piece, the old man's and the woman's testimony by reconstructing the timing, the layout of the apartment, the marks on the nose, and the eyeglasses. What I want from you is a reading that takes both the film and the debate seriously: does the film actually argue for reductionism, or does it merely show that one particular set of testimonial beliefs happened to be defeasible? And does it register how much of the jury's own deliberation runs on testimony — each juror taking another's word for what was said, or for what a piece of evidence means — so that the film seems to depend on the very default trust whose undoing it dramatizes?

I'd also like the ethics of testimony brought in alongside the epistemology, because the film is full of moments where credibility is granted or withheld on grounds that have nothing to do with evidence: the rant about "these people" that empties the room, the way Juror 11's accent is used to discount him, the background assumption that the defendant's own account cannot be trusted because of who he is. Does the film show that the reductionist/anti-reductionist question is inseparable from the prior question of who is believed by default — that the demand for independent grounds can itself be a way of withholding the trust one extends to insiders? Where does the film resist this reading, and where does it stack the deck, such that an honest critic would have to concede the deck is stacked? Close by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments on their own do not, and flag any scene a reader who only half-remembers the movie should go back and rewatch, since I want to send the group away with something concrete to look at rather than just a verdict.
