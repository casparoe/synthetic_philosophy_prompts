I would like you to audit a position set I have arrived at on commercial sex, and tell me whether it hangs together. Here is roughly where I stand. First, sex work is work: the sale of sexual services is a legitimate contract, and the people who do it should have the ordinary protections of labour law, including health and safety, the right to organize, and recourse to a court when a client assaults them or refuses to pay. Second, the wrong in the industry, wherever there is one, lies in coercion, poverty, immigration status and stigma rather than in the exchange of sex for money as such, so a person who sells sex knowingly and without duress is not thereby degraded. Third, I would not criminalize the seller under any scheme, and I would rather not criminalize the buyer either; I am persuaded that decriminalizing the transaction as a whole, on the New Zealand pattern, does more for workers' safety than the Nordic approach of prosecuting the purchase while leaving the seller alone, which I take to push the trade away from police protection. Fourth, I nonetheless think the Nordic model gets something right that the consumer framing gets wrong: sexual access is not an ordinary consumer good, and I want the law and public institutions to express as much even where nobody is jailed, so no public advertising of sexual services, no state brokering, and no official treatment of the industry as just another sector. Fifth, I would keep it a serious offence for a third party to organize or profit from another person's sex work; whatever else changes, pimping stays illegal. Sixth, I hold a broadly harm-based view of criminal law: conduct should be criminal only where it wrongs or endangers others, and the state should not use the criminal law to enforce a contested conception of sexual morality. Seventh, I accept the thought that some goods are the kind of thing that can be freely given but not properly sold, and I take sex to be plausibly among them, though I do not want that thought to carry much weight in policy.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a survey of the literature. Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only appear to conflict, and how severe is each genuine tension, ordered from most serious to least. For each genuine tension, say what has to give, or what the cheapest revision is that leaves the rest of the position intact, and what that revision costs me. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most at the least cost, and which apparent tensions I should stop worrying about, with the reason each dissolves. Be concrete: wherever a commitment is doing abstract work, test it against a case, such as a worker who does the job for money, finds it boring, and resents being pitied; a nineteen-year-old whose boyfriend takes her earnings; a client who is lonely and harmless; and two workers who want to rent premises together and split the rent. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables.
