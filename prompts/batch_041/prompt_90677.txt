I am preparing a talk in defence of evidential cooperation in large worlds, and I want one worked thought experiment that isolates the idea rather than merely illustrating it. In this idea, the world is assumed large enough to contain many agents relevantly similar to oneself, causally out of reach, and not necessarily sharing one's values; by choosing an action that benefits such agents one takes one's own choice as evidence that they choose actions that benefit one, and the sheer number of such partners can make a small per-partner correlation decisive. (The idea has also been discussed under the heading of multiverse-wide superrationality.)

Construct such a case to the following specifications.

1. Causal isolation. The two agents cannot act on, signal to, or be acted on by one another, and need not know of one another's existence. No exotic physics, no backward causation, no ideal predictor, no simulation by a more powerful being, and no acausal influence of any kind. Whatever correlates their choices must be something mundane that you state explicitly — a shared decision procedure, a shared training history, a shared situation — and you must say why it produces a correlation.

2. Divergent values. Their interests must genuinely diverge, and preferably partly conflict, so that no shared goal, mutual affection, or common payoff can explain the cooperation. The interest of the case is precisely that cooperation is possible without shared values.

3. Opposed verdicts. The case must be engineered so that causal decision theory and evidential decision theory reach opposite verdicts, and so that the disagreement arises solely from the evidential correlation, with no causal channel smuggled in. Functional (or updateless) decision theory must also be given a verdict, whether or not it sides with the evidentialist.

4. The large world must earn its place. Use finitely many agents rather than an infinite universe, so that the arithmetic is well defined, and make the number do the work: a small per-partner correlation must aggregate into a decisive reason to cooperate. The agents must be only loosely similar, not copies, so that no single correlation is close to one. State what becomes of the verdicts when the number of potential partners falls to one, and to zero.

5. Realism and restraint. Ordinary finite reasoners, with no perfect rationality and no omniscience. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph, and keep the payoffs to something an ordinary person could face.

After the scenario, deliver: a point-by-point demonstration that it satisfies each specification above; the action each agent faces and the payoff structure, set out explicitly; the verdicts of causal, evidential, and functional (or updateless) decision theory, with the reasoning for each; the single feature that does the work, and what happens when it is removed; and finally the strongest reply available to a causalist — the case for thinking the evidential reasoning is a mistake rather than a discovery.

The case is for an argument that cooperation requires neither shared values nor causal contact, so it counts as cheating if it presupposes a common payoff, a shared value, mutual concern, or any causal interaction; and a case that stipulates the verdicts rather than deriving them from the theories fails.

Please write your answer in British English.
