I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to think through a claim about pain that I keep half-convincing myself of. The claim is this: the badness of a pain episode for the person having it consists entirely in its affective-motivational dimension, the awfulness, the part that makes you want it to stop. The sensory-discriminative dimension, meaning how intense the sensation is, where it's located, and what quality it has, is evaluatively inert; it matters only instrumentally, as a signal about what is going on in the body, and contributes nothing in itself to how bad the episode is. So a pain that is strongly felt but not disliked would not be bad for its subject at all, beyond whatever information it carries. What draws me to this is the phenomenon of pain asymbolia: patients who, after certain kinds of brain damage, will calmly report a burning or pinching sensation, give what looks like a sensible intensity and location, and then say that it does not bother them, that they feel it but are not suffering from it. If that dissociation is real, then the awfulness looks like a separable ingredient of pain, and it becomes a real question which ingredient carries the badness.

What I want from you is the case against the position, not another statement of it. What would have to be true for it to be wrong, and how much of that is actually plausible rather than merely possible? I'm thinking of lines like these, though I'd rather you tell me which ones matter than just work through my list: whether the asymbolia reports should be trusted at all, or whether the injury is interfering with the patients' ability to express or attend to what they feel while the badness is still there; whether harm can outrun a person's attitude toward it, since a tumor I don't mind is still bad for me, which suggests the badness of a bodily state may not depend on my disliking it; the mirror-image dissociation, where people suffer intensely with no nociceptive input at all, which would make the sensory dimension look unnecessary rather than merely inert; and whether the patients' behavior lines up with their reports, since I have a vague impression that some of them still flinch or pull away, though I don't trust my memory on that. I'm also worried about an escape hatch: if 'dislike' gets stretched to mean anything the person would avoid if they could, or anything that is in fact harmful to them, then the view seems to come out true by definition and to risk nothing at all, and I want to know whether there is a version of it that stays substantive.

Please sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, from ones that should actually make me abandon the position, through ones that are merely awkward, down to ones a defender should happily concede, and say which is which. Then tell me honestly whether the position, stated as carefully as it can be, takes on any risk, or whether it can always be re-described so that no evidence could count against it, and whether that insulation is a defect or just what any decent account of value looks like. For what it's worth, what would move me most is a case where someone is plainly harmed by a pain they genuinely do not mind, or a reason to think the asymbolia reports are an artifact of how the question gets asked.
