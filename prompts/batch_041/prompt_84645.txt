I spent thirty-five years as a field entomologist, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of mind seminar at my local university—partly to argue, mostly to learn. The convenor has now asked me to run one 90-minute session for a mixed group of about eighteen people: advanced undergraduates, a few graduate students, and a handful of adult auditors. The room has movable chairs, a whiteboard, and no computers. I want the session to make them feel the force of the idea that an ant colony might have a mind of its own—the thought experiment sometimes called Aunt Hillary—without my lecturing at them first. So please design a runnable activity or game that puts the idea in the rules rather than in a speech.

Use the real house-hunting behavior of certain rock ants as the armature: scouts assess candidate nest sites; they recruit nestmates by tandem running, in which one ant leads another to a site; and the colony commits to a site once enough ants have gathered there—a quorum threshold—after which the ants switch to carrying brood. In the activity the participants should be the ants, with only local information and those two channels of communication: tandem running and quorum counting. No global announcements, no one comparing all the sites. Give them four or five candidate nests with hidden quality, brood tokens, distinct roles, materials, a clear payoff or success condition that needs no real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The rules should be arranged so that a colony-level decision emerges that no individual intended or can fully report. Then give me a structured debrief that makes them try to locate—or fail to locate—the colony's belief, desire, and decision, and that introduces the philosophical idea only after they have felt the puzzle. Map moments in the game onto the idea: local recruitment, quorum threshold, commitment, and the absence of any central decider. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—participants trying to be a queen or central planner, over-recruiting to a mediocre site, confusion about hidden information, and whatever else you foresee—and how to handle it. Be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce: it can make the intentional stance vivid, but it cannot settle whether the colony has phenomenal consciousness, and the human participants cannot fully shed their individual meta-awareness. Say what I, as facilitator, must supply in the debrief. No reading lists, citations, or bibliography—I want the activity itself. Keep the whole design under 1,000 words, in prose, with a short timeline table only if it earns its place. Do not name the thought experiment until the debrief.
