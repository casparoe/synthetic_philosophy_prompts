Ok so I sit on our national memorial and commemorations board — we review proposed monuments, plaques, museum exhibits, that sort of thing, and part of our brief is supposedly to keep the country honest about its defeats instead of letting them get prettied up. Our rulings so far, more or less:

- We rejected a plaque for a Confederate cemetery. Every date and name on it was correct, but it described the dead as defenders of their homeland, and the surrounding text pushed the states-rights line.
- We approved a Dunkirk memorial that states plainly it was a retreat and a defeat, credits the escape mostly to the weather and a German command decision, and never calls it a miracle or a triumph of the spirit.
- We approved a Masada exhibit that lays out the siege, the mass suicide, and the arguments among archaeologists, and treats the whole symbol-of-defiance reading as something later generations did with the story rather than as what the event itself meant.
- We turned down a monument to the 1389 battle in Kosovo that cast the defeat as the origin of a permanent national destiny.
- We turned down a veterans' group's proposal to commemorate a 20th century evacuation disaster with wording to the effect that the dead had died so that we could be born. The facts on it were fine; the group's own filing said the point was to shore up support for a current deployment.

Now next month's file. It's a memorial to a massacre committed against our own side about a hundred years ago. Every fact on it is right. There's no redemption language at all — no they-did-not-die-in-vain, no sacrifice, nothing like that. But the sponsor's submission says straight out that the point of the memorial is to strengthen our position in an ongoing territorial dispute with our neighbour, and the wording is definately chosen to embarrass them.

What should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I keep flip-floping because I can't work out whether the rule we've actually been running is "no turning a defeat into a redemption story" or "no using the dead for present day politics" — both seem to fit every ruling we've made — or maybe some third rule I haven't spotted. So: what does each candidate rule predict for the new file, which one should I go with and why, which past ruling is the closest precedent, and what's a case I could put to the board that would actually force those two rules apart?
