Me and two friends who do political theory have been going around in circles for about a year on the boundary problem — who actually counts as part of the demos when we say "the people" get to decide. Not the easy version where we all agree the franchise should cover adult citizens and not tourists. The version where we've split into two camps and nobody has moved an inch.

Camp one: the demos is everyone whose interests are affected by the decision. If the dam upstream floods your village you're in, passport or no passport. Camp two: no, the demos is everyone subject to the decision's coercion — the demos is whoever the state can actually bind, because legitimacy is about being ruled, not about being touched. We've been beating each other with the same two examples all year. The downwind country that gets wrecked by somebody else's emissions: affected to hell, subject to nothing. The guy prosecuted under a law that barely touches his life: bound hard, barely affected. Each example is a knockout blow against the other camp and neither example has ever changed anyone's mind. So I want a diagnosis, not a winner.

My own hunch, tell me if its dumb: the two camps aren't really disagreeing about the criterion of inclusion. One of them is answering "who may be coerced / who is bound by this" and the other is answering "who gets to be a participant in making this" — a question about the grounds of obligation vs a question about the composition of the decision procedure. Under one heading ("who belongs to the demos") they might be answering two different questions, and the all-affected side has a further problem: any pre-political criterion for drawing the line seems to have to come from outside politics, which is weird, while the all-subjected side just swallows the circularity — you can't ask the people who the people are without already knowing who gets to vote.

One thing that might be a clue. In the units-of-selection fight in biology, a lot of the heat went out once people separated the thing that gets copied from the thing that does the interacting — half the disagreement turned out to be two camps using "unit" for two different roles. I keep wondering if "demos" is doing the same trick, naming a role (the body that authorizes) and a set (the people who are bound) at once.

So: is this a verbal disagreement, two questions under one name, a hidden empirical/sociological disagreement about how coercion actually works, or a bedrock split about whether legitimacy is voluntarist (grounded in who agreed) or non-voluntarist (grounded in who's subject)? Be concrete about what each camp is actually committed to, dont just survey the literature. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, dropping one of the questions, or admitting the whole thing is a pseudo-problem. If you think nothing would move it, say so and say why.
