So I'm a science journalist — I mostly write about perception and the philosophy of mind — and I'm working on a piece about the colour phi phenomenon. You probably know it: flash a blue dot up top, then a red dot lower down a fraction of a second later, and at the right timing people see one dot travelling across the gap. Fine, that's just apparent motion. But ask them what happened to the colour and they'll swear the dot turned red partway along the path — a colour change they report at a moment when the second dot hadn't been shown yet. Which can't be right, since the information that it's red doesn't exist yet at the point where they say they saw it. Dennett's old Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque framing is where everyone goes with this: either the memory gets rewritten after the fact, or there's no single moment at which the experience is finished and handed to you. I'm not asking you to re-litigate that, I know it's been done to death.

What I want instead is the counterfactual-history move, because I think it dodges something the usual debate skips. William James died in 1910, long before any of this was filmed or written up. Suppose he hadn't. Suppose he'd seen the demos and had the modern word "postdiction" available — the idea that the brain can hand you a perceptual content whose apparent time-stamp is earlier than the stimulus that produced it. What would James have made of it?

I want you to actually commit. Would he have thought the paradox was real, or would he have said the mistake is ours — that we're assuming experience arrives with a time-stamp on a single objective clock, which is more or less what his specious-present picture denies? Would he have been an Orwellian, a Stalinesque, or refused the choice? What would he have said about the fact that the "when" of an experience is something the subject has to judge, and that those judgments are themselves manipulable? Where would he have said the experimenters were begging the question, and what would he have predicted the next experiment would show — anything concrete enough that we could call him wrong?

One thing that nags at me and I'd like him on: there are other effects where the timing seems to run the other way. Put something moving on a screen and flash a separate object next to it, and the moving thing looks like it's a little ahead of where it actually was — as if the brain extrapolates forward instead of patching backward. If both are real, "postdiction" is too tidy a slogan. Would James have seen a contradiction there, or exactly what you'd expect from a stream of consciousness with a thick now?
