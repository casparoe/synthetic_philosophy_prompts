I've been going round in circles with a friend about whether it makes any sense to say that evolution has reasons for the things it builds. His line is that when a biologist says a trait exists in order to do something, that's just a shorthand we project onto nature, and nothing is really done for a reason unless there's a mind doing the reasoning. My line is that you can have something doing the work of a reason without any reasoner at all: a signal, a reflex, a bit of anatomy can track a genuine consideration (the distance to the predator, the cost of the display), can be sensitive to counter-considerations, and can misfire in ways that are the right kind of wrong. It's the sort of thing that can be right or wrong, and none of that needs anybody to entertain anything. I've seen the name free-floating rationale attached to this, and I think it's the hinge of the whole argument.

What I want from you is the most important implications of taking that idea seriously, not a summary of it. In particular, what follows for three things: (1) whether claims about what a trait is for can be straightforwardly true or false, or whether they're just useful projections; (2) whether the idea lets us say that immune systems, market prices, or trained neural nets have reasons in anything more than a loose metaphor; and (3) whether it collapses the difference between having a reason and merely being as if you had one, or whether that distinction can be rebuilt on some other footing. If it turns out to licence saying that a thermostat has reasons, then say so plainly and tell me where the interesting line is afterwards — don't fudge it to keep the intuition alive.

Don't just lay out the positions and leave me to pick. Take one. Tell me which of these implications actually matter and which are red herrings, and commit to a view on whether the free-floating version of a reason is a real reason or a useful fiction. Be honest about where you're least sure.
