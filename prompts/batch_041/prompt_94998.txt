Next term I've got two hours with a first-year seminar on the special composition question. They've had exactly one week of metaphysics, so basically none. Don't explain it to me -- plan the explanation. What has to be in place before the question even sounds like a question? My worry is that they'll hear 'when do some things compose a further thing?' as asking when we're allowed to call a heap a heap, or as the identity-over-time question, and I can't tell which of those is the worse default to start from.

I want the whole session carried by one case, and I'd rather it not be a case they'll have met in a textbook. Two candidates I'm playing with: (a) a slime mold that lives as separate single cells, aggregates into one crawling slug when it starves, then breaks apart again; (b) a coral colony -- genetically one individual, but functionally alot of little mouths. Pick whichever does more work, say what the loser fails to show, and say what the winning case has to have (and lack) if it's going to put pressure on both the 'obviously chairs exist' reaction and the 'obviously any two things make a thing' reaction.

Then the misconception map: which answers they'll jump to and in what order, where the 'this is just a verbal dispute about how we use the word thing' reading will surface, and exactly where in the sequence I should head it off -- or whether I should let it sit and do damage for a bit, since it might be the fastest route into the actual problem. What do I refuse to get into at this level, and what's one question I can ask at the end that shows they've got the shape of the problem rather than three labels?
