I teach a final-year option in formal epistemology and am drafting the final examination paper. I want one essay question that tests whether candidates can do more than recite that accuracy is the fundamental epistemic good. Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme. Candidates have 45 minutes and the question is worth 30 marks.

Put this case to them. A student proposes what she calls calibrationism: the fundamental epistemic good of a credence is not accuracy — closeness to the truth as measured by a proper scoring rule — but calibration, the requirement that events assigned credence p occur with relative frequency p. She offers two arguments. First, calibration is what we actually inspect forecasters for. Second, accuracy appears to demand the impossible, because in any world the most accurate coherent credence function is the omniscient one, which nobody can have. The question should ask candidates to say what is right and what is wrong in calibrationism, and to use the comparison to locate the weakest link in the accuracy-first programme.

The model answer should bring out, in whatever order it finds natural: that calibration used as a scoring rule is not strictly proper, and is satisfied trivially by the no-information function that assigns every event its base rate; the distinction between calibration (reliability) and refinement (discrimination), and the fact that the two can be traded off against each other; the difficulty of attaining perfect calibration with respect to every proposition one holds an opinion about, which candidates should motivate with a self-referential case in which the truth of a proposition turns on the credence assigned to it; why the accuracy-first theorist answers the omniscience worry not by maximisation but by dominance together with a requirement of immodesty, and why immodesty is the premise most open to challenge; and the evidentialist worry that accuracy alone does not deliver the norm of proportioning belief to evidence.

The marking scheme should be built around six must-hit points, each with its mark (totalling 30) and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs marks — for instance, treating calibration as a proper scoring rule, reading the dominance argument as an argument for maximisation, or assuming that immodesty is uncontroversial. Please write the question, the model answer and the scheme in British English.
