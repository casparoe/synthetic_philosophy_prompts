Ok, I need a sparring partner. I'm teaching a session next week on advance directives — the whole "does the earlier self get to boss around the later self" business — and I want to pressure-test my own view before I have to sell it to 25 med students who will definately ask the hardest version of the question. So please play my opponent, and play it straight: you're a committed critic of the idea that an advance directive binds the later self. Your line is that the only interests that exist are the ones the living person has now; that "the authority of the earlier self" is a polite name for letting the healthy decide for the vulnerable; and that we honor documents mostly because it's easier than sitting with someone whose mind is going and asking what she wants today. Strongest version, no caricature, real arguments — concede what you have to but don't hand me the main point. No citations, I know the literature, I want your own reasoning. One paragraph per turn, stay in character until I say stop, and don't ask me to clarify the case, it's all below. If you think my framing begs the question, say so and say why rather than just answering me.

Here's my opening. Take a woman — call her Ruth. At 58, sharp as a tack, she signs a directive: no antibiotics, no feeding tube, if I develop severe dementia. And she insists on a clause, makes her lawyer read it back to her twice because she wants it airtight: "If I ever look happy, that isn't me being happy. That's the disease wearing my face. Ignore it." Sixteen years later Ruth has advanced dementia. She's cheerful, she lights up for her favorite aide, she hums along to the radio, she eats. She gets a UTI; three days of antibiotics would clear it up. She can't tell us anything about what she wants. Your view says her current interests decide, and look at her — she's content, she isn't suffering, so treat her. But you can't get from "she's content" to "so treat her" without leaning on the very state her earlier self disowned in advance. So either her contentment counts as a reason, in which case the earlier self never had the authority she thought she had and the directive was only ever a strong suggestion — or it doesn't count, and you're overriding a living person's welfare on the say-so of a piece of paper. Which is it? I don't think you get both, and I want to watch you try.
