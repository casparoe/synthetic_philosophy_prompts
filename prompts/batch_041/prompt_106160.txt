I'm writing on Xunzi's account of ritual (li) as a solution to the problem of motivation, and I want to test the Mencius–Xunzi disagreement against a real case rather than the usual thought experiments. Use the Milgram obedience experiments as the episode: ordinary volunteers, an unseen "learner," escalating shocks administered under a calm experimenter's insistence, a majority continuing to the maximum, most showing visible distress, and a substantial minority refusing outright.

Map it in both directions. My provisional reading is that Xunzi should predict something like this — that conduct is governed by the external scaffolding of authority and ritual, so a lab coat and a script can override an ordinary person's reluctance to injure someone — which would make the case a vindication of his mechanism. But I think that is too quick: the very scaffolding he treats as the source of orderly conduct is what produces cruelty here, and I want you to say whether that is a genuine difficulty for him or whether his account of ritual can distinguish the two outcomes. Then take Mencius. His paradigm is the immediate wince at the child at the well, and Milgram's design removes the sufferer from sight, which may either expose the limits of his account or instead activate his own idea of "extending" a sprout beyond the case in front of one; consider also the Ox Mountain passage, where a naturally good nature is ruined by its surroundings. Does any of that save him, or does it merely relocate the problem?

End with the diagnosis I actually care about: whether the two are disagreeing about human nature at all, or about which mechanism reliably actualizes a nature they both take to be perfectible — and which of them the Milgram evidence actually bears on.
