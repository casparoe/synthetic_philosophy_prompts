I'm in a slightly annoying situation where two things I care about have collided. I'm writing my undergrad thesis on weakness of will, and at the same time I'm three weeks into a genuine attempt to stick to commitments — gym four times a week, thesis two hours a day before I check my phone — and I've already skipped the gym twice and I'm behind on the thesis. So the philosophy and the behavioral econ stuff feel like they should be the same conversation, and I've started to suspect they're actually talking past each other. I've sketched an essay and I'd like you to poke holes in it.

The argument goes like this. The hyperbolic discounting story — present bias, preference reversal, why I'd rather have 20 euros today than 25 next week but somehow prefer the 25 when both are a year out — explains diachronic inconsistency. My preferences at t1 and t2 come apart, so I do things my earlier self would have vetoed, and the natural fixes are commitment devices (deadlines, gym buddies, apps that shame you). Fine, but that isn't the philosophical problem of akrasia. The philosophical problem is synchronic: at the very moment of acting I judge that A is best all things considered, and I do B anyway. Discounting can't get at that, because discounting is just part of how I rank the options — so if I take the immediate thing, I'm taking what I, right now, hot and tired and bored, most prefer. Which means the "judgment" that A is best was never my all-things-considered preference; it was a prediction, or a wish, or a fact about my cooler self that I happen to remember. So the model either redescribes akrasia as a commitment problem rather than a will problem, or it quietly deletes the thing we wanted explained. The one escape hatch I can see is if the judgment itself is what gets discounted: the payoff of acting on my considered judgment, or of being the kind of person who does that, is always deferred, so present bias eats it before I ever get to cash it in. I've been calling that meta-discounting, I have half a page on it, and I dont know if it's a real distinction or just me being clever.

Could you comment on this? Specifically: (a) where am I getting the philosophy wrong — is the synchronic/diachronic split doing the work I think it's doing, or have I just invented a label for a distinction that's already standard and that I'm now restating badly? (b) Is the meta-discounting move anything, or does it collapse into ordinary discounting of a delayed benefit, in which case it's about payoffs and not about the judgment at all? And (c) is this actually interesting to anyone but me — my gut says someone will object that if I judge A best and do B, then I simply didn't judge it best, and I want to know whether that's a real objection or just a definitional fiat that makes the problem vanish.
