I am a backend engineer — distributed systems, storage, and infrastructure — with no formal training in philosophy. Everything I think with comes from that vocabulary, and I would like to know how much of a philosophical puzzle that vocabulary can actually carry. The puzzle I have in mind is self-deception.

As I understand it, deception ordinarily has two roles: a deceiver who knows the truth and wants the other party not to have it, and the party who is fooled. Self-deception asks one system to occupy both roles at once. Stated flatly, that seems to require one mind to know that p and believe that not-p, or to hold both beliefs at once — which is why the puzzle has a static form (can a mind simply be in that state?) and a dynamic form (by what sequence of steps does a mind get itself into that state, given that the entry into the state is supposed to be something the mind does, and given that a strategy known to be a trick is hard to be taken in by?). Philosophers seem to divide roughly three ways. Some treat the contradiction as a reductio and conclude that what we call self-deception is really just ordinary motivated belief formation with no intention to deceive at all. Some keep the two-role structure by splitting the agent into parts or sub-agents, so that one part hides something from another, and then owe an account of how the parts compose into one person. And some attack an assumption the other two share — for instance by denying that the hidden side is belief at all (perhaps it is suspicion, fear, or a low degree of confidence rather than full belief), or by denying that believing is the sort of thing one can decide to do, or by denying that deception is the right category in the first place.

Restate each of these three positions, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: processes and their private state; message passing, and what the absence of a message can and cannot convey; shared memory; caches and staleness; write-ahead logs, and what gets logged versus what never gets written; access control and information hiding; module boundaries and interfaces; consistency models, strong versus eventual; conflict resolution when replicas are reconciled; monitoring and observability; and, where it matters, the difference between a system that has no record of p and a system that has a record of not-p. Take me to be fluent enough in these ideas to follow a technically precise translation rather than an analogy for beginners.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical positions survive the translation intact? Which survive only in a distorted form, and what exactly is distorted? Which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I especially want you to flag the places where my vocabulary quietly does work that the philosophy does not license: my habit of treating a system as something an outside observer defines and delimits, my assumption that module boundaries are given rather than negotiated, and my instinct that a contradictory state is merely a bug that some reconciliation rule will clean up. Is that last instinct a genuine dissolution of the paradox, or does it show that belief in my vocabulary means something weaker than it does for a person? Finally, make the judgment call: does the residue indicate that my framework is missing something real — the normativity of belief, the unity of the subject, the difference between not looking and being unable to see — or does it indicate that the original puzzle is less substantive than it appears, and largely an artifact of how the philosophers' vocabulary bundles intention, belief, and agency into a single package? Do not resolve this by quietly assuming my framework and declaring the philosophers confused; if a translation requires an assumption my vocabulary does not contain, say so and say what it costs.
