For the last six years I have run a small team that builds and scores questionnaires for a regional health service — patient-reported outcome measures for pain, fatigue and low mood, mostly — and I have no academic training beyond an adult-education certificate in the philosophy of science, which is how I came to be invited to give a short paper to the fortnightly seminar attached to the local university's psychometrics group. I want to use that slot to argue something I have come to believe and cannot get anyone at work to take seriously: that the numbers my team produces are not measurements, and that the obstacle is a feature of the procedures rather than of the subject matter. The thought, which I have been calling ascription universality in my notes, is that a procedure earns the name of measurement only if it can be applied, in principle, to any instance of the attribute it claims to ascribe — one can in principle assign a length to any length, a mass to any mass, a duration to any duration — and that this unlimited applicability, rather than precision or numbers as such, is what gives quantitative science its peculiar epistemic dominance, its capacity to support inferences that merely classificatory schemes cannot. My questionnaires fail the condition, and not incidentally: the items are calibrated on a particular population, a score means something only relative to that population and that item set, and the same number in a different population does not ascribe the same amount of anything. So what my team does is rank people within a frame; it is not the ascription of a quantity, and I want to argue that this changes what we are entitled to infer — that a three-point improvement need not be the same amount of improvement in two different clinics, and that we should stop writing the word measurement on our reports.

The room will not be of one mind. Two of the regulars are psychometricians of the item-response school who have spent years defending questionnaires against precisely the charge that they measure nothing, and who will hear my paper as an attack on their working lives; one is a philosopher of science with operationalist sympathies who thinks that demanding an attribute that exists independently of the procedure is itself the error; one is a consultant rheumatologist who uses the scores to decide about treatment and cares only whether they help; and one is a sociologist who thinks the only interesting question about quantification is who benefits from it. Please write me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight objections I will actually get from these four people — not the ones I would get from a general audience of sceptics about psychology — ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in my opening two minutes to take the sting out of the worst of it, what I should hold even if it costs me the room, and which single question I am most hoping nobody asks. Do not send me a reading list; I cannot get at journals and would not know what to do with one.

I would rather be told I am confused than be flattered, so be blunt about the parts of this that may be my own muddle: if my gloss on universality is a garbled version of something the psychometricians will recognise and dismantle, say so and say what the honest version of my point would be. Tell me which of your predicted objections you are least confident about and what I would have to hear on the night to conclude that you ranked them wrongly — if you think the psychometricians will shrug rather than fight, say that instead. Finish with a single line beginning Verdict: naming the one objection that must be answered if the paper is to survive.
