Two friends of mine, Kathy and Owen, spent most of a four-hour drive home from a walking weekend arguing about the flooding of our village hall cellar, and I was in the back seat with nothing to do but listen. Neither of them has read any philosophy, and nor have I. They have asked me to settle it, and I would rather have your help than guess.

What happened was this. In February the cellar flooded. The drain in the lane outside had been blocked with leaves for a fortnight, so the rainwater that would normally have run away pooled up and came in over the cellar step. And a downpipe had come away from the gutter and was emptying itself down the wall over the airbrick, so water was getting in that way as well. Either of those on its own would have been enough to flood the cellar.

Kathy said the blocked drain had caused the flood, since if the drain had been clear the water would have run away and the cellar would have stayed dry. Owen said that could not be right: with the downpipe pouring in as it was, the cellar would have flooded even with a clear drain, and the same went the other way round, so her test said neither of them caused the flood — which is nonsense, since the flood plainly had causes.

Kathy said the test only needed care. Ask it the other way round, she said: if the downpipe had stayed on the wall, would the blocked drain on its own have flooded the cellar? Yes. And if the drain had been clear, would the loose downpipe on its own have flooded it? Yes. Each would have done the whole job by itself, and that is what it is for something to be a cause. Owen asked whether the cracked gully in the yard was a cause on that reckoning too, since it would have done the whole job by itself if the water had ever reached it. Kathy said no: the water never reached the gully, the gully was doing nothing, and the drain was. Owen said that reply gave the game away, because it meant the counterfactual was never doing the work; what she was really going on was which things were actually doing something at the time.

He then said her test gives whatever answer you like, depending on how you fill in the imaginary world. Hold everything else as it actually was and clear the drain, and the cellar floods anyway, so the drain is no cause. But take the nearest world in which the drain is cleared — the odd-job man coming on the Saturday, as he would have done had his mother not gone into hospital, and mending the downpipe in the same morning, since both jobs were on his list — and the cellar stays dry, so the drain is a cause after all. Nothing about the water changes between those two; only the handyman's diary changes.

Kathy said the diary had nothing to do with it: you have to hold the world still and ask what one thing would have done, and we ask that sort of question all the time — we say a spark caused a fire even though nobody could have taken the spark away and left everything else untouched. Owen said that if that is the question she wants answered, they are not arguing about the same thing at all: she wants to know what would have made a difference in a world held still, and he wants to know what actually let the water in that night. The drain had been blocked for a fortnight and nothing had happened; the downpipe came off on the Tuesday and by Friday the cellar was wet. Whatever her test says, those two were not doing the same kind of work.

I could not follow all of it, and I am not taking sides. I would like a point-by-point judgement: take their claims in turn, including the gully and the handyman's diary, and tell me which stand up and which do not, and why. Then tell me what both of them are missing, because I have a feeling that something is going on underneath the argument that neither of them has put their finger on. I am not after a verdict on who is the more reasonable of the two, but on which of their specific claims are sound and who, if either, has the better of it.
