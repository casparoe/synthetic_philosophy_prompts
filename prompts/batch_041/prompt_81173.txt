I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and I've been handed two sessions of an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of belief — the Clifford–James exchange, with a little of the contemporary evidentialism grafted on at the end. My students follow the arguments competently, but they keep filing the whole dispute under a narrowly technical question about what one may believe when the evidence runs out, and I can't get them to see that it is also a question about how beliefs are held in common, sustained by one person for another, and policed between people. For the second session I want to teach the problem through Ibsen's The Wild Duck rather than through more epistemology, and I'd like you to work out the reading with me before I put it in front of a class. The play is from 1884, a dozen years before James's lecture reached print, so I'm not asking about influence or about what Ibsen had read; I'm asking whether the play dramatises the issue more sharply than either essay does, and where it forces us to refine the positions rather than merely illustrate them. Assume my students will have read the play in translation, and that they are not theatre people, so the reading has to be carried by what happens and by what the characters want, not by close attention to wording.

What I want is a real reading, not an allegory. Begin with the obvious mapping and then break it. Gregers Werle arrives as the evidentialist conscience of the play, insisting that Hjalmar be told the truth about Gina and about Hedvig's paternity, and he is the character the tradition most enjoys blaming for the catastrophe; Relling, who prescribes the sustaining fiction usually rendered as the life-lie and treats it as a condition of Hjalmar's continued functioning, looks like the Jamesian in the room. I think that mapping is unstable in ways that matter. Relling does not hold the lie himself; he administers it to other people and seems to assume that the ordinary person cannot bear the truth, which makes him something closer to a paternalist about other people's beliefs than to anyone James would recognise — James is defending a believer's own licence to risk a belief on a genuine option, not a bystander's licence to manage someone else's. The conditions James sets out, that the option be live, forced, and momentous and undecidable on intellectual grounds, look to me like conditions on the first-person case, and I suspect the play is mostly about the third-person case: not what I may believe, but what I may require you to believe, and what I owe you once I have decided you are living a lie. Gregers's demand that Hjalmar live up to what he calls the claim of the ideal is a demand made on another person's epistemic life, and the play's cruelty is that the demand is finally answered by a fourteen-year-old girl rather than by Hjalmar.

So: tell me which scenes carry the argument and why — the dinner party where Gregers learns the story, the loft with the wounded duck and the rabbits, old Ekdal's shooting, the walk on which Gregers tells Hjalmar what he knows, the household afterwards with Hjalmar's coldness and his insistence on taking over the business and the accounts, the moment he says he would like to wring the duck's neck, Gregers's proposal that Hedvig sacrifice the duck to win her father back, the pistol and the shot in the loft, where critics have long disagreed about what she meant to do. Be faithful in both directions. Don't reduce the play to a case study: the tragicomic register, the self-regard that Hjalmar mistakes for grief, the great invention he never finishes and the couch he lies on to think about it, and the duck itself as a symbol that refuses to be cashed in cleanly, all need to be doing work in the reading rather than being scenery. And don't flatten the philosophers to fit the plot: James is not defending self-deception, and Clifford's principle is about the formation of belief and the social cost of credulity, not about rudeness at the dinner table. Two questions I would like answered directly: does the play, in the end, vindicate Clifford, given that it punishes the truth-teller and rewards the man who keeps the fiction going — or does it only punish the manner of the telling, and what is the difference? And what does the play give us that the two essays cannot: is it the social maintenance of belief, the harms of truth-telling as a distinct category from the harms of credulity, or something about the third-person demand that neither James nor Clifford has the vocabulary for? No citations and no extended quotation — I know the text and will do the textual work myself; I want the argument, and a couple of scenes I can put in front of twenty undergraduates as the opening question.
