I have come to think that superrationality is not a curiosity but the correct account of rational choice in symmetric games: where the parties know themselves to be reasoning alike, each should ask which single strategy, adopted by all of them, would maximise every participant's payoff — hence cooperation in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, and hence the Platonia case, in which twenty people are told that whoever sends a telegram by noon tomorrow receives a billion dollars, provided that exactly one of them does, with any discussion ruled out (the answer, on my view, being to send one with probability one in twenty). I want the honest case against that position, ordered from what would genuinely sink it to what is merely awkward, together with an account of what it predicts that its rivals do not.

In particular: how much weight is carried by the undefined notion of two agents reasoning alike, and does any precise version of it either collapse into precommitment or confine the theory to games almost nobody ever faces? Is superrationality genuinely distinct from evidential decision theory, from team reasoning, or from a correlated equilibrium — and if it is not, is that fatal or merely tidy? Which counterexamples bite hardest, and which would a superrationalist cheerfully concede? I would be most moved by a demonstration that superrational play is self-undermining over repeated encounters, or that its central recommendation is unstable under arbitrarily small asymmetries; tell me whether such demonstrations exist or can be constructed.

Finally, what should I make of the Luring Lottery, which Scientific American actually ran in 1983: readers posted a card bearing the number of entries they claimed, the prize being a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, and several entered a googolplex while others wrote expressions designed to evaluate to the largest number that would fit, so that the prize became a vanishing fraction of a cent and no winner could be identified. Does that refute the theory, or only show that people are not superrational — and is the second reply too cheap to serve as a defence? Be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated by qualification, and to say whether that is or is not a problem for a view of this kind. Please answer in British English.
