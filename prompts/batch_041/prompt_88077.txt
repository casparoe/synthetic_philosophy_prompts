Over the past year, mostly while grading essays for a large introductory course, I have been collecting a kind of sentence that I cannot classify, and I would like to know whether it has a name — and, more to the point, whether the name covers everything I have collected. The specimens share a shape: the first half commits the speaker to something, and the second half, in the same breath, disowns the commitment. A colleague writes that the grant will come through, and adds that he does not believe it will. A student tells me she has finished the chapter, and then that she is not sure she thinks she has. I once wrote in a diary that I was going to leave the department and, a line later, that I had no intention of leaving it. Nothing here is contradictory in the way a sentence and its negation are: I can describe a person of whom both halves are true without any strain, and it is only when the person described is the one speaking that the thing becomes unsayable — and even then not always. The oddness vanishes if I move it into the past tense, or into the second or third person, where the same content is merely sad or merely gossip; it is milder in a private notebook than in a memo; and it survives, in some form, when I replace 'believe' with 'know', 'remember', or 'be certain', though I cannot tell whether in the same form. Two cases have defeated me. First, when I substitute a group for the individual — the committee has concluded that the building is sound, though none of us believes it is — the sentence becomes perfectly usable, which puzzles me, since I do not know what a committee's concluding something amounts to if not the conclusions of its members. Second, I cannot decide whether 'I will have the draft to you on Friday, though I have no intention of finishing it' belongs with the others or with something else, or how it stands to a promise made in bad faith; the pull to call it a malfunction is the same, the diagnosis, I suspect, is not.

I know the neighbouring territory well enough to know that none of it is quite my quarry. I know the self-referential paradoxes, where a sentence contrives to undermine itself, and I know what people mean when they call an utterance a 'performative contradiction' — the relativist who asserts his relativism, the sceptic who argues that no one can argue. In my specimens nothing refers to itself and nothing contradicts anything: the speaker may be right on both counts, and yet the sentence is not one a person can be in a position to utter. I have a nagging sense that there is a name for this that I read once and have since lost, and I am asking because I want more than the name. What I want is the joints. What exactly is defective here — is the defect in the meaning of the sentence or in the act of uttering it? Why should the defect track tense, person, register, and the verb in the second half, when it does not track whether the two halves can both be true? Are the 'know', 'remember', and 'intend' versions the same phenomenon, or a family of cousins, and where is the boundary that separates them? What becomes of the phenomenon when the speaker is a group? And is there anything to be learned from the fact that the defect disappears in the past tense, or is that a red herring? If there is one thing here with one name, give me the name and the boundary of the thing it names; if I have been sweeping several things into one pile, draw the lines and tell me which of them, if any, has never been named. I am not asking for a reading list; I would rather have the analysis.
