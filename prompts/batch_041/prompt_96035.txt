Ok so I need help untangling an argument a friend in my dept keeps hitting me with, and I cant find the leak. He's an unabashed aggregator -- the fifty-years-of-torture-versus-a-huge-number-of-people-each-getting-one-dust-speck-in-the-eye thing is his home turf -- and last week he laid this out on a napkin:

(1) One dust speck in the eye for a moment makes a life go slightly worse than it otherwise would have. Very small, but it's a harm.
(2) There's no harm so small that enough of them can't outweigh a large harm. Refusing this is just refusing to count.
(3) "Worse than" is transitive.
(4) Now set the specks aside. Take one second of mild discomfort -- a stubbed toe, a wave of nausea.
(5) That second can be carved into as many sub-intervals as you like. A thousand, a billion, more.
(6) Each sub-interval contains some discomfort, so by (1) each one is a harm.
(7) By (2), enough of them outweigh anything at all, and nothing stops you from making as many as you need.
(8) So one second of mild discomfort is worse than fifty years of torture.
(9) Which is absurd. So one of the steps above is false.

I'm not giving up (2), because if I do I don't see how the dust-speck case is supposed to get off the ground at all. But (5)-(7) feels like a magic trick, and every time I try to say why, the sentence that comes out is "you can't count the parts of a discomfort as separate harms," which sounds like I'm just asserting that tiny harms aren't harms -- the thing under dispute. So: is the sleight of hand in the slicing, or is it deeper, down in (2) where I want it not to be? Name the step I should drop, then price it for me -- especially what it costs me about dust specks.
