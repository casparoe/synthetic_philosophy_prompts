My friend and I got into this stupid-long argument the other night — like three hours, past midnight — about whether the stuff we casually talk about actually exists. Tables, obviously yes. But then countries, companies, the number seven, a song, a person's "self." She kept saying I was assuming the question even makes sense, and every time I pinned down an answer she'd show me how the opposite answer was just as good. I went to bed genuinely rattled. Now I want to do that to my students.

So: I run a 75-minute session, about 14 undergrads, mixed majors, in a course that brushes up against Buddhist philosophy but they've had basically zero exposure to it. I want an activity where they feel the force of this exhaustion themselves — where they run into it, before I name anything, and ideally the name doesn't come up till the debrief.

My half-baked idea, which you should feel free to gut and rebuild: I tape up four corners of the room, labeled It exists / It doesn't exist / It both exists and doesn't / It neither exists nor doesn't. I read out a case, everybody walks to a corner and stands there. Then I hit that corner with an objection, and whoever can't answer it has to physically move to a different corner. New case, repeat, harder. I want them to notice they keep migrating, that nobody can settle, and that most of them end up cycling through all four corners before the hour's out. Then the debrief maps each corner onto a position and each objection onto the argument that makes that corner wobble.

What I actually need from you:

- Four or five cases, in order, getting harder. I want at least one physical object, one that exists only by agreement (a border, a corporation, a game — something like that), one fictional thing, and one that's a process rather than a thing (a wave, a flame, a note being sung). The last one has to be a case where even the "neither" corner gets uncomfortable, so nobody can just camp there and feel clever.
- The exact objections I throw at each corner, phrased so each one pushes you toward one of the other corners rather than just being a bland "why?" I want them to feel the tug to move instead of being told to.
- A rule that keeps the sharp ones from winning by sheer stubbornness. I was thinking: you're allowed to defend your corner, but only once per case, and the defense has to be worded differently from your last one — so holding a position costs you something instead of earning you points. Something in that spirit, or better.
- Roles or a scoring scheme if it needs one (I don't want real money, obviously). Materials, a timeline that fits 75 minutes, and how many rounds I can realistically get through.
- A debrief that ties specific moments in the activity to the actual structure — I want them to be able to point at "the moment half the room defected from Both to Neither" and see what that was. And name the thing properly here, not earlier.
- What predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each. I already suspect someone will declare it all relativism, someone else will get genuinely upset, and a couple will treat it as a debate to win.
- Be honest about what the activity *can't* do. I have a feeling it can manufacture the vertigo but not whatever comes after it — the part where you're supposed to be fine with things existing conventionally and that's not a cop-out. If that's something I have to just say out loud, tell me, and tell me how to say it without it landing as a lame "and then everyone clapped" ending.

No bibliography, no reading list — I don't need sources, I need it to actually run. Also no lecture up front, I mean it, they should be confused and moving around before they know what hit them.
