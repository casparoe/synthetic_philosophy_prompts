I'm a philosophy professor about to retire, and the department has asked me to give the commencement address at our little graduation ceremony this spring — the one where maybe forty students walk and their families fill a small hall. Here's my problem. I've been a Quinean my whole career, which for me means there just aren't any objective moral values sitting out there in the world for me to point the graduates toward. No mind-independent facts about what anyone ought to do. But a commencement address is basically one long string of "oughts," and I refuse to cheat — I don't want to stand up there mouthing moral truths I privately think are fictions just because it's expected of me. At the same time I can't turn it into a metaethics lecture; the room is split. Half of them are my colleagues and two or three sharp grad students who know exactly what I mean when I say values aren't in the catalogue of what there is. The other half are parents, grandparents, aunts, people who've driven four hours and want to hear something warm and, frankly, true-sounding.

So write me the adress. Give it to me as exactly five numbered movements — just the speech itself, no intro, no outro, nothing wrapped around it. And be honest with me rather than polite: if a Quinean commencement address is actually a contradiction in terms and can't be done without bad faith, or if I've got my own position muddled (a colleague once insisted Quine wasn't really a non-cognitivist so much as a naturalist about norms who just treated ethical talk as another region of the web — am I wrong about him?), then say so straight instead of humoring me and writing it anyway. I'd rather hear it now than find out on the podium.
