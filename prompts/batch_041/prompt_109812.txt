Please act as a historian of early modern philosophy with a secondary specialization in philosophy of art and in theories of personal identity, and help me work through a difficult stretch of Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Spectacles. I am reconstructing the argument from a general grasp of the whole rather than from a text in front of me, so what follows is my own paraphrase of the relevant passage and not a quotation; please treat it as a reconstruction to be corrected wherever it misrepresents the original, and tell me plainly if you think I have misremembered the shape of the argument.

The paraphrase. Rousseau is asking whether a theatre ought to be established in Geneva, and in the course of the argument he pauses over the profession of the actor. His claim, as I read it, is that the actor's art is precisely the art of counterfeiting himself: of putting on a character other than his own, of appearing in public as someone he is not, of rendering the passions of another person so vividly that the audience takes them for his own, while he himself remains composed and calculating behind them. He then argues that this art differs from the other imitative arts. A painter or a poet imitates with materials external to himself, but the actor's instrument is his own person, his face, his voice, his body, his capacity for feeling. From this he draws the conclusion that the actor cannot confine the counterfeiting to the stage: the habit of feigning passion, of possessing a self manufactured for the eyes of others, must seep into the man himself, so that the actor ends by being a professional deceiver even in private life. He supports this with a historical observation, that the profession has long been treated as infamous, and that the Church, which tolerated many disreputable trades, refused the actor the sacraments and burial.

Here is where I get stuck, and where I would like help.

First, this looks like an argument that presupposes a fairly strong thesis about the self: that a person has one authentic character, that the deliberate and repeated performance of another character threatens or hollows out that authentic character, and that the threat is specific to acting because the actor's instrument is himself. Rousseau does not, as far as I can see, state such a theory anywhere in the text. Is it there implicitly? If so, what exactly is it, and is it coherent? If not, what is the argument really resting on: a causal-psychological claim about the effects of habit, a claim about the social meaning of the profession, a claim about the nature of representation, or something else?

Second, the instrument argument seems either to prove too little or to prove too much. If the danger is that the self is used as an instrument, then the singer, the dancer, the preacher, the teacher, and the orator all use themselves as instruments, and Rousseau admires at least some of these. If the danger is the feigning of passion, then the novelist feigns too, and Rousseau is a novelist. Which premise is doing the real work, and can the argument be made to cut against actors alone?

Third, and most troubling to me, Rousseau elsewhere in the same work praises the public festival, at which the citizens are at once spectators and performers, dancing and competing in the open air rather than watching a spectacle staged by others. But if the danger lies in the gap between the performing self and the real self, the festival would appear to be a mass exercise in exactly the self-presentation he condemns, merely with a more wholesome content. If instead the danger lies in something about the institution of the theatre — the box office, the separation of audience from performer, the professionalization of pleasure, the need to flatter a paying public — then the critique of the actor looks like rhetoric hung on an institutional argument. Which reading fits the text better?

Fourth, I notice that the claim that the dramatist must please his public, and therefore can only flatter and never correct the mores the public already has, would seem to apply to Rousseau himself, who is addressing the public of Geneva and trying to move it. Does the argument about the theatre's inability to improve us prove too much against the Letter itself? Is there a principled distinction between the playwright's flattery and the moralist's persuasion, or is Rousseau implicitly relying on the difference between a commercial spectacle and a civic appeal, or between fiction and argument?

What I would like from you, in order: (1) a careful reconstruction of the argument of the passage, distinguishing its premises and marking which are essential and which are rhetorical; (2) an explicit statement of the theory of the self, if any, that the actor argument requires, with your assessment of its plausibility; (3) an account of how the critique of the actor relates to the praise of the festival — whether they are consistent, and under what reading; (4) whether the argument can be restated so that it survives without the strong selfhood thesis, and what would be lost or gained in the restatement; and (5) the strongest objection to Rousseau's position here, together with his best reply on his own behalf. Please begin by brainstorming the possible readings before settling on one, and be explicit about which parts of my paraphrase you take to misrepresent the text.
