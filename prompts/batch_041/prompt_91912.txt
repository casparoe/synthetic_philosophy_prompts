I'm in a political theory seminar that keeps approaching the same question from three directions — the German constitutional tradition of informational self-determination, the American distrust of the administrative state together with its broad tolerance for collecting data that has already passed through third parties, and the Chinese apparatus of social credit and trustworthiness scoring — and I've come to think that everyone in the room is quietly relying on a theory of when the state may legitimately acquire, keep, and use information about its citizens without ever saying what such a theory would have to do. Before we read another account, I want the specification on paper. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of legitimate state information-gathering must deliver. Give me the cases it has to get right — the census and the tax return, without which no modern state functions; the criminal investigation built on evidence nobody consented to produce; the welfare file assembled from a lifetime of encounters with agencies; the commercial data a citizen hands over freely to a company and the state then buys or compels; the wholly innocent person who genuinely has nothing to hide and can still be wronged; the sharp divergence among German, American, and Chinese arrangements on nearly identical facts — and the distinctions it must not collapse (secrecy versus privacy versus autonomy; collection versus retention versus use versus onward disclosure; a record that is harmless on its own and potent in aggregate; what a person chooses to reveal versus what is compelled from her; the state acting as protector versus the state acting as threat). Then the constraints from how states actually behave that the theory cannot contradict: that mass data collection is a precondition of taxation, public health, and welfare administration; that the state is not a counterparty a citizen can refuse or bargain with; that otherwise similar liberal democracies reach very different conclusions here. For each requirement, say what is doing the work — which case or consideration forces it — and mark which are genuine constraints on any theory and which are the commitments of one tradition dressed up as requirements.

Two things I most want to see. First, whether some subset of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied: I suspect that any theory grounding privacy in individual consent, or in a property right over one's data, will fail to explain the wrong done to someone who sincerely does not care about being watched, and if so I want that recorded as a result of the list rather than papered over. Second, which single requirement does the most to eliminate the popular candidates — the nothing-to-hide line, Mill-style harm accounts, and the view that privacy is nothing more than a bundle of context-specific norms — and whether that requirement is one any theory can actually meet. Skip the scoring of the three regimes; I'll do that in class.

And please push back if the task is the wrong shape. If how much citizens should hide from the state is really three questions — one about the ethics of concealment, one about the legitimacy of state collection, and one about the design of institutions — rather than one, or if a single theory spanning these three regimes is a category mistake, say so plainly instead of assembling a list to fit the framing. American English, please.
