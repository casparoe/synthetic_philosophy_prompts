You are writing the after-dinner address I will deliver at the close of the annual assembly of the Fairhaven Health Trust, a mid-sized foundation that funds maternal and child health in a dozen low-income countries. The room divides roughly evenly into two groups with no shared vocabulary. One group is trustees and major donors, nearly all economists or former finance people, who hear any appeal to equality as an appeal to make someone worse off and will say so before the coffee is poured. The other is field directors and community health workers, most of them from the regions where we work, for whom the purpose of the work has never been the total number of lives saved but the fact that the people they serve should stop being the ones at the bottom of every table.

A live proposal must also be addressed, because the board votes in the morning. A district council in one of our partner regions has asked us to fund what it calls a Parity Initiative. It would improve no one's health: no clinic, no drug, no clean water. Its budget would go to an annual published ranking of the region's districts by maternal mortality, and to a premium paid to whichever districts rank worst, on condition that the premium is spent on the ranking and its ceremony — a plaque, a hall, the cost of an audit. The council argues that the ranking is what gives the worst-off districts standing at the tables where budgets are actually set, and that such standing, sustained over years, is worth more than any clinic we could buy this season. The trustees regard the proposal as a scheme for making everyone worse off in the name of equality and intend to vote it down. The council's representatives will be in the room and will hear the vote as a verdict on whether their standing counts.

Write the address: about twelve minutes spoken, in my voice — plain, unhurried, faintly wry, no jargon, no footnotes, no borrowed authority. Do not cite any work, name any living person, or quote anyone; if the argument needs a distinction, make it in my own words. The words prioritarian and leveling down should not appear at all; the board has worn them out and the room will flinch. The philosophical constraint is the real work of the speech. It must give a coherent account of why the value of equality is not the value of a ranking — why it is never a reason to bring anyone down — without thereby conceding the trustees' conclusion that only absolute totals matter. It must therefore show the sense in which the field directors are right and the sense in which the trustees are right, and show why that distinction, rather than a split-the-difference compromise, settles the Parity Initiative. Neither half of the room may be treated as a caricature: the field directors will notice at once if they are being humored, and the trustees will notice if the argument quietly substitutes sentiment for cost. Close by stating plainly what I will recommend to the board in the morning, and give me one sentence I can say to the council's representatives if the recommendation is the one they will not want to hear.
