I am assembling an instrument for a graduate seminar on decision theory under uncertainty: a decision tree that sorts a reader into the position they actually hold about the Ellsberg choices, rather than the position they would name if asked which model they favour. Students will use it on themselves and I will use it as a diagnostic for where a discussion has stalled, so the sorting must turn on what someone says about concrete choices and never on which label they like. Assume the reader knows the standard cases — the urn of known composition against the urn of unknown composition, and the three-colour version with its two pairwise comparisons — and can answer questions about them without being told what any answer implies.

Start the tree from a commitment shared by everyone in the debate, namely that the Ellsberg pattern is either to be accommodated by a theory of rational choice or explained away by one, with the reader not yet having decided which. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of model names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions that occupy those combinations. Every internal node must be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each answer must name its child node or leaf. Nodes may have two or three answers; where a node's answers can be combined rather than chosen among, say so and handle the combination explicitly instead of forcing a binary choice. Between nine and twelve leaves, and no branch more than five nodes below the root.

At a minimum the tree must sort on the following distinctions, each of which must appear as a labelled branch rather than as background commentary:

1. Whether the reader would retract the pattern on reflection, treating it as a performance error, an attention artefact, or a debunkable preference, or would retain it as a considered preference.
2. Whether the reader keeps or abandons the sure-thing principle — the principle that an act's ranking cannot be affected by altering what both acts pay off in states where they agree. The node should make clear, in the reader's own terms, what is at stake in giving the principle up, without telling the reader which way to go.
3. Two aversions that are routinely run together and that the tree must separate. The first is aversion to unknown odds as such, holding the act fixed: a preference for the urn of known composition when the payoff structure is otherwise identical. The second is aversion to the absence of hedging: a preference for a coin flip between two acts over either act alone, even when each act is unattractive on its own. A position may exhibit either without the other, so at least one node must be a case that pulls them apart, for instance a choice between a fair coin between two unattractive prospects and a single one of those prospects.
4. Whether the phenomenon is taken to be intrinsic to the event or relative to the source and to the comparison being made. The node should be a case in which the same objective probabilities are presented through two different sources, or in which the reader is first shown a clearly better or clearly worse source, the question being whether the preference moves.
5. Whether the reader treats the following two cases alike: an event whose probability is not well defined for anyone, such as a coin whose bias will be fixed by a future process nobody can observe, and an event whose probability is well defined but unknown to the reader, such as a coin already flipped but not yet revealed. A position that treats them alike and one that does not are different positions and should land on different leaves.
6. Whether the object of uncertainty is a single probability distribution, a set of them, a distribution over them, or a non-additive weighting of events — and, where the answer is a set or a distribution, whether the reader's attitude toward it is a separate item, such as a value attached to imprecision or a penalty on deviation from a reference probability, or is instead read off the shape of the functional itself.

Positions that must appear as leaves, described by their commitments rather than by author names: the view that keeps expected utility and the sure-thing principle and calls the pattern an error; the view that represents belief by a non-additive weighting of events, with the aversion appearing as convexity; the view that evaluates each act by its worst case over a set of admissible probabilities; the view that interpolates between best and worst case with a single index of attitude toward imprecision; the two-level view that combines a probability distribution over probabilities with a concave second-order utility; the robustness view on which priors far from a reference one carry a penalty whose weight is a single parameter; the adversarial view on which the unknown composition is best modelled as a choice made by a party whose interests oppose the reader's; the source- and comparison-based view on which the phenomenon is psychological and descriptive rather than a feature of rational preference; the view that the phenomenon is a heterogeneous family of effects for which no single representation should be sought; and, if the space admits it, a leaf for a position nobody has defended.

Two things must be reported honestly rather than smoothed over. First, every combination of answers that no position occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis of whether the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, every pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, with an account of why the standard cases fail to separate them and what further choice data would, including pairs that are distinct in name only because one is a special or limiting case of the other.

Return the instrument as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- `root`: string, the id of the root node.
- `nodes`: array of objects, each with `id` (string); `question` (string, phrased so a reader can answer it without knowing any position name); `cases` (array of strings, each a sentence describing a concrete choice situation the question turns on); and `answers` (array of objects, each with `answer` (string, the reader's reply in the reader's own terms), `leads_to` (string, the id of the child node or leaf), and `comment` (string or null, anything the reader needs in order to answer honestly, for instance a note that two answers are compatible rather than exclusive)).
- `leaves`: array of objects, each with `id` (string); `position` (string, a descriptive name, or `unnamed`); `commitment` (string, one sentence stating what the position holds); `awkward_commitment` (string, one sentence stating the commitment its defenders find hardest to keep); `reachable_via` (array of strings, the answer paths from the root that arrive here); and `probe_that_reveals_it` (string, the single choice situation that would most quickly tell an interviewer whether someone belongs here).
- `empty_combinations`: array of objects, each with `path` (array of strings, the sequence of answers), `diagnosis` (one of `incoherent`, `unattractive`, `unexplored`), and `explanation` (string, one or two sentences).
- `indistinguishable_pairs`: array of objects, each with `leaf_a` (string), `leaf_b` (string), `why` (string, why the tree's cases cannot separate them), and `separating_data` (string, what further choice data would, or a statement that nothing in the classic setup will).
- `design_note`: string, at most four sentences, on any distinction you wanted to use as a branch and could not, and what the instrument therefore leaves unresolved.

Do not supply citations, dates, or the names of authors. Where a position has a standard label, give it; where it does not, write `unnamed`. Do not reproduce anyone's text.
