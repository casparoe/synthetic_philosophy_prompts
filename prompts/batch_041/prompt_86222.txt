So two friends of mine, Dana and Milo, got into a long argument at our reading group last week and I definately couldn't tell who had the better of it. It was about Solomonoff induction and whether it actually justifies Occam's razor or just renames it. I'll try to reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can.

Dana: "Solomonoff finally gives us a real foundation for preferring simple hypotheses. You fix a universal machine, give every hypothesis a prior proportional to 2 to the minus its description length, and that's a universal prior. Short programs get exponentially more prior mass, and the posterior converges to the truth for any computable data source. So Occam's razor isn't some methodological preference we bolt on afterward, it's a theorem."

Milo: "I don't buy that it's a theorem about simplicity. The prior depends on which universal machine you pick. The invariance theorem only says two machines' complexity measures differ by an additive constant c, which is a multiplicative factor of 2^c between the priors. That constant never washes out at any finite stage. So for any hypothesis I care about, I can build a machine that makes it the short one. You've shown that some language favors short programs, not that the world does."

Dana: "The constant is bounded, and on any data source both machines can compute, the two predictors converge to the same limit, so the disagreement is transient. You can't get machine-independence for free, but bounded dependence is the most anyone could ask for. And the machines that matter -- the standard prefix-free ones -- agree on every hypothesis anyone would actually consider; the disagreement only bites for hypotheses so baroque that nobody would propose them."

Milo: "That last sentence is the problem. 'Nobody would propose them' is Occam's razor, used as a premise to show the machine-dependence doesn't matter, and then you turn around and conclude Occam's razor is justified. That's circular. Also, program length is only one sense of simplicity. Scientists usually mean fewer free parameters, or more symmetry, or a unified ontology, and nothing says those line up with short programs. And the convergence guarantee assumes the truth is computable. If the world isn't a computable process, the theorem says nothing."

Dana: "If the truth isn't computable then no method has a guarantee, so that's not a special problem for Solomonoff. And your point about other senses of simplicity cuts both ways: 'fewer free parameters' isn't even well-defined, whereas description length is. Better a precise notion that might diverge from scientists' intuitions than a vague one that matches them. As for the machine, you have to fix it in advance, and once you do, the constant is fixed. If you let yourself pick the machine after seeing the data, you're just overfitting, and that's not a flaw in the framework."

Milo: "Fine, fix it in advance -- but then what tells you to fix that one? Any reason you give is either arbitrary, or it's an appeal to simplicity again. And even granting the limit result, the rate of convergence can be arbitrarily slow, so at every finite stage the machine's constant can be doing the work. You've got a beautiful asymptotic story and no finite-time content."

And they just went in circles after that.
