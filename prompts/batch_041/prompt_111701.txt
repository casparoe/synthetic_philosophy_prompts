After thirty-five years in an experimental biophysics laboratory I retired, and I have spent the last several years reading philosophy of mind, first on my own and more recently in a departmental reading group. I am due to present my position to that group next month and would like an audit of it beforehand. I am a physicalist, and by both temperament and training I am drawn to computational explanations; I also think a science of cognition ought to be able to say what the brain computes. But I have been persuaded of a number of things that I suspect may not all sit comfortably together, and I would rather discover this now than in front of an audience.

Here is what I hold.

1. Cognitive capacities — perception, memory, inference, motor planning — are to be explained by specifying computations over representations. This is the correct level at which to conduct a science of cognition, not a placeholder for a neurophysiology that has not arrived yet.

2. For any physical system there exists a description under which it implements any finite-state automaton one cares to name. I regard this as established rather than as a rhetorical flourish: the physical facts about a system do not single out one computational description of it, so implementation is not a property that the physics fixes by itself.

3. A thinker's beliefs and experiences have determinate contents. What I mean when I mean that water is wet is not a matter of anyone's descriptive convenience, and it is not up for grabs.

4. What a system genuinely computes is fixed by what it was selected to do. A device with the causal profile of a visual cortex that came into being by chance — by erosion, say — performs no computations in the relevant sense, however exactly its parts mirror the cortex's.

5. What is computed, and why, can be characterized independently of the algorithm that computes it and of the machinery that runs the algorithm. A psychology at that level can in principle proceed without waiting on the details of the substrate.

6. I am a physicalist, and I take the brain's dynamics to be continuous. Discrete states are an idealization the modeler imposes on a system that does not come partitioned that way.

7. Phenomenal experience is a feature of certain computational processes and requires no ingredient beyond the physical; there is nothing left over for a further explanatory gap to be about.

8. None of this amounts to dualism. Whatever is true at the computational level is realized in the physical; the higher level is not an additional layer of fact.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these stand in genuine tension with which, ordered by severity? Which pairs only appear to conflict and can be reconciled at no cost? Where a tension is genuine, what is the cheapest revision that removes it, and what does that revision cost me elsewhere in the list? Finally, if a single change would clear up the greatest share of the trouble, tell me which one it is and what I lose by making it.

I have no stake in preserving any particular item. I want to know what the package costs, not to be told that it is consistent.
