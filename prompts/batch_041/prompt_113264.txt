I'm a science-and-ideas journalist at work on a long feature about the widening circle of animal-welfare law. Several jurisdictions have already extended their statutes to cephalopods and to some crustaceans, and the campaigners I keep interviewing now want to push the line further, to insects; the trouble is that the whole regulatory question seems to bottom out in a claim about the distribution of phenomenal consciousness across the animal kingdom, and almost everyone I put it to answers with a shrug or a slogan. So I'd like your actual credence in a fairly precise proposition: that phenomenal consciousness — that there is something it is like to be the creature — is present in at least some insects, and is therefore not confined to vertebrates. I don't want the reply to dissolve into "it depends which theory of consciousness you hold"; I want a number, and then I want the number taken apart.

Please decompose it into at least the following terms, with a separate figure and a one-line justification for each: first, your credence in each of the main families of theory about the neural basis of consciousness, naming the families you're actually using; second, for each family, the probability that an insect nervous system satisfies its conditions, given that the family is correct; third, the probability that the behavioral and functional evidence usually marshalled for insects — flexible learning, motivational trade-offs between a noxious stimulus and a reward, responses that don't look reflexive — tracks phenomenal consciousness rather than mere information processing; and fourth, the probability that consciousness comes in degrees rather than being all-or-nothing, since that changes what "present in insects" could even mean. There is one further term I keep turning over and cannot settle, and I'd like it in explicitly: the probability that "phenomenal consciousness" picks out a single natural kind shared across species at all, as opposed to a cluster concept whose extension shifts depending on which cluster of features you privilege. If it is the latter, then "where is the boundary?" may have no determinate answer, and I want that possibility reflected in your bottom line rather than quietly assumed away.

Then show me how the terms combine into the single figure, tell me which term the result is most sensitive to, and tell me which one experiment or one theoretical result would move your number most, and in which direction. My own number hovers around 0.3 and I couldn't defend it to save my life. Please give the reasoning in ordinary prose, and end with your final credence as a decimal between 0 and 1 in the form \answer{0.xx}.
