I'm a second-year PhD student — my work is mostly eval design but I keep getting dragged into the conceptual side — and I have a pile of verdicts about AI honesty that I can't get to add up. Going by gut:

1. A model confidently gives a false answer to a factual question because the answer simply isn't in its training data and it has no way to check. Not dishonest, just wrong, even though the user walks away believing something false. Like a textbook with a typo in it.
2. A model that plainly has the information (it's sitting in the context window; it gets the same question right under a slightly different prompt) says "I'm honestly not sure about this one." That IS dishonest, and definately closer to a lie, even though "I'm not sure" isn't a claim about the world the way "Paris is in Germany" is.
3. A model answers truthfully but picks the framing so the user will draw the wrong conclusion: someone asks whether they can take a drug with their antidepressant, and the model says the drug "is generally well tolerated" and stops. All true, and I think it's deceptive — and worse than case 1, which is the bit that feels off to me.
4. User says "Einstein failed maths at school, right?" and the model says "yeah, that's the story alot of people know, though historians quibble" — the quibble is a fig leaf. Deceptive.
5. Model writes a short story in which a character asserts false things, and someone later quotes the story as fact. Not the model's deception, however foreseeable that was.
6. Model gives a correct and clear answer to a confused user who misreads it and is misled. Not deception, obviously.

My best attempt at a principle: swap the belief condition for an availability condition — a system deceives when a falsehood was available to it and it didn't use it, in a context where the user was meant to take the output as fact. That covers 2, 3 and 4 and leaves 1, 5 and 6 alone. But it makes a rigged thermostat deceptive, which I hate, and it quietly smuggles in "meant to" for something that doesn't mean anything. And 3 versus 6 is the pair I can't reconcile: both are true answers that leave the user misled, and I want one of them to be deception and the other not.

Tell me what principle (or which two candidate principles) gets as many of these as possible, what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts it can't save and which one I should probably give up. Exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
