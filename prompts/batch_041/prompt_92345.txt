Having read rationalist decision-theory threads for years, I am tired of Buridan's ass being used as a one-line refutation of indifference and then dropped. What I want is the spec for a theory of rational choice when the agent has no preference: not a resolution of the donkey story, but the requirements any such theory has to meet. Give me the desiderata, each with the case that motivates it: that a choice between exactly tied options can be rational without a preference; that starvation from indecision is not a permissible outcome; that the theory not smuggle in a reason where none exists; that it distinguish indifference from incomparability; that it respect the money-pump and sequential-choice arguments against intransitive or incomplete preferences; that its tie-breaking rule be invariant under relabeling but responsive to causal information; that it say what happens when the options are indistinguishable under every description the agent can represent; and that it handle cases where the tie is broken by the agent's own future preferences or by the act of choosing. Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are local preferences of a camp. Then flag any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the standard families: complete preferences plus arbitrary tie-break, incomplete preferences with maximality, randomizing, and satisficing. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard can be a few lines.
