I'm presenting at a joint session next week — the epistemology reading group has invited the motor-learning lab — and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the position I'll be defending. My thesis is that knowing how to φ is a genuine cognitive achievement but is not a species of propositional knowledge. What it is, I argue, is the possession of a reliable disposition to φ that is guided by a practical representation: a representation whose content is a procedure rather than a fact, and which can be assessed as correct or incorrect — on track or off track — without being true or false. The intellectualist is therefore right that know-how answers to a standard of correctness, and the Rylean is right that it is not a kind of knowing that; both are wrong to think those two claims exhaust the options. My central argument turns on what I call recalcitrant skills: skills that are reason-responsive — they improve when the right piece of advice is given mid-performance, and degrade when the agent tries to follow an explicit rule — yet whose exercise the agent cannot articulate, and in some cases cannot even recognize when it is described back to her afterwards. A surgeon's feel for when tissue is about to tear is my running example: she adjusts to the instruction to go lighter and wait in a way that shows she is tracking something, but no proposition she could entertain captures what she tracks.

The audience is the problem. The epistemologists are mostly intellectualists of the sort who hold that know-how just is knowledge of a proposition under a practical mode of presentation, and they will treat my practical representation as a proposition wearing a disguise. A couple of them work on the gradability of knowledge and will press on whether know-how comes in degrees and what my view says about that. The lab people are mostly enactivists and embodied-cognition researchers who distrust representation talk on principle, and they will say my practical representation is an explanatory fiction that does no work the bare disposition is not already doing. There is also one person in the room who studies automaticity and will have data on how expert performance actually degrades under verbal instruction; I cannot tell which side she will come down on, and I suspect her question is the dangerous one.

Give me the prep sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from these people, in their own vocabulary — where the epistemologists and the enactivists are making the same complaint in different words, say so and give me both phrasings. Order them by how much damage they would do, not by how likely they are. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than defend, which objection I should treat as the one that has to land for the view to survive the session, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
