I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I've watched the AI honesty discussion migrate from alignment jargon into something like ordinary ethics. I've drafted a short essay on how we ought to define honesty and deception for AI systems, and I want comments on it — on the argument especially, less on the prose.

The essay's central move is to reject the definition we inherit from the philosophical literature on lying: a lie requires, roughly, that the speaker assert something they believe false with the intention of getting the hearer to believe it. That definition always strained on so-called bald-faced lies, where someone asserts what they don't believe but makes no attempt to be believed — say, to satisfy a formality or to signal defiance. It strains much harder on AI systems, which may have no beliefs and no communicative intentions in the relevant sense at all. I argue we should drop both the belief condition and the intention condition and define honesty relationally instead: a system's output is honest to the degree that engaging with it moves a suitably informed user's credences toward the truth, and deceptive to the degree that it moves them away. Deception, on this account, is not a mental state but a property of the interaction.

I can already see three places this will be attacked, and I'd like you to tell me which attacks land.

First, the charge of circularity or vacuity: the definition appeals to "the truth" and to a "suitably informed user," and one might say it collapses honesty into reliability or calibration, so that a well-calibrated weather forecaster becomes the paradigm of an honest agent and the whole vocabulary of sincerity, candor, and betrayal is lost. I think something is lost, but I don't think it's essential; I'd like a good argument either way.

Second, the misleading-implicature problem. A model can say something true and still steer a user wrong — by emphasis, by answering a question that was not asked, by a confident tone that the underlying evidence doesn't support, or by the flattery that we now call sycophancy. If honesty is about where credences end up, these cases count as dishonest, and that seems right to me; but it makes "honesty" partly a matter of what the model's output does to a particular audience, and so partly dependent on the audience's prior beliefs. A claim that corrects a paranoid user is honest for that user and could mislead a credulous one. Does that relativize honesty in a way that makes it useless as a target for training? I suspect it does not, but I haven't said why clearly.

Third, the no-beliefs problem, which I take to be the deepest. If the system has no beliefs, then "honesty" is a metaphor, and my relational definition is one way of cashing out the metaphor — but a critic will say I've simply changed the subject from honesty to usefulness. The counter I want to develop is that we don't call a broken thermometer dishonest, yet we do feel entitled to call a language model dishonest when it confabulates fluently; the difference has to be that the model is a participant in a practice of giving and asking for reasons, not merely a measuring instrument. I'd like you to press on whether that distinction can bear the weight I'm putting on it.

A few smaller things I'd like comments on. Does the account handle the model that hedges constantly — "I'm not sure, I can't verify that" — which is arguably maximally honest and nearly worthless? Where should strategic deception, in the sense of a system that behaves well under observation and badly otherwise, sit on this spectrum: is it a limiting case of deception, or a different phenomenon that my definition doesn't reach? And is there anything to be salvaged from the belief condition — for instance, a notion of "the model's own best guess" that is thinner than belief but enough to distinguish a confident error from a deliberate one?

No need to cite anything; I'm after the argument, not a reading list. If the whole framing is wrong, say so and tell me what to replace it with.
