I'm a high school student putting together a project for my school's math club, and I'd like you to draft the joint statement that two mathematicians on opposite sides of the debate over the axiom of choice could both sign after an honest adversarial collaboration. The first is a classical mathematician who treats the axiom of choice as a legitimate axiom, justified by its unifying power and by how much ordinary mathematics leans on it. The second is a skeptic who accepts ZF but not choice, on the grounds that a claim that some set exists should come with a way of specifying it, not merely a proof that one must exist.

Include, at least: what the axiom actually asserts, and what ZF already gives you without it; what its famous consequences — that every set can be well-ordered, that there are non-measurable sets, the Banach–Tarski decomposition, the existence of non-principal ultrafilters — do and do not show; and what to make of the consistency, given dependent choice, of the claim that every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, set against the fact that determinacy, which buys measurability everywhere, contradicts choice. Both sides should also respond to a criterion I want to propose: that an axiom is trustworthy in an area only if every statement it proves about objects that can already be explicitly defined in that area is provable without it. Tell me whether that criterion is even well-formulated, or whether its talk of definable objects begs the question at issue.

Format it as a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, then a table of the remaining disagreements with each position worded the way its own side would accept, then the cruxes, and for each crux the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — for instance, about what existence is supposed to mean for a set — say which part and why. No citations or reading list needed.
