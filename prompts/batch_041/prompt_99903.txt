I teach a 100-minute seminar on the ethics of the far future, about 16 upper-level students, and I want to run a game that makes them actually feel the fanaticism problem in their gut instead of just nodding at it. By fanaticism I mean the thing where a tiny probability of an astronomically huge amount of future value just steamrolls every other consideration in the expected-value calculus, so you end up concluding we should sink basically everything we've got into whatever nudges the odds of a good far future up by an eyelash. I want them to feel the pull of that first — to see why it's genuinely hard to resist on its own terms — and then feel what happens to that pull when you inject a credence that we're probably living in a simulation. Don't hand me a lecture on it. I want the arithmetic and the temptation to do the work, and the students to arrive at the fanatic conclusion themselves out of their own choices, before I name anything.

The whole thing should basically run itself off the rules. Round 1, the incentives should push them into the fanatic corner. Then a sealed envelope changes the parameters and they have to redo it in Round 2 and see how the ground moves under them — and ideally not all in the same direction, I want some of them to conclude the simulation stuff makes it worse. Don't use the words fanaticism or simulation in front of them until the debrief.

Format it like this, with these headings and roughly these lengths:

"Setup" — ~200 words. The room, the groups, the roles, the materials I need to prep. Keep it cheap and printable.

"Round 1: the rules" — ~300 words. The budget, the buckets they're allocating between, the payoff scheme, and how scoring works. Make the scoring legible enough that they can do the math themselves and argue about it.

"Why Round 1 goes fanatic" — ~200 words. Explain the mechanism, i.e. what feature of the payoff scheme is doing the work, so I can see it before I run it.

"The reveal" — ~150 words. What's in the envelope, when I hand it out, and how to phrase it so it lands as a physical fact about the world rather than as a new rule I'm imposing.

"Round 2" — ~250 words. What changes, what they re-decide, and how the scoring differs. I want the damping (or anti-damping) to come out of the mechanics, not out of my commentary.

"Debrief" — ~450 words. Map what happened in each round onto the actual philosophical moves: the fanaticism objection to longtermism, and the various distinct routes by which a simulation credence might blunt it or sharpen it. They should be able to see that "simulation damps fanaticism" is several different claims wearing one coat, and that some of them are much stronger than others.

"What'll go wrong the first time" — ~250 words. Honest failure modes and how to handle them.

"What the game can't show them" — ~150 words. Be frank about which part of the idea the mechanics can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply it myself.

One constraint: everything the students handle — the cards, the envelope, the scoring sheet — should stand on its own for someone who's never heard of any of this. No names of philosophers, no citations, nothing that requires having read anything beforehand. And please don't lean on any specific published version of the argument; if a claim is contested, build it so the students hit the contest themselves.
