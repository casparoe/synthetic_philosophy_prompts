ok so for my philosophy/psych elective we had to pick a topic for this thing our teacher calls an adversarial collaboration, where instead of having a debate you have to write the document the two sides would both actually put their names on. I got stuck with guilt vs shame (the assignment also wants us to get into whether a culture can choose which one it runs on) and I have two friends who have definately been fighting about this for three weeks. J is guilt-first -- guilt is obviously the better moral emotion, shame just makes people hide and any culture leaning on shame is being a little cruel. R is shame-first -- he says guilt is thin bookkeeping, it's a transaction you can pay off and stay the same person, whereas shame is about who you are, so it actually changes people and holds a community together. Could you write the joint statement they could both sign? I want: a numbered list of claims they both accept (about what each emotion is even tracking, what they do to behavior, and what's true of shame cultures and guilt cultures); then the exact places they still disagree, with each side's view written the way that side would want it worded, not some watered down middle; then the cruxes, like the sub-questions where if you answered it one way one of them would have to move; and for each crux a concrete case or piece of evidence they'd both agree actually bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down before we check. If you think part of their fight is just about words, say which part and why.

Also please use the rubric we came up with in class, because both of them already said it's fair, which is that a moral emotion is better the more it (a) aims at the thing that really deserves that reaction, (b) reliably gets people to repair or change instead of just hide, and (c) doesn't depend on someone watching or on beliefs we don't think are true. And make both of them do predictions on a case I made up, call it the empty stadium: you do something genuinely bad and no one ever finds out, no God, no imagined audience either. Does guilt still fire? Does shame go dark, and if it does, is that shame admitting it was never really about the wrongness, or is it just honest about being a social emotion? R keeps saying internalized shame is still shame and J keeps saying that's just guilt in a costume, so I think that's a crux. Then the second half is the culture-choosing bit -- can a society actually decide to go from shame-heavy to guilt-heavy, or is that not a thing you choose? They don't even agree on whether "a culture's dominant moral emotion" is a real thing or just an average over really different families, so make that a crux too, with each of them saying what they'd predict (schools? second-gen immigrants? whatever makes a good test).
