Background: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, but I fell into the Bayesian rabbit hole after reading too many blog posts, and one bit of it has been nagging me for weeks. The version I can actually do: you learn some fact for certain, you throw out all the worlds where it's false, renormalize, done. Then someone points out that you almost never learn a fact for certain — you learn that it's probably true, or you hear it from someone whose reliability you're guessing at — and there's a fix for that, Jeffrey conditionalization, I think the philosopher was Richard Jeffrey. Instead of learning E outright, you end up with new probabilities spread over a bunch of mutually exclusive options, and your new credence in any hypothesis is your old conditional credence in each option, weighted by its new probability. I've almost certainly butchered the statement of it, so correct me as you go. What bothers me is the fine print. It seems to need the options to be a real partition of something, and it holds fixed your old conditional probabilities of the hypothesis given each option — I've seen this called the rigidity assumption — which sounds like exactly the thing that ought to be up for grabs. And I gather that when what you learn is itself some claim about a conditional probability, or when the evidence is just a pile of stories that contradict eachother, the rule either goes silent or hands down an answer a lot of people think is obviously wrong. That's the state of my understanding; please fix it before you lean on it.

Here's why I'm asking. My film club did Rashomon last week and it's still rattling around in my head, and I volunteered to give the short intro talk when we run it again for new people. So: read the movie through this material. What I can't shake is that you get four flatly incompatible accounts of the same afternoon in the woods — the bandit's, the wife's, the dead samurai's through the medium, and finally the woodcutter's at the gate — and none of them plays like an honest mistake; each one is shaped by what the teller needs to be true about himself or herself. Meanwhile the three men sheltering from the rain are each trying to hold onto some general opinion about people, and every story knocks it around a little. What I want to know is whether the film is actually dramatizing updating on uncertain evidence in the technical sense — is there any point where a character could be described as doing something like the Jeffrey move — or whether it's showing a case where the machinery just doesn't apply, because the four accounts aren't a partition of anything (they overlap, they contradict, they aren't even answers to the same question), and because the thing you'd most want to estimate, how far to trust these people, is the very thing the evidence is about. Related: here the evidence isn't the events, it's the reports, and I'd like you to make something of that if it's a real distinction and not me being sloppy.

And please be straight with me about where the reading breaks down. Some of what the movie is doing — shame, self-deception, the weird class friction between the bandit and the samurai, the wife's position in all of it — probably isn't a probability story at all, and I'd rather say that in my talk than force the analogy and have someone call me out. The ending especially: the woodcutter, who has just admitted he stole the dagger from the scene, takes the abandoned baby home, and you can watch the priest's faith in people come back. Is that an update, is it a decision, and can the Bayesian even tell the difference there? No need for references, I'm not writing a paper — I just want the reading, plus a clear-eyed note on what it can't cover.
