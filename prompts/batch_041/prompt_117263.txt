Not an academic, just somebody who watches alot of movies and reads history on my own, and I've got something stuck in my head I can't get out.

I rewatched Schindler's List a few weeks ago and it sent me down a rabbit hole. I ended up reading about the Historikerstreit, that fight in West Germany in the 80s over whether the Holocaust was a one-off thing or whether it's fair to line it up next to other mass killings and compare them. Nolte and the people on his side said it wasn't unique, that it grew out of the same century of violence as everything else, and Habermas and his side said no, treating it as comparable relativizes it and lets Germany off the hook. Then I read Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust, where the argument is that the Holocaust wasn't some medieval barbarism that modernity was supposed to cure, it was a product of modernity: the bureaucracy, the factories, the paperwork, the division of labor into little pieces so that nobody's hands were ever actually dirty.

Here's what's bugging me. In the movie, what saves the Jews is a list. Typed up, filed, names on it. And what kills everybody else is also lists — the ghetto gets emptied through paperwork, and Schindler gets his people out by having Stern classify them as essential war workers and by bribing the right officials. So the exact same machinery that makes the killing possible is what makes the rescue possible. Does that blow up Bauman? If the apparatus is neutral and it just comes down to who's pulling the levers, then it isn't modernity's fault, it's people, and his whole thesis is wrong. Or is the movie secretly proving him right, because it takes one rich weirdo with nerve and a suitcase of money to fight the machine, and basically everybody else, including plenty of perfectly decent people, just shows up and does their job?

Then there's Göth shooting people off his balcony for no reason at all, which is the opposite of the cool, tidy, bureaucratic evil Bauman is describing. So which does the movie think explains it, the filing cabinet or the sadist? I honestly can't tell, and I'd like to know if that's me being dense or if the movie is actually torn about it.

And then the ending, the survivors and the actors walking up to his grave and putting stones on it, the whole thing built like a redemption story. Doesn't that put the movie on one side of the uniqueness fight? If the Holocaust can be told as a story where a good man saves a thousand people and everybody cries at the end, hasn't it become comparable to every other rescue movie ever made, which is definately the thing Habermas was scared of? Or is it fine because it really happened and the guy really did it?

So what I want is a reading of the movie through both of these, Bauman and the uniqueness fight, honest in both directions. Where does the movie back Bauman up, where does it undercut him, which side of the uniqueness argument does it land on if it lands on one at all, and what does it show that the arguments by themselves don't? And if I'm misremembering or misreading something, say so — I've seen it three times but the last time was years ago.
