I host a small philosophy-of-mind podcast, and next week's guest defends an uncompromising version of the multiple drafts model of consciousness: on her view there is no single locus or moment at which a content becomes conscious, and a question like whether the subject was conscious of the flash the moment it appeared, or only later once the brain had revised its own records, has no determinate answer, because the content is fixed by the brain's ongoing interpretive activity, which runs both forward and backward over its own traces. She insists, though, that this is neither quietism nor eliminativism in disguise. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press the substance of the position rather than inviting her to restate it. I want at least one question each on (i) how retrospective fixation of content can be true without retrocausation; (ii) the hidden-homunculus worry — if drafts get revised, something must do the revising, and it is not clear why that reviser is not the Cartesian theater under a new name; (iii) whether her appeal to a narrative center of gravity does any explanatory work that the bare claim that there are contentful states does not; (iv) how the view is supposed to differ from higher-order theories, given that her revisions look like higher-order interpretations; and (v) what empirical result would count against the no-determinate-time thesis. For each of those five, add the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with a question about what, if anything, the view implies where the timing of experience has practical stakes — for instance, when pain is said to begin under anesthesia.
