I'm an amateur birder who's fallen down a rabbit hole reading about what makes two populations the same species, and I've hit a shape of thing I can't find a name for. Picture a chain of populations strung in a ring around some barrier — a mountain range, a desert, the Arctic: each neighbouring pair interbreeds wherever the two meet, so by any interbreeding standard each neighbouring pair is one species. But the two populations at the far ends of the chain meet on the other side and don't interbreed at all, so they're plainly two species. So 'is the same species as' comes out reflexive and symmetric but not transitive: A is the same as B, B the same as C, all the way round, and yet A is not the same as Z. And it isn't just biology — it's the same shape as a chain of dialects where each village understands its neighbours but the two ends of the valley can't understand each other at all. What bugs me is that nobody treats either case as a contradiction; they just say the ends are different and carry on. Is there a name for this shape, a sameness relation that everyone assumes is an equivalence relation but isn't, and is the culprit vagueness, so it's really just the heap paradox over again, or something else, given that each step on its own looks perfectly determinate rather than borderline? If there's no established name, what would you call it, and where does it part company with the sorites, with ordinary disagreement about a borderline case, and with the thought that 'same' might be relative to what sort of thing you're talking about? And please seperate what's actually settled here from what's still genuinely argued about by people who work on it, rather than letting a contested view sound like the consensus.
