Please give me a careful conceptual explanation of what Bertrand's paradox actually demonstrates about the principle of indifference. I am not after a recitation of the three canonical answers as a curiosity; I want the underlying issue, and I want you to work with a distinction of mine along the way.

Set the paradox up in your own words: a chord is said to be drawn at random in a circle, and depending on whether one randomizes over the endpoints on the circumference, over the point where the chord meets a fixed radius, or over the midpoint across the whole disk, one arrives at probabilities of one third, one half, and one quarter that the chord is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Each procedure is uniform with respect to some natural parameter, and the bare demand that the chord be random does not by itself choose among them.

Now the distinction I would like you to use. Call outcome-indifference the move of assigning equal probability to a finite set of discrete, mutually exclusive outcomes that appear symmetric, as with the faces of a die. Call parameter-indifference the move of treating some continuous parameter as uniformly distributed. My working suspicion is that the paradox discredits parameter-indifference while leaving outcome-indifference intact, and that its deeper lesson is that a probability requires a physical chance setup — a symmetry or a randomizing mechanism — and cannot be conjured from ignorance alone.

Please say whether that suspicion holds up. In doing so, engage with two standard replies, which I state here in my own words rather than quoting. On the ill-posedness reply, the paradox merely shows that a random chord names no determinate chance setup, so there is no single answer to be right or wrong. On the invariance reply, one should require the answer to be invariant under the symmetries of the situation, a demand that does single out one of the three values. I would like to know which of the three positions — my suspicion, the ill-posedness reply, or the invariance reply — best captures what is going on; whether the invariance reply genuinely rescues the principle of indifference or quietly substitutes a physical assumption in its place; and what work, if any, the principle of indifference can still legitimately do once the paradox is properly understood.

At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict, and where in your reasoning you would least trust yourself.
