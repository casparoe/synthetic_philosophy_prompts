A museum I advise has adopted an internal Historical Certainty rubric for its ancient and colonial galleries. It assigns Tier 1 to events attested by at least one near-contemporary written source; Tier 2 to events attested only by later written sources that cite lost near-contemporary ones; Tier 3 to events preserved only in oral traditions recorded centuries afterward; and Tier 4 to events inferred solely from archaeology. Two further rules govern borderline cases: a single written source outranks several independent oral traditions, and silence in a written archive counts as evidence that the event did not occur. The curators are proud of the rubric but uneasy, because in the colonial collection it downgrades indigenous oral histories while upgrading a lone missionary journal, and because it treats the absence of writing as a defect in the evidence rather than a fact about who was writing. What philosophical commitments about testimony, memory, evidence, and historical knowledge does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the rubric strictly requires from what merely reflects a bias toward written records, say whether its parts are consistent and where it is underdetermined, and explain what would have to change if it were replaced by an account that asks whether a transmission process reliably preserves true beliefs rather than whether it is written.
