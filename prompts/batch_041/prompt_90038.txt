In my seminar on the ontological argument, a classmate keeps invoking the dictum that "existence is not a predicate," most recently to wave off Gödel's modal proof on the ground that it treats necessary existence as a positive property of individuals, which he says is simply the mistake Kant diagnosed. I suspect the slogan is being made to carry more weight than it can bear. Lay out the distinct precise claims "existence is not a predicate" could be making, from the trivial to the radical — for instance, that existence adds nothing to the concept of a thing; that it is not a great-making property; that it is not a first-order property of individuals at all but a second-order or quantificational notion; and whatever else the slogan has historically been made to mean. For each reading, say whether it is defensible, whether it is trivial or substantive, and whether it genuinely bears on Anselm's or Descartes's versions of the argument. Then say which of those readings, if any, tells against Gödel's proof, given that his argument derives God's necessary existence from the positivity of that property rather than counting existence among a concept's perfections. Give me the readings as a menu with verdicts, and close by saying which reading my classmate most likely has in mind.
