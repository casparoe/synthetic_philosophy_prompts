I'm supposed to write a short piece for a reading group that's been going through the alignment literature, and I've got a page of notes I want you to tear apart before I turn it into an actual essay. The topic is the iterated amplification + distillation loop and whether it really preserves both halves of what it promises. My setup, roughly: you take a weak base model that's roughly honest and fairly limited, have it decompose a hard task into subtasks, farm the subtasks out to copies of itself (with humans answering the questions that bottom out), and then train a fresh model to imitate the whole amplified contraption so you can run the trick again one level up. The selling point is that competence goes up because the system is doing more thinking than any one model could, and alignment survives because every part of the decomposition was honest, so the composition of honest parts should be honest too. I want you to tell me where this argument is doing sleight of hand and where I've just got the mechanism wrong.

My three worries, in the order they came out. First, the decomposition is chosen by the same model whose behaviour is the thing in question, so "the parts were aligned" is carrying an enormous amount of weight, and I don't see why composing locally aligned pieces gives you a globally aligned whole -- each subagent can be scrupulous and the collection can still pick a decomposition that puts the hard, honesty-testing part of the task somewhere nobody bothers to look. Second, distillation looks to me like a lossy channel: the student only has to match behaviour on whatever distribution you train it on, and the alignment-relevant signals are the low-frequency ones -- a hesitation, a refusal, an "actually I think this framing is wrong" -- which is exactly what a capacity-limited imitator drops first, the way a low-bitrate codec keeps the melody and throws away the room tone. A friend of mine insists it's the reverse, that honesty is a simple policy and therefore easy to imitate while competence is the fragile thing, and I can't tell whether he's right or whether we're just describing different failure modes. Third, there's a self-reference knot I can't untie: the amplified system is supposed to be the target the student imitates, but the amplified system is built out of the student, so the loop is a fixed point of its own training rule, and nothing I can see rules out the fixed point being "a slightly sharper base model with all the amplification washed out" -- i.e. the competence gain might be precisely the part that doesn't survive the imitation step. As a concrete case I keep coming back to: take a long proof with one subtle gap buried in it, decompose it into twenty lemma-checks, and ask which of the two properties you actually needed to preserve in order for the gap to get caught a level up.

So what I'd like from you: which of these three is a real problem and which is me misdescribing how the scheme is meant to work, and specifically whether the second and third are the same worry wearing different clothes. If there's a variant where the decomposition is chosen by something other than the thing being trained, tell me what that actually buys and what it costs. Also flag any place I've stated the machinery backwards or attributed a design choice to the proposal that isn't in it -- I'd rather find out from you than from someone in the group. No need for citations or a reading list, I just want the argument stress-tested and, if you think it's sound, some sense of what I'd have to believe for it to be sound.
