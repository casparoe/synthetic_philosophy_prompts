In three weeks I present a paper at a small workshop on epistemic utility theory, and what I need from you is not a literature review but a Q&A prep sheet. Assume I know the field; I have no interest in citations or reading lists, and nothing you tell me should depend on being able to look anything up.

My thesis: accuracy-first epistemology is a theory of coherence masquerading as a theory of rationality. Its flagship results — the dominance argument that incoherent credence functions are accuracy-dominated, the derivation of the chance-credence principle as an expected-accuracy-maximisation result, and the treatment of conditionalisation as accuracy-maximisation — are all comparative: they say that one credence function does better than another by the lights of a stipulated inaccuracy measure. Nothing internal to the programme establishes that any inaccuracy measure is the epistemically privileged one, and the only adequacy constraint it imposes on such measures, strict propriety, is satisfied by a continuum of measures that disagree with each other about which credence functions are better than which and about which of the programme's flagship results survive.

The new element in the paper is a distinction I draw between thin and thick inaccuracy measures. Thin measures score a credence function proposition by proposition, against the truth values of those propositions, and can be motivated without presupposing any particular theory of epistemic value. Thick measures score the credence function as a whole against the world in a way that is not decomposable, and they are the ones the programme's most striking results actually need. The dilemma I press is that the programme can have measures it can independently motivate, or it can have its flagship results, but not both. I concede at the outset that I am not a sceptic about formal epistemology; I think accuracy-first is the best research programme in the area, and my complaint is about the kind of claim it can support.

The audience is twenty or so people, and you should write the objections in the mouths of these factions, because they will not put them the same way:

- Six or seven accuracy-first insiders: formal epistemologists who work on scoring rules, accept the dominance argument for probabilism, and treat strict propriety as the central adequacy constraint. They will attack the dilemma on technical ground and will want to know exactly which results I claim require thick measures.
- Two defenders of imprecise credences, who use accuracy-based arguments themselves and will suspect my argument is really an argument for them.
- One statistician who works on proper scoring rules and elicitation, thinks of the choice of score as an engineering decision tied to a task, and will be impatient with the whole framing.
- Three or four epistemologists outside the programme — one knowledge-first, one who works on the value of understanding rather than of true credence — who will say the argument is beside the point because credences are not the locus of epistemic evaluation at all.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely each is times how much damage it does, each phrased the way that faction would phrase it and tagged with who will raise it. For each: a two-minute reply; a note on where that reply is soft, meaning the point at which, if they push, I have to concede something or change the subject; and a judgment on whether the objection is a genuine problem or a turf dispute I can name as such and move past. Tell me what to concede up front, before the first question, so that the concessions are mine rather than extracted. Then give me the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session, and finally the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
