Last weekend a friend and I argued for the better part of a day about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and we ended up unable to tell whether we were disagreeing about the mathematics, about what the mathematics is permitted to conclude, or about what a moral argument may presuppose. I would like you to adjudicate, and I would like you to do so in a way that commits you.

Please take the role of a philosopher of economics and decision theorist with an expert-level command of social choice theory and welfare economics, and reason carefully rather than quickly. Before assigning any numbers, spend a first section brainstorming and laying out the sub-questions on which the answer turns, so that I can see the joints of the argument; then, and only then, commit to the numbers.

My friend's position is that Harsanyi's theorem settles the matter in favor of utilitarianism. Roughly: if each individual's preferences are represented by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function, if social preferences are themselves coherent under uncertainty, and if the social ranking is welfarist — a function of individual utilities alone — and respects a Pareto condition, then the social welfare function must be a positive weighted sum of individual utilities; and the separate impartial-observer argument, run from behind a veil of ignorance, is supposed to fix the weights equally, so that distributive justice just is the maximization of total or average utility. My position is that the theorem proves something much narrower, and that most of the normative work is done by assumptions that are either independently implausible or quietly assume the conclusion. We cannot get past this, and I suspect we are arguing at cross purposes.

Give me your credence that the following proposition is true: that Harsanyi's theorem, understood as above and combined with the impartial-observer argument, is a sound argument for utilitarianism as the correct theory of distributive justice. I want a number, and I want the derivation decomposed, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each of the following, plus any terms you think I have left out:

- whether the theorem's formal assumptions are jointly acceptable, especially the requirement that social preferences themselves obey the expected-utility axioms — the sure-thing principle imposed at the level of the group rather than the individual — and the welfarist restriction that the social ranking depend on individual utilities and on nothing else;
- whether the theorem delivers utilitarianism or only the additive form with the weights left free, so that the equal-weights step has to be supplied by the separate impartial-observer argument;
- whether the equiprobability premise at the heart of that argument — that behind the veil one must regard being any particular person as equally likely — is defensible or question-begging;
- whether a social ranking built out of individual expected utilities can even register the thought that an equal-chance lottery is fairer than handing an indivisible good to one of two equally situated people who have equal claims to it, a thought I keep pressing on my friend and that he keeps telling me the formalism is under no obligation to capture;
- whether the interpersonal comparability of utility that a weighted sum presupposes is defensible;
- whether a theorem about preferences over lotteries can be carried over at all to the moral question of how to weigh persons against one another.

Then combine the terms into a single number, tell me which term the number is most sensitive to, and tell me what argument or evidence would move it most. My friend also insists that any attempt to escape the conclusion by replacing the sum with a concave transform of individual utilities — prioritarianism, in effect — is merely utilitarianism with a different utility scale; I want your verdict on that claim too, and it should bear on one of your terms.

For calibration: my credence is about 0.3 and his is about 0.85. Tell me where the disagreement between us most likely lives, and which of us is mislocating it.

I do not want a neutral survey of the positions on offer. Take a definite position, choose the numbers you actually believe, and defend them; where you think my framing of the question is the real problem, say so and then answer the question you think I should have asked — but still give me a number.
