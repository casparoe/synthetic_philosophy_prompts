Consider a game played with a fair coin tossed until the first head appears. If the first head falls on toss n, the payoff is 2^n / n pounds when n is odd and minus 2^n / n pounds when n is even, so that the payoffs run +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, −64/6, and so on, with outcome n carrying probability 2^−n. This game is sometimes called the Pasadena game. It has no expectation in the Lebesgue sense: the probability-weighted positive payoffs sum to +∞ and the probability-weighted negative payoffs sum to −∞. If instead one sums the outcomes in the natural order of n, the weighted payoffs form the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges to the natural logarithm of 2, roughly 0.693; but that series is only conditionally convergent, so a rearrangement of its terms can be made to converge to any real value at all, or to diverge. Adding one pound to every payoff produces a variant, sometimes called the Altadena game, whose natural-order expectation is 1 + ln 2, and which pays more than the original in every single outcome, yet which is equally without a Lebesgue expectation. The St Petersburg game, paying 2^n on toss n, is the older and better-known relative of these puzzles.

Write an essay on the following controversy: does the Pasadena game have a well-defined expected value, and does the answer settle what a rational agent should do when offered the chance to play it? Weigh the principal positions. One holds that the expectation is simply undefined, so expected-utility theory issues no verdict, and the case is either a counterexample to that theory's adequacy or a demonstration that rationality is silent here. Another holds that the expectation is ln 2, because the ordering of outcomes by n is not an arbitrary rearrangement but belongs to the specification of the game, so the rearrangement objection is beside the point. A third holds that although the strict measure-theoretic expectation is undefined, there is a privileged limiting quantity — the limit, as the game is truncated at ever later tosses, of the expectations of the truncated games — and that this is what rational choice should track. A fourth treats the undefinedness as a defect of the theory rather than of the game, and looks to extend or replace expected-utility theory with dominance reasoning, relative expectations, incomplete preference orderings, or some other device. A fifth denies that rational preference must be complete over all prospects, and reads the case as evidence for that denial.

Reach and defend a verdict. Say whether the game has a well-defined expected value, and under which notion of expectation; whether an agent should accept it in exchange for a sure £0.50, £0.70, or £1,000; and whether the Altadena variant, which dominates the Pasadena game pointwise, forces a verdict where the Pasadena game alone does not. Address the variant in which the payoffs are specified directly in units of utility rather than money, so that diminishing marginal utility cannot be invoked; say whether your verdict changes. Say also whether the fact that no casino could actually run the game, and no coin could be tossed indefinitely, does any work in resolving the puzzle or is merely a distraction. Finally, state what the case shows about expected-utility theory as a theory of rational choice, and about the rationality of wagers with infinite or undefined payoffs of the kind that arise in Pascal-style arguments.

You need not name or cite any literature; argue from the structure of the case. Be explicit at each stage about which notion of expected value you are using, and do not let the verdict rest on an unargued choice between those notions.
