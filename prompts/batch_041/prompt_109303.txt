I'm assembling the adequacy conditions for an interpretation of probability before we evaluate any of the standard positions, and I want the criteria themselves on the table first. Write the desiderata that any interpretation of probability must satisfy to count as adequate: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, and the results it must not contradict — with the case or consideration that motivates each requirement. Separate genuine requirements from the parochial demands of a single camp; mark which you take to be non-negotiable and which are optional, and state plainly whether any subset of the list cannot be jointly satisfied. Two issues I especially want you to wrestle with. First, whether empirical adequacy — agreeing with observed frequencies and with decision-theoretic use — can be the whole of the requirement, or whether an interpretation must also say what a probability is, given that rival interpretations can agree on every prediction and differ only over what is real. Second, whether the demand that an interpretation cover single, unrepeatable events (one particular radioactive decay, a one-shot bet, the probability that this coin lands heads on its only toss) is a genuine desideratum or an artifact of decision theory, given that our best physics assigns probabilities to individual systems rather than to ensembles. Then briefly score frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity against your list, and say which single requirement does the most work in eliminating contenders.
