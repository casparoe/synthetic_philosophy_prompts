I teach a 90-minute session on the physics of information to about 16 undergraduates, mixed physics and philosophy, and I want to run an activity in which they feel the force of the Maxwell's demon puzzle and of its resolution rather than hearing either one stated. The paradox itself is familiar to them, so the activity has to earn its keep in the accounting: I want the rules and the payoffs, not the debrief, to carry them from the demon's winning to the demon's having borrowed against a memory it must clear.

Design it in full: the setting, the roles or teams, the materials (I can prepare cards, boards, and tokens; nothing electronic), the rules, the payoff scheme with its actual numbers, and a minute-by-minute plan for the session.

Three things matter to me. First, the group must genuinely appear to win at some point: a demon that sorts a stream of molecules — cards drawn from a shuffled deck, two colours standing for fast and slow — and collects work-tokens at a rate that beats the no-demon baseline, with the group noticing that this looks like a violation of the second law. Second, the demon's memory has to be a finite register it must reuse, and the books have to close over a complete cycle, with a net gain of at most zero once the cost of clearing that register is counted. Work out the arithmetic explicitly and show it to me so I can check it before I run the game, and choose the token prices so that the numbers carry meaning rather than being arbitrary. Third, the debrief must map each move of the game onto the corresponding step of the paradox and its resolution, and must be precise about where the entropy is actually paid and about what a demon that never had to reset its memory could and could not achieve. Say plainly which feature of the physics the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

Tell me as well what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the confusions, the degenerate strategies, the accounting that will fail to land — and how to head each off.

Approach this as a designer of physics simulations for non-specialists who also knows the information-theoretic literature well. Think the thermodynamics of your own design through before you write it up: sketch two or three candidate accounting schemes, test whether their books balance, then commit to the one you can defend and give me your reasons. One design, not a menu.
