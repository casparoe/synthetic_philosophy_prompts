This is a bit of a wierd one, but bear with me. I've spent most of the last year reading about Turchin's secular cycles — partly general interest, partly because I'm thirty-four with a PhD and a string of fixed-term contracts, and it's hard not to read elite overproduction as a personal attack. Then my mum's side of the family, who claim to be descended from a low-ranking samurai household that lost the lot in the Meiji period, got me thinking about the Tokugawa collapse as a test case.

So: take the Tokugawa-to-Meiji transition — the last century or so of Tokugawa rule through to the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 — and analyse it through the structural-demographic theory of secular cycles. Map the parts onto the case: popular immiseration, elite overproduction, state fiscal crisis, intraelite conflict, mass mobilisation. I know the history reasonably well, so skip the potted version with Perry's ships and the Boshin war; what I want is what the theory says should have been happening, what actually happened, and where the mapping strains. No reading list please, I've got enough books on the go.

Two things I want you to take seriously, because I think they're where it gets interesting. First, I suspect Tokugawa Japan isn't a case of elite overproduction at all but of elite immiseration: the warrior caste was a few per cent of the population and more or less fixed in number, but its income was assessed in rice while the money economy inflated around it, and samurai were legally barred from farming and trade, so the squeeze came through the value of the position rather than the number of aspirants. If that's right, does the theory have a slot for it, or does it just relabel? Second, the theory seems much better at describing how cycles break than how they get absorbed — civil war, revolution, collapse, the surplus elite dispersed or killed. Japan looks like the other kind: the status abolished, the stipends commuted into bonds, a slice of the caste folded into the new bureaucracy and army, the rest pushed towards commerce, and the ones who wouldn't go quietly crushed at Satsuma. Is what I'm calling an absorptive resolution a coherent category, or is it just what happens every time? And can the theory say anything about which of the two you get — in particular, can it handle a state that survives by inventing new elite positions fast enough to soak up the surplus? I don't think the state's capacity to expand elite employment is in the model at all, but you tell me.

Last thing: the theory is meant to be endogenous, and 1853 is an exogenous shock if anything is. Does that make this a test of the theory, or a way of letting it off the hook?

American English for the answer please — I'm forwarding it to a colleague in Chicago. And definately be honest where the theory goes quiet rather than inventing a mechanism to cover the gap.
