I've been writing something on causation by omission and I keep going in circles, so before I look at anyones theory I want the spec nailed down first. Tell me what any adequate theory of "causing something by omitting to act" has to be able to do.

Give me the requirements list -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cant blur, and the things it cant contradict -- with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Cases I want covered, and I'd rather you build them out than gesture at them:

- the gardener who doesnt water the plant and it dies (classic, but say exactly what makes it count)
- someone who could have saved a stranger at trivial cost and didnt, where theres no special relationship and no duty
- a case where acting was possible but wouldve required something outrageous, so we feel the "omission" shouldnt count as a cause at all
- two people who each independently omit, where either one acting alone would have prevented the harm, so neither omission was sufficient on its own
- a case where one persons omission preempts anothers (the second would have acted, but only if the first had acted first, which they didnt)
- a second-order one: someone omits to omit -- they fail to carry out a failure -- if you can make that coherent, and say whether the theory even has room for it
- and one where the "agent" isnt a person at all: a self-driving system that doesnt brake. Does the omission have to be located in a person, or can it sit in the system, or nowhere?

Distinctions I dont want collapsed: causing vs. allowing; doing vs. omitting; the omission being a cause vs. the omission being blameworthy; absence of a cause vs. a cause that is itself an absence; and "a cause" vs. "the cause" (the selection thing).

Constraints it shouldnt violate: nothing that makes causation an intrinsic, local, positive relation between events if that just rules out absences by fiat; it should fit with how we already reason in law (where omission liability seems to need a duty) and in science (where absences do explanatory work); and it shouldnt make "what caused it" depend on "what ought to have happened," since that looks backwards -- though I suspect thats exactly where the trouble is, so tell me if the theory is stuck needing norms just to pick out which omissions even count.

One more thing to test: someone will tell you an omission is a cause just in case it belongs in the best explanation of the outcome. Run that proposal through your list and tell me where it breaks.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp, flag any pair you think cant be satisfied together (thats the part I actually want), and say which single requirement does the most to kill off the popular theories.
