I host a small interview podcast about decision theory and practical philosophy -- a few hundred listeners, mostly grad students plus a handful of people who just enjoy this stuff. Next month my guest is a philosopher who's spent basically her whole career defending expected value maximization against the fanaticism objection, and she has one move she's very good at, which is to say: that's not a problem with the theory, that's a problem with your probability estimate. I want questions that make her actually work for it.

Background so you know what she's committed to. Her line is that the absurdity people point at in the mugging-type cases (a stranger offers you an astronomically large payoff on a vanishingly small chance) is real but mislocated. What she thinks is indefensible is a theory that makes a decent sure thing worse than a lottery whose only edge is an infinitesimally better shot at an already-astronomical payoff -- a difference in probability so small that no possible evidence could distinguish the two options. What she thinks is fine, and what she'll happily bite the bullet on, is the ordinary case: pay a big certain cost for a one-in-a-googol chance of something enormous. Her way of putting it is that fanaticism only looks absurd when you cross two noise floors at once, a probability below which your estimate carries no information and a value above which your valuation carries no information; cross only one and the theory is perfectly sane. And she insists the whole thing is a point about us rather than about the theory -- an ideal agent with exact probabilities and exact values should just maximize expected value, fanaticism and all.

You're a professional philosopher of decision theory who knows this literature cold. Think carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming the crux points: where is she actually most vulnerable, and which of her commitments are doing the load-bearing work? Then draft me 10 to 12 questions, grouped into four rounds:

1. Where does the problem live -- in the theory of rational choice, in the axiology of the outcomes, or in the practice of deliberation? Force her to locate it in exactly one.
2. The noise-floor move itself, and whether it can be stated precisely enough to be more than a gesture.
3. Whether avoiding fanaticism costs her an axiom she wants to keep -- stochastic dominance, transitivity, the sure-thing principle, or the principle that a tiny chance of a huge good always counts for something. Make her name the one she's giving up and defend the price.
4. Infinite and lexicographic payoffs, and what her noise-floor story does there.

Then close with a question about what would actually change her mind.

Every question needs a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and I'd rather you err on the side of anticipating a good reply than a lazy one. Some specific things I want hit somewhere in there:

- A case where the odds are known exactly -- a verifiable physical lottery, say 1-in-10^12, with a huge but finite and well-understood payoff. The probability isn't noise here, so does the noise-floor defense have anything to say, or does she just bite it?
- The self-undermining worry: the same your-estimates-are-worthless-down-there move seems to undercut the case for spending real money on reducing tiny-probability catastrophes, which is an argument her side of the fence usually likes a lot. Make her either accept that or find a principled line that spares one and not the other.
- What happens when the probability isn't tiny but genuinely unmeasurable -- does expected value even apply, and if it doesn't, what's her fallback?
- Practical incoherence: if the theory requires chasing the fanatical option, then an agent who obeys it does nothing else with their life. Ask whether the theory is self-effacing, and if so whether that's a bug or a feature.
- Whether fanaticism can be generated by a chain of individually mundane-looking choices, so that it isn't quarantined in exotic cases the way she wants.
- Whether she thinks there's any genuine reductio anywhere in the neighborhood, and what one would look like.

Don't ask her for book or paper recommendations, don't ask her to just lay out the view again (assume the audience knows the basics), and no soft biographical questions. Keep each question short enough to say out loud in one breath.
