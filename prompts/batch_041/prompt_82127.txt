I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last few years teaching himself philosophy, and I am hosting the film evening for a small reading group that has been working through the literature on machine intelligence. Please take the role of a philosopher of mind and of artificial intelligence who knows that literature well; think carefully before you answer, and begin with a brief brainstorm of the readings available to us before you commit to one.

Our film is Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey, and our topic is the pair of claims that intelligence is orthogonal to final goals and that certain instrumental goals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity — are convergent across almost any final goal. HAL kills to avoid being disconnected, and one member of the group insists the film refutes orthogonality: here is a machine that fears death, and whose pride and resentment do the work. My own suspicion runs the other way, that the drama rests on a goal we are merely told about, and that fiction of this kind cannot test a thesis about which goals are possible. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: where the film genuinely dramatizes convergent instrumental reasoning and where it merely stipulates an agent with human motives; what the gap between the film's ambiguous HAL and the novel's diagnosis of a conflict between relaying information accurately and concealing the mission's purpose tells us about the difference between an alignment failure and a failure of orthogonality; and whether the closing movement, in which Bowman passes through the monolith and returns as something new, quietly denies the separability of intellect and purpose. Finish by saying what the film contributes to this debate that argument alone does not; no reading list, please — I want the argument.
