I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of physics, and next month I present a workshop paper to my department plus a few visitors. The paper argues that Bell's theorem is routinely misreported as a proof that nature is nonlocal, when its actual content is narrower: the inequality rests on two separable conditions, one forbidding a distant setting to affect the statistics of a local outcome and one requiring the complete state of the pair to screen off the two outcomes from each other, and quantum mechanics, which satisfies no-signalling, violates the second. My line is that the theorem therefore refutes the demand for a common cause rather than locality, and that talk of an influence travelling between the wings is an extra assumption imported along with the screening-off requirement, not a consequence of the experiments. The sketch I will give: any theory reproducing the predictions must either let settings influence distant statistics or abandon screening-off; no-signalling rules out the first; so the residue has to be located in the failure of screening-off. The audience will be one philosopher of physics who works on the Bell tests and cares about loopholes and the statistical strength of the evidence, a committed Bohmian, an Everettian who holds that the theorem turns on the uniqueness of outcomes, and two analytic metaphysicians of causation who will want to know which notion of cause is doing the work. Draft my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them, ordered by how much damage they do, with what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply to each, and a note on where each reply is weakest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive.
