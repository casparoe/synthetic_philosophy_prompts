Two camps in the philosophy of time have been talking past one another for decades. They have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and you are to write the joint statement that both camps can put their names to. The statement must stand on its own: no citations, no names of authors or works, no appeal to who has said what, and no bibliography.

The two camps

Camp A, the law-grounded arrow. The arrow of time is an objective feature of the world. The fundamental dynamical laws are, to a very good approximation, invariant under time reversal, and so cannot by themselves distinguish earlier from later; the distinction must be supplied by a further posit of law-like standing. That posit is a boundary condition: the low-entropy initial macrostate of the universe, standardly called the Past Hypothesis. Together with the dynamical laws and a statistical postulate linking entropy to the phase-space volume of the region compatible with a macrostate, it yields the second law, and thereby the records, traces, memories, and causal asymmetries that give the world its temporal orientation. On this view the arrow belongs to the world described rather than to our description of it; the Past Hypothesis is a statement about a particular initial condition, but it earns its keep in our best physical theory in the way a law does.

Camp B, the perspectival arrow. Entropy is defined only relative to a choice of macroscopic variables, that is, to a coarse-graining of the underlying microstate space, and the second law is a statement about what is overwhelmingly typical for a system so described, not something the microscopic dynamics require. The asymmetries of memory, record, deliberation, and causation are asymmetries of the standpoint of an agent embedded in a system and carrying traces of its own earlier states; the direction such an agent calls past is fixed by the way its traces run, not by any description-independent feature of the world. Camp B need not deny that our universe began in a low-entropy macrostate; it treats that as a contingent fact about our history rather than as a law, and denies that there is a fact of the matter, independent of a coarse-graining and of an agent's orientation, about which direction is the direction of time.

Shared ground. Neither camp disputes the following, and you may draw on it freely: the near time-reversal invariance of the dynamical laws, together with the qualification that time-reversal symmetry is not respected in every sector of fundamental physics; the reversibility objection, that any entropy increase can in principle be undone by a sufficiently fine-grained intervention; the statistical rather than exceptionless character of the second law, including the existence of exact fluctuation relations governing the probability of entropy-decreasing trajectories; the fact that entropy is defined relative to a coarse-graining; the evidence that the observable universe began in a very low-entropy state; the existence of records, traces, and memories, and their correlation with entropy gradients; the distinction between the entropy of the universe as a whole and the many local entropy decreases that occur within it; and the observation that self-gravitating systems can gain entropy by clumping, which complicates any straightforward identification of the arrow with the growth of disorder.

The document you produce should contain the following parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of propositions both camps sign, including the shared physics and any shared methodological commitments about what would count as an explanation of the arrow. Where you think a proposed claim is too vague to be worth signing, say so rather than papering over it.

2. Disagreements. A table with one row per live disagreement and columns for the point at issue, Camp A's formulation of its own position, Camp B's formulation of its own position, and a judgement as to whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal. Each formulation must be one its own camp would accept as fair; a formulation only one side would endorse as neutral does not belong in the table.

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each, say why it is a crux and what each camp presently expects.

4. Evidence for each crux. At least one case, thought experiment, or established result that both camps agree bears on the crux, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Where you judge that no shared evidence could settle a crux even in principle, say so and explain why.

5. The stipulated cases. All four must be treated, and for each, each camp must state what it predicts.

(i) Reversal. An isolated macroscopic system is allowed to evolve so that its coarse-grained entropy rises; a procedure then reverses its microscopic velocities, so that for a time its entropy falls. The reversal procedure itself costs entropy elsewhere in the world. Does the system's arrow reverse? Would an observer inside it experience a reversed arrow? Is the episode a counterexample to the second law, an illustration of it, or neither?

(ii) Two-ended universe. A closed universe whose entropy is stipulated to be low at both temporal ends and maximal at the turnaround between them, the second branch mirroring the first, with observers on both branches whose records point outward, each toward its own end. Does each observer have an arrow? Does Camp A need one boundary condition, two, or a single symmetric one? Does the case favour Camp B's claim that the arrow is standpoint-relative, or is it neutral between the camps?

(iii) Fluctuation observer. A universe that has reached equilibrium, in which a rare statistical fluctuation briefly assembles an isolated observer with a coherent store of apparent memories referring to no actual history. What is that observer's arrow? Are its apparent memories records? And what, if anything, does the case show about whether the low-entropy boundary condition must have law-like standing rather than merely being a fact? Each camp should also state its prediction about the relative frequency, in the relevant ensemble, of observers like that one and observers like us.

(iv) Local arrows. Entropy falls locally all the time, in refrigerators, in crystallisation, in the growth of structure under gravity, while the entropy of the universe as a whole rises. Is there a local arrow without a global one, and if so is there any reason to call the global one the arrow? Each camp should say whether it regards the phrase the arrow of time as well-defined at the level of the whole universe, and if not, what should replace it.

Close with a short section stating what would actually move each camp, whether any such evidence could in principle be obtained, and which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal and why.
