For several years I have been a regular guest at a small interdisciplinary seminar — mostly philosophers, with a few of us novelists tolerated for our supposed sensitivity to language — and the same dispute returns every term. It is the sorites. Someone produces the heap, the bald man, the rich man who loses a penny; someone else observes that the reasoning is impeccable if the premises are, and that the premises look unassailable; and then the room divides into the camps that anyone who has read even a little of the literature would recognize: those who insist there must be a sharp boundary we simply cannot locate, those who say the predicate has no boundary to find and that the sentences are neither true nor false, those who say truth comes in degrees and that the argument trades on a false dichotomy, and those who say the whole trouble is contextual and that the predicate quietly shifts its meaning as we walk the forced march. Nobody has moved in a decade. The objections to each view are as well known as the views themselves: the first camp is asked where the boundary lies and can only say somewhere; the degree theorist is asked what degree of truth to assign to the conditional premise and, depending on the answer, must either abandon modus ponens or concede a hidden sharp step; the contextualist is asked why the shift is so hard to hear. I have begun to suspect that the stall is not a sign that the problem is deep but that the parties are not, in fact, disagreeing about the same thing.

My own hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, since I am not a philosopher and am aware of the presumption — is that the phrase borderline case is doing at least two quite different jobs in the debate, and that the camps have quietly divided over which job matters. For some, a borderline case is a case we are ignorant about: there is a fact of the matter, and our epistemic situation is defective. For others, it is a case about which there is no fact of the matter: the question of whether the man is bald has no answer to be ignorant of. And a third group, I think, treats it as a case whose classification is up to us — a matter of decision or stipulation rather than discovery — which is the way I, as a novelist, am accustomed to think about the properties of my characters, since whether a fictional woman is tall is settled by what I write and not by any fact about her that I could be wrong about. If that is right, then the long quarrel may be a quarrel about what kind of thing a borderline case is, conducted by people who have each presupposed an answer.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement, a merely verbal one, or several different questions wearing a single name; if it is substantive, is the hidden disagreement factual, formal, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for; and is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely hard. Ground the diagnosis in what the camps actually say — the price each pays, the intuitions each is willing to sacrifice — rather than in a survey. Then tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. And if my hypothesis about the borderline case is confused, say so plainly and tell me where it goes wrong.
