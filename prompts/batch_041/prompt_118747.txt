I've recently been put in charge of designing the evaluation scorecard for a research group I manage, and a colleague — a former economist — has been arguing that the entire exercise is doomed. He cites the well-known aphorism that once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure, and he says it is now underwritten by a tidy taxonomy of failure modes: sometimes the metric is merely a noisy proxy for what we actually care about, so pushing it toward extremes mostly selects for the noise; sometimes the metric is correlated with the goal but not causally connected to it, so intervening on the metric simply breaks the correlation rather than improving anything; sometimes the metric tracks the goal well across the range we have observed but not out at the extremes we would be pushing toward; and sometimes the people being measured just game the number on purpose. His conclusion is that because the aphorism recurs in all these forms, every quantitative target will inevitably be corrupted the moment it is set, and we should therefore scrap metrics altogether and trust the researchers' own judgment instead.

I do not believe that conclusion, but I have been unable to say precisely why it is wrong. If the phenomenon really does show up across all those variants — and I grant that each of them sounds like something I have seen happen — does it not follow that any target I could set will degrade the thing it was meant to track? That would be a strange result for a practice that so many organizations seem unable to do without. This has become urgent for me because I have to hand in the scorecard next month, and I would rather not either build something useless or give up on measurement out of superstition.

Could you diagnose the argument for me — what it gets right, exactly where the inference breaks down, and what the aphorism actually does and does not license? I would also like to know which parts of this are settled and which remain genuinely contested among people who study measurement and incentives, so please resist presenting a contested position as if it were the consensus; where the disagreement is real, say so plainly and give me the strongest version of each side.
