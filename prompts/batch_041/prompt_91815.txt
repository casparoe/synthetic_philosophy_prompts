I am a co-owner of a small manufacturing firm, and my business partner is one of my oldest friends. The firm has just made a real profit for the first time, and we now have to decide what to do with it, and we cannot agree. He wants to reinvest nearly all of it in the business — new machinery, more staff, a second site — on the view that our first duty is to build something durable that can keep people employed for decades. I want to put the bulk of it into cutting what our processes do to the environment, even though that will slow us down, because I do not think it is ours to make a living by damaging the ground we stand on. We have argued about this for months and got nowhere; the money sits there, and the resentment builds.

Lately I have caught myself thinking that there must be some arrangement — a phased programme, a split of the funds, some combination of the two ambitions — that we would both genuinely prefer to this deadlock, even though neither of us would get exactly what we want. But I cannot tell whether that thought is wisdom or merely exhaustion. Is a settlement that neither of us loves really better than continuing to press for what I believe is right? And is it even coherent to treat our commitments here as things that can be weighed and traded against one another, the way two people might swap goods they happen to value differently — or does treating the environment as something to be traded against profit simply repeat the very mistake I am objecting to?

Please give me your honest view, and write it in American English. I would rather have an answer that engages the moral structure of the situation than one that tells me to communicate better.
