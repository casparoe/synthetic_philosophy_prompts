I chair the education committee of an international schools network, and on Thursday we vote on whether to adopt a compulsory comparative module for 15- and 16-year-olds on how nations live with difficult pasts. The draft, prepared by a curator trained in Berlin, arranges four 'memory rooms' — Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, that is, its decades-long effort to work through the Nazi past; Japan's wartime empire; Turkey and the Armenians; and the American South — each divided into 'what happened' and 'how they live with it now.' Students are then assessed on a single rubric whose top criterion is 'acknowledgment by the state,' and the module closes with a pledge wall on which they write what they will do so that it never happens again. Two board members, raised in Istanbul and Osaka, object that this does not compare these histories but grades them, teaching students that some nations are simply further along; a third objects that the pledge wall asks children to answer for crimes committed before their grandparents were born. The curator replies that refusing to compare is itself a political choice and that discomfort is the point. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote: a clear recommendation — adopt as written, reject, or adopt with specified conditions — with reasons the curator and the dissenting members could each respond to, and a fair statement of the strongest case for whichever side you come down against.
