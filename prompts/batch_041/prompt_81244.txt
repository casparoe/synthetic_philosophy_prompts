I do calibration for a living -- I check and adjust lab balances and temperature probes, and half my week goes on the question of how you know an instrument is honest when the only thing you can check it against is another instrument that somebody else already signed off on. A couple of years ago that got me reading philosophy of knowledge on my own, and Iv'e been stuck on one knot ever since: you look at a wall, it looks red, you believe it's red, and then from that belief you conclude your eyes are working fine -- and you turn around and use that conclusion to back up the original belief. Nobody I argue with about this thinks it's clean, but we can't agree on where the dirt is. One friend says the red belief was never the problem, the trouble is the second step; another says the trouble is that you can run the same move on anything, including reading a thermometer to check the thermometer, so you get knowledge about your own reliability on the cheap; a third says the whole fishiness is an illusion caused by mixing up whether a belief is justified with whether you're justified in leaning on the process that produced it. My coworker added something I can't shake: when you try to calibrate a scale against itself, what kills you isn't randomness, it's that the error is consistent -- a crooked scale lies to you in the same direction every time, and that's exactly what makes it feel trustworthy. So maybe the real question is when a thing's failure modes are independent of the check and when they aren't, and the rest of the debate is downstream of that. Then there's the version where you don't do the move once but build up a track record over months, and I honestly can't tell if that's the same problem or a different one, plus the nagging cases of memory and logic, where the only instrument available is the one you're trying to certify, and the question of how you even say which "process" is on trial -- "my eyes," or "my eyes in this light at this distance" -- since the answer seems to change whether the record looks good. My own half-baked fix is that in real metrology you're never the only instrument: you get to lean on somebody else's gear, and maybe the epistemic version is that my color vision is trustworthy because your color vision is an independent check, which would make the hard cases exactly the ones where there's no second instrument anywhere. I don't know if that solves anything or just pushes the problem back a step.
