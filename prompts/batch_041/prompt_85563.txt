Treat the Concert of Europe — the settlement and congress system that ran from the Congress of Vienna to the outbreak of the First World War — as a test case for Kant's argument in Perpetual Peace. Kant's preliminary articles prohibit, among other things, standing armies, war loans, and forcible interference in another state's constitution; his definitive articles require a republican constitution, a federation of free states rather than a mere balance of power, and a cosmopolitan right of hospitality. He also argues that a republic can be organized even for a nation of devils, provided they are intelligent, because the problem is one of mechanism rather than moral improvement; and he treats the balance of power as an unstable substitute for a genuine federation.

Map the Concert onto these articles one by one: which were satisfied, which were violated, and which had no application at all. Then tell me what the mapping shows. Does the Concert's success in avoiding a general European war for a century count as evidence for Kant's mechanism, or does it show that a balance of power can do the work he assigned to a federation? Does its collapse in 1914 confirm his prediction that a mere balance must fail, or does it show that the republican and federal conditions were doing no real work? Attend especially to what Kant's categories do not capture — nationalism, the colonial periphery, public opinion, and the fact that the settlement was made by monarchies rather than republics. Where Kant is silent, say so rather than forcing him to speak. I want the analysis to run in both directions: Kant illuminating the Concert, and the Concert testing Kant.
