I have been reading a book on self-reference in mathematics, art and music, and it has pushed me into writing an essay on how marks on a page, or patterns of bits, ever come to mean anything at all. A friend who read the draft over coffee has handed me an argument that I cannot fault but am certain proves far too much, and I should like you to find the flaw in it — or, if there is none, to tell me which claim I must surrender and what surrendering it costs. Here it is. (1) A pattern of marks or bits has no meaning in itself: nothing about the ink, or the voltages, tells you what it stands for. (2) Meaning appears only when something at the receiving end relates the pattern to a structure it already possesses, so the meaning is contributed by the receiver. (3) The receiver must know how to perform that relating, and any instructions one gives it are themselves just another pattern. (4) Hence no message can carry its own interpretation: however long the chain of instructions, the last link is always something the receiver brings rather than something the message contains. (5) But if the meaning is brought entirely by the receiver, nothing in the pattern constrains the relating — the receiver may relate it to any structure whatever. (6) Therefore one and the same text may mean anything to anyone, and there is no fact of the matter as to what it means.

Now, (6) plainly proves too much. On it a misreading is never wrong; two translators of a single sentence cannot genuinely disagree; a man who reads a railway timetable as a love letter is doing nothing different from reading it correctly; and my essay, once written, would mean whatever its reader pleased, which is not a position an essay can decently hold about itself. So something has gone wrong, and I want to know what. I should say at once that I am attached to (1) and (2), which seem to me simply true, and that I have no wish to rescue the picture of meaning as a private thing locked up inside a text. What I want is to be told where the slide occurs, why that step looked so plausible, and what the alternatives would cost me: if I refuse (5), must I give up the thought that meaning is always meaning-for-someone; and if instead I keep (5) and refuse (6), what notion of constraint am I thereby smuggling in?
