I have no training in philosophy, but there is a puzzle I first met in a popular book and cannot shake, and I would like your help turning my vague sense of it into a number. Imagine a universe that contains nothing at all except two iron spheres, exactly alike in size, shape, mass, color, temperature, and every other feature you could name, floating in an otherwise empty space and arranged so that neither one is nearer the center of anything than the other. The question is whether such a world contains two things or only one, and whether we should believe the principle that things which do not differ in any way are really the same thing. I want your honest credence, a number between zero and one, that the following claim is true: it is possible for there to be two numerically distinct objects that share every qualitative property, so that the identity of indiscernibles, taken at full generality, is false. I would also like you to say what, if anything, could settle the matter.

Rather than just a number, I want the reasoning spread out. Give me the sub-claims your number depends on, a rough probability for each, and one line of justification for each, then combine them into a single figure and show me how they combine. The sub-claims I am most curious about are these. First, whether the scenario can be coherently conceived at all, or whether there is some hidden contradiction in describing a world with nothing but two perfectly alike things. Second, how much weight to give to the thought that conceivability is a guide to possibility, which I gather is contested, and how contested it really is. Third, what counts as a property: if being this very sphere counts as a feature, then of course the two differ, but that seems to make the principle trivially true, and I want to know whether ruling that out is a legitimate move or a cheat. Fourth, something I ran into recently and cannot place: I read that in physics, two arrangements that differ only by swapping two particles of the same kind are counted as one and the same state rather than two. I do not know whether that is a deep fact about identity or just a bookkeeping convention, and I would like you to tell me how much, if at all, it should move my number. Fifth, whether the whole dispute changes if the world is understood as a structure with positions rather than as a collection of things, so that the two spheres are two places in a pattern rather than two occupants. Finally, tell me which of these sub-claims my number is most sensitive to, and what line of argument or what kind of evidence would move it the most.

Please write the whole reply as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a curious friend. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no numbered lists, and no tables.
