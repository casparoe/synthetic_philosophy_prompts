I'm a second-year PhD student in a weird hybrid cog-sci/philosophy-of-mind department, and I have to bring a position paper to a seminar on teleology and agency next week, so I want to road-test the thing before I commit to writing it up properly. Background you probably know, but I'll state it since it's doing all the work: the sphexishness wasp. She paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it outside while she goes in to inspect the tunnel, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's in there, when she comes back out she just drags it to the mouth again and goes back in to inspect — and you can apparently keep her looping like that more or less indefinitely. Dennett and Hofstadter both love this case, and Hofstadter's name for the phenomenon is sphexishness. The moral everyone draws is that behaviour can look like means-end planning while there's no representation of any goal anywhere in the system, plus the scary corollary that the only difference between her and us might be the length of the loop.

Here's what I want to defend. I think sphexishness is a red herring, and more strongly that the rigid/flexible axis does no metaphysical work at all for agency or free will. Flexible just means a bigger, more conditionally branching program; it's a difference of degree along one dimension, and the wasp and I sit on the same axis, just far apart on it. What actually draws a line isn't how plastic the behaviour is but whether the states driving it have content — whether the system is sensitive to what its representations mean rather than to their causal shape. So a creature with a tiny behavioural repertoire but genuine goal-representations counts as an agent, and a system that adapts wildly while tracking nothing (a thermostat that learns, a very fancy reflex) does not. On my picture the wasp is a non-agent for the content reason, not the rigidity reason, which is what makes the sphexishness anxiety misplaced: the answer to am I just a big wasp isn't no, my loops are longer, it's that loops were never the issue. It also means the meta-level fix — being able to notice and revise my own routines — buys me nothing extra, and that's the part I suspect is where it breaks.

So what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked, roughly from if this is right I have to give up the view down to awkward but survivable, and I want the ones a defender of my view would themselves concede, not just what a critic would throw at me. Some of the things I'm chewing on: whether there are cases where plasticity does all the work and content is idle, or conversely content without any real behavioural flexibility, and whether I'd still want to call that agency; whether sensitivity to content can be made precise without smuggling flexibility back in, or whether it just collapses into the interpreter's stance, in which case I've built my line out of something observer-relative and it's circular; whether the wasp maybe not being as rigid as the story suggests — I've read somewhere that she does eventually adapt if you keep at it, and that re-inspecting a nest you've just been disturbed at is arguably the smart move anyway — helps me or hurts me, since if rigid was the wrong description of her all along that's either a point for me or else it shows the rigid/flexible distinction is in the describer's eye, which is also sort of my view but now threatens to make it unfalsifiable; and whether the whole thing just collapses into some existing compatibilist line (reason-responsiveness, or the Frankfurt-style move about which mechanism actually produced the action), in which case I'd like to know which one and what, if anything, the collapse costs me. I'm also intrested in a straight verdict at the end: does this view risk anything at all, and if it doesn't, is that fatal for a view of this kind or just the price of a conceptual claim?
