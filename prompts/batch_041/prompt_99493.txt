I write a column on markets and ideas, and there is one sentence that keeps being used on me in place of an argument: the market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run — usually attributed to Benjamin Graham, and almost always produced by someone who wants to close a conversation about whether a particular price is simply wrong. It came up again last week with a strategist I was interviewing after a panel on market efficiency; he granted that the price I was asking about might be a vote at the moment, but said the weighing would arrive, so my question was a category error rather than a live one. I have since heard the same sentence carry something much weaker, roughly that prices are noisy over short horizons and informative over long ones, and something much stronger, that value is a fact of the matter and the market is guaranteed to find it, and I have begun to suspect that the strength of the reading tracks how much work the speaker needs the sentence to do.

What I want is not an essay on Graham or a history of the aphorism but a menu of the precise claims the sentence could be making, with a verdict on each, so that I can tell what is being smuggled in. Two things make me doubt the ambitious readings. First, if the eventual weighing is knowable in advance, anyone who knows it should be trading on it now, and a market full of people who already know how the long run ends ought not to look like this one; I have been told there is a formal result — a no-trade theorem, I think it is called — to the effect that when everyone's rationality and the structure of information are common knowledge, nobody trades at all, because the offer itself reveals what the offerer knows, and there is a companion argument that prices cannot be so informative that gathering information stops paying for itself, which would make the fully weighed long run a state the market can never occupy. If that is right, then persistent divergence between vote and weight looks less like patience being rewarded than like the weight's not being common knowledge, and I am left unsure what the weighing machine is weighing. Second, the sentence seems to identify the long run as the horizon at which price and value agree, which threatens to make it true by construction: for an asset whose value has no independent yardstick — a currency, a token, a claim on nothing but other people's future willingness to pay — I cannot see what the scale would be measuring.

Please reply with exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no opening paragraph, no closing summary. Each item should be one or two sentences that state one precise claim the sentence could be making, tag it as trivial, defensible, false, or self-undermining, and say whether that claim would actually license the use my strategist put it to. Order them from the emptiest reading to the most ambitious, and reserve the fifth for the reading you think he was actually running on.
