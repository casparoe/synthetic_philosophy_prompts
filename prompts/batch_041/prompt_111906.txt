I'm trying to pin down how confident I should be about what Maimonides actually thought about animal sacrifice, and I keep going back and forth. My read of the Guide of the Perplexed is that the sacrificial laws were basically an accommodation -- God took a mode of worship the Israelites were already soaked in from the surrounding idolatrous culture and redirected it toward Himself, rather than demanding they drop it cold turkey, because switching the object of a familiar practice is easier than abolishing the practice. But people keep telling me that can't be the whole story: he also lays out the entire sacrificial system in the Mishneh Torah as binding law, and Nachmanides, in his commentary on Leviticus (I think around the opening chapters), pushes back hard that the sacrifices were wanted and cherished for their own sake, so reading them as a concession is a kind of insult to them. What's your actual credence that the accommodation reading is what Maimonides really held -- take the claim to be that he thought sacrifice was commanded chiefly because of the historical situation of the Israelites and not because it was intrinsically the best or most fitting way to worship? I don't want "it depends how you read him"; I want a number plus the decomposition. How much rides on taking the Guide's surface at face value vs. the esoteric-reading thesis that he hides his real view, how much on the Mishneh Torah codification, how much on the messianic question (does he actually expect sacrifice to resume, or is there a hint it lapses?), how much on Nachmanides' objection landing or not, and whatever else you'd add. Give a rough probability and one line of justification per term, then how they combine, then which term you're least sure of and what would move it most.
