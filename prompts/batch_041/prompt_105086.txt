I am preparing to teach the dispute between counterpart theory and transworld identity in a metaphysics course for advanced undergraduates who already have the possible-worlds apparatus and the rudiments of rigid designation in hand. Do not explain the dispute to me; plan how I should explain it. I want the explanation to make the choice between the two positions feel forced rather than arbitrary, so the centre of gravity of your answer should be the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it: what must already be in place before the question "which individual in the other world is the one we are talking about?" can even be posed, and which commitment of the standard possible-worlds framework is what makes that question unavoidable.

Second, I want one example to carry the entire session, and I want you to choose it deliberately rather than reaching for the worn ones. Say what the example must have — a de re modal claim whose truth the students will not dispute, an individual whose identity across the worlds in question is genuinely underdetermined, and enough distance from the textbook cases that the students cannot simply consult a memorised verdict — and what it must lack: an independent essence or haecceity that would settle the cross-world identification for free, and any accidental feature that would let the two views agree by luck. If the familiar Humphrey-style case can do this work, say so and explain why; if it cannot, propose a replacement and defend it.

Third, I want the misconceptions the students will form, in the order they will form them, with a note on exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. At a minimum I expect them to run together "there is no transworld identity" with "counterpart theory"; to treat the counterpart relation as identity wearing a disguise; to conclude that the view makes de re modal claims about them false, or merely about somebody else; to assume that defending transworld identity requires an essence or a haecceity; and to dismiss the whole dispute as a notational variant that makes no difference to what is true. Tell me which of these are genuinely misconceptions and which are live objections the students are entitled to press.

Fourth, I want the session to leave the students with a sharp question rather than a verdict, so build toward the question whether counterpart theory is offered as a translation of ordinary modal discourse that preserves its truth-conditions or as a revision of it, together with the related question whether the counterpart relation is a primitive of the theory or is defined from similarity. Say where in the sequence that pair of questions should surface, and what the students should be able to say about it by the end that they could not say at the start.

Finally, give me one question to put to the room at the end that would show whether they have grasped the shape of the dispute rather than merely memorised two positions, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so explicitly.
