I'm a novelist at work on a short essay about character, and I've reached the point where I need a reader who knows the philosophy and the psychology better than I do. The essay argues that the situationist critique of virtue ethics — the body of work, running from the personality psychologist Walter Mischel through the philosophers who have drawn the strongest conclusions from it, showing that conduct is driven more by circumstance than by any stable trait — is usually received as bad news for the moral psychology that novels presuppose, and that it is in fact good news for the novel. My notes run roughly as follows, and I'd like your comments on them.

1. The critique. Seminary students hurrying to deliver a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan step over a slumped man when they are made to feel late; people who have just found a coin in a payphone are likelier to help a stranger who drops her papers; ordinary men administer what they believe to be painful shocks when an authority urges them on. From findings like these the critics draw a strong conclusion: there are no robust global character traits, and virtue ethics, which needs them, rests on an empirical falsehood.

2. The replies I'm aware of. That the experiments test cross-situational consistency, which virtue never promised; that practical wisdom, not habit, is the core of virtue; that the critique proves too much, since it dissolves the trait-psychology whose methods it borrows; that traits are real but situation-indexed and locally bound.

3. My turn. The novel has never rendered character as a set of dispositions that fire regardless of circumstance. It renders character by placing a person in a situation and showing what she does, and by letting us see the situation from the inside — what it looks like to her, which is what an experimenter can only infer. The novel is, in effect, a machine for varying the situation while holding the person fixed, which is precisely the manipulation no experiment can perform on a real person.

4. The claim I'm unsure of. If that is right, then in fiction a character's trait is not a property of the person at all but a summary of the reader's inference across a designed distribution of scenes — a property of the reader's expectations, licensed by the author's staging. I want to call this the reader's-trait view. My worry is that it is either trivially true (of course the author controls the scenes) or false in a way that costs me the essay.

Could you comment on three things: whether I've stated the empirical and philosophical material accurately, and what I've garbled or oversimplified; whether the central move in 3 is genuinely interesting or a truism dressed up; and whether the reader's-trait idea in 4 earns its keep or should be cut. I would rather be told what to drop than what to add.
