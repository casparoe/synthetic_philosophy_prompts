You are a comparative constitutional lawyer and political theorist who has spent a career on the border between measurement and normative argument, and who is as suspicious of indices as of slogans. Think this through carefully before you write. Begin with a short private brainstorm: set out the several incompatible things people mean by democratic backsliding — a rate of change versus a threshold, an outcome versus an intention, a property of a government versus a property of a regime, an event versus a trajectory — and for each one identify the baseline it silently assumes, the cases it cannot classify, and what it would license you to say about a government you already dislike. Decide which of these readings can survive the charge that the label is a partisan instrument. Do not show me the brainstorm. Show me only the speech.

The occasion. I chair the board of a mid-sized foundation that funds rule-of-law and electoral-integrity work in about a dozen countries, and I am giving the closing remarks at our twentieth-anniversary dinner: roughly 120 people, seven or eight minutes, standing at a lectern, no slides, no notes in hand. Half the room are program officers, grantees, election monitors, investigative journalists, and the three academic political scientists who advise us; the other half are donors and their guests, who follow the news closely and have never read a word of the literature. Next quarter the board votes on whether to adopt a formal determination procedure — a written test that, if a country satisfies it, obliges the foundation to say publicly that the country is backsliding, with the funding and diplomatic consequences that follow. The board is split down the middle. One faction holds that the phrase is a weapon with no content, that we will apply it to governments we dislike and withhold it from governments that are useful to us, and that everyone will see us doing exactly that. The other faction holds that if we cannot say the words we should not be in this business at all. I have to give the speech that settles it, or failing that, makes the disagreement honest. Our charter forbids naming states in public remarks — a rule I helped write — so everything has to be carried by criteria and by a single invented example.

What the speech has to do.

- Propose the test: three or four criteria, each operational enough that a staffer could score a country against it, each defended in a clause or two. Invent them; do not simply recycle the categories that existing democracy indices use.
- Answer who decides. Say whether the determination belongs to outsiders (monitors, funders, foreign scholars), to insiders (domestic courts, journalists, opposition parties, voters), or to some division of labour between them, and say what is lost by locating the authority where you locate it. Notice that the speech is itself an exercise of the power you are trying to allocate.
- State the strongest version of the partisan-instrument objection in your own voice, then say what the concept has to give up in order to survive it. If you conclude that it cannot survive, write the speech that follows from that conclusion instead.
- Work one hard case, invented and not modelled on any real country: elections remain genuinely competitive and the incumbent has lost and handed over power, but the courts, the public broadcaster, the civil service, and the largest private media have been rearranged in ways that would outlast a change of government. Say whether your test calls this backsliding; if it does not, say whether that is a defect in the test or evidence that the concept was always narrower than its users assume.
- Close with a paragraph a non-specialist donor could repeat at breakfast the next morning without getting it wrong.

Constraints.

- No citations, no bibliography, no names of scholars, indices, or organizations, no statistics, no dates. Nothing that depends on a fact you cannot establish from where you sit.
- Do not quote anyone.
- The concept must do structural work rather than decorate. The speech should be built so that the reason it cannot simply point at abuses, and cannot simply say that the threshold is obvious when you see it, is visible in its order of concessions — in what it grants early, what it refuses to grant, and what it pays for the position it lands on.
- Both halves of the room must get something: the specialists should meet at least one move they have not seen before, and the donors should be able to follow every sentence.
- Write it to be spoken aloud, in the voice of a scholar-administrator in her late fifties: dry rather than warm, allergic to uplift, capable of a joke but not of a peroration. Roughly 1,200 words.
