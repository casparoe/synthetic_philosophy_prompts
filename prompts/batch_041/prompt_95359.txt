For the past year I have been sitting in on a reading group — a mix of philosophers of biology, two working systematists, and a couple of graduate students in ecology — that has been trying to settle what species are, and I have reached the unhappy conclusion that we are not going to converge, and that this is not because anyone is lazy or uninformed. Everyone in the room accepts the same catalogue of awkward cases: lineages that exchange genes freely enough to look like a single species yet stay distinct in the field; ring species, where the two ends of the ring behave as separate species while a chain of interbreeding intermediates connects them; asexual lineages with no gene flow at all; cryptic species that are reproductively isolated but morphologically indistinguishable; and hybrids and polyploids that look like species caught in the act of forming. Nobody disputes any of that. What they dispute is what follows from it.

The positions, stated as fairly as I can manage. (1) Species names a single, unified kind of thing in nature — a real level in the hierarchy of life — whatever the messy cases show about our ability to detect it. (2) Species taxa are real but are not kinds: they are particular, historically extended entities, more like individual organisms or lineages than like chemical elements, so the demand that they share a defining property is misplaced from the start. (3) There is no single thing that the term species picks out, and the honest response is pluralism — either about our concepts or about the living world itself — with different criteria counting as legitimate for different purposes. (4) The term is a convenience whose theoretical work is done better by talking about populations, lineages, gene flow, and ecological niches, and the search for its essence is a dead end we should simply abandon.

My own hypothesis, which I would like you to assess rather than accept, is that the impasse is largely an artifact of running two questions together under one word: whether particular species taxa are real entities, and whether the general category species is a natural kind — with a third, practical question about which grouping we should use for a given purpose thrown in on top. If those are genuinely separate, then much of the disagreement may be verbal, or may reduce to a disagreement about how to use the word real, and a clean distinction might dissolve it. But I am not confident, and I want the diagnosis earned rather than asserted. So: is this impasse verbal, substantive, or a case of different questions sharing a heading? If it is substantive, where exactly does the disagreement live — in the facts, in what is allowed to count as a fact, in what an explanation of classification is supposed to do, or in starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is there any case, actual or constructed, on which these positions make different predictions or issue different recommendations — and if the parties turn out to agree on every practical consequence, including which populations get protected and which get split in a field guide, what does that show about the dispute? I am also curious whether this deadlock is of the same type as the fragmentation of other biological theoretical terms, gene and fitness being the obvious ones, where the eventual response was not to crown a winner but to index the term to a purpose — or whether the species case differs in kind, perhaps because of the institutional weight the word carries in conservation law, listing decisions, and textbook taxonomy, stakes the gene and fitness disputes never had.

Given your diagnosis, finish by telling me what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, an empirical discovery, a change of question — or nothing, with an argument for why nothing should be expected. I would much rather have a diagnosis with reasons than a survey of the literature, so please engage the four positions above specifically rather than with a generic realism-versus-antirealism framing, and be explicit about which parts of the disagreement a new empirical finding could touch and which parts it could not.
