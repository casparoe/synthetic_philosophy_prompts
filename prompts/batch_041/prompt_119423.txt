Write a mock excerpt from the employee handbook of a fictional mid-sized company (name it; a logistics or software firm will serve), in which the People Operations department has adopted as formal policy what P. F. Strawson, in "Freedom and Resentment" (1962), calls the objective attitude: the stance we ordinarily reserve for small children, for the severely deranged, and for agents acting under compulsion, and which Strawson contrasts with the participant attitude — the stance we occupy when we hold someone responsible, resent them, feel gratitude toward them, forgive them, or love them. The handbook's author, a Chief People Officer with a competent but unspecialized grasp of the essay, has concluded that the objective attitude is the only stance a determinist can honestly sustain toward employees, and has drafted the policy accordingly. Write that policy straight, in the author's voice. The comedy must come from the philosophy being correct rather than from the author being a fool; a specialist in Strawson should recognize that whoever wrote the piece understands the essay, and should find the satire fair.

Requirements:

- Form and register: deadpan corporate prose, numbered sections with headings, approximately 800–1,000 words. Include at least one appendix in the form of a document to be completed (for example, a request for reclassification as an object of treatment, or an application to be restored to participant status), and at least one short rubric or table. Suggested but optional sections: the criteria and expiry terms for exemption, a manager's script for the performance conversation, a transition procedure, and a reference to an in-universe pilot programme that was quietly abandoned, explained in the same deadpan register as everything else.

- Use Strawson's machinery correctly, as he uses it, and let the coherence of the policy do the work. At a minimum the piece must handle: (i) the reactive attitudes and their division into the personal ones (resentment, gratitude), the vicarious ones (indignation and approbation felt on another's behalf), and the self-directed ones (guilt, remorse); (ii) the objective attitude itself, including Strawson's observation that it is a resource available to us toward anyone and that it is the stance of treatment, management, and the causal-explanatory view of a person; (iii) the excusing conditions, repurposed as administrative categories — the agent who is not a responsible agent at all, the agent whose act was not really his, the agent who could not help it; and (iv) the point that adopting the objective attitude toward everyone would mean the end of the relationships and responses in which the participant stance consists, which is Strawson's reason for holding that it cannot be a general policy.

- Include a clarification, in the author's own voice, that "objective" here does not mean fair, impartial, or unbiased, and that staff have repeatedly misread it that way in the feedback portal. The author himself must not commit this error.

- The document must contain at least one moment where the author's own reactive attitudes leak through the corporate register — a flash of irritation, an unmistakable note of gratitude, or a note of having been hurt — with no winking and no authorial signal that this is the point. It should read as an ordinary inconsistency in a real document, and a reader should be able to see, unprompted, that the objective attitude cannot be sustained as a general policy.

- By the end, the reader should have felt the genuine question: not whether determinism is true, but what is lost, and whether it is anything we can coherently resolve to give up, when the participant stance is retired. Do not state this question anywhere in the piece; let the handbook's own procedures raise it.

- Do not stack the deck metaphysically. The author's determinism should be the ordinary, defensible sort, and his position should be recognizable as the one Strawson attributes to the pessimist in the free will debate — that if determinism is true the reactive attitudes are unjustified and ought to be abandoned — rather than a caricature of it.

- No real quotations. Every quotation in the piece must be in-universe document text: a clause, an internal memo, a line from the pilot programme's report. Do not attribute any wording to Strawson or to any other real person, and do not place anything in quotation marks as though it came from a real text. Paraphrase his ideas freely.

- Satirize the practice, not a person: the target is the administrative fantasy of managing human beings as objects of treatment, not any living individual or actual company.

- A reader who has never encountered Strawson should be able to follow the piece and find it funny; a reader who knows the essay should find nothing in it to wince at.
