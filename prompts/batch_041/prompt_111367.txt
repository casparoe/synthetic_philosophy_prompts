I teach a 90-minute session in a graduate seminar on rational choice and social ontology — eighteen students, a mix of philosophers and economists, several of whom have already had the standard prisoner's-dilemma treatment and are consequently a bit smug about it. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the pull of superrationality in their hands rather than hear it named, and I want the pull to come from the structure of the game itself, not from anything I say at the front of the room. The target is the cluster of ideas Hofstadter raised in his 1983 Scientific American column on dilemmas for superrational thinkers and the luring lottery, later folded into Metamagical Themas: a prize whose payout depends on how many people reach for it, so that each person's individually impeccable reasoning — claiming can only help me, and my claiming exerts no causal influence on whether anyone else claims — ends up dissolving the very thing everyone was reaching for. Please design the runnable version of that, with rules, materials, roles, and a payoff scheme that uses no real money or at most a trivial real stake (candy, coffee-shop cards, a few points of ungraded course credit — something the students will actually care about for ten minutes but won't resent losing).

A few constraints on the design, since the obvious version of this game fails pedagogically. It is not enough to have a prize divided equally among all claimants; in that game claiming weakly dominates passing, so the individually rational answer and the same-reasoning-in-all-positions answer coincide, and the students learn nothing. I need a payout rule that generates a genuine divergence: the natural way is a congestion or threshold effect, so that the prize is paid only when the number of claimants is small relative to the group (or so that each additional claimant destroys more value than it captures), together with a rollover or side-payment so that universal abstention is not simply the same nothing as universal grabbing. Then run it as a ladder of rounds that vary only the information and identity conditions — simultaneous and anonymous; open pre-play discussion allowed; play against a partner who each participant is told is reasoning through exactly the same problem in exactly the same way; and one round with a planted participant who has been privately instructed to claim regardless of anything anyone says. Each round should be short enough that you can play four or five and still leave thirty minutes for the debrief. Give me the exact materials and the script for the setup, the seating or card-passing arrangement, a timeline in minutes, and the payoff tables for each round so I can photocopy them. Tell me how to handle the moment when someone works out that a coordinated small number of claimants would do best, and the moment when the group turns on the planted claimant.

The debrief is where I need the most help, and I want it mapped to specific things that will have happened rather than to a lecture. Show me how to point at the moment a student said some version of it doesn't matter what I do, at the moment someone else said but we're all in the same position, and at the moment the group's ex post story about who was selfish diverged from what the payoff structure actually rewarded. I want the debrief to distinguish at least four things that will have been in the room: dominance reasoning, superrationality as the constraint that identical reasoners reach identical answers, team or we-mode reasoning about what is best for us, and the causal-versus-evidential objection that my choice cannot reach into anyone else's head. Then tell me honestly what this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself — in particular, I suspect it cannot reproduce the scale that gives the luring lottery its bite, since eighteen people who can see each other are nothing like a readership of hundreds of thousands, and it probably cannot reproduce the case where agents are causally sealed off from one another yet known to be reasoning alike, which is exactly where the position has whatever force it has. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the collapse into haggling, the free-rider who spoils the cooperative round, the student who insists the whole thing is just a coordination problem and refuses to be puzzled — and how to keep each of those from swallowing the session.
