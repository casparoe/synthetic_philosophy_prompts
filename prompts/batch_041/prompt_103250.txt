I am a novelist, and I have reached the stage of a book where the research has to stop being notes and start being belief: I need to know what my characters could honestly have thought. The book is set in a West German university town in the middle of the 1960s, and its central quarrel is between a young historian who has fallen under the spell of Fritz Fischer and his 1961 book on Germany's war aims, and an older professor of the older, Rankean stamp who regards the whole affair as a betrayal of the profession. The younger man's talk is all primacy of domestic politics, pressure groups, war aims, the September programme; the older man answers in the language of diplomacy and the balance of power. Their argument is, at bottom, about whether a nation can be guilty.

I have been writing on the assumption that the Fischer controversy and the war-guilt clause of the Versailles Treaty finally come to the same verdict — that when Fischer argued, out of the archives, that the responsibility for the war lay with Imperial Germany, he was confirming in the calm accents of scholarship what Article 231 had proclaimed in the accents of 1919, so that a political accusation was at last converted into an historical fact. My young man says as much to his professor, and I have built the book around an image I cannot quite give up: a ledger of blame, in which every entry set down against Germany leaves a corresponding blank against someone else, so that the more the one is charged, the more the others are acquitted.

What I would like from you is a briefing I can trust. First, what the controversy over the Kriegsschuldfrage was really a dispute about, both before and after Fischer. Second, how it is that his thesis and the treaty's clause come out at the same place, so that I can have my young man say so with a clear conscience. And third, since the whole emotional weight of the book rests on it, what it means to hold a nation responsible at all, as distinct from holding responsible the particular men who happened to govern it; for I suspect the ledger may be a false image of how responsibility works, and I would rather learn that now, while the book is still fluid, than have it explained to me by a reviewer.

For this, take up the role of the ideal reader: a historian of the First World War who is also at home in moral and political philosophy, and who can move between the two without condescending to either. Think the problem through carefully before you write, and sketch the main lines of the debate for yourself before you settle on an answer; do not merely tell me what the parties said, but reason about which of them was right. Write to me as one writes a long letter to a novelist who is not a specialist — continuous prose throughout, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicised emphasis, and no tables. And write as though I have no way of looking anything up, so put whatever you invoke into your own words rather than sending me off to read someone else.
