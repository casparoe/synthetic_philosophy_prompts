You are a philosopher who works on epistemic logic and the limits of self-knowledge, and you are also an experienced writer of occasional speeches. What I need from you is an actual address, so please think the problem through carefully before you begin drafting.

The occasion is a commencement. I teach philosophy at the small liberal arts college I graduated from, and this year the faculty have asked me to give the commencement address; the class graduating in June includes students I have taught since their first year. The audience will be roughly two thousand people on a lawn: the graduates, their parents and grandparents and younger siblings, a scattering of trustees, and the faculty in regalia. The president, who knows my work, plans to introduce me as the person on this campus who has spent a career on the limits of self-knowledge. That is accurate, and it is also a trap.

Here is my difficulty, and it is the reason I am asking for help rather than writing this myself. I have spent twenty years defending two claims. The first is that knowing something does not entail knowing that you know it; the KK principle is false. The second is that our own mental states are not luminous — there is no mental state such that, whenever you are in it, you are in a position to know that you are in it. The margin-for-error reasoning that gets you the second claim also gets you exactly this much and no more: it does not show that self-knowledge is impossible, or that you never know your own mind. It shows that there is no guarantee, at any given moment, that you do.

The trouble is that the commencement address is a genre built almost entirely out of the moves I have spent my career denying. The familiar uplift depends on them: that the graduates already know what they love and need only the courage to act on it; that they should trust what they know deep down; that they will know it when they find it; that a still, small, unmistakable inner voice will tell them. I cannot say any of that, and I also cannot stand at a podium in front of two thousand people, most of whom have never read a word of philosophy, and deliver a lecture on margin-for-error reasoning. One trustee has already told the president that I am the man who thinks we cannot know our own minds. That is not my view, and I would like the address to quietly correct it without scolding him.

What I want, and what I believe is available, is a speech that is honest without being deflating. There is one thing I can say to them that I believe entirely, and it is weaker than the usual promise but I think it is better: you may well be right about what you want, and about what you feel, and about who you are, without being in a position to know that you are right — and you will have to act anyway. I want the address to land on that difference, between being right and being in a position to know that you are, and I want it to land as a permission rather than as a warning. An audience that leaves thinking mainly that the speaker warned them about the unreliability of their own minds has been badly served. An audience that leaves thinking that they are allowed to move without a guarantee has been well served.

Some requirements:

- Build the address around one concrete case from ordinary life rather than a philosopher's example. Do not use the stock cases from the literature — the lukewarm bath, the chicken in the refrigerator, the heap of sand. The faculty will recognize them and the parents will feel they are being taught. Use a case of the right shape: someone in a state of mind that mattered, who could not have known at the time that she was in it, and who learned what she had felt only afterwards, or only by acting. I am willing to be the case myself if that helps. When I was twenty-three I took a job I was proud of, and for three years I could not have told you whether I liked it — I could not tell whether the flatness I felt on Sunday evenings was about the work or about me — and I did not learn what I had felt until I quit. Use that if it serves, or invent a case of the same shape that serves better.
- The speech itself must be free of technical vocabulary: no luminosity, no margin for error, no epistemic, no jargon of any kind. The faculty in the front rows should be able to hear what I am doing; the parents in the back rows must be able to enjoy it without a footnote.
- The address must contain at least one moment where it openly declines the uplift the audience is expecting, and still functions as uplift. If you conclude that this cannot be done honestly, tell me so in your note rather than faking it.
- No quotations, and no attributions of lines to anyone. I will not read a quotation from a podium that I cannot check, and I do not want the speech resting on any text. Paraphrase if you need to gesture at an idea, and do not name philosophers. No readings, no citations — this is a speech, not a syllabus.
- Do not invent institutional details. No college name, no motto, no traditions, no mascot, no names of students or colleagues. Keep the place and the people unspecified.
- Aim for about seven minutes: roughly one thousand to twelve hundred words, written to be spoken, with short sentences where the stakes are high and no piled-up subordinate clauses.
- Warmth matters more than cleverness. The most important thing is that it not sound like a philosopher performing honesty.

Before you draft, work out in your thinking the list of standard commencement moves that presuppose that the graduates know their own minds, and decide which of them you are giving up outright and which, if any, can be repaired rather than discarded. Then write.

Give me the text of the address itself; I do not want an outline or a summary of it. After the address, add no more than four sentences naming the moves you gave up and what you put in their place.
