I'm a science-and-ideas journalist — I write a weekly column and a newsletter, mostly philosophy of science and decision theory — and I've definately become the person in my friend group who explains betting to everyone else, which is a long story involving a poker night, a spreadsheet, and one friend who has since become unbearable about spreadsheets. I'm doing a piece on Kelly betting and the thing people call geometric rationality, and I've decided it should open with a joke rather than three paragraphs of setup, because the setup always reads like a textbook and a joke might actually carry the idea. So: write me the joke.

Here's the material it needs to be funny about, so you're not guessing at it. Kelly betting: when you get to bet repeatedly on a favourable gamble, the growth-maximising stake is a fixed fraction of your bankroll, and betting bigger than that wrecks you through compounding even though every individual bet is +EV. Maximising expected log wealth gives you the same stake, which is where the geometric bit comes from — you're pushing up the geometric mean of your outcomes, not the arithmetic one — and that same stake also happens to be what you'd choose if you only cared about the very next bet, which I'd always assumed was a coincidence and apparently isn't. The part I find funniest, and the part I want the joke to live off, is that the Kelly bettor says no to bets with positive expected value: bets the plain expected-value maximiser takes without blinking, because what's being put at risk is the bankroll all the future bets are going to need. My favourite illustration of the flavour, though don't just transcribe this one — offer someone a coin that lands their way 60 per cent of the time at even money, all or nothing, stake the entire bankroll: the EV maximiser snaps it up, the Kelly bettor says no, because zero is absorbing and no amount of edge gets you back out of it. So the punchline has to turn on the actual content; someone who knows this stuff should laugh at the right moment, and someone who doesn't shouldn't be able to laugh just because there's a nerdy reference in there. No names, no papers, no citations, nothing that only lands if you've read one particular blog post — it has to work for a reader with no internet and no library, which is also to say, don't put anything in it you'd be guessing at.

Take yourself to be someone who knows this material cold and has also actually written comedy — a decision theorist who's done a few open mics, say — and have a proper think before you commit: brainstorm a few angles, notice which of them are just puns in a lab coat, and write the one that lands rather than the first thing you think of. Then stick a short note underneath, a few sentences, saying where the joke takes liberties with the theory or leans on a contested reading of it (is it pretending Kelly is the uniquely rational policy? is it smuggling in a claim about repeated bets, or about knowing the probabilities exactly? is the non-ergodicity framing doing more work than it can bear?), and tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration about Kelly betting, if it turned out to be right, would make you rewrite the punchline rather than keep it. I'd rather have an honest caveat than a joke that's quietly wrong. Oh, and its going in a column, so it has to survive being read by people who don't know what a bankroll is.
