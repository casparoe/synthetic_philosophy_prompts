I run a monthly reading group for people who like philosophy but aren't in the field -- a few lapsed grad students, a couple of retired engineers, one guy who somehow brings up Buddhism in every session. Next month I'm doing infinite ethics and I've got about seventy minutes. Don't explain the topic to me, plan the session for me: what order the ideas have to come in so the puzzle lands as a real puzzle and not a party trick, what single example should carry the whole thing, which misconceptions they'll walk in with and which ones they'll form as I go, exactly where in the sequence I should head each of those off, and one thing I can ask at the end that tells me whether they've got the structure of the problem rather than just been amused by infinity. And please don't tell me to assign them any reading, this crowd won't do it and it'll just eat the time.

My instinct is to build it around a case I made up rather than Hilbert's hotel, because they've all heard Hilbert's hotel and it makes the whole area feel like a cute trick rather than a problem. Here's the case: universe A is a countable infinity of people, every one of them with a life that's barely worth living, call it +1. Universe B is the same infinite line of people at +1, except it also contains a second countable infinity of people whose lives are miserable, call it -1. Total welfare in A is infinite; in B it's infinity minus infinity, which is exactly the kind of thing that makes the whole framework wobble. Is A better than B? I want the session to get them to see that the obvious answer (yes, obviously, B has all that extra suffering piled on) is doing something they haven't earned yet, and that the reason the framework wobbles isn't just that infinity is weird. If my case is the wrong one for that job, tell me what to swap in and why, and tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level.

Two things I care about. First, my fear is that they'll leave thinking the lesson is 'infinity is confusing, therefore nothing can be said,' which is definately not what I want them to take away -- so somewhere the sequence has to earn the idea that some comparisons are still tractable even when the totals aren't. Second, be careful to separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested. I don't want to hand these people a confident consensus that turns out to be one camp's position wearing a lab coat. Mark clearly which parts of the picture are agreed and which are live disputes, and if you think my +1 / -1 framing smuggles in an assumption -- that there's a fact about how much good a life contains, or that these amounts can be added across an infinite population at all -- say so.
