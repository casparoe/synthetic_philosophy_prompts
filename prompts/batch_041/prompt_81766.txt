I host a monthly interview series on the philosophy of science, and the guest for the next episode is a philosopher of science whose recent work defends a revised falsificationism. Her central move is to relocate the demarcation criterion from statements to what she calls error-detection regimes. A discipline is scientific, on her account, to the extent that it sustains a standing practice of putting its claims to tests whose possible outcomes are fixed in advance and whose failure would, by the discipline's own stated rules of procedure, oblige it to revise or abandon the claim. Two features of the position matter for the interview. First, she does not require individual hypotheses to be falsifiable: a hypothesis that has been insulated from test can persist within a scientific discipline, on her account, provided the discipline's procedures would eventually force it out. Second, she insists that the view is continuous with Popper's project rather than a sociological replacement for it: the criterion remains normative, but its subject matter is the practice of error-detection rather than the logical form of sentences. She further maintains that the familiar objections — that a test always bears on a conjunction of claims rather than on a single hypothesis, that accepting a test result is itself a decision, that some form of ad hoc rescue is always available — all target the statement-level version of falsificationism and therefore leave her version untouched.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions for the episode, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, plus a one-line note stating the specific move the question is designed to force her to make. The questions should be usable as written by an interviewer who has read her work but who is not a specialist, and they should be answerable from the position as described above together with general philosophy of science; do not ask her to name books, papers, or authors, and do not ask her to summarize Popper.

The set must include at least one question on each of the following, in whatever order makes for the best interview:

- The conjunction problem as it applies to regimes rather than statements: since every test bears on a bundle of hypothesis plus auxiliaries, ask what within the practice fixes the blame for a failed test, and what would prevent a discipline from counting as error-detecting simply by assigning blame to an auxiliary every time.
- The status of the test outcome that is supposed to be fixed in advance: who fixes it, by what authority, and what stops the specification from being renegotiated once the result is in — and whether the answer collapses her view into the conventionalism she wants to avoid.
- The eventually clause: what distinguishes an insulated hypothesis that a regime will in fact force out from one that it will not, given that the eventual forcing is not observable at any particular time; press on whether the clause does any work or merely postpones the demarcation.
- Statistical and probabilistic hypotheses: no finite run of outcomes refutes a probability claim, so ask what failure looks like for a regime testing such a claim, and whether registering it requires a significance convention that is itself not testable.
- Self-application: whether her own criterion meets her own standard, and if she replies that it is a proposal for how to use a word rather than a claim about the world, press on whether that makes the choice arbitrary and what could rationally constrain it.
- The degree-theoretic consequence: if scientific status comes in degrees, what her view implies for a discipline that has the institutions but no successful tests, and for one with successful tests but no institutions.
- Whether a regime can be identified independently of the demarcation it is meant to ground, or whether the account is circular — the practice being picked out by the very feature it is supposed to explain.

Close the interview with a question about what would count against her account, and make it one she cannot answer by redescribing her view as a stipulation about how the word science ought to be used.
