You are a philosopher of science with a working specialism in formal epistemology and the theory of rational belief change. I want you to act as an adjudicator rather than an advocate. Work out what is actually at issue before you judge anything; where a verdict depends on a contested assumption, say which assumption and why it matters, rather than smoothing the disagreement over. I am not asking for a literature survey or for attributions of these positions to anyone — I want the argument done properly from the ideas themselves.

Background. At a reading group last week we went through the standard treatment of Jeffrey conditionalization. The rule, as it was presented to us: when an observation leaves you short of certainty about every proposition, you hold your conditional probabilities on the evidence partition fixed and re-average them using your new probabilities for the members of that partition. The illustration was the familiar one: I look at a cloth by candlelight, it looks green to me, and because candlelight distorts colour I end up, say, 0.7 confident that the cloth is green rather than certain of it.

Two other members, Rosalind and Dominic, kept arguing for forty minutes after the meeting ended. What follows is my best reconstruction of the exchange. I have not taken sides, and I would like you to judge the moves as they were made rather than the conclusion either of them would prefer.

(1) Dominic: the rule solves a problem that does not exist. What I learn when the cloth looks green to me is a fact about me — that I am having a greenish visual experience of this cloth, now — and that is something I can be certain of. Certain evidence is exactly what ordinary conditionalization was designed for: condition on it and I am finished. Nor do I need any exotic likelihoods for that; how often a green cloth looks green in candlelight, and how often a white one does, is ordinary empirical knowledge. So-called uncertain evidence is always certain evidence about one's own experience, and probability kinematics is at best a convenience of notation.

(2) Rosalind: in the case as set up, there is nothing there to condition on. My uncertainty is not about how the cloth looks but about what colour it is; I can be certain of the one and unsure of the other. If the experiential proposition was already certain, conditioning on it does nothing at all — a proposition whose probability is 1 before and 1 after cannot be what moves my credence in 'green' from where it starts to where it ends. Your version quietly substitutes a different case, one in which I do not yet know how the cloth will look; and then the likelihoods you need are likelihoods for a description of the experience fine-grained enough to determine what I should conclude, which is precisely the knowledge the original case denies me.

(3) Dominic: then your rule has the agent changing her mind with nothing to change it. On your own account my certainties about my experience are the same before and after; if the evidence is unchanged, the credences should be unchanged too, and a rule that lets them move anyway describes drift rather than prescribing anything. It is one thing to say that experience is evidentially relevant without being certain; it is another to say that a credence can rationally shift while nothing at all is learned.

(4) Rosalind: you are assuming that evidence must arrive as a proposition the agent is certain of, and that is exactly the assumption the case is meant to put under pressure. On the rule's own account the input is not a certain proposition but a new distribution over the evidence partition, and the change in that distribution just is what the observation contributes. The rule does not say that nothing was learned; it says that what is learned need not be something the agent now takes to be certain.

(5) Dominic: then the agent must supply as input a precise new probability for the partition — to know that it is 0.7 and not 0.65 — and must hold her conditionals on that partition fixed across the very observation that moves the marginal. Why should the conditional probability of the cloth's being green given how it looks survive an observation that is, after all, evidence about how badly candlelight misleads me? If the observation bears on my perceptual reliability, the conditionals move, and the rule's fixed point moves with them.

(6) Rosalind: the invariance is a constraint, not an empirical claim. It marks off the case in which you are taking in evidence from the case in which you are re-evaluating your way of gathering it; if your conditionals move, you are doing the second thing, and the rule tells you to re-derive them and apply it again. And the demand for a precise input is a demand every rule faces — ordinary conditionalization also requires you to know what your evidence is.

(7) Dominic: at least conditioning on the experience takes the answer out of the agent's hands. If I condition on the experiential proposition, my new credences follow from the experience together with my prior. With probability kinematics I must additionally decide how confident to be in each member of the partition, and the rule says nothing about how to choose the partition; and successive updates on different partitions do not commute, so the order in which I happen to notice things leaves a permanent trace in my credences. A norm of belief change should not depend on the order in which the evidence happens to arrive.

(8) Rosalind: that order-dependence is what you should expect. The second observation is not the same item of evidence once the first has been absorbed, so this is not one body of evidence being processed in two different orders. And conditioning on experience faces the same choice of how finely to describe the experience, with no guarantee that the answers for different choices agree with one another.

(a) A short preliminary: state the rule in your own terms, and identify which of its features are definitional and which are substantive commitments.
(b) A move-by-move adjudication of (1) to (8), rating each from clearly mistaken, through questionable and defensible, to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Where a move is right in part, separate the part that stands from the part that does not.
(c) The two or three deepest points of genuine disagreement between them, as distinct from points where they are talking past each other or using 'evidence' in different senses.
(d) Whether the candlelight case really does require a rule other than ordinary conditionalization, and if so under what assumptions; and if Dominic's reduction can be made to work, exactly what it costs.
(e) Who has the better of the exchange overall, and what both of them are missing.

Be as precise as the material allows, and do not paper over the places where the answer turns on a substantive assumption about what evidence is.
