I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works on formal semantics, and we ended exactly where we began, which has left me wondering whether we were even disagreeing about the same thing. She is a supervaluationist about vagueness: on her view a predicate like 'tall' or 'heap' has multiple admissible precisifications, a sentence counts as true when it comes out true on all of them and false when false on all of them, and in the borderline region it is neither — truth-value gaps, but classical logic preserved by redefining validity in terms of supertruth. I hold the epistemicist line instead: the predicate has a unique, perfectly determinate extension, bivalence holds all the way down, and the reason a borderline case feels unsettled is that the boundary is unknowable rather than absent. What struck me afterwards is that we agreed on almost every datum — the sorites series, the tolerance intuition, the existence of higher-order vagueness — and diverged only in what we did with them. She treats the thought that a vague predicate cannot have a sharp cut-off as a constraint any adequate theory has to honour, and concludes that bivalence fails; I treat bivalence and classical consequence as non-negotiable and conclude that the tolerance intuition is an artifact of our epistemic limits. When I pressed her on where the boundary of 'definitely tall' sits, she produced the higher-order hierarchy; when she pressed me, I produced the hierarchy of unknowability; and it occurred to me, mid-argument, that we might have been drawing the same formal picture and disagreeing only about which term gets to be primitive.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I want it to engage with what each of us actually says rather than with a survey of the literature. Is this a verbal dispute — the two of us assigning different truth-conditions to 'true' or 'borderline' and then talking past each other — or a substantive disagreement about whether the world really contains a boundary that our language is tracking? Is it a disagreement about which intuitions are allowed to count as data, or about the standards a semantic theory has to meet (preserve classical logic versus accommodate tolerance), or is there a hidden formal result doing the work — for instance, a translation showing the two frameworks are interdefinable and differ only in what they take as primitive, which would make the quarrel interpretive rather than factual? And if that last hypothesis is right, is there a genuine question left over that the translation leaves untouched, or is the whole debate a sign that we have been asking a question that admits of two equally good regimentations and no fact of the matter? My own tentative hypothesis is that neither of us is confused about the logic and that the real disagreement is about which intuition is permitted to be non-negotiable, but I hold that loosely. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a proof, a case one side cannot accommodate without cheating, a concession about which side is answering a different question — or whether I should accept that the impasse is permanent and stop losing sleep over it.
