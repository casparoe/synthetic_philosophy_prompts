Here's the setup. There's an old fight about what the "norm of assertion" is: what has to be true of you for it to be okay to flat-out say that something is the case. Some people say you've gotta know it. Some say you only need to believe it. Some say it just needs to be true. That fight I already know about. What I want is for you to take a newer idea seriously and see whether it actually beats the others.

Let's call it the credit account. Rough idea: your allowed to flat-out assert that p just in case you'd be willing to put your credibility on the line for p — meaning if it turns out false, you'd accept that you owed people something (a correction, an apology, a hit to your reputation), and you wouldn't feel that the loss was unfair or a sucker's game. No knowledge required, maybe not even belief required — just that willingness to be on the hook.

So write me an essay on whether the credit account is genuinely better than the knowledge account (and the belief and truth accounts, if they're even worth bothering with). Test it against the hard cases: the person who guesses right by pure luck, the person with excellent evidence for something false, the liar who knows better, the person who says something true but has no clue. Where does the credit account shine, and where does it fall over?

Don't end all wishy-washy. Land on a verdict — is the credit account the right story, or does it quietly collapse into one of the older views, or is it a real rival with its own turf?
