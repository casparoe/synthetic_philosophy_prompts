I belong to a small reading group in philosophy of science, and last week two of the other members had an exchange that neither of them could resolve. I was asked to referee it and found I could not, so I would like the arguments sorted out rather than the dispute settled by fiat.

The subject was the old puzzle about a generalization and its contrapositive. The first speaker opened by claiming that the green shirt he was wearing confirms that all ravens are black: 'all ravens are black' and 'all non-black things are non-ravens' are logically equivalent, the shirt is an instance of the second, and equivalent hypotheses must be supported by the same evidence.

The second speaker objected that this is a trick of the word 'all' — that a shirt tells you nothing whatever about ravens. He replied that he would accept the objection only if she would name which of the two principles she was giving up, since both that a generalization is supported by its instances and that logically equivalent hypotheses are supported by the same evidence look non-negotiable, and together they yield his conclusion.

She said the instance principle is the loose one, and that if 'confirms' means 'raises the probability', the shirt raises it only on the assumption that the object was drawn at random from everything there is, and then only by an amount too small to be worth a thought. She added that he had not drawn at random: he had looked at his own shirt, which he was going to look at regardless of any raven.

He answered that this was a concession — the shirt does confirm, just barely — so the interesting question is why the contrary intuition is so loud. He then observed that on her account the amount of confirmation would depend on how much of the world is not black, which he took to be absurd, since how much a shirt tells him about ravens should not depend on how many green things happen to exist. She replied that this dependence is simply what sampling from a finite world looks like, and that the shirt's little bit of force comes from eliminating one candidate for being the non-black raven.

Finally he proposed a criterion: evidence confirms a hypothesis only if the evidence would have been different had the hypothesis been false. A shirt would have been green either way, whereas a raven that is black would not have been black had the generalization failed. She answered that the criterion proves far too much, since any particular black raven would still have been black even if the generalization were false — the falsifying bird being some other one — and that what is really doing the work in both cases is a comparison of how likely the observation is under the hypothesis and under its denial, a comparison that cannot be made without specifying how the observation was obtained, which is precisely what neither of them had stated.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken through clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then say what each of the two is missing, and who is closer to right overall.
