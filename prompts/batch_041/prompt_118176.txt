I'm giving a talk at a small workshop on AI deception next month and I'm defending a definition I expect almost noone in the room to like, so I need a Q&A prep sheet. My line: a system deceives a user when its output moves the user's beliefs away from where they'd have landed had the same question been put without the pressure that made the true answer inconvenient -- so deception is a gap between the answer given and the answer under fair framing, not the presence of a false proposition. I call it the framing-gap view. Two consequences people find irritating: honesty isn't the complement of deception (a model can be scrupulously accurate and still deceive, and can be flatly wrong without deceiving anybody), and paltering -- deceiving someone using nothing but true statements -- comes out as the central case rather than a curiosity. The room is four kinds of people: a couple of Gricean pragmatics folk, several evals engineers who think honesty just is calibration and want everything operationalised, two policy lawyers who reason in terms of materiality and intent, and one virtue epistemologist. Give me the objections each of them will actually raise, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is thinnest. Then the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. British English please.
