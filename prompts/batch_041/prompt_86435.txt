I am preparing a one-page handout for the opening meeting of a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and I want that handout to be a decision tree rather than a summary of the literature. The tree should take a reader who begins from the bare admission that we do in fact resent, blame, feel gratitude toward, and forgive one another — and who is willing to treat those attitudes as something other than idle noises — and route that reader, by a sequence of yes/no questions, to one of the positions in the debate that P. F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes set going.

Design constraints:

- Every internal node must be phrased as a question the reader can answer on the basis of a concrete case or a first-order claim, without already knowing which position it leads to. No node may simply ask whether the reader accepts Strawson's view, and no node may name a position as part of the question.
- The branching questions, not a list of labels, must carry the structure. Build the logical space first — the combinations of answers the questions permit — and then locate the actual positions within it.
- Use the distinctions that genuinely do the separating work, and make the reader commit on each: whether the appropriateness of resentment turns on the agent's having had a strong ability to do otherwise or only on the quality of will her action expressed; whether determinism, if true, would threaten the attitudes or leave them untouched; whether suspending resentment toward someone is a matter of exempting her from the participant stance altogether or of excusing a particular act within it; whether the attitudes make responsibility claims true or merely track an independent fact; and whether blame and resentment should be assessed one by one or as a single package.
- The tree must include at least the following seven leaves, described here by their commitments rather than by their labels, since I want you to decide whether each has a standard name: (i) the attitudes are self-standing, and the demand for a justification of them from outside our practices is confused; (ii) blame is governed by a norm of fairness, so the attitudes are warranted when the agent had the capacity to meet a reasonable expectation; (iii) a thin attributability sense of responsibility should be separated from a thicker accountability sense, with the reactive attitudes belonging to the latter; (iv) blame and resentment express the value we place on a relationship rather than tracking desert; (v) the attitudes cannot be justified in a desert-entailing form and blame as such should be given up, perhaps in favor of a detached analogue; (vi) they are warranted only if the agent had a strong ability to do otherwise; (vii) they are warranted whenever the action expressed an objectionable quality of will, determinism notwithstanding. Name any leaf that has a standard name, and mark as unnamed any that does not.
- Flag every pair of leaves your questions cannot separate, and say in one sentence whether the two are genuinely distinct or merely differently labeled.

Two probes must appear somewhere in the tree, because the usual summaries skip them: reactive attitudes directed at the dead, and the objective attitude turned on oneself. Use them to force the reader to say whether the attitudes depend on the possibility of reciprocation and answerability or are independent of it.

Keep the tree to roughly eight to twelve nodes. For each leaf, add one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the thing a defender of that position has to swallow. Do not include a reading list, and do not include citations.
