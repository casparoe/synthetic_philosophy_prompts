I've lived in Rotterdam for six years and Osaka for four, and I've ended up with a pile of gut feelings about when it's ok to say the true thing and when it's ok to say the nice thing, and I can't get them to line up. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, is behind them, and what it costs me.

Here's what I think. A Dutch colleague once told me straight out that the middle of my presentation made no sense — unasked, in front of two other people — and I was grateful. My boss in Osaka told me it might be worth looking at that part again, everyone in the room understood it meant it was bad, and I was grateful for that too, maybe more. A friend asked what I thought of his business plan and I told him it was good when I didn't think it was, and that still bothers me. I tell my mum her cooking is lovely when the chicken is dry, and I dont feel a thing about it. I told my four year old her drawing was the best one on the fridge, same. My neighbour asked whether I'd liked the speech he gave at his dad's funeral — it was a mess, he'd already given it, and I said it was lovely. That one nags at me and I don't know why, since nothing could be changed by then.

Here's a guess at my own principle: it's fine to leave someone in the dark but not to put them in the dark. It might be worth another look leaves you unsure; the cheque is in the post puts a false thing in your head. But telling my mum I loved the dry chicken also puts a false thing in her head, and I'd do it again tomorrow.

My other guess is that it depends what the person does with it. Dry chicken, nobody does anything. Business plan, he remorgages the house. But that makes my neighbour's speech fine, and it isn't, and it also can't explain why the same words would have annoyed me from a Dutchman — if a Dutch colleague had said it might be worth another look at my slides I'd have thought he was being slimy. So the words are fine in Osaka and not in Rotterdam, which means the principle has to say something about what the listener expects. But expectation can't be the whole story either, because the person waiting for the cheque also expects the truth, and lying to them is still wrong.

The one I keep going round on: my aunt has dementia and asks me most days whether her son has rung. He hasn't, not in years. I say he's been busy and will ring soon. It doesn't change anything she does, she won't remember it by dinner, and it stops her crying for an hour. Some days I think it's obviously fine, other days I think I'm stealing something from her, and I can't tell which day I'm right on.

So: what's the principle, stated properly, that gets most of these? Which of my gut calls does it make me give up, and is there a version that saves all of them, or does it get ugly? And say what it predicts about something I haven't thought of — pick your own case, maybe one where the person could act on the truth but only in a way that hurts them. No need for any reading, I'm not a student, I just want the answer.
