I'm in the middle of a long essay for a seminar that's cross-listed between the philosophy and English departments — the topic is moral luck, and we've spent weeks on Nagel's four flavours of it (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal) and on Williams's lorry driver who, through no fault of his own, runs over a child and is never the same again even though nobody blames him. The whole time I kept thinking about Hamlet, which I've taught twice to undergrads and somehow never once connected to any of this. Here's the thing that's eating at me: the play is practically a casebook. Hamlet stabs through the arras in his mother's closet and kills Polonius when he meant to kill Claudius — wrong man, same intent — and the play, and Hamlet himself, treat the deed as simply his, no asterisk about the luck of who happened to be standing behind the curtain. He doesn't seem to feel the special kind of regret Williams says the unlucky agent feels; he says something dismissive over the body and moves on, and the play barely pauses. Then the ending is decided by a swapped rapier and a cup nobody meant for Gertrude to drink, and Hamlet's late remark about a divinity that shapes our ends however we rough-hew them sounds like the whole thesis in verse. But I can't tell wether Shakespeare is dramatizing Nagel's point — that what we're morally on the hook for is partly a matter of luck we don't control — or quietly resisting it, because the play also seems to insist that character is fate: that it's Hamlet's hesitancy, his melancholy, the rottenness of Elsinore he was born into, that damns him, and the luck is just the mechanism.

So what I want from you is a real reading of Hamlet through the moral-luck literature, not a plot summary and not a list of themes. Which of Nagel's kinds of luck does the play actually dramatize, and which does it ignore or refuse? Does it come down on the side of the theorists or against them — does it show us that moral assessment is luck-infected, or does it stage a world where providence and conscience still hold agents fully to account, so that the philosophers' picture starts to look like a secular distortion? Point me to the scenes and speeches that carry the weight, especially the killing of Polonius, the graveyard, and the duel, and say honestly where the reading breaks down and the play resists it — I don't want the play reduced to an illustration of a paper, and I don't want the theory bent to fit the play. And finish by telling me what the play adds to the debate that the arguments alone can't: does Shakespeare see something about luck and blame that Nagel and Williams miss? No citations or reading lists needed, I've got the texts — I just need the argument.
