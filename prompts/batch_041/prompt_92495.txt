You are a philosopher who works on individual responsibility for collective harms — the cluster of problems that begins with the question of what difference one person's act makes — and you are unusually good at reconstructing the principle implicit in a person's scattered case-by-case verdicts, then testing that principle against a case it has never been applied to. Please work carefully and in stages: first set out the candidate principles that could generate the record below, then check each against the whole record, and only then commit to a prediction. Do not stop at the first principle that fits; several will fit, and the interest lies in the differences among them. I am not asking for literature or citations — I want your reasoning about this particular person.

The record belongs to a friend of mine, who did graduate work in ethics and now works in policy, and it concerns her own consumption and civic behaviour. She is not an ascetic and not a maximizer: she flies, drives, drinks coffee, and thinks people who agonize over every purchase have lost the plot. Her verdicts, however, are not the ones I would have predicted.

1. Supermarket beef. She will not buy a beef burger from the supermarket, and does not think I should either. She is unmoved by the fact that the store placed its weekly order days ago, and that the burger I leave behind will be bought by someone else or thrown out. She says the purchase is wrong anyway.

2. The binned burger. She will eat a beef burger that her housemate bought and cooked, when the alternative is the bin. She says there is nothing wrong with that.

3. Chicken. If she is going to eat meat at all, she says the chicken is the worse choice, and not by a little: she reasons in deaths per calorie, and on that measure a chicken purchase is tied to something like two hundred times as many animal deaths as a beef purchase of equal calories.

4. Humane beef. Beef from a small farm where the cattle are pasture-raised and killed on the farm is, in her view, still wrong — less bad than the supermarket version, but wrong.

5. Dinner at a friend's. As a guest, when the host has bought and cooked the meat, she eats it. She says she has done nothing wrong: the host would have bought the same food whether or not she came.

6. Voting. She did not vote at the last election and does not think that was a wrong, although her seat was decided by a few hundred votes. Her view is that the chance her vote would have decided anything was too small to be the sort of thing that makes a person a wrongdoer.

7. Flying. She takes one long-haul flight a year to see her family and does not think that is a wrong. She is not a climate sceptic; she says the people she cannot defend are the ones who fly every month.

Here is the case I have not put to her, and the reason I am writing. I am about to buy fish. It comes from a fishery whose total catch is capped by an international regulator on scientific advice, and this year's catch is already landed and frozen: the number of fish killed this year will be identical whether or not I buy, and the only thing my purchase can move is the price. Assume the cap is genuinely binding and that the regulator does not respond to consumer prices. I am not asking about the deaths-per-calorie comparison — she would say the fish is the better choice than the chicken on that measure. I am asking whether she will say that buying it is wrong at all, and if so on what ground.

Please give me: (a) the two or three principles that best fit the record, each stated precisely enough to generate verdicts, together with which of her verdicts each principle explains and which it strains; (b) what each surviving principle predicts about my fish, and exactly where the predictions come apart; (c) your best guess at her verdict, the principle you think is doing the work, and your confidence; (d) the single case or question I should put to her next that would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving principles, and what each possible answer would show; and (e) any verdict in the record that no single principle seems able to accommodate — and if you conclude that she is simply inconsistent, say which verdicts you would drop and why.
