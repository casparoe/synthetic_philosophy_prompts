Write the closing speech for the final assembly of a village primary school that has served the same parish for a hundred and thirty years and is now being shut down, its remaining pupils to be bussed to a larger school in the next town. The speaker is the last head teacher. The audience is the final cohort of children, their parents and grandparents, alumni who have returned for the occasion, and a local councillor who will speak afterwards and has already told the newspaper that the children's education will improve.

The speech must give an idea structural work rather than ornamental mention: that the closure is a loss over and above any harm to the people involved -- over and above the children's disrupted schooling and over and above anything anyone will remember -- because every generation who taught or studied here, back to the founders, worked on the assumption that there would be a continuing future of the school, and closing it breaks something that all those projects presupposed. The head teacher should confront the obvious objection head-on: if no one will be left to be saddened by the absence, what is the loss supposed to consist in? The answer must not appeal to a school spirit, to an afterlife, or to the consolation that the school will live on in memory; the speech should make plain why promising that the school will live on in memory answers a different question from the one being asked. One grandparent has been saying that a thing which ends well is simply finished and needs no continuation, and the speech should answer that too, without turning into a lecture. About 700 words, in the voice of someone speaking to neighbours.
