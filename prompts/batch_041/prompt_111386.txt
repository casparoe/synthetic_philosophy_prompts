I teach a third-year seminar on philosophy of the social sciences, and the final is a 90-minute in-class essay exam. Last year I set a question on Popper's case against historicism and got back forty near-identical recitations of the same four steps -- knowledge grows, we can't predict the growth of knowledge, so we can't predict history, so no laws of historical development -- and almost nobody willing to say whether the argument is actually any good. So this year I want one essay question that makes them get their hands dirty with the load-bearing premise. In particular I want them to have to decide whether Popper needs only the claim that the growth of knowledge is unpredictable, or the much stronger claim that it is lawless, and whether he can actually get the stronger one without begging a question against his opponent; and I want them to have to say what would count as a law of history in the first place, because half of them can't tell a trend from a law and the other half think that once you've said 'conditional prediction' the whole thing is over. I'd also like the question to force them to deal with the objection that comes up in office hours every single year, namely that we do seem able to forecast some social things quite well and quite far out -- population being the standard example, and there's a whole industry of projecting it decades ahead -- so either those forecasts aren't what they look like or Popper's line is too strong. And last term one student tried to argue that Popper is hoist by his own petard, because his own appeal to unintended consequences, against the view that whatever happens in society is just somebody's plan being executed, is itself doing law-like work; I don't want to hand them that answer, but I want the question to make it available to the good ones.

So please write me: the essay question itself (one question, answerable in 90 minutes, at most a couple of sub-parts, and it shouldn't hinge on anything they'd have to have looked up -- no dates, no obscure texts, no names they won't have seen); a model answer of about 800 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer has to contain, the specific moves that cap a candidate at B level, and a one-line note for each point on how candidates typically get it wrong. Don't bother with references or page numbers in the model answer -- they're working from excerpts and lectures and citations aren't the point -- and please don't have the model answer lean on anything that only an online search could supply, since the exam is closed-book and effectively offline. We do read the Popper book plus a bit of Hempel on covering laws and some Hayek, so the model answer can take those as fair game, but the question itself should be answerable by someone who only really understood the Popper and thought hard about it.
