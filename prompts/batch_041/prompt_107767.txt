I would like a careful conceptual explanation of the difference between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness, built around a case that I think exposes the real fault line rather than around the textbook contrast.

Imagine a community that introduces a predicate for a purpose of its own. A licensing board must classify buildings as safe for occupancy along a continuous scale of structural soundness, and it writes down an explicit rule fixing where the cutoff falls. Suppose the rule is itself stated in vague terms, so that there are buildings about which it is unclear whether the rule classifies them as safe, and further buildings about which it is unclear whether they fall in that first unclear zone. The community has no further facts to consult: the predicate's extension is fixed entirely by the rule and by the ordinary meanings of the words in it.

Against that background, please explain the following in an intuitive register, without softening the technical points.

First, what epistemicism says is going on when a competent speaker cannot tell whether a given building is safe, and what it says about the truth value of the sentence asserting that such a building is safe. In particular, explain what work the claim that there is a sharp but unknowable cutoff is doing, and why an epistemicist insists that positing such a cutoff does not make the predicate non-vague.

Second, what supervaluationism says instead, and why it is willing to say that the sentence is neither true nor false while still holding that the corresponding disjunction — that the building either is safe or is not — is true. Explain clearly how both of those verdicts can be maintained at once, and what the notion of a precisification is supposed to be doing here.

Third, the higher-order vagueness problem as it bears on the contrast. For a supervaluationist, what makes a building definitely safe rather than merely safe? Explain why the same reasoning that opened a gap at the first order seems to reopen one at every higher order, and why a critic charges that this either collapses into a sharp higher-order boundary — and so into a version of epistemicism — or else runs into an infinite regress. Say what a supervaluationist can and cannot say in reply.

Fourth, tell me whether the two views actually disagree about anything at the level of use, or whether they agree on every verdict a competent speaker would reach and differ only in the metaphysical and semantic story they tell about those verdicts. If the latter, say what kind of consideration could make the dispute more than terminological.

I am not interested in a survey of who held which view, nor in references. I want the conceptual machinery laid out so that I can see precisely where the two accounts diverge and why each is tempted by its own picture.
