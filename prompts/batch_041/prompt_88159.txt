I have no philosophical training, so what follows is my own attempt to think a puzzle through, and I would rather you tell me where my framing is confused than politely answer around it.

The case. I am stranded in a desert and a driver stops. He will take me to town only if I promise to pay him a thousand dollars on arrival. Once we arrive I can simply walk away: he cannot sue me, he cannot harm me, and we will never meet again. It seems clear that I should make the promise. It is equally clear that, once we arrive, handing over the money will be against my interest. So the plan appears to be: promise, mean it while I say it, and then keep the money. I cannot decide whether that is a deep fact about rationality or evidence that I have described the case badly.

The claim I want a number on. I have come to suspect that whenever a case is said to show it can be rational to do something irrational, the paradox comes from mixing two time frames. The act that looks irrational turns out to serve the agent's interest measured over a longer stretch, while the act that looks rational serves the same interest over a shorter one. Fix the stretch you are measuring, and the contradiction dissolves. On this view there is no genuine rational irrationality, only a single interest pulling in different directions at different horizons. Applied to the hitchhiker: promising serves my interest in getting out of the desert; paying serves my interest in being the sort of person whose promises are believed; keeping the money serves my interest in being rich; and there is no single verdict about what is rational, because there was never a single stretch of time to consult.

Why I doubt my own claim. I tried to build a case where the two pull apart, and I am unsure whether it works. Same desert, but suppose I feel no guilt about such things and I am travelling to a place where no one will ever learn what I did, while I very much care about keeping my money. The driver still insists on a promise. If my claim is right, the promise should still be rational, since it gets me out of the desert. But if the only thing that makes paying rational is some longer-run benefit, then in this version there is nothing left to make it rational, and the promise starts to look less like a promise than like a lie told to get a ride. That makes me suspect my claim is really about psychology rather than about rationality: it works because most of us are built to feel bad about breaking our word, and it may say nothing about what someone not built that way has reason to do.

My number. For the claim that the hitchhiker case is fully explained by conflicting time frames, with nothing left over, I would say about 0.5. I am least sure whether my constructed case succeeds, since someone who holds my claim will reply that a person with no guilt and no future is not a person we can reason about, and that needing to strip all that away shows the claim is right rather than wrong.

What I am asking you. Give me your own credence that the claim is correct, that rational irrationality is an illusion produced by mixing time frames, and then decompose it the way you think the question actually breaks down. The parts I can see are: whether promising and paying serve one interest at different horizons or two different interests; whether a genuine intention can survive the agent's knowledge that he will not act on it; whether the case changes once reputation, guilt, and future dealings are removed; and whether rational has a single meaning here at all. If you would carve it differently, use your own carving. Give a number and one line of justification for each part, show how the parts combine into your final figure, say which part the figure is most sensitive to and what would move it most, and tell me which part accounts for the difference between my 0.5 and your number. Finally, describe the strongest counterexample you can to the claim, a case in which the intention is rational and the act is not, and the gap is not a matter of time frame.
