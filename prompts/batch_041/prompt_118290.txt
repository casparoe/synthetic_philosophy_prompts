For the better part of forty years I worked on the peripheral sensory physiology of decapod crustaceans, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy in an unguided way; the upshot is that I now hold three views about moral status that I can state cleanly but cannot get to lie down together, and a young colleague in the philosophy department and I have been arguing about them over lunch for most of a year. Before I describe what I want, one practical note: I have no library at hand and no appetite for being sent to the literature. What I need is a piece of conceptual engineering I can turn over in my head — the case itself, and the reasoning that comes out of it — not a reading list.

The three views are these. The first treats sentience as a scalar and moral status as strictly increasing in it, with the interests of individuals summed, so that a very great many organisms each of very slight sentience may outweigh a few organisms of considerable sentience. The second is a threshold view: moral status is not a magnitude but a standing, absent below a line and full above it, the line being drawn by the presence of a certain kind of integrative machinery — a nervous system that gathers signals from many sites into a state governing the animal's behaviour as a whole, rather than a bundle of local reflex arcs. The third, which I came across in the recent animal-welfare literature and find the most interesting of the three, separates two things the first view runs together: the probability that a system is sentient at all, and how much welfare is at stake if it is — the width of the band between its best and its worst possible states — so that the expected weight of an individual is a product of the two factors rather than a single degree. What I want the case for is a suspicion of mine that the first view is not a distinct position at all, but the third with one of its two factors quietly mislabelled.

So: construct a case in which the three come apart, and if you can, one in which the scalar view reaches the verdict that defenders of the other two would call perverse — not because the numbers are rigged, but because it has conflated two axes the third view keeps separate; the divergence should turn on the structure of the case rather than on the particular magnitudes, so that it survives reasonable variation in the numbers. Make it a single forced choice between two courses of action, drawn from ordinary practice — a laboratory, a fishery, a pest-management programme, a conservation budget, something of that kind — populated by animals whose neuroanatomy is described only in terms on which all three views can agree. I do not want anything stipulated about whether this or that creature is conscious, and I do not want the case to rest on any contested empirical claim about whether insects or crabs feel pain; the point is to watch the theories disagree about the same description of the facts, not to adjudicate the facts. No science fiction, no trolleys, no brains in vats; and if a thermostat appears, it should appear only as the limiting case at the bottom of the scale, never as the device that produces the answer. Then give me, for each of the three views, the verdict and the reasoning that generates it — not the verdict alone — and tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work and what each verdict becomes when that feature is removed or reversed. If you can manage it without clutter, let the threshold view and the two-factor view also part company somewhere in the case, so that I am not looking at a simple two-against-one. Finally, take whichever view comes off worst and put the strongest attack on the case into the mouth of its most competent defender: I want to know whether that attack is a disagreement about the moral theory or a complaint about the facts I chose to stipulate, since if it is the latter, the case has failed at the very thing I am asking of it.
