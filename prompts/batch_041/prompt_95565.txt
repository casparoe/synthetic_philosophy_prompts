ok so i've been lurking on LessWrong and various rationalist blogs for like a decade, and i've mostly absorbed the whole shut up and multiply consequentialist vibe, but lately i've been trying to steelman deontology and i keep bouncing off the same wall.

Heres the thing. A deontologist says you cant push the fat man off the bridge even to save five people. Fine. But then the five die. And by the deontologists own lights those five deaths are violations of the same right not to be killed that the constraint was supposed to protect. So the constraint ends up permitting *more* of the exact thing it forbids — five rights-violations instead of one. Same with the murderer at the door: you refuse to lie, so he kills the person you were hiding, and now you've got a death you could have prevented by a much smaller wrong. I've seen this called the paradox of deontology and honestly it feels pretty damning.

My question is how this actually gets resolved. Like, is there a standard move the way there is for the Liar paradox, where you pick a theory of truth that blocks the contradiction and then everyone argues about which theory is right? Or is the real situation just that deontology is formally inconsistent, in which case why do so many serious philosophers keep defending it instead of treating it like a solved-and-dismissed problem? If there's an actual technical fix out there i want to know what it is. And if theres no fix, i want to understand exactly what the deontologist thinks the constraint is doing such that this isnt a refutation of the whole view. Bonus points if you tell me whether paradox is even the right word for it, or if im misdescribing what kind of problem this is.
