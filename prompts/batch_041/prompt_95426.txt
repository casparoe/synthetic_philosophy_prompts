The regress runs as follows. If a proposition consists of parts — an object and a property, say — and if some further item, a tie or relation of predication, is required to hold those parts together, then the tie must itself be tied to each of them, and so on without end. Write an essay assessing whether this regress is a genuine obstacle to treating propositions as structured yet unified entities. Address the reply that the regress is an artefact of a picture on which the parts are independently complete and unity is imposed from outside, and that on the better view unity is primitive: the whole proposition is what unifies, and there is no further tie to be unified. Does this reply solve the problem or evade it — does it explain the unity of the proposition, or merely relabel it as a brute fact, and if the latter, is that a defect? Is it even coherent to speak of a structured proposition that is not unified, i.e. a mere heap of constituents, and if not, what work is the word "unified" doing? Say what a regress argument must establish in order to be decisive, and whether this one meets that bar. End with a clear verdict on whether propositions can be both structured and genuinely unified, and state what a theory must concede in order to secure that.
