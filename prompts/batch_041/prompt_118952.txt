I've been going back and forth with a freind about the common prior assumption -- the bit that makes Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result run, where two agents who share a prior and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors can't keep on disagreeing. We both suspect the CPA is doing way more work than it's entitled to, and we want to know which of the usual defences actually hold up. Here are the five I've collected, glossed so you can see what I have in mind:

(a) the rationality line -- a rational agent's priors are fixed by logic and evidence alone, so two rational agents can't have different priors, and a differing prior just marks someone as irrational;
(b) the convergence line -- it's a harmless idealisation, since as evidence piles up the likelihood swamps the prior and posteriors converge anyway (usual caveats about absolute continuity and the true model being in the support), so the CPA never has to be literally true;
(c) the reflection line -- your prior should equal the prior you'd have if you fully took into account how you came by it, a self-consistency condition that is supposed to collapse into a shared prior;
(d) the Harsanyi line -- to model agents at all you have to treat them as differing only in information, so a common prior is just what it means for two people to be reasoning about the same world;
(e) the convention line -- the CPA isn't a claim about rationality at all, it's a normalisation that lets us state disagreement in a common currency, and its failures are the interesting data rather than a mistake.

Rank these five by how much you'd trust each to hold up under scrutiny as a justification for leaning on the CPA -- most trustworthy first. Give me exactly five numbered items, one per defence, a sentence or two each on where it lands and why, and no intro or conclusion.
