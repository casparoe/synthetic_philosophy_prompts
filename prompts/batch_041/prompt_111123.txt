Compose a joke about moral worth: the Kantian idea that an action is morally good only when it is done from duty and not merely in conformity with duty, together with the rival view that what matters instead is that the agent acts for the right reasons, whether or not the thought that they are right is what moves her.

Build the joke on the following case rather than on one of the stock illustrations — and please avoid the two that everyone reaches for, the honest shopkeeper and the helper who does the right thing while believing he is doing wrong.

At a small firm, four colleagues contribute the same amount, month after month, to the same hardship fund. The first says she gives because it is the right thing to do, and that she would give whether or not she felt like it. The second says he gives because he could not bear to be the sort of person who walks past a colleague in trouble. The third says he gives because he would feel unbearable guilt if he stopped. The fourth says he began giving years ago to impress the woman who ran the fund, who left the firm long ago, and that he has never found a reason to stop.

The four are indistinguishable in what they do, and the leading accounts of moral worth sort them differently. Let the punchline turn on that divergence — most sharply, on whether an agent's motive must contain the thought that the act is right or need only track the feature that makes it right — or, if you can make it land, on the older suspicion that scrupulousness is itself a way of pleasing oneself, so that the man who gives from dread of his own guilt comes out looking like the most self-interested of the four.

Keep it short: a setup and a punchline, not a sketch. Someone who has read the literature should find it funny, and someone who has not should be able to see, after one sentence of explanation, exactly what claim the joke is making. Then, in no more than two sentences, say which account of moral worth the punchline commits you to and which rival account would have to regard it as incoherent rather than merely unfunny. Please avoid the generic format in which a Kantian, a utilitarian and a virtue ethicist walk into a bar; the humour has to come from the substance of the positions, not from the fact that philosophers are strange.
