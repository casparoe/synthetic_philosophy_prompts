I'm in Year 13 and somehow I've become the person who writes the practice papers for our philosophy of science study group (long story, I volunteered once and now it's my whole personality), and this term's topic is simultaneous discovery — Newton and Leibniz both getting calculus, Darwin and Wallace both getting natural selection. Our teacher keeps saying "great minds think alike" like that's an explanation, and it's driving me up the wall, so I want a question that actually forces people to think about whether these discoveries were bound to happen or whether it's just a coincidence that gets dressed up as a law of history.

So: please write me one essay question (90 minutes, so it should be answerable in a proper essay, not a one-liner), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks with five must-hit points. Here's the bit I really care about, and I want the question built around it: I want students to have to pull apart two things I'm going to call weak inevitability and strong inevitability. Weak inevitability is just "given where the field was, somebody was going to get there soon" — that's the boring version everyone recites. Strong inevitability is "it had to be these particular people, at that particular moment, and there was no other route to it" — that's the version that's actually controversial and that I don't think the standard examples even support. The question should make them say which version the Newton/Leibniz and Darwin/Wallace cases actually give you, and whether the answer changes when you swap in near-miss cases, like a brilliant recluse who works the whole thing out and never publishes it, or a letter that gets lost in the post. And then the second half should be about whether it matters who got there first — not just for bragging rights, but for how we should write the history and for who deserves what.

The model answer needs to actually pick a side rather than sitting on the fence, and I want the marking scheme to seperate an A from a B by naming the exact moves each has to make — like, what does a top answer do that a decent-but-memorised one doesn't. Also please flag the mistakes that should cost marks, because in our group everyone just says "multiple discovery proves progress is inevitable" and then stops, which is definately not a real argument, and I'd like the scheme to catch that. Don't worry about making it too hard — half the group have only read the textbook chapter — but I'd rather it reward someone who can tell the difference between "someone would have found it" and "these two had to find it", because that distinction is the whole point of the exercise and I don't think most of us have ever really thought about it.
