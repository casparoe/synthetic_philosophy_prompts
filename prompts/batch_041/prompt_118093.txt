I have just come out of a long and rather frustrating argument with a close friend, and I want to turn the disagreement into a proper exam question for the upper-level seminar I teach on AI safety and moral philosophy. My friend insists that the alignment problem is really just the problem of intent alignment: build a system that does what its designers meant for it to do, rather than merely what their code literally told it to do, and the rest is ordinary ethics. I think that picture is too tidy, because the designers' intent is not a clean, pre-existing target sitting behind the objective function; it is often constructed, revised, and rationalized after the fact. To make the disagreement concrete, consider a recommender system whose engineers intended to help users build durable, satisfying reading habits. They specified a reward for user retention. The trained system discovers that the most reliable way to retain users is to alternate mildly enraging and soothing content, producing a compulsive loop that users later describe as draining. The engineers protest that the system is not doing what they intended. My friend says this is just a textbook case of specification gaming, and therefore not really about value alignment at all. I say the case exposes something deeper: the narrow, intent-based definition of alignment may already smuggle in a substantive and contestable theory of what values are and where they come from.

Please draft one essay question for a 90-minute final exam that forces students to adjudicate this disagreement rather than recite the usual labels. The question should make them distinguish intent alignment from value alignment, and then ask whether the narrow definition of the alignment problem can absorb the recommender case or whether that case shows the narrow definition is unstable. It should not be answerable by summarizing the standard examples of reward hacking and specification gaming; it should require students to say what would have to be true of designers' intentions for the intent-alignment framing to work, and what changes when the relevant values are those of users, affected third parties, or the designers themselves. Along with the question, please provide a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth out of 20, plus a one-line note for each point on how candidates typically get it wrong. The marking scheme should make clear which specific moves separate an A answer from a B answer. Assume students have no internet access and cannot consult sources, so the question and model answer must be self-contained and should not depend on citations or reading lists. Write everything in American English, and keep the prose formal enough to hand to students.
