Two frameworks are often used to say what a psychiatric diagnostic category is, and they are usually kept apart. The first is the homeostatic property cluster account, associated with Richard Boyd: a category is a natural kind when its members share a cluster of properties whose co-occurrence is sustained by underlying causal mechanisms, even though no single property is necessary and the boundaries of the kind may be vague. The second is Ian Hacking's account of looping effects and interactive kinds: classifying people can change the people classified, whose altered behaviour and self-understanding can in turn change the category, so that the kind and its members are modified by the very act of classification.

I want a comparison of these two, not a summary of each. Address the following.

First, are they genuine rivals, or do they answer different questions? Say precisely what each is a theory of, and where the two could both be true of the same category.

Second, evaluate a claim I have been trying to formulate: that the two can be reconciled by treating the classificatory practice itself as one of the homeostatic mechanisms that maintains the property cluster. On this view an interactive kind is simply a property cluster whose sustaining mechanism includes the fact that people are classified. Is this a real reconciliation, or does it quietly change what "homeostatic property cluster" was supposed to mean — in particular, does it preserve the thought that the kind is discovered rather than made, and that its boundaries are fixed by something other than our classificatory interests?

Third, work the comparison through one case: prolonged grief disorder, added to the DSM-5-TR in 2022 and included in the ICD-11, whose boundary against ordinary grief is contested and whose symptoms — yearning, identity disturbance, a sense of life's meaninglessness — are plausibly shaped by how the bereaved understand what is happening to them. Which framework explains the case better, and where do the two make different predictions that could in principle be tested?

Finally, construct one further case, real or hypothetical, in which the two frameworks would give opposite verdicts about whether the category is a natural kind, and say which verdict you would endorse and why. Be explicit about which parts of the comparison turn on how "natural kind" is defined rather than on empirical facts.
