I am assembling a small set of jokes for a graduate reading group on social choice theory, and I need one joke about Sen's liberal paradox: a joke that is genuinely funny to someone who knows the theorem from the inside, and that cannot be told at all by someone who does not.

It has to be built on the theorem's machinery rather than on its fame, so let it instantiate the structure and let the listener watch the structure close. Two people; each decisive over a pair of alternatives that concerns only themselves; the two decisive pairs overlapping on a single pivot alternative, namely the outcome in which neither of them does the thing in question; at least one of the two ranking the other's private business in a way that is, from where they stand, perfectly reasonable; and the two of them agreeing on a pair of outcomes, so that weak Pareto has something to bite on. The contradiction should arrive as the punchline, and it should arrive in the exact form the theorem gives it: each person's protected pair is respected, the pair on which they agree is respected, and the social ranking nevertheless places one outcome above another that both of them prefer to it — with the outcome each of them was entitled to decide for themselves coming out below the one they both agree is worse.

I do not want the standard illustration — the Prude, the Lewd, and the novel — nor a cosmetic re-skin of it (two readers, one book, the option of leaving it unread). Invent a setting of your own, preferably one in which the listener's first reaction is that there is no problem here at all, because each party is only minding their own business.

Nobody in the joke may be irrational, malicious, or inconsistent, and nobody may be tricked; the impossibility must come out of the two principles alone. The listener should be able to see that the only available repair is to change someone's preferences — to stop one of them caring about the other's private sphere — rather than to correct anyone's reasoning.

Nor may the joke be reducible to a joke about voting or about Condorcet cycling. No vote is taken in it and no majority appears anywhere; if the punchline survives being told about a committee instead of two people, it is the wrong joke.

Keep it short enough to tell in under a minute: the humour should lie entirely in recognition, and a listener who has never heard of the theorem should find it puzzling rather than funny. Give me a single joke, not a set of variations, and then, in one sentence afterwards, name the exact feature the punchline turns on and say what becomes of the joke if that feature is removed, so that I can confirm it is not accidentally a joke about something else.
