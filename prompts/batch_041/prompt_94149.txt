Two members of a reading group I sit in on spent an evening arguing about the norm of assertion, and I was asked to referee without being sure I could. Roughly how it went:

(1) Mira: you should assert only what you know. Devon: too strong — my flatmate told me the number 12 bus is rerouted this month, I believed him, I passed it on, and I did nothing wrong even though I couldn't rule out that he'd misread the notice. So the standard has to be weaker than knowledge, something like justified belief, or perhaps just truth. Mira: in that case you did know; testimony is how most of us know most things, and defeasible evidence is still evidence. Devon: that is the move where "knows" quietly gets redefined as "has whatever it takes to assert properly," which makes the thesis true by construction and impossible to test.

(2) Devon: then take a doctor in a morning huddle who says "it's a PE" to colleagues who have agreed in advance that each of them will say their leading hypothesis out loud. Nobody thinks she must know, and nobody thinks she has done anything wrong. If knowledge is the norm, either she is breaking it or the norm varies with context. Mira: that is a guess, not an assertion; the invitation changes what speech act she is performing, so it is not a counterexample. Devon: she says it flatly, in the indicative, and her colleagues hear a claim about the patient. And a norm you cannot be blamed for breaking is a counsel of perfection, not a norm — the two are not the same thing.

(3) Ben — Devon, rather: the witness who knows the defendant was at the scene but asserts it mainly because he wants him convicted has knowledge and yet has done something wrong; so knowledge is not sufficient for whatever it is that makes an assertion in order. Mira: his assertion is epistemically fine, and the objectionable thing is a separate, ethical question about what you may do with an assertion. Distinguish the licence to assert — what makes it all right to say something — from the fidelity of the assertion — whether it fits the facts. Devon: then you must accept that a faultless false assertion is still a violation of your norm, so "violation" has come loose from criticism, and a norm that can be broken with complete impunity is doing no work.

Please adjudicate move by move, rating each main claim from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, then say who has the better case on the central question — whether the norm of assertion is knowledge or something weaker, and whether there is one norm here or several — and what both of them are missing. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate the question.
