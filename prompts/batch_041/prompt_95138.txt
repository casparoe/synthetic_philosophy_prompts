I spent last night arguing with a friend who works in AI safety about whether we can put ethics into a machine, and he kept coming back to one comparison: writing a machine's ethics is like writing a legal code. You draft the statutes — do not deceive, do not injure, defer to the human operator — and the machine then applies them to the case in front of it much as a judge applies the law: no discretion worth the name, no judgment calls, just the rule and the facts. That is how he reached his conclusion, which is that the problem is fundamentally one of drafting: get the code right, verify that the behavior conforms, and you have an ethical machine.

I think the analogy is carrying more weight than he admits, but I could not say precisely where. Acting as a philosopher of law and of mind with a specialty in machine ethics, please audit the comparison. Begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — saying what plays the role of the statute, the judge, the case, precedent, the legislature, the appeal, the jurisdiction, and the official who is answerable when something goes wrong. Then say where the mapping breaks: which features of an actual legal system the machine's situation lacks, and which of those absences bear on his conclusion rather than being decorative. Give particular attention to whether the rules a machine can execute can have the settled core and contested penumbra that legal rules have; to what happens when two of the machine's rules collide and no higher rule resolves the conflict; and to whether behaving as the code requires, for the reasons the code supplies, is the same thing as being guided by it. Separate the disanalogies that sink his conclusion from those that merely complicate it, state plainly whether the conclusion survives, and if it does not, sketch the comparison you would put in its place.

Lead with your verdict, then the table, then the argument. Think the whole thing through carefully before you begin writing, and keep the response under 800 words.
