I sit on the advisory board of a foundation that is about to commit eight postdoctoral fellowships, three years each, to conceptual work on the distribution of consciousness across animals. The remit is deliberately narrow: philosophy and theory, no new experiments, and nothing on human consciousness except where it constrains the animal case. The board has to choose what the cohort works on, and we have concluded that three or four questions funded properly beat a dozen funded thinly.

Our difficulty is a mismatch. The demand side -- welfare law, slaughter and transport rules, the steady push of legal protection further out along the tree of life -- wants a distribution: a verdict, taxon by taxon, about who is in and who is out. The supply side offers theory-laden markers, capacities that dissociate across taxa, and a concept of consciousness that may be graded, cluster-like, or too coarse to yield determinate verdicts at the margins. I would rather have a definite ordering I can argue with than a menu of considerations.

Please set the agenda. Candidates I want you to weigh, along with any you would add:

- Determinacy: whether there is a fact of the matter about borderline taxa at all, or whether "consciousness" is the kind of concept whose extension is partly fixed by our interests, so that some cases are unsettled rather than merely unknown.
- The cluster problem: the capacities we treat as evidence in humans -- flexible attention, cross-modal integration, metacognition, valenced experience -- come apart in invertebrates and in simple vertebrates. What follows when they dissociate, and should any one of them be treated as load-bearing?
- Marker calibration: every behavioral or neural marker is validated against human report, so applying it to a fish or an insect assumes a bridge we have not built. Is there any non-circular way to fix what a marker is a marker of?
- Testability: whether any current theory of consciousness could, even in principle, be confirmed or refuted in a non-human animal, given that we observe only structure and behavior -- and whether that renders the enterprise unfalsifiable.
- The target: phenomenal consciousness, sentience, valenced experience, or access -- and whether the ethically urgent question is the same as the theoretically tractable one. A colleague proposes that we drop consciousness as the criterion altogether and fund work on which states matter to an animal from the inside, whatever the metaphysics turns out to be. I cannot tell whether that is a clarification or a change of subject, and I would like you to say.

Name the three questions you would fund, ranked, plus at most one you would fund only if the first three proved intractable. For each, say why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers rather than experimentalists, and how neglected it currently is. I want the ordering defended, not surveyed; I am not asking for a reading list.

Format the response as follows: "Where the difficulty actually lies" (~200 words), diagnosing the mismatch and what it implies for what we should fund; "The agenda" (~500 words), the ranked questions with a short case for each; "Why this order" (~300 words), stating the criteria you used -- upstreamness, tractability, neglectedness, how much downstream work depends on the answer -- and the trade-offs between them; "What would change the ranking" (~200 words), naming the argument or evidence that would move a question up or down; and "Where this is most likely wrong" (~150 words), identifying the step in your reasoning most likely to fail and what would lead you to a different verdict.
