I write novels for a living, which means I spend my days trying to make strangers feel things about people who don't exist, so I've started keeping a list of my own reactions to see if theres a rule under them. Here's the list.

A woman in my writing group wrote a story where the narrator lets her brother drown. I felt for her the whole way through, no blame at all. Same story in the news last month, real woman, real brother — I wanted her put away. My oldest friend just got the fellowship I've been chasing for three years, and my first reaction was a hot little flare of something ugly, and I've decided that's fine, that's the right reaction. My neighbor's kid has a mild ear infection and she's beside herself with worry and I think less of her for it — she's being ridiculous. A guy I know donated a kidney and told me, unprompted, that he mostly did it because he wanted people to think well of him; I still think the donation was a good thing, and I still can't stand him. And I can't make myself care about a flood that drowned thousands unless there's one photo with one face in it, and then I care about that one person enormously, and I'm fairly sure the caring-without-the-photo is something I'd only be faking.

My rough rule is: I approve of a feeling when I can imagine having it in that situation, and I blame it when it's too big or too small for the situation. Against the list it half-works and half-doesnt. The neighbor can't help her worry any more than I can help my flare of envy — same involuntariness, opposite verdicts from me. And if approval just tracks what I can imagine, the flood case is hopeless, because I can't imagine thousands and I still think I ought to.

I have a feeling Smith got here first — the impartial spectator, and that bit about the earthquake in China that a humane man would grieve over in the abstract and then sleep fine through. Am I just reinventing him badly? If his account is the right one, which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? If mine is closer, what breaks in his?
