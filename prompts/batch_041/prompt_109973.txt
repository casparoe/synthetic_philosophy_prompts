ok, i need to prep for a Q&A and i think this one is going to be rough.

Context: I'm giving a talk next month at a small interdisciplinary animal welfare workshop, about a dozen people, everyone in one room the whole day so there's nowhere to hide. The room is mostly invertebrate neurobiologists — two work on nociception in insects, one on crustaceans — and their default posture is that "pain" talk about anything without a vertebrate-style brain is anthropomorphic hand-waving. They care about mechanism and evidence standards above everything else. Then there's two welfare economists who build animal-welfare metrics for a living; for them, if you can't put a number on it, it isn't a policy question. And one utilitarian philosopher who's broadly on my side but will go straight for the decision theory.

What I'm defending: the scale argument for caring about insects, in a repaired form. The standard version — the chance they're sentient is tiny, but there are an absurd number of them, so multiply — is a sitting duck, and I want to stop defending it. My version has three planks.

1. The probability isn't a free parameter. Sentience is either ancestral (in which case insects very likely have it) or it arose convergently more than once (in which case, unless someone has a positive mechanism argument that excludes arthropods specifically, the per-lineage probability can't be pushed arbitrarily close to zero). Either route gives you a floor, and the floor is exactly what the naive version is missing.
2. Moral weight per head should scale with integrative capacity rather than being flat. My crude proxy is neuron count raised to some exponent less than one, which I know is hacky and I know is coming for me. But a small per-head weight times an astronomical headcount still swamps everything else.
3. What follows from this isn't "treat insects as sentient." It's a reversal of the burden of proof for cheap interventions — pesticide regimes, farm-level practices where the cost of changing is close to zero.

So: what will I actually get from this room? I know the nociception-versus-pain thing is coming, and I know someone will call plank 1 a Pascal's mugging with extra steps, but I want the real list, ordered by how much damage each does, in the words these people would actually use, with a note on which of my three planks each objection is really aimed at. Don't give me the objections I'd get at a philosophy conference; I can get those anywhere.

Give it to me as:

- "Bottom line" (~100 words): the single thing I should say first, and the fight I should try to have rather than the one they'll try to have with me.
