I host a fortnightly discussion group at my local library — about eighteen adults, no specialist background, ninety minutes, and I would rather they arrive at the ideas themselves than hear them from me. Next month I want to build the session around the gender-equality paradox: the striking finding that some measured differences between men and women come out larger in richer, more gender-equal countries than in poorer and more traditional ones. Design an activity in which the group encounters the puzzle through their own choices rather than being told about it, so that they end up weighing whether the pattern reflects something innate, something cultural, or something about how the figures are produced — including the thought that where constraints are weaker people's choices may simply track their preferences more closely, whereas in a poorer society both sexes are pushed toward the same secure options. Give me the rules, roles, materials, and the scoring or forced choices that hold it together; a debrief that maps what happened onto those rival readings and onto the further question of what, if anything, the pattern implies about how we should judge a society's arrangements; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it; and an honest note on which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce.
