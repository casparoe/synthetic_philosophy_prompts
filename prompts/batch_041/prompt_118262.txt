I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in the history and philosophy of science, and my department has asked me to deliver the charge to the graduating class at this year's commencement ceremony: eight to ten minutes, roughly 1,100 to 1,300 words, spoken before an audience of graduating seniors and their families, most of whom have no background in the field, together with the department faculty, who have a great deal. I want to build the address around a distinction that has become central to my dissertation, because I think it is both true and of real use to people who are about to begin something.

The distinction is between two claims that talk of simultaneous discovery habitually runs together. The first is that a discovery was bound to be made by someone, more or less when it was made: given the state of the field, the methods at hand, and the questions everyone was already asking, the move was in the air. The second is that the discovery was bound to take the form it took, that its content would have been the same whoever arrived first. The second does not follow from the first, and the speech should make that gap visible through a single case treated in enough detail for a general audience: the calculus, where the occurrence looks overdetermined, two men working from different problems and reaching the same techniques within a few years of each other, while the content diverged sharply. One worked geometrically, in terms of quantities generated by motion, in the service of his physics; the other worked algorithmically, with a notation that turned differentiation and integration into operations one could carry out almost mechanically, and it is his notation that the following century mostly wrote in. I would rather have one case treated properly than four cases named; a second may be gestured at in a clause, no more.

Three requirements, and one prohibition. First, the speech must not treat the bare fact that two people arrived at something independently as self-evident evidence of inevitability: in both of the canonical pairs the parties were in contact, corresponding, sometimes through the same intermediary, which is precisely why the independence is itself disputed among historians. What I take to be the real evidence for inevitability is convergence from different starting points: different problems, different formalisms, the same result. I want that claim made in a way a family in the third row can follow. Second, a colleague in my department, a historian of science, has told me flatly that any talk of a discovery being in the air is hindsight in disguise; the speech should either meet that objection in two sentences or drop the claim. Third, it must not promise the graduates that merit is rewarded: in each of the pairs I work on, the dispute over priority was bitter and one party lost, and the audience will include people who are about to come second. It should not be a deflationary speech, and it should not be the standard commencement consolation either: no assurances that the future belongs to them, no dictionary definition of the word commencement, no jargon that is not earned in the same sentence, no claim I would not defend in a seminar. Quote nothing and cite nothing; the case I have chosen is famous enough that the two names carry it on their own, and I would rather it be understood than impressive.

It should sound like a speech and not an essay with a greeting, and it should end on something I could say sincerely and that a twenty-two-year-old could use on Monday.
