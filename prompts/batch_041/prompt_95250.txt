I've spent the past decade covering consciousness research, and before I commit to a position in print I want my methodological commitments audited rather than defended. Here is where I have landed. (1) Heterophenomenology is the right core method for a science of consciousness: the scientist takes a subject's utterances as data about what the subject believes and is disposed to say, treats the resulting heterophenomenological world as a controlled construction rather than a transcript of experience, and brackets the question whether the subject's reports are accurate to experience. (2) I am a realist about phenomenal consciousness — there really is something it is like to be a subject, and that is a fact to be explained, not explained away. (3) I take the hard problem to be a genuine problem and not a verbal muddle. (4) I also think trained first-person methods — sustained introspective practice, careful phenomenological description of one's own experience — can yield evidence about the character of experience itself, not merely about what a subject is inclined to report. (5) I think a mature science of consciousness should be able to explain why subjects believe and say what they do about their experience.

Tell me which of these are actually in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real tension is. For each, say what would have to give and what the repair costs; rank the tensions by severity; and name the single revision that would resolve the most at once. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
