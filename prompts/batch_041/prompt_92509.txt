I have been trying to understand the common prior assumption in Bayesian game theory, including the version sometimes called the Harsanyi doctrine, and its connection to Aumann's agreement theorem. As I understand it, the theorem shows that two rational agents who share a common prior and whose posterior beliefs are common knowledge cannot disagree. But the common prior assumption is just an assumption—there is no way to verify that real people have identical priors—so doesn't the theorem simply assume away the possibility of rational disagreement? Why is it treated as a substantive result about rationality rather than a modeling artifact? And if we drop the common prior assumption, is there any principled reason to expect rational agents to converge in their beliefs, or is persistent disagreement simply the default? I would like a clear account of what the theorem actually establishes and what work the common prior assumption is doing in it.
