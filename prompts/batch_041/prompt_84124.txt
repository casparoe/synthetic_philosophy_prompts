I have no academic training in philosophy — I read economics at university and now work as a bookkeeper — but I have spent a long time thinking on my own about the simulation argument and about the case for caring overwhelmingly about the far future, and I have written up an argument that I would like you to comment on. I am not asking for a survey of what other people have said about this; I want to know whether my own reasoning holds up.

Here is the argument, step by step.

1. The far-future case. The future could contain an enormous number of people — many more than have ever lived, if we last a long time and spread out. If anything we do now has even a very small chance of changing how many people there eventually are, or how well their lives go, then the expected number of people affected is enormous. So we should be willing to accept large costs now for even small chances of a better far future. That, as I understand it, is the case for what people call fanaticism about the future.

2. The simulation argument. At least one of three claims must be true: nearly all civilizations at roughly our stage of development destroy themselves before they can run large simulations of minds like ours; civilizations that can run such simulations choose not to; or we are almost certainly living in one. I see no strong reason to believe either of the first two, so I put a good deal of weight on the third. I have read that this argument depends on some rule for thinking of oneself as a random observer among all observers, and I have never understood that rule well enough to use it, so I have left it out.

3. The dampening step. If we are almost certainly simulated, then what we do only changes what happens inside the simulation. The real future — the one belonging to whoever built and runs our world — is not in our hands, and it may be far larger and longer than anything inside the simulation. So the leverage the far-future case credited us with is smaller than it looked: at most we shift a small part of a much larger whole. It seems to follow that the case for organizing our lives around the far future is weaker than it first appeared.

4. Where I get stuck. Two things bother me. First, the number of future people inside our world does not change when I learn that our world might be a simulation — the simulation could run for a very long time, and every one of those people would still be affected by what we do. The dampening step seems to depend on the simulation being short, or on the simulated people counting for less, and I cannot see where either of those comes from. Second, being simulated is a claim about where we are, not about what we can do. It tells us what kind of world we are in; by itself it says nothing about how much is at stake in that world.

5. The repair I want to propose. I think the dampening argument runs together two claims. (a) If simulated people count for as much as anyone else, then the simulation argument does not dampen anything at all — if anything it raises the stakes, because the beings running the simulation may be running very many simulations, so a single underlying event might affect far more beings than we had assumed. (b) If simulated people count for less, then the fanaticism case is dampened, but the work is done by the claim about moral status, not by the simulation argument. So the simulation argument on its own is doing no independent work. The only version I can get to work is a different one: that a world like ours is more likely to be shut off or reset early than to run for billions of years, so the expected size of the future we can influence is smaller than the far-future case assumes — but that is a claim about how simulations tend to be run, not about whether we are in one.

I would like your comments in this order. First, on accuracy: which of my steps are sound, which are question-begging, and where have I used a term in a way that quietly hides a move — in particular, tell me whether leaving out the random-observer rule in step 2 damages anything that follows. Second, on the repair: is the choice I set out in step 5 exhaustive, or is there a way to get the dampening result without either denying that simulated people count or simply assuming it? Third, on what I have not considered at all. I suspect there is something important about how one should reason when the claim at issue is about one's own location rather than about the world, and something about whether the simulators' world is even a sensible place to locate the value at stake, but I cannot put my finger on either. Please be concrete, and tell me where I am wrong rather than where I am vague.
