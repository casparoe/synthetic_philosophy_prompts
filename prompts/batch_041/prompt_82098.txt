I've spent years in decision-theory threads on rationalist blogs, and I no longer believe the slogan that coherence just means behaving like an expected-utility maximizer. I've been writing down what I actually think about cases, and I want the principle behind my verdicts, or a demonstration that there isn't one. (1) An agent with a strict cycle over three outcomes — prefers A to B, B to C, C to A — who will pay a small sum to move from any one to the next: I think something is genuinely wrong with her, and wrong with the preferences themselves, not merely with the fact that someone might exploit her. (2) The same cycle in an agent whom circumstance never puts in a position to be pumped: my gut still says defect, though I can't point to anything she does wrong. (3) The same cycle plus a standing policy of refusing all such trades, so the pump never runs: here I waver, since the policy is the kind of self-binding I admire elsewhere, yet the preferences underneath are unchanged. (4) An agent with no preference at all between A and B, who won't trade either way: I think she is perfectly fine, and I notice this is the mirror image of (1) — a gap in her ordering rather than a loop — yet I want to convict the loop and acquit the gap. (5) An agent whose preferences over lotteries violate Independence in the Allais pattern, though her preferences over outcomes are ordered and stable, and whom I can convict only by a chain of bets stretched out over time: my gut says her preferences are defensible, and that the staged exploitation argument, whatever else it shows, does not show she is incoherent. (6) An agent who prefers A to B under one description and B to A under another, and will pay to switch back and forth: bad, but I suspect a different badness than (1) — instability across framings rather than a defect in the ordering itself.

My tentative principle is that the defect is not exploitability but unusability as a plan: you can act on a gap, by picking arbitrarily and moving on, but not on a loop, and odd risk attitudes are tolerable so long as they stay fixed across descriptions. Test that against the cases, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and say what it predicts for an agent who has a cycle but also a second-order preference to be rid of it, and would pay to have her preferences changed. Then tell me what would change your mind about the principle you land on, and where its reasoning is most likely to fail.
