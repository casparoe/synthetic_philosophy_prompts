here's the question (section B, 10 marks):

Using the slur / neutral counterpart pair we discussed in class (write them S and N), set out the main disagreement between accounts that locate a slur's derogation in the word's meaning and accounts that locate it in what speakers do with the word. Then say which side you think handles (i) how slurs behave under negation and in conditionals, and (ii) reclaimed in-group uses. Be explicit about what each account predicts for each set of data — don't just describe the data.

and here's the answer i've got in front of me:

Both sides agree that S and N apply to the same group, that S is derogatory in a way N isn't, and that this isn't just a matter of the speaker's tone of voice — its built into the word. Where they differ is where the derogation sits. On the semantic account, S's meaning has two parts: the descriptive part it shares with N, and a derogatory part. On the pragmatic account, S means what N means, and the derogation is something the speaker conveys rather than says — either as an implicature or as something the hearer works out from the choice of word.

The embedding data looks like it favours the pragmatic side. If the derogation were part of what the sentence asserts, then "there are no Ss in this building" would assert nothing about the group being inferior, so nothing derogatory would be put across — the whole point of a negation is to take the content out of play. But hearers still flinch at the negated sentence, and people still object to it. So the derogation isn't asserted content; it survives embedding; and that's the pattern the pragmatic account predicts.

Reclamation points the same way. When members of the group use S among themselves, the descriptive content stays but the derogation goes, and it can even work as a term of solidarity. On the semantic account that should be impossible: if the derogation is in the word's meaning then every use of the word should derogate, whoever is speaking. So reclamation shows that the meaning of S isn't fixed by the word, but shifts with who is speaking and why — which is a pragmatic fact, not a semantic one.

There's also the classroom case. In a seminar we can mention S while discussing it and nothing derogatory gets communicated. If the word carried the derogation in its meaning, that shouldn't happen either. So the offence is a matter of what speakers do with the word, not of what the word contains.

Overall the pragmatic account is better supported. The semantic account does explain why there's something wrong with S that isn't wrong with N, but it overpredicts: it can't make room for reclamation or for mention.
