At a seminar on intergenerational ethics, two colleagues of mine, Nadia and Theo, spent most of the session locked in an argument about whether pure time preference — discounting a benefit merely because it arrives later, once uncertainty and differences in marginal value have been set aside — is rationally permissible. I stayed out of it and would like the exchange adjudicated move by move rather than settled in the abstract.

Nadia opened with the claim that the timing of a benefit cannot by itself make it worth less. That a good arrives in 2125 rather than 2025 gives no reason to care about it less, any more than the fact that it accrues to someone else gives a reason to care about it less; the same argument that forbids a bias toward what is near in space forbids a bias toward what is near in time. She added that the two discounts people normally reach for are not pure ones at all: the chance that no one will be around to receive the benefit, and the fact that a richer future makes each additional unit worth less welfare. Subtract those, she said, and the residual rate is doing no evaluative work.

Theo replied that she was conflating two different things. Discounting *consumption* is indeed mostly the second of her two factors, and he granted it; but a positive rate on welfare itself need not be a mistake, because it can express a coherent preference about *when* a good occurs rather than a false belief about how much it is worth. If I prefer the concert this Friday to an equally good concert a year out, I have not made a factual error. He then pressed a symmetry objection: Nadia wants the future treated as on a par with the present, yet no one thinks we should be equally exercised about benefits we could have conferred on the past; if time itself is what is doing the work, the past should count as much as the future, and it plainly does not.

Nadia answered that the concert case compares two different goods, since anticipation, memory, and the person who undergoes the experience all differ; hold the good genuinely fixed and the date is inert. As for the past and future, the asymmetry is precisely what shows that what we care about is the causal relation rather than the date — we cannot affect the past, so there is no question of allocating anything to it, which is why the past looks undiscounted for reasons having nothing to do with the value of later goods. On convergence: that an infinite sum needs a discount to converge is a fact about one modeling device, not a reason to think the discount is ethically right; infinite streams could instead be ranked by an overtaking or limit rule.

Theo conceded the overtaking point but said he still saw a gap between "I cannot justify this rate to an impartial observer" and "it is irrational to have it," and suspected Nadia needed the first to reach the second. He floated a further thought: perhaps the far future should be weighted less not because it is later but because it is less determinate, so our descriptions of what will be at stake there are correspondingly less reliable. Nadia replied that this is the epistemic discount again in a different hat, and that once uncertainty, growth, and determinacy are stripped out, whatever pure time preference remains is a bare preference for the near and should go.

Please adjudicate point by point: the timing-is-inert claim; the concert analogy; the past/future symmetry objection; the convergence argument; and the determinacy proposal. Say which claims are sound, which are confused, and which are defensible but overstated, and then say what both of them are missing — including whether there is a live justification for pure time preference that neither of them has so much as mentioned.
