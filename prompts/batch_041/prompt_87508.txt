I teach a 90-minute session in a cognitive science seminar, about 14 people, mostly first- and second-year grad students from philosophy, psych and CS, and none of them have ever worked with a formal model of analogy. What I want them to walk out having *felt* — not heard described — is conceptual slippage: the moment halfway through a small task where the rule you're applying is obviously the right one, and then the next item comes along and the very same rule now reads as a different rule. Not a mistake, not a lapse of attention — the concepts doing the work (same, next, more, group, repeat, reverse) don't come with fixed boundaries, and the context quietly decides which reading is live. I want them to catch themselves in the act of sliding, and I want them to notice that two of them can apply "the same" rule to the same puzzle and get different answers with neither of them being sloppy.

I want this to happen in a microworld that's too bare to hide in: strings of letters and a short list of allowed moves, that kind of thing, something with no prior expertise to fall back on, where a rule can be stated in a single sentence and still be ambiguous. Its too small a world to let anyone coast on knowing the right answer already.

Some raw material I've been sketching, use it or bin it. A puzzle of the form "pmm → pmn, so zzz → ?", where the trouble starts the moment you ask what happens at the end of the alphabet — is the successor of z nothing, or a, or does that move simply break and something else take over in its place? And where a rule like "change the last letter to the next one" turns out to mean different things depending on whether "the last letter" is the final character, the final group of identical letters, or the final run in some other sense. I also like the trick of leaving one puzzle up on the board, letting everyone commit to an answer and write down the rule that produced it, then changing the target slightly and watching the same people answer the original puzzle differently. If you've got a cleaner microworld, use yours instead.
