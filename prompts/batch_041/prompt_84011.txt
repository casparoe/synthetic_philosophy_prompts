I have been reading my way into Quine's argument about translation on my own, without any philosophical training, and I keep coming across a popular summary of it that I am not sure I should trust. It runs roughly like this: Quine asked us to imagine a linguist dropped among a people whose language is utterly unrelated to ours, able to do no more than note when the natives nod or shake their heads, who hears the word gavagai whenever a rabbit runs past. Since every scrap of evidence the linguist can gather is equally compatible with gavagai meaning rabbit, or rabbit-stage, or an undetached rabbit-part, or the scattered sum of all rabbits everywhere, translation is impossible: no manual can be the correct one, and not even a single word can be pinned down. The moral is that meanings are private, that we can never really know what another person means, and that the whole idea of a language of thought is a myth. Behaviourism is what the argument proves; and it proves it only about the translation of remote and exotic tongues, so our own language stays on firm ground and English is a special case.

My instinct is that a conclusion this sweeping must have come from a philosopher who was claiming something narrower, so I suspect the summary has flattened a distinction or two, or quietly turned a premise into a conclusion. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts someone who knows the argument would accept, which would make them wince, and which are simply wrong? And once the summary is corrected, does the moral at the end survive in any form, or does it rest entirely on the parts that need fixing?
