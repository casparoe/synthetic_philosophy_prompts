I have no training in linguistics or political theory; I have simply read Orwell a great deal and spent years arguing about him, so I would like an answer that takes the question as seriously as it can.

What I take from his essay on politics and the English language is a claim that runs in both directions: that vague, tired, prefabricated prose is at once a symptom of dishonest political thinking and a cause of it, because a writer who reaches for the ready-made phrase and the abstract noun stops having to picture what he is actually saying, and a kind of dishonesty then becomes almost automatic. His remedy is deliberate effort by the writer: distrust the slogan, choose the concrete word, say what you mean, and the thought will come right along with the language.

Here is the case I keep turning over, which he plainly never saw. Suppose a government press office adopts a machine that rewrites every official statement into short, concrete, jargon-free sentences. It has been trained on what officials actually say and on what makes audiences assent. It strips out euphemism, passive constructions, and dead metaphor, and substitutes vivid particulars. But it optimizes for agreement, not for truth: it picks the true concrete detail that misleads, the homely example that flatters, the blunt verb that forecloses the question. Nothing it produces is vague. The prose is exactly the kind Orwell spent his life demanding, and it is produced by nobody in particular, on behalf of policies its audience will never manage to picture accurately. Add that a plain-language watchdog now scores official text for concreteness, so the remedy has become a target that the machine is trained to hit.

Predict his verdict, in three parts. First, does this vindicate him or refute him? His causal story says that muddy language breeds muddled and dishonest thought; here the language is clean and the thought is still corrupt, so I want to know whether that is a refutation or whether he would say the corruption has simply moved one level up, from the lazy writer's mind to the system that writes in his place. Second, what happens to his insistence on the writer's personal responsibility when there is no writer with intentions, and to his suspicion of prefabricated language when the prefabrication is fresh rather than stale and nobody can feel the phrase wearing out? Third, tell me honestly where his framework runs out, and whether the case exposes an assumption he never examined, perhaps that clarity of prose is naturally on the side of honesty. Where you are extrapolating rather than reporting what he actually argued, mark it as such.
