A legislature has enacted a Statute of Sincerity, making it an offense to assert what one does not believe, and the body charged with enforcing it must now issue its internal guidance. Write that guidance.

The document should be in the register of a commission that is conscientious, precedent-minded, and progressively aware that it has been handed an impossible brief: numbered findings, a definitions section, a schedule of exemptions, a note of dissent, a recommendation. Roughly 800 to 1000 words. It is written for the commission's own enforcement officers, not for philosophers, so it names no thinkers, cites no scholarship, and does not pause to remark that the pattern it is investigating has a name in the literature. It must nonetheless get that pattern exactly right: the conjunction of a claim with the speaker's own disavowal of belief in that claim, as in "It is raining, but I do not believe it is raining."

The substance of the piece is the commission's slow discovery of what it has been asked to police. Its findings should include the following.

- Such sentences are not contradictions and are often true, so the Act cannot be enforced by any test of the content of what was said. The offense must therefore lie somewhere else; the commission should locate it, haltingly, in the act of assertion rather than in the proposition asserted, and should register how awkward that is for a statute drafted in terms of propositions.
- The pattern survives only in the first person present tense. The commission must report that the same content raises no difficulty in the past tense, in the second or third person, or when embedded in a conditional, a disjunction, or a supposition, and must then attempt a redraft of the Act that has the same reach as the phenomenon. It should fail to produce one.
- It must classify neighboring cases: the speaker who asserts a claim and professes belief in its negation; the speaker who asserts a claim and denies knowing it; the speaker who merely hedges, saying that it is raining though he may be wrong. Some of these belong in the punishable category and some do not, and the commission should concede that its line looks arbitrary from the outside.
- The offense is a state of mind at the moment of speech, which raises two problems the commission cannot solve. A subject who does not believe a proposition cannot believe it on command, so the Act cannot be complied with by obeying it; and the Act presumes that speakers have reliable access to their own beliefs, which the commission's prosecutions have shown to be false in the cases of self-deception it has had to handle. Its remedies for both should be the ordinary administrative ones, and should be quietly absurd.
- A closing section in which the commission half-endorses, and cannot quite bring itself to write down, what it calls the constitutive reading: that the assertion does not describe the speaker's belief but expresses it, so that the gap the Act was written to close does not exist. The commission should draw the consequence for the Act — repeal, or a redraft satisfied by every utterance and therefore meaning nothing — and the dissent should come from the one commissioner who holds that the whole exercise presupposes belief to be voluntary.

The object of the satire is the assumption that sincerity is a compliance property: something a rule can require and an officer can check. The comedy should come from the philosophy rather than from generic bureaucratic satire; keep it deadpan, with no winking and no parody of any actual agency or official. A philosopher of language should find that the commission has identified the real defect correctly — that it is not a contradiction, not a lie, not a matter of content, and not something a legislature can reach — while a reader who has never encountered the problem should be able to follow the memorandum and find it funny.
