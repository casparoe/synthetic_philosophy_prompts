I want your credence in a precise claim about consequentialist cluelessness, decomposed into its parts rather than delivered as a bare number or hedged into "it depends".

Fix the following setup. Take an ordinary act — say, an hour spent helping a stranger, or £50 redirected from consumption to an effective charity. Assess it consequentially, and extend the causal horizon without limit: include the act's effects on the future through others' reactions to it, through the precedent it sets, through its marginal contribution to a social equilibrium, through the behaviour it makes slightly more likely in strangers who never learn of it, and so on. Let the moral weights be those of the true but unknown theory of value, and represent our uncertainty about those weights honestly. Now call an act divergently clueless if, under this assessment, its expected value fails to converge: the sum is dominated by tail scenarios in which the act's causal footprint is amplified without bound, in magnitude and in sign alike, so that the expected value has no determinate sign and any sign we assign is an artefact of where we truncate the horizon or the probability distribution.

My question is your credence in what I will call the Divergence Thesis: that for essentially any act available to a typical individual, the assessment just described renders the act divergently clueless. I want the decomposition, with a rough number and a line of justification for each term, and then the combination. The terms I would expect to see, though you should add or replace as you think right:

(a) the probability that the correct causal model of social and historical influence has amplification-heavy tails rather than damping ones, so that small differences propagate rather than wash out;
(b) the probability that the true axiology assigns non-negligible weight to effects distant in time and in causal chain from the act;
(c) the probability that, even granting (a) and (b), a cancellation or symmetry argument restores convergence — for instance that amplification scenarios are about as likely to invert the sign as to magnify it, so that the expectation is dominated by terms that cancel;
(d) the probability that bounding the value function, or adopting a saturating measure of moral importance, is both legitimate and sufficient to restore a determinate sign, and that this is not merely a stipulation that hides the problem;
(e) the probability that the notion of an act's expected value over an unbounded horizon is well-defined enough for the Divergence Thesis to have a truth value at all, rather than being a question about our formalism;
(f) anything else you think belongs.

Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and what would move it most — a revision to the causal model, to the axiology, or to the decision-theoretic formalism. Finally, a diagnostic: if the Divergence Thesis is true, does that tell against consequentialism as a criterion of rightness, or only against our ability to apply it? Give your credence that the correct response is to retain consequentialism as the criterion while abandoning it as a guide, and say where someone at 0.9 and someone at 0.2 on the Divergence Thesis most plausibly disagree — with which sub-term, not merely with the conclusion.
