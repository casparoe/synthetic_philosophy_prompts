I'm a third-year undergraduate in philosophy, currently taking a seminar on Quine and the philosophy of language, and next week I have to present my term paper at our departmental philosophy of language reading group — twenty minutes of presentation, then a long question period that usually decides what people think of the paper. I'd like a prep sheet rather than reassurance. My thesis is that the indeterminacy of translation — the gavagai problem — does not disappear once the linguist has complete behavioral evidence, because a deviant translation manual can always be saved by relocating the disagreement out of the lexicon and into the semantics of the language's non-indicative constructions (future, hypothetical, and reported-speech markers), so that every new elicitation meant to refute the manual gets absorbed as a difference in how those markers are read rather than as an error in the translation of a word. My central illustration is a constructed example, not fieldwork — I built it for the paper, so please treat the data as stipulated rather than empirical: a hypothetical language, Retha, in which speakers use a single term, 'sula', in every context where an English speaker would use either "to borrow" or "to steal". Every sentence I can elicit is consistent both with 'sula' meaning a disjunction and with 'sula' meaning a single thick concept English lacks, roughly "taking that is licensed by the community's expectations, whatever those happen to be". The two manuals agree on every elicitable sentence except those containing the hypothetical marker 'ne', where they diverge, and each manual can repair the divergence by adjusting the semantics of 'ne' at no cost to anything else. The room will be: two field linguists who did dissertations on undescribed languages and are allergic to armchair examples; one Chomskyan who thinks the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument settled this debate decades ago; one Quine loyalist who accepts the indeterminacy thesis but thinks my version concedes far too much; and one formal semanticist who works on tense and modality, whom I expect to be the most dangerous person present.

Please write me the prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually get, in roughly the order I will get them, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, ordered by how much damage each does to my thesis rather than by how likely it is, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft, plus what I should concede up front before anyone has to press me. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Be blunt about which of my replies is weakest and why, and tell me what would change your mind about which objections have real force: which single objection, if it landed, should make me abandon the thesis rather than patch it, and what I could show you that would make you say the thesis survives as it stands.
