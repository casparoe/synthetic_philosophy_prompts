Two colleagues of mine, neither of them a specialist in the area, spent the discussion period of a departmental seminar on the philosophy of mind locked in a dispute about pain that neither would give up, and I spent the hour as a bystander. The clinical anchor was pain asymbolia, the syndrome in which pain is experienced without its unpleasantness: after damage to the insula, and also after psychosurgical procedures such as cingulotomy, patients can localise a noxious stimulus and describe its intensity and quality while denying that it bothers them at all. They will report that they are in pain and then smile, decline to complain, and show none of the ordinary signs of wanting it to stop. The mechanism usually proposed for this is a disconnection between the insular cortex, where the sensation is registered, and the limbic structures that ordinarily attach an aversive charge to it. Take that description as fixed for the sake of argument; the dispute was not about the neurology but about what, if anything, the syndrome shows about why pain is bad.

Reconstructing the exchange as fairly as I can, it went like this.

1. A: asymbolia shows that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensation. The patient has the pain — same location, same intensity, same quality — and none of the suffering. So what makes pain bad is a separate, added component, the affective one; the sensation by itself is information, and information is not bad for anyone. The badness of pain is therefore contingent and removable.

2. B: that is too quick. Either the patient is not really having pain, in which case 'hurts' has quietly changed meaning and the case tells us nothing about pain, or the patient is having pain and it is still bad for them, just not in a way that makes them mind. Badness for a subject does not require the subject's aversion. A harm you are indifferent to is still a harm; people can be blind to, or mistaken about, what is bad for them.

3. A: then the claim that it is bad for them has no one home. If there is no unpleasantness, no avoidance, no wish for it to stop, and the patient will choose the stimulus again tomorrow, what is left of 'bad for them' except a verdict pronounced from outside on a state the subject cannot find anything wrong with? Either badness shows up somewhere in experience, or it is a free-floating label.

4. B: two things. First, a distinction: asymbolia might be subtraction — ordinary pain with the affective component deleted — or substitution — a state that mimics pain's sensory profile but is a different kind of state altogether. The clinical evidence, which consists mostly of patients' reports that they can localise and grade the stimulus, underdetermines which. If substitution is right, the case is no more evidence about pain's badness than a machine that prints 'Ouch' would be. Second, and more troubling for A: when the patient says 'it hurts,' we do not know whether that is an avowal, an expression of how things feel, or a classification, a report that a stimulus of the noxious type has been applied. On the second reading, the patient need not be in pain at all in the phenomenal sense.

5. A: the substitution hypothesis is idle unless you can say what would distinguish it, and the disconnection story you yourself granted makes subtraction the more economical reading. Consider also the obvious experiment: if we could induce temporary asymbolia in an ordinary person — a hedonic mute — and she then chose the noxious stimulus over a mild itch, you would have to say she was mistaken about her own life. And the practical upshot, which is where this started: if suffering is a component that can be deleted while the sensory and protective functions remain, then asymbolia is a proof of concept for engineering suffering out of creatures rather than blunting their pain.

6. B: that inference is what I doubt. A creature with intact nociception and no aversion would injure itself; the informational role of pain may not be separable from its aversive role in anything that has to act. And the hedonic mute would only show that we should defer to reports — but reports are exactly what conflict here, since the asymbolic patient reports pain and reports no complaint in the same breath.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication of these six moves: for each, whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Where a move rests on a claim about the syndrome, say what that claim is and whether the evidence actually supports it — in particular, whether asymbolia is better read as a hedonic deficit (the unpleasantness is gone) or as a motivational disconnection (the unpleasantness may be present but no longer moves the patient), and what turns on the difference. Say also which premises each speaker is committed to beyond what they actually said, and what both of them are missing. Finally, tell me whether the practical inference in move 5 survives the exchange, and if so in what weakened form.
