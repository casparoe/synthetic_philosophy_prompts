Ive been reading a lot about when war is justified and I keep running into a move that I can't find a name for, so I'm hoping someone here knows it.

The move goes like this. Someone says: the soldiers fighting for the unjust side are just as morally OK to kill, and just as OK to do the killing, as the soldiers on the just side. Why? Because none of them can be expected to know which side is just. The guy in the invading army was told his country had been attacked first, or was raised on it, or the evidence really was murky. So he's not to blame. And then — this is the part I care about — the person goes straight from 'he's not to blame' to 'he's allowed to do it', as in, he has as much right to shoot as the other guy does.

Heres my problem. Everywhere else we don't make that jump. If a doctor gives a patient a drug he had every reason to believe was safe, and the patient dies, we say he did something wrong and simply isn't to blame for it. We don't say he was allowed to kill the patient. If a juror convicts a man on evidence that later turns out to be fabricated, and the juror truly couldn't have known, we say the juror is blameless, not that he was entitled to convict. So not being able to know gets you off the hook without making the act OK. In war, people seem to think it does both at once.

So: is there a name for this specific slide, from 'he's not to blame' to 'he's permitted'? I'm not asking about the general debate over whether the two sides in a war are really equal. I want the name of the move where a lack of blame gets quietly promoted into a right. If there's a nearby concept that's almost this but not quite, tell me how it differs. And I don't just want a label. I want to know where the move works and where it doesn't: is there ever a case where 'he couldn't have known better' actually does hand someone a permission and not merely an excuse, and if so, what separates those cases from the doctor? Is the war case special because the ignorance is symmetrical — both sides get it — where the doctor's is one-sided? If there's no established name for the move, coin one and tell me exactly what it covers and what it doesnt.
