I have just moved home after three years in Sweden, where my partner and I took parental leave under a system that seemed almost embarrassingly generous and paid tax at a rate that would end a political career here. Now that we are back, the conversation is always the same, and it tends to end with a friend of mine who teaches comparative politics telling me that I am admiring something that cannot be reproduced. His argument is a single figure, and he is fond of it. Institutions, he says, are like plants. The taxes and the benefits are the flower: visible, describable, easy to admire. What sustains them is the soil — a small, homogeneous population that has spent centuries learning to trust its neighbours and its state. One may admire the flower, but if it is dug up and replanted in foreign soil it will wither, because soil cannot be manufactured. Hence the Nordic model cannot be exported, and anyone here who promises otherwise is selling cut flowers.

I would like an audit of the figure rather than a verdict on the politics. Set out the mapping explicitly — what corresponds to the flower, the soil, the gardener, the climate, the weeds — and then tell me where it breaks. Some of the breaks will be trivial (plants do not vote) and I do not care about those; I want the ones that matter for the conclusion being drawn. In particular: does the figure smuggle in a one-way causal story, in which trust produces institutions but institutions never produce trust, and does that story survive what is known about how these systems were actually assembled? Does it treat the soil as a single substance when it is bundling together size, homogeneity, historical accident, and trust, and can a figure that never says which of those is doing the work be tested at all — or does it merely obscure the differences among the Nordic countries themselves? What weight can it carry given that the institutions are recent constructions that borrowed freely from elsewhere; if plants cannot be transplanted, how did this garden grow? And does the conclusion he wants — that the model is not exportable, and that domestic proposals in its image are doomed — survive the disanalogies you identify, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion all along? My own hunch is that he is right about the conclusion and wrong about the reason, but I would like that tested rather than confirmed. If a better figure exists, one that would let us ask which parts of the arrangement travel and which do not, I would rather have that.
