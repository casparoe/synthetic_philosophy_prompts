I run a small podcast where I interview philosophers for a grad-student audience, and next month's guest has me a bit nervous. She's spent the last few years defending the claim that individual contributions to big collective harms -- your steak, your flight, your vote, your decision not to vote -- aren't, strictly speaking, wrong at all, because no single one of them makes any difference to what happens. She's not trolling: her line is that the inefficacy objection is a point about causation rather than morality, and that once you grant it, the moral question just relocates to practices and institutions. But she's added a corollary I haven't seen anyone else make, and it's the thing I most want to get at: she says the individual duty is *strongest* when a collective outcome is already near a tipping point (a few more defectors and the practice collapses) and *weakest* when the practice is rock solid -- so individual obligation peaks exactly where it's least needed and bottoms out exactly where it's most needed. I want you to draft eight to ten interview questions that don't let her just restate the causal claim, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.

At least one question each on: (a) the lumpy-harm case, where everyone's water use is harmless until the well runs dry and you happen to be the one whose bucket tips it; (b) the difference between *making* a difference and *being part of* the thing that makes the difference; (c) a referendum you know will be decided by exactly one vote, you just don't know whether it's yours -- press her on why a one-in-ten-million chance times a very large harm comes out as nothing rather than as something small, and what principled cutoff she's using; (d) whether accepting her view makes you a free rider on everyone else's restraint, and whether that's a distinct wrong even if the causal claim is true; and (e) the aggregation problem -- if no individual act is wrong, where exactly does the wrongness of the practice live, and doesn't the answer she gives either smuggle the individual act back in or leave the practice wrong without anyone ever doing anything wrong? I also want one question about whether the view can be publicized without being self-undermining: if everyone believed it, nothing would ever tip, so does she owe us a story about who's supposed to do the tipping. Make the aggregation one the hardest to answer and don't let the set drift into "here's what the literature says" -- she knows the literature, I want her working live. Also I'm taking a course on collective responsibility right now and I keep noticing that my intuitions about the voting case and the meat case come apart, so if you can build in a question that makes her say something about why those two feel different, that'd be great, and please flag anywhere I've set up a follow-up that she can defuse with a single sentence, cause that's definately what I'm worried about.
