I'm a novelist, about halfway through a book set in a made-up port city I've half invented, and I keep hitting the same wall: my outsider protagonist does something the locals find appalling — skips the tip, walks straight across the square instead of around the fountain like everyone else, barges into what turns out to be a queue — and I can never decide whether he's done something actually wrong or is just a foreigner who doesnt know the drill. This matters more than it sounds, because the whole third act hinges on whether the reader reads him as a decent man out of his depth or a man who simply doesn't care, and I don't have a philosophy background or the time to get one.

So: turn whatever the philosophy of convention and moral obligation really says about this into a decision procedure I can run at my desk in ten minutes, per scene, and tell me which local rules actually bind a visitor and which are just local colour he's allowed to blow through. The rules I'm working with: tipping is close to compulsory and stiffing someone reads as a theft-adjacent insult; the "queue" at the fish market is a loose crowd where whoever speaks first gets served, so pushing in isn't really a thing; jaywalking is fine, but walking on the tiled centre strip of the square is a real taboo, like spitting in a church; turning up to dinner empty-handed is unremarkable, but turning up early is a grave slight.

Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand the decision back to me because its a values call rather than a fact. Then run it on three of those — the tip, the queue, and the early arrival — including the one where it tells me my favourite scene doesn't work. And be straight with me about where the procedure will misfire and what it just can't settle.
