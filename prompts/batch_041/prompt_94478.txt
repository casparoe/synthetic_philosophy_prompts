I'm a novelist — three books, none of them any good at selling — and I'm about 250 pages into a new one whose whole third act turns on one word in an insurance policy. The policy covers total destruction of a vineyard by frost; the frost kills roughly 60% of the buds, and no more than that. The insurer's expert is a supervaluationist about vagueness (I think the camp goes back to Kit Fine, but dont hold me to it) — he keeps insisting there's no fact of the matter about whether the vineyard was destroyed, that the sentence is neither true nor false, yet that it's still true that either it was destroyed or it wasn't, which I gather is supposed to be the clever part. The claimant's expert is an epistemicist, a Williamson man, who thinks there is a fact of the matter — a perfectly sharp line running somewhere through the rows of vines — and that we can never know where it falls, or even which side of it we're on. I want the argument in the hearing to be the intellectual climax of the book, and I do not want it to be a Wikipedia-level sketch of "one side says vague means unknown, the other says vague means no truth value." So: what are the most important implications of each position — the ones that would actually change what a judge does, what the lawyers argue, what my characters should feel about their own case — rather than the technical machinery for its own sake?

I'm especially interested in the implications that fall out of the parts of the two views I find hardest to swallow, so please don't skip them: that on the supervaluationist line, not true and false come apart, so what exactly may a lawyer infer from the judge's finding that the destruction wasn't proven, or that it wasn't proven not to have happened? That the borderline seems to have no sharp edge of its own — it's vague where the vagueness starts — which looks like it threatens the two views in quite different ways, and I want to know which one it hurts more and why. And the epistemicist's trouble that a line nobody could ever in principle know seems like an odd thing for the world to contain, plus whether the very same argument that makes borderline knowledge impossible (something about not being able to rule out nearby cases where you'd be wrong?) also eats into ordinary, everyday knowledge, which would bother me a lot as a novelist since I need my characters to know things. If the two views also imply different things about how I should be writing the inside of my characters' heads — whether there's a fact about, say, how much the winemaker loved the place, or only a range of admissible sharpenings of that — say so, that's genuinely useful to me.

I dont need a reading list or citations, I'm not writing a dissertation, and honestly I'd rather you reason it out from the two positions than recite who said what. Rank the implications by how much they matter, tell me how confident you are in each one, and be explicit about the one you're least sure of — if there's a claim in there you'd bet against, I want to know before I build a courtroom scene on it. Also my brother-in-law (a logician, insufferable) claims I've got supervaluationism backwards, so if I have, correct me.
