Read Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove through the lens of G. E. M. Anscombe's argument that Truman's decision to bomb Hiroshima and Nagasaki was murder. I am less interested in a verdict on Truman than in whether the film dramatizes or evades the structure of Anscombe's position. Anscombe's claim is not merely that the bombings had terrible consequences, nor that Truman was a bad man; it is that intentionally killing the innocent is absolutely prohibited, that this prohibition does not bend to consequences, and that the willingness to treat it as bendable corrupts practical reasoning. The film, by contrast, is a satire of nuclear deterrence: its characters are not deliberate mass murderers but panicked, libidinous, bureaucratic men; the Doomsday Machine is a deterrent that fails because it is secret; and the apocalypse arrives by accident, through a chain of human error. That contrast is the puzzle.

I want a reading that takes both the film and Anscombe seriously. In particular, distinguish two things the film keeps sliding together: (a) intending to kill the innocent as a means, and (b) intending to create a credible threat whose execution would kill the innocent. Deterrence lives in the gap between (a) and (b). Does the film show that gap collapsing—that maintaining a credible threat is already to intend the act? Or does it show something Anscombe's argument cannot accommodate: that in a world of Doomsday Machines and accidental launches, the relevant moral category is not intention but the structure of institutions and the distribution of decision-making? Where does the film vindicate Anscombe's absolutism, and where does it make her look like a moralist whose categories are too clean for the world the film depicts?
