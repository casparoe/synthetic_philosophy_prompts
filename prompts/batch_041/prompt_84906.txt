A student in my Hobbes seminar last term offered the following as the state of nature done properly — the dark forest, as the class christened it — and I have not been able to fault it since. (1) Two civilisations that can detect each other can never be certain of each other's intentions, since intentions can be feigned and alter with power. (2) Technological capacity can leap without warning, so a neighbour that is harmless today may be able to annihilate you tomorrow. (3) Across interstellar distances there is no enforceable agreement and no second chance, so the cost of wrongly trusting a neighbour is extinction, while the cost of destroying one is no more than the loss of a possible ally. (4) So a rational civilisation destroys any neighbour it detects, and any civilisation that becomes detectable is destroyed. (5) Hence the silence of the sky. I would rather keep (5) than (4) if that is an option, but I want the price stated.

What I cannot accept is the parity. By these premises the first nuclear power was obliged to destroy any state that might follow it, and I am obliged to shoot my neighbour on the chance that he is arming himself. I cannot see what the stars have that the Cold War and my street lack. Which step should I cut, what does cutting it cost, and why did it look unobjectionable? Give me your own verdict, with your confidence marked at each step, rather than a survey of positions; and if my parity objection is confused, or the argument too underspecified to support a verdict step by step, say so plainly instead of humouring me.
