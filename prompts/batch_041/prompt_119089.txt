I chair the consent review subcommittee at a mid-sized academic medical center. We review roughly twenty protocols a week — interventional trials, biobank and data-use proposals, and a growing number of studies that recruit through the hospital's patient portal — and we have to reach a validity call on each within a few days, sometimes on a single pass through a consent form and a recruitment script. My reviewers are clinicians, nurses, and two community members; none of them is a philosopher; their decisions gate whether a study opens; and anything they cannot settle comes to me, with the full board hearing appeals. I want to hand them a reusable procedure, not a seminar.

The board has been arguing about the same questions for years and has converged on a working set of distinctions that the procedure should preserve. Treat these as fixed starting points rather than things to relitigate:

- Consent can fail at the level of the act (what was actually agreed to), the level of the process (how the agreement was obtained), or the level of the person (whether this person could authorize this particular decision at this time). A form that is flawless at one level can be void at another.
- Coercion requires a threat, and a threat requires that the person be made worse off relative to some baseline. The choice of baseline — what they would have had without us, what they are entitled to, or the status quo — does most of the work in every hard case, and we have never agreed on which baseline to use.
- An offer cannot coerce, but it can be an undue inducement, and the tests differ: coercion is about the wrongness of the pressure, undue inducement is about whether the offer distorts judgment or exploits a bad situation.
- Deception comes in kinds: about the nature of what is being done, about its consequences, about the alternatives, and mere non-disclosure of something the person would want to know. The last is the one our forms keep sliding past.
- Competence is decision-relative, not global. The same person may be able to authorize a blood draw and not a washout of their current medication.
- Legal validity and moral validity can come apart, and when they do, we want to know which one the procedure is tracking.

What I want from you is the procedure itself, in a form a non-philosopher can run at a desk in fifteen minutes:

1. The questions to ask, in order, with the order justified — which question must be settled before which, and why.
2. For each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a red flag that stops the review rather than merely being noted.
3. The point at which the procedure returns a hard no, and the point at which it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is a value judgment rather than a fact. Mark the latter explicitly; my reviewers keep treating those as if they were factual.
4. Any test you build in that a form can pass while the consent is still hollow — valid on paper and worthless in practice — and what the procedure should do about that, if anything.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work at each step:

(a) A Phase I study paying healthy volunteers $2,000 for ten inpatient days, in a city where $2,000 is roughly a month's take-home pay for the population being recruited.
(b) A cancer trial in which the trial drug is free and the standard-of-care drug is not, recruiting patients whose own oncologist is also the site investigator.
(c) A portal-based study that tells participants it concerns health and mood but is actually measuring whether people with a given diagnosis respond differently to one message framing versus another, with debriefing at the end.

For each case, say where the procedure lands, what it cannot settle, and — where the procedure's answer differs from what a thoughtful reviewer would want — say so plainly instead of smoothing it over.

Close with a short section on where the procedure will misfire: the cases it will pass that should have been stopped, the cases it will stop that should have passed, and which of the distinctions above it flattens in the course of becoming usable. Write the whole thing in American English, as a document I can hand to the subcommittee rather than an essay about consent.
