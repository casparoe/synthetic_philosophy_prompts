Last week our philosophy of mind reading group spent the whole session on Dennett's two-bitser and Searle's Chinese room, and I came out more confused than I went in. What's bugging me is that both of them are fighting over whether a machine can have original intentionality as opposed to the borrowed, observer-relative kind, but they're fighting it against a background where the mental is already understood in broadly naturalistic terms, and I keep wondering what Franz Brentano would have made of the whole thing, since he's the one who put intentionality at the center of the mind in the first place. He obviously never saw a computer, so I don't want the lazy line that he'd just deny machines can think. I want you to actually work out where his commitments land.

Here's what I'd want his verdict on. First the plain two-bitser, the coin gadget that takes real quarters and spits back slugs. Second, something I think is genuinely new and that I haven't seen anyone run past Brentano: a language model trained only to predict the next chunk of text, where the researchers later discover it has a seperate internal state that tracks where the black queen is whenever it reads through chess games, and they can edit that state and thereby change what it predicts about the queen. Nobody built that state in by hand; it fell out of training, and it seems to be about the queen in something like the way a belief is about its object. Third, a thing I keep circling back to: the model will happily carry on about a person who doesn't exist, and Brentano's own criterion was directedness at an object that need not exist, so does the nonexistence actually help the machine's case, or is it a red herring that only looks relevant because his formulation was so thin?

What I really want is Brentano's predicted verdicts on those three, plus whether he'd accept any original/derived distinction at all or insist its all one kind of thing. Then be honest about which parts of this are his actual commitments and which are you extrapolating on his behalf. And if his position ends up forcing him to give up something he plainly wanted to keep, like a clean line between the mental and the merely mechanical, tell me which, and pick whichever reading of him does the least violence to the texts. Skip the citations, I can't check them right now anyway, just give me the reasoning.
