I am a retired chemist — forty years in reaction kinetics — who has spent the last several years trying to teach himself some history and philosophy, and I have joined a reading group that is working through the literature on the outbreak of the First World War. One move recurs in our discussions, and it troubles me. A friend in the group, an engineer by training, maintains that the alliance system was a row of dominoes: the assassination at Sarajevo nudged the first tile, each commitment toppled the next, and within weeks the whole table was down. He concludes that the war was something that happened to Europe rather than something anyone did, and therefore that the long quarrel over German responsibility — the quarrel that still goes by Fischer's name, and that turns on his claim that his own country's leaders deliberately instigated a war in order to become a world power, against an older picture of a continent sliding into a conflict nobody wanted — is misconceived from the outset. My own instinct, I confess, is to say the same thing in the vocabulary of my old trade: a chain reaction, an avalanche, a system pushed past its critical point. The whole point of such a description is that the outcome is fixed by the structure of the medium and not by any decision within it, and nobody thinks to blame a falling mountain.

Lay out the correspondence for me explicitly — what in the political case answers to the tiles, to the table, to the initial push, to the gravity or stored energy that keeps the falls coming, to the spacing between the pieces — and then tell me where the mapping fails. I am chiefly interested in which of the disanalogies matter for the use my friend is making of the analogy and which are merely decorative. It seems to me, though I hold the thought loosely, that the domino picture quietly imports inertness, interchangeability, and the absence of anyone who might have arrested the sequence; that it takes the alignment of the pieces as given, when the alliances themselves were built, hedged and misread; and that a chain of decisions, each made by someone who might have decided otherwise, is not the same thing as a chain of falling tiles even when the two look alike on a diagram. A colleague in the group who reads philosophy has complicated matters by suggesting that 1914 is less like dominoes than like the case of a firing squad in which five shooters fire at once and no single one of them is necessary for the death — an example meant to show that responsibility does not require that things would have gone differently without you. I should like to know whether that comparison earns its keep here, or whether it merely relocates the difficulty.

Finally, and this is what I care about most: suppose the domino picture survives in whatever weakened form you settle upon. Does the conclusion my friend draws from it survive with it — that no one, and in particular no nation, can properly be blamed? Or was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along, so that a better comparison would leave responsibility standing? I am trying to understand what it would even mean to hold a nation responsible. Is the bearer of it the state, the leadership, or the ordinary people who paid for the war; how does it accommodate those who dissented at the time and those not yet born who inherit the debt; and is guilt of this kind a moral verdict at all, or something closer to the legal footing that the 1919 peace treaty sought when it made German responsibility the basis of its reparations — a clause the Germans read as a verdict of guilt, though it was drafted to do a narrower job? If a better analogy than falling tiles is available — I am half-persuaded by some picture of a group of men each choosing under pressure while telling himself that the others would act regardless — I should like to see it stated and defended, together with an account of exactly what it can and cannot carry.
