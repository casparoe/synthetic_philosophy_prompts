I'm a graduate student in philosophy, midway through a dissertation chapter on value conflict, and I've been going around in circles with two friends in a reading group about my own position; I've now written it out for a workshop and would like it stress-tested before I present. Here is the package, as flatly as I can put it. (1) Value pluralism: there are several irreducible kinds of value — welfare, beauty, knowledge, justice, loyalty, and others — and no common measure into which they can all be converted. (2) The relations two options can bear to each other are not exhausted by better, worse, and exactly equal. I hold that there is a further relation, which I'll call being on a par, in which two options are comparable but neither is better than the other, and where a small improvement to one does not make it better than the other (so they are not equal either); and a still further relation, outright incomparability, in which the options are not even on a par. I also think parity is the common case in the hard choices that interest me, and that outright incomparability is rarer than people assume. (3) These relations are objective: whether two options stand on a par, or fail to be comparable at all, is a fact about the values at stake, not a fact about my ignorance or the coarseness of my descriptions. (4) Rationality never falls silent: when two options are on a par, each is permissible, and no further fact selects between them. (5) All the same, some resolutions of a hard choice are better than others; to flip a coin, or to take the easier option because it is easier, is to treat goods as interchangeable when they are not, whereas a good resolution is one that expresses a commitment I can own as mine. (6) Choosing is itself reason-generating: before I commit, neither option is the thing to do; the commitment is what makes the chosen one the thing to do, so that the choice defines me rather than tracking a reason that was already there. (7) Regret for the option I did not take is rational even when I chose permissibly, and the option I passed over keeps its full claim on me rather than merely figuring as a loss I happen to feel. (8) A choice, once made, is settled: I should stop deliberating and stop reopening the question, and I should not go on treating the option I passed over as one I had as much reason to take. (9) Practical rationality is incompatible with being exploitable by a sequence of choices — a rational agent must not be pumpable — which I take to require the usual transitivity of preference.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these genuinely conflict, and how badly — please rank the tensions by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad. For each, say what would have to give and what that would cost, and identify the pairs that only look like they conflict and dissolve once stated properly. I'd also like to know whether any of the nine is doing no work at all, or is quietly presupposed by another. At the end, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the surviving package is still a recognizable position or has collapsed into something I did not intend. I am not after a literature review or a reading list; I want to know what my own commitments commit me to.
