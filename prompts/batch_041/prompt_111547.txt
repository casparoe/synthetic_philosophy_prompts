I have been arguing about laws of nature on and off for several years, and the dispute between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of lawhood has stopped being productive in a way I want diagnosed rather than adjudicated. The Humean line, as I understand it, is that laws are certain generalizations that earn their status by figuring as axioms in the best systematization of the world's total pattern of occurrent fact; the regularities are all there is, and their lawhood consists in the role they play in that systematization, together with whatever counterfactual and explanatory work that role underwrites. The anti-Humean line is that laws are not regularities however well systematized: a generalization is a law because something in the world makes the corresponding regularity non-accidental, whether that something is a governing relation between universals or the natures of the properties involved, so that the regularity holds of necessity rather than by coincidence.

Everyone in this dispute knows everyone else's moves, and the moves have not changed in decades. The anti-Humean presses an explanatory demand: a regularity plus nothing else cannot explain why its instances go as they do, and a mere summary of what happens cannot by itself account for the counterfactual, inductive, and intervention-based reasoning that we take laws to license. The Humean replies that this demand is a demand for an unintelligible extra, that no one has ever said what governing consists in over and above its effects, and that a sufficiently well-chosen systematization delivers everything we legitimately want from lawhood, counterfactuals included. Each side's considerations seem, to the other side, simply not to bear on the question. I am not asking which camp is right. I want to know what this disagreement actually is.

My hypothesis, offered for testing rather than endorsement, is that the two sides have silently assigned different jobs to the word 'law' and are each answering the other's question with their own answer. The Humean treats lawhood as downstream of explanatory practice: what makes something a law is the role it plays in our best account of the world. The anti-Humean treats explanatory practice as downstream of lawhood: the generalization explains, and supports counterfactuals, because a governing fact obtains. If that is the structure, the appearance of contradiction comes from a shared but unexamined assumption that one item must simultaneously ground modality, underwrite counterfactuals, license induction, and anchor the metaphysics of science, and the impasse is what results when no single item can do all those jobs at once.

So: is that hypothesis right, or is a substantive disagreement hiding underneath it? I want to know whether the two packages differ on any matter of fact, modal or otherwise, or whether they are equivalent descriptions that differ only in the direction in which they run the explanation. Is the residual dispute really about the theory of explanation, specifically about whether a legitimate explanation may be global and systematic rather than local and productive, rather than about laws at all? Is an equivocation on 'necessity', or on 'governing' as a causal metaphor smuggled into metaphysics, doing the work? And are there stable intermediate positions that break the symmetry, for instance a Humean account that admits primitive modality into the mosaic, or an anti-Humean one that keeps necessary laws but gives up governing, or do such hybrids collapse back into one camp or the other?

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, an argument that one side's explanatory demand is illegitimate or question-begging, a demonstration that the packages are equivalent so that the choice is pragmatic, or an argument that nothing should be expected here because the two sides are expressing different and equally defensible valuations of explanation. If the honest answer is that no argument, theorem, or empirical result could settle it, say so, and say whether that is a defect in the debate or simply a fact about the subject matter.
