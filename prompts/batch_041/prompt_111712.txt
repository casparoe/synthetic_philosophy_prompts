QUESTION (20 marks). Evolutionary psychology is often accused of trading in 'just-so stories.' (a) Set out the objection as clearly as you can, and identify the specific criticism of adaptationist reasoning it grows out of, with at least one concrete example from the literature. (b) Then assess whether the objection is a fair criticism of evolutionary psychology as a research field, not merely of its weakest practitioners. In (b), be explicit about which claims you take to be the field's core, and about what evidence would tell for or against them.

STUDENT ANSWER. Evolutionary psychology is often accused of telling 'just-so stories,' by which the critics mean that its explanations are unfalsifiable. Because natural selection can be invoked after the fact to explain almost any trait, the story is always available and nothing is ever ruled out, so the explanation has no predictive content and doesn't really count as science. The objection is usually traced back to Gould and Lewontin's attack on the adaptationist programme, which they accused of assuming in advance that every trait is an adaptation and then looking only for the selection pressure that built it, ignoring drift, developmental constraint and byproducts. Their emblem for this was the spandrels of San Marco — the decorative spaces that look designed but are really forced by the architecture.

The charge lands against lazy adaptationism, but it isn't fair to evolutionary psychology as a field, because the field doesn't just tell stories — it makes predictions and tests them. The clearest case is Cosmides and Tooby's work on social exchange. They reasoned that a mind shaped by selection for cooperative exchange would contain a special-purpose cheater-detection mechanism, and predicted that the Wason selection task, which people perform badly in its abstract form, would get much easier once it was recast as a social-contract problem. That is exactly what was found. The effect has since been replicated across many cultures, which confirms the massive modularity thesis: the mind is not a general-purpose problem solver but a collection of domain-specific mechanisms, each shaped by selection in the environment of evolutionary adaptedness — for humans, the Pleistocene savanna.

So the just-so story objection is a fair warning against untestable storytelling, but as a blanket charge against evolutionary psychology it fails, because the field's flagship results are just the kind of prediction-and-test the critics say it can't produce.
