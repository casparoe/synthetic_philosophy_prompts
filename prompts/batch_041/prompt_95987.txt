I am planning a ninety-minute session for a graduate reading group in cognitive science — students who have had a single course in philosophy of mind and are fluent in the mind–body problem but have never encountered epiphenomenalism as a position in its own right. The topic is epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment. I want a teaching plan, not an exposition of the view. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be secured before epiphenomenalism can be stated as a distinct thesis rather than absorbed into generic dualism or eliminativism, and at what point the paradox can be raised so that it lands as a structural problem rather than a cheap gotcha. Nominate the one case that should carry the session. I have been assuming phantom limb pain, where the patient's report and the felt pain are both downstream of the same cortical activity, but say so if something else carries it better; and explain why the philosophical zombie, tempting as it is, should not be the carrier. Then map the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface — that epiphenomenalism is just dualism; that qualia which cause nothing might as well not exist; that the paradox is a refutation rather than a demand for a specific repair; that introspection is being treated as infallible — and add the ones I am not anticipating. For each, tell me exactly where in the sequence to head it off and what to say. I also want the group to see two separable worries that are usually run together: the causal worry, that a judgment about an experience cannot be caused by the experience, and the epistemic worry, that such a judgment cannot then give us knowledge of it; arrange the sequence so that it forces that separation rather than asserting it. Finish with the single closing question that would show whether they have grasped the paradox as a live problem rather than memorized it as a refutation, and tell me what to leave out at this level — I would rather openly refuse to cover the finer points of the causal-exclusion literature than rush them. Lead with your recommended structure in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of what I have asked.
