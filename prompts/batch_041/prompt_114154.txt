I've been covering the effective altruism and longtermism beat for a few years now, and there's one fight I keep walking into and never see resolved. It goes roughly like this. The critics say that any morality which just adds everyone's wellbeing into a single total is treating the human race as if it were one big person — but there's no one who actually experiences the gain that gets traded against someone else's loss, so the tradeoff is a fiction, or worse, a license to use one person for another's benefit. This objection is fifty years old and comes out of Rawls and Nozick, and I still hear it in covid triage panels. The utilitarians I talk to shrug and say: fine, but whats the alternative? Somebody has to decide what to do, and refusing to add up just means you're privileging the loudest or the luckiest. I've been in a reading group where this has gone around in circles for two years. Nobody's facts are in dispute. The critics aren't denying that two is more than one, and the utilitarians aren't denying that people are distinct.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it substantive — a real clash about whether there's such a thing as a good that isn't good for anyone in particular — or is it a difference in what each side will accept as a reason, or two different questions wearing one label, one about what makes a decision right and one about what could be said to the person who loses? My hunch is it's mostly the last one, and that the objection proves too much anyway, since it looks like it would also condemn any risky policy or insurance scheme where we don't yet know who ends up paying. But maybe thats too cute: the whole point may be that within a single life their's someone who bears both the risk and the payoff, and across lives there isn't.

Tell me what the disagreement actually is, using what each side says rather than a tour of the literature, and then what would count as progress here — a distinction, a counterexample, a change of question — or whether the honest answer is that it can't be resolved, and why.
