You have three years and two graduate students, and you want to spend them on the philosophy of pain, using pain asymbolia as the lever: the syndrome in which patients with certain brain lesions report that they feel pain but are untroubled by it, showing little withdrawal or distress, with a standing dispute over whether they feel pain at all or only register damage. Take the reports at face value, and take seriously the possibility that asymbolia prises apart two things usually run together: pain's being bad for the person who has it, and pain's mattering, in the sense of giving anyone -- the sufferer or a bystander -- a reason to stop it. Which open questions should the group pursue, in what order, and why? I want the reasoning more than the list: which questions are upstream of which, which would actually shift a live downstream debate (whether pain is bad intrinsically or because it is unwanted, whether affectless pain is still pain, how to evaluate analgesics that dull suffering without dulling sensation, what to say about pain in infants and animals), which are tractable for philosophers rather than clinicians, and what evidence over the next two years should move a question up or down the order. Finally, name the questions you would deprioritize as artifacts of our categories rather than real joints in the phenomena.
