I teach an upper-level seminar that mixes philosophy and cognitive-science students, and I want to run a session on the induction of concepts from examples: the moment when a learner has to go from a handful of labeled cases to a rule, and nothing in the cases themselves announces which rule. Eighteen students, ninety minutes, one room with movable chairs. I want them to feel in their hands three things. First, that finitely many examples underdetermine the concept. Second, that what feels like simply seeing the pattern is really the work of a prior hypothesis space they carried in with them. Third, that a concept can fail to be expressible in the vocabulary they assumed, so no quantity of further evidence will ever make it reachable. I want the standard labels for these ideas to surface only in the debrief, after they have already run into the wall.

Design the activity around a deck of cards sorted into two labeled piles in the familiar Bongard manner, but with two additions. First, the hidden rule must not be a property of individual cards: build it so that it depends on a card's position in the sequence, or on its relation to the card before it, so that a team whose hypotheses are all of the form "classify each card on its own" can never fit the data no matter how long it stares. Second, hand the teams a restricted formal vocabulary in which they must eventually write their rule down — a fixed, short list of permitted predicates and connectives — and choose the hidden rule so that it is not expressible in that vocabulary. The vocabulary stands in for their prior hypothesis space, and the sting is that it looks generous until they need the one predicate it withholds.

Lay out the sequence of phases with timings; the training deck and the held-out deck (give me the actual cards, both as raw labels and as whatever content is printed on them, and make sure several genuinely different rules fit every training card and come apart only on the held-out ones); the restricted vocabulary I distribute; the rule-writing and wagering mechanics; and a scoring scheme with no real money that rewards calibrated confidence and punishes both over-general and over-specific rules. Then a debrief that maps each phase onto a piece of the idea, states what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including what to do when a team produces a rule that fits the entire training deck yet is not the intended one, and when a team protests that the intended rule is unfair — and is candid about what the activity cannot reproduce: chiefly that I chose the true rule in advance, so the room may leave still believing there was a fact of the matter all along, and that a vocabulary handed out in advance is a thin stand-in for a hypothesis space that real learners never get to inspect.
