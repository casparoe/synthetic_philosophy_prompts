I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent the better part of a year arguing about politics with friends, and I keep running into the same puzzle, and I would rather get the puzzle straight before I read anything else about it. Please take the role of a political philosopher who works on the ethics of public life — someone patient with an amateur but unwilling to soften the hard parts. The puzzle, as I have it, is this. We seem to want two incompatible things from anyone who holds power: that they never do certain things (torture a prisoner, lie to the public, let some people die so that more may live), and that they be willing to do exactly those things when the alternative is a catastrophe. And when one of them does it, we do not simply sigh and say that was the right call; we also seem to want guilt, an apology, perhaps a resignation or a prosecution, as though something genuinely wrong had occurred that the rightness of the outcome does not erase. I want the specification, not the defence of any particular answer: what would an account of this have to get right in order to be adequate? Begin by brainstorming the candidate requirements and only then commit to a list — I want to see the pruning, not just the survivors.

The cases and distinctions I would want the list tested against, at a minimum: the ticking-bomb case in which the wrong act is the only way to prevent a massacre; the case in which a mayor lies to stop a riot and the lie works; the case in which the same act is done by someone who wants the outcome for himself rather than for the public; the case in which it later turns out the act was not in fact necessary, though it was reasonably believed to be; the case in which the harm is spread across many officials so that no single person did it, which I have heard described as a different problem from this one, and I want to know whether the account must distinguish them; and the case in which the person who does the deed is not the person who benefits from it. Two suggestions friends have made that I suspect any account must confront rather than dodge: first, that perhaps it is the office that is dirty and not the person, so that a clean man may hold a dirty post; second, that the whole phenomenon is moral luck or tragedy with a grander name, and that an account unable to say what is different about it has failed.

So: the requirements list, each requirement paired with the case or consideration that motivates it; a judgement on which of them are genuine requirements and which are merely the prejudices of one camp — I have a suspicion that much of what people demand here is smuggled in from a particular view about what the word wrong means, and I would like that exposed; the part I most want, any subset of the requirements that looks jointly unsatisfiable, with the argument, since if two of the things we demand cannot both be had then the list itself is the result; and a closing note on which single requirement does the most to eliminate the popular families of answers (the straight consequentialist line, the absolutist line, the everything-is-tragic-conflict line). Think it through carefully and show your work on the tensions rather than smoothing them over. I do not need citations, references, or a reading list — I will track those down myself — and please write it as continuous prose with a clear enumerated list inside it.
