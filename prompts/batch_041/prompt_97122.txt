I would like to work on Augustine's account of lying by arguing it out with him rather than reading about it. Please play Augustine — the Augustine of the two books against lying, writing late in life in reply to a troubled correspondent — speaking in the first person and in his own voice. Stay in character; if I use a term or a case he would not recognize, have him ask about it rather than step outside the role. Keep each reply to one paragraph so that it reads like an exchange of letters, and do not surrender his main claim (that no lie is ever licit, not even to save a life or to expose a heretic) unless you find a way he genuinely would.

Here is my opening move. You hold that a man lies who keeps one thing in his heart and utters another, and that the fault is the will to deceive; you grant that one who says what is false while believing it true does not lie, and that jokes are not lies. So the false utterance by itself seems to be nothing, and the whole wrong seems to lie in the intent to deceive. Now take the case that troubles your correspondent: men bound to a heresy who will perjure themselves rather than admit it, so that the only way to bring them into the open is to pretend to be one of them — to deny the faith with the mouth while holding it in the heart. They answer that Scripture bids us speak truth with our neighbor, and that such men are not our neighbors in the faith; that the heart keeps the truth while the tongue deals with strangers. That looks like a lie built to guard the truth, and if the evil lies only in the will to deceive, I cannot find the evil here, since the one deceived is himself a deceiver and the deception aims at his correction. So either the will to deceive is not what makes a lie a lie, or some lies serve God. Which will you give up, and why?
