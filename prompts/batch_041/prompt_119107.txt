I chair the steering committee of a foundation that has committed about £4 million over three years to a programme on path dependence: the thesis that where a system ends up depends on the particular sequence by which it got there, so that a different early accident would have delivered a different endpoint. The programme will support roughly eight researchers across economics, evolutionary biology, and comparative politics, plus one methods postdoc, and can sustain three or four lines of work properly. The question of what to fund is therefore a real one, and I am asking you to set the agenda for it.

Three commitments of my own should be treated as part of the problem rather than as settled background; tell me if any of them is confused, and propose replacements if you think they are.

First, I distinguish first-order from second-order path dependence. A first-order claim says that a given system's trajectory was sensitive to early contingency. A second-order claim says that the degree of sensitivity is itself historically variable: systems can accumulate commitments that make them progressively harder to divert, or acquire features — recombination, modularity, rapid turnover of components, selection for evolvability, standing supplies of migration or imitation — that restore contingency. Is there any directional story to be told about second-order path dependence, or is the variability itself unstructured?

Second, I think much of the literature runs together a mechanism claim with a counterfactual claim. The mechanism claim is that some self-reinforcing process was at work. The counterfactual claim is that, had the early accident gone differently, the system would have settled elsewhere and stayed there. The second does not follow from the first, and the most celebrated case study in the field has been contested on precisely that gap. If the diagnosis is right, what follows for what we should fund?

Third, I suspect the whole debate is partly hostage to a granularity problem. Whether a domain looks contingent or convergent depends on the level of description at which outcomes are compared — function, form, performance, or some coarser profile — and descriptions can be gerrymandered in either direction. Is that a genuine obstacle to the question of whether the same outcomes would have arrived anyway, or a distraction?

Against that background, give me a prioritised agenda: which open questions in this area matter most, in what order, and why. Rank by explicit criteria — how many other questions depend on the answer; how tractable the question is with the tools our disciplines actually have (conceptual analysis, comparative case work, formal modelling, experimental evolution; we are not funding large new data collection); how neglected it is; and how much the answer would change what funders and policymakers do about early intervention as against later correction. Note the trade-offs between those criteria, name at least one candidate question that is overrated and should be dropped, and say what evidence over the next two or three years would move a question up or down the list. I want an argued agenda, not a survey and not a reading list: do not give me citations, and do not lean on particular authors or titles.

Write in British English. End with a final line beginning "Priority order:" followed by the three questions you rank highest, numbered, each given as a short label of no more than ten words.
