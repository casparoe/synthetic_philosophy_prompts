I am a retired physical chemist — thirty years in a spectroscopy lab before I traded the bench for a reading chair — and for the past two years I have been working through moral philosophy without a teacher, which means my instincts are still those of a laboratory: when a chain of reasoning yields a contradiction, I assume the chain is broken somewhere and set about finding the joint that failed. I have lately been reading about antinatalism, and about the asymmetry that is generally credited to Benatar — the set of claims that pain is bad and pleasure good when they occur, that the absence of pain is good even where there is no one to experience that good, and that the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived by it. I believed I had a firm grip on this until a colleague in our reading group walked me into the following argument, which I have not been able to escape and whose final step I find genuinely repellent. I would like you to tell me which step to reject, why that step looked so innocent when we wrote it down, and what I must surrender in order to reject it.

Here is the argument as it stood on the whiteboard. (1) When a person exists, her pleasure is good for her and her pain is bad for her. (2) The absence of pain is good, even when no one exists to enjoy that absence. (3) The absence of pleasure is not bad unless there exists someone who is deprived of it. (4) If one state of affairs is good in some respect and bad in none, while another is bad in some respect, then the first is better than the second. (5) Compare the state in which a given person is brought into existence, her life containing a great deal of pleasure and some pain, with the state in which she is never brought into existence at all. (6) In the latter state there is no pain, which by (2) is good; there is no pleasure either, but by (3) this is not bad. (7) In the former state there is pleasure, good by (1), and there is pain, bad by (1). (8) Hence the state of never existing is good in one respect and bad in none, so by (4) it is better than existing. (9) Nowhere did the reasoning depend on how much pleasure the life contained, so it holds even for a life of unbroken bliss. (10) Therefore it is better that no one ever be brought into existence — and since the same ledger can be drawn for a person already alive, by setting her continued existence against her ceasing to exist, it is better that each of us cease to exist as well. My colleague takes the whole thing to be a reductio of the asymmetry and would throw it out entire; I am less sure, because I suspect a single joint has failed and the rest of the frame is sound, and because I would far rather keep the asymmetry than accept (10). I should say plainly which claims I am most reluctant to part with: I would give up almost anything before (1), which seems to me the plainest moral datum I possess, and I would be sorry to lose (3), which strikes me as the only thing standing between the asymmetry and a general pessimism about all possible lives. Tell me where the argument goes wrong, or, if it does not, which premise is least costly to abandon and what that abandonment quietly commits me to.
