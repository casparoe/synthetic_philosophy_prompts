Since taking my retirement I have been trying to make up for a lifetime spent almost entirely in the physical sciences, and for the past few months I have been sitting in on a reading group that is working its way through the paradoxes of knowledge. Next week is our last meeting and I have been asked to close it, which is how I have come to spend three evenings on the surprise examination. I follow the pupil's reasoning without difficulty: the examination cannot be given on the last day, because by the evening before it would be the only day left and therefore no surprise; and once the last day is struck out the day before it falls in the same way, and so on back through the week until the announcement has eaten itself entirely. I follow equally well what happens next, namely that the examination is given, that it is a surprise, and that the clever pupil is left with nothing to say. What I cannot get straight, and what I would like a poem to help me see rather than a paragraph to tell me, is the sense in which the argument is supposed to be defective rather than merely unlucky. I have a suspicion, which I cannot yet defend, that it has something to do with the pupil needing to go on trusting the announcement at precisely the point where he has just finished proving that it cannot be trusted, but I may well have the diagnosis backwards.

What I would like, then, is a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, two refrains — spoken in the voice of that pupil in the moment the examination is served and he realises that today is the day. Let the first refrain be the teacher's announcement, and the second the conclusion the pupil drew from it. A reader who knows the paradox should be able to tell at once which is which, and I would like the closing quatrain to set the two side by side in such a way that neither cancels the other — the announcement vindicated, the inference shown to have been something other than stupidity. Since by hypothesis the pupil cannot know the day, he must not name it: if he reckons backwards through the week he must count positions rather than days, and that counting ought to be visible in the way the stanzas turn. Loose iambic pentameter is welcome, but scansion is not what I care about. I care that the moment at which his reasoning quietly requires the announcement to hold true is carried by the fall of the lines rather than stated outright, and that a reader who has never heard of the paradox still finds a poem at the end of it.
