ok so for this seminar I have to hand in a "here's my line on Mengzi and Xunzi on human nature, defend it" paper, and before I commit to anything I want someone to check whether my commitments actually fit together, because I have a nagging feeling that they don't and I can't see where the leak is.

Heres roughly where I've landed:

1. Xunzi is basically right that what we're born with, left to itself, is just desire and self-regard -- the stuff that makes us fight over scarce things -- and that what actually makes people decent is ritual practice and deliberate effort, the whole "wei" business. Crooked wood doesn't steam itself straight.

2. Mengzi is basically right that there's something inborn that answers to other people's distress -- the kid about to fall down a well case does it for me every time -- and that this is the seed of morality, not just a lucky byproduct of good training.

3. When Mengzi says human nature is good I take that as a claim about what we are, not the thin claim that we could turn out good. Same for Xunzi's "bad": not "we could go bad" but "this is the raw material you're working with."

4. Moral failure is basically environmental. Bad harvests, bad rulers, bad company. Nobody is rotten by constitution, people get submerged in circumstances.

5. The ritual that Xunzi thinks does all the work is a human invention -- the sages made it, gradually, over generations, out of nothing given. I don't see a paradox there.

6. The Mengzi/Xunzi fight is a real disagreement, not two people using "nature" in different senses and talking past each other.

7. I think the basic moral emotion is shame -- the sense of being seen and found wanting, the thing ritual trains -- and that compassion is downstream of it, more like what tells you you've already failed someone.

8. I don't think "human nature is good/bad" is a prediction about how people will behave. It's more like advice about what to do with what you've got.

My guess is the trouble is number 5, but honestly I might be looking in the wrong place entirely. What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which pairs are genuinely in tension and how bad, which ones only look bad and why they dissolve, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me to take it. Rank them by severity -- I'd much rather know which single commitment is doing most of the damage than get eight bullet points all weighted the same. And if it turns out two of these are in tension only becuase I've stated one of them more strongly than I need to, say which one and what the weaker version buys me.
