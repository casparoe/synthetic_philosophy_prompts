I teach a 400-level seminar in the philosophy of medicine, and I am now writing the final exam. A recurring theme of the course has been the dispute between naturalist and normativist accounts of disease — the biostatistical account and the harmful-dysfunction analysis — and by this point my students can reproduce both in their sleep, which is exactly the problem: I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting either view, but that also does not depend on their having memorized some obscure paper, since the exam is closed-book and they may not bring notes. So I have built the question around a case that the two familiar accounts handle asymmetrically, together with a third position that I sketched in the last two lectures and that exists nowhere in the literature, so that the students have to reason rather than remember. Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme worth 30 marks that names the specific moves a top answer must contain, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each move wrong.

The case I have in mind, which the question should state as a stipulation for the exam rather than as a fact the students are expected to check, is adult lactase non-persistence: the ordinary mammalian pattern in which the enzyme that digests milk sugar is downregulated after weaning. In the species as a whole this is the statistically typical condition, and the persistence variant is the recent and geographically localized exception. Consider two individuals who are physiologically identical in this respect. The first lives in a society whose food supply depends on dairy; the second lives in a society with no dairy at all and no expectation that adults can digest it. The first has symptoms, distress, and a demand for treatment; the second notices nothing. The question should ask students to do four things. First, to say what the biostatistical account must say about each individual, and what it must say if the reference class is drawn more narrowly than the species, and to explain why the choice of reference class is not settled by the biology. Second, to say what the harmful-dysfunction analysis must say about each individual, and what happens to that analysis if the relevant function is specified by the mechanism's selected effect rather than by its causal role. Third, to assess the position I presented in lecture, which I called the two-index account: a state counts as a disease only if some mechanism fails to perform its selected function and that failure impairs the organism's ability to meet the demands of its actual environment. Here the students should have to say whether the second index lets the account retain the value-free status the naturalist wants or whether it merely relocates the reference-class problem — the demands of the actual environment being just as much a matter of choice as the reference class — and to distinguish the version in which the environment is taken descriptively from the version in which it is fixed by a privileged, species-typical baseline. Fourth, to take a side and to name precisely what their side has to give up. The question should be pitched for a 90-minute essay, and everything should be in American English.
