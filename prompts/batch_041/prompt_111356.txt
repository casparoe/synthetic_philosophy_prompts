Spent most of last night arguing with my friend Priya about Gödel, Escher, Bach — she thinks the "strange loop" stuff is profound, I think it's a pun dressed up as a thesis — and by the end I'd half-convinced myself the disagreement is actually a decent research question and not just a pub arguement.

So: I'm applying for a small six-month humanities grant (roughly a day a week of my time) and I need a short research proposal, maybe 1500 words. I'll sketch my half-formed idea below; you turn it into something a review panel would take seriously. No citations please — refer to positions and arguments in plain terms and I'll add the references myself, I don't want to be sitting here fact-checking invented papers.

The idea: when people use the Gödelian analogy for minds, they smuggle two different things under one name. One is diagonal self-reference — the trick where a sentence, via coding, ends up saying something about itself, which is what actually drives the incompleteness proof. The other is a loop: a system that contains a model of itself and can therefore represent its own states. The book's rhetoric slides between the two constantly, and I suspect most of the "and therefore self-awareness" work is being done by the second, while all the mystery comes from the first. If that's right, the Gödelian part is doing much less than advertised — but the loop part might be doing something real, and that's the bit worth nailing down.

What I want out of the proposal: a question stated so it has a determinate answer — roughly, which features of the Gödel construction are actually needed to underwrite any of the mental properties people hang on it (self-reference, self-knowledge, self-modeling, and if we're being bold, self-awareness), and whether those features are specific to Gödelian diagonalization or turn up in any system that models itself. Then why the standard takes on this leave the question open — and please don't just rehash the old Lucas/Penrose "minds aren't machines" fight, that's not what I'm after; I want the analysis of the analogy, not a verdict on mechanism. Then the method, and I want it concrete: lay out the candidate features, and for each one find the cheapest formal or computational witness that has it — a program that prints its own source code, the diagonal in the halting problem, a system that predicts its own next output — and then check which of the mind-properties each witness can and can't carry. If diagonalization can be stripped out and the rest still stands, that's a result; if it can't, that's a result too. Say what would count as the project failing — my honest worry is that the distinction turns out to be verbal, in which case I'd rather the proposal admit that up front than pretend otherwise. And keep the scope honest for six months at a day a week: no new theorems, toy examples only.

Format: start straight in with the proposal itself, no throat-clearing. And at the end add a short note telling me how confident you are in the framing you've just handed me, and which single claim in it your least sure about — I'd rather know where it's shaky before the panel does.
