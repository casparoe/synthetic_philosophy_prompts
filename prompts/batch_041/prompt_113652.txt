I present a fortnightly interview podcast for a graduate seminar in technology policy, and next month's guest is a researcher who has spent a decade designing and evaluating containment measures for advanced machine-learning systems — output tripwires, behavioural monitors, sandboxing, the whole apparatus — and who has become, to the visible irritation of two of our faculty, a public advocate of what he calls the custody thesis. His claim is not merely that such measures are a useful stopgap while alignment research matures. It is that the project of making a model want the right thing is close to unfalsifiable, whereas the only safety claims we can genuinely evaluate are claims about what we can still do to a system whose internals and intentions we cannot inspect, on the assumption that it may be working against us. He bundles three sub-claims with this: that interpretability work belongs on the control ledger rather than the alignment one, since a probe into a model's internals is simply another instrument for constraining what the system can bring off; that control failures and alignment failures have different shapes, the former degrading and the latter snapping, so a straight expected-cost comparison between the two paradigms is rigged from the outset; and that most arguments about an alignment tax quietly assume the very thing in dispute, namely that we shall one day be able to verify a model's intentions rather than merely its behaviour under observation.

I should like eight to ten questions that go well past letting him restate the thesis. Please include at least one question pressing the objection that containment only ever buys time, since a system sufficiently more capable than its monitors can be expected to defeat any tripwire we build; one pressing the objection that control presupposes a specification, in that you must already know what would count as a violation before you can wire anything up, so the alignment problem has not been dodged but relocated; one on the symmetry of his own scepticism, since if the evidence for a model's good intentions cannot be assessed, neither can the evidence that his tripwires would hold under pressure; and one on the fact that the paradigm moves trust from the model to a small number of human operators, who may be compromised, coerced or merely tired. For each question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and close with a question about what he would accept as a decisive demonstration that the control paradigm has failed.
