Two friends of mine — one a painter, the other a literature professor who teaches aesthetics — spent most of a dinner party arguing, and I stayed out of it because I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it. It began when the painter said a novel the three of us had read was, for all its craft, not really good, and the professor said that just meant he hadn't liked it. I've set out the exchange as fairly as I can.

(1) The painter: when I call a work good, I am not reporting a feeling of mine. That is why we argue about such claims, why we try to get others to see what we see, why we think some people's judgments are worth more than others', and why a person can simply be wrong about a book. Nobody treats "this tastes salty to me" as something to dispute.

(2) The professor: people do dispute whether a dish is over-salted, and no one supposes saltiness is a property sitting in the dish waiting for the right instrument. The disputing shows that we care and that we use these claims to coordinate, not that there is a fact of the matter. And the skeptical point survives all of this: when two people with the same training, the same attention, and no prejudice simply feel differently, there is nothing left to appeal to. On this much, he said, Hume had it right — "good" is a compliment we pay to what pleases us.

(3) The painter: that is not Hume's point. The appeal is not to any one person's feeling but to the joint verdict of the good critics — those with strong sense, delicate feeling, long practice, wide comparison, and freedom from prejudice — which is why we defer to the critic who has read and looked at more, and why the untrained verdict counts for less.

(4) The professor: that only moves the problem. Either you can say, without circularity, who the good critics are and why their verdicts are the correct ones, in which case you have a real standard and the subjectivism is quietly abandoned, or you cannot, in which case "the good critics" means "whoever currently holds the credentials," and the painter has given up the objectivity he wanted. Nor does an idealized critic help: if no actual person is the ideal judge, the standard belongs to nobody, and it cannot tell either of us who is right tonight.

(5) The painter: it is a false dilemma. A standard can be constituted by a practice of judging rather than discovered outside it — as the standard for a good chess move is constituted by the game rather than found in nature. And I can say who the good critics are without circularity: the ones who notice more, distinguish more finely, and can give reasons that survive scrutiny.

(6) The professor: chess has a goal and rules, so it comes with a criterion built in; art has neither, and the painter keeps borrowing the authority of a criterion while denying that any exists. The colorblindness comparison also cuts against him. We can give a colorblind man an independent test that he fails, so we know he is missing something; there is no independent test that a reader with no feeling for this novel fails. His verdict is merely different from ours.

(7) The painter: there is such a test. You show him what is in the book — the pattern of the sentences, the way a scene is prepared three hundred pages earlier, the turn of the last line — and he either comes to see it or he does not. If he does not, we say his reading is thin, and that is not the same as saying we disliked his reading. The professor replied that this begs the question, since "coming to see it" already means "having the right response."

I have numbered the moves. Please adjudicate them one at a time, saying for each whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible but incomplete, and briefly why. I am especially interested in three things: whether the dilemma in (4) is really exhaustive, and what (5) and (7) would each have to establish in order to escape it; whether the chess analogy and the colorblindness point in (6) do the damage the professor thinks they do; and whether the position the painter states in (3) is better read as a form of subjectivism with social machinery attached or as a genuine standard in the ordinary sense. Finish by saying what both of them are missing — some distinction or possibility that neither has put on the table — and who has the stronger case overall.
