I am a doctoral student working on the epistemology of self-doubt, and I have constructed an argument for a limited form of epistemic akrasia that now strikes me as proving far more than I can accept. It runs as follows. (1) A belief is rational just in case it fits the believer's total evidence. (2) Evidence concerning how one arrived at a belief — higher-order evidence — is itself part of one's total evidence. (3) Hence, when the higher-order evidence indicates that one's first-order reasoning on some question has probably misfired, the belief that reasoning produced does not fit one's total evidence, and one ought not to hold it. (4) Consider, however, a case: I add a long column of figures correctly and the sum is right, yet I know from experience that on columns of this length I err four times in ten; here I am strongly inclined to say both that I am justified in believing the sum I reached, since the arithmetic is sound, and that I am justified in sharply lowering my confidence, since my track record is poor. (5) If (4) is correct, then (1)–(3) are false and rational akrasia is not merely possible but ordinary; if (1)–(3) are correct, then my verdict in (4) is simply an error, and the soundness of the arithmetic is irrelevant to justification. (6) Taking the first horn, it follows that whenever first-order and higher-order evidence diverge one may rationally believe p while judging that one ought not to believe it — which proves far too much, since it would equally license the bigot who concedes his own bias to go on believing. I am most attached to (1) and would rather not surrender the idea that justification is a matter of fitting the evidence. Tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; let the fifth item state the consideration that would most likely reverse your verdict, or the point at which your reasoning is most vulnerable.
