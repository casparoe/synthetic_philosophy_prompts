My friend and I spent about two hours last night arguing over a page of Schopenhauer and I came away less sure what it even says than when we started. Backstory: I'm slowly making my way through the pessimism essays in one of those old Victorian translations, mainly because this same friend keeps quoting them at me, and I hit this stretch. First: "I know of no greater absurdity than that propounded by most systems of philosophy in declaring evil to be negative in its character. Evil is just what is positive; it makes its own existence felt." He goes on to say Leibnitz is especially keen on defending this and that his argument for it is a paltry sophism, and then: "It is the good which is negative; in other words, happiness and satisfaction always imply some desire fulfilled, some state of pain brought to an end." A little further down there's the bit where he says if you want to know whether pleasure outweighs pain in this world, "let him compare the respective feelings of two animals, one of which is engaged in eating the other." And near the end, the one that started the fight: "If you try to imagine, as nearly as you can, what an amount of misery, pain and suffering of every kind the sun shines upon in its course, you will admit that it would be much better if on the earth as little as on the moon the sun were able to call forth the phenomena of life; and if, here as there, the surface were still in a crystalline state."

My friend says this is basically negative utilitarianism in embryo — pain is the only thing that's really there, pleasure is just the absence of pain, so the only thing that matters morally is reducing suffering, and the moon line is the dead giveaway. I pushed back that the positive/negative thing looks like it's doing something else entirely: it reads to me like a claim about the metaphysics of the will, or about what desire is (wanting as a lack, so satisfaction is just a hole getting plugged), or maybe he's just needling the old idea that evil is a privation, a mere absence of good, and flipping it. None of that obviously tells you anything about which states have intrinsic value or how they rank. He thinks I'm overcomplicating it.

So, help. Two things I really want sorted out. (1) In that passage, what work is the positive/negative contrast actually doing — is it a claim about the reality of evil as against the privation view he's mocking, a phenomenological claim about desire and satisfaction, or an axiological ranking of pains and pleasures? And which of those, if any, gets you the asymmetry that negative utilitarianism needs, namely that relieving suffering always outranks producing pleasure? (2) The two-animals line — is that an argument or just an invitation to agree with him? What is it supposed to be comparing, exactly, and does it smuggle in the conclusion? Don't hand me a smooth summary of the essay; I want the actual structure of the reasoning, and where the seams are. Also tell me how confident you are in the reading you give me and single out the one step you're least sure about, because I'd rather know where the interpretation is shaky than get an answer that sounds equally sure of everything.
