Problem Set Question

Individuals decide in sequence whether to adopt a new technology (A) or retain the status quo (B). Each person observes the full sequence of earlier choices but not the private signals on which those choices were based; each holds a private signal that is informative but noisy about which option is better, and all signals are drawn from the same distribution. Address the following.

(a) Define an informational cascade and distinguish it from herd behavior in general.
(b) Explain why, once a cascade has formed, it can be rational for a later individual to disregard her own private information.
(c) Explain the sense in which informational cascades are said to be fragile, and what this implies for how durable they are and how they begin.
(d) A separate literature describes "reputational herding," in which a decision-maker disregards her private information not because she believes the crowd is better informed, but because she fears the career consequences of being seen to deviate. State how reputational herding differs from an informational cascade, and say whether the two can be told apart from observed choices alone.

Student's Submitted Answer

(a) An informational cascade arises when people rationally decide that the best course is to follow the actions of those who came before them, because their own private information is too weak to overturn what the accumulated choices already reveal. Once the public belief is sufficiently strong, the private signal is swamped and the individual's optimal action is to imitate. Herding is simply this: a situation in which individuals do what others are doing. Because a cascade is defined by imitative behavior, "cascade" and "herd" name the same phenomenon — where a cascade exists a herd exists, and where a herd exists a cascade exists.

(b) The individual is Bayesian, so it is entirely rational. Given the prior and the observed sequence of choices, the posterior already favors the action the crowd has chosen; her own signal is a single draw from a noisy distribution, so adding it leaves the posterior roughly where it was. Disregarding her signal is therefore not a lapse but the correct response to the information carried by others' choices — which is why the phenomenon is often called rational herding.

(c) Cascades are fragile in that the private information they suppress is never recovered, so the outcome may well be the wrong one. Once a cascade has formed, however, it is self-reinforcing: each new person who follows adds another observation to the public record, and so the cascade becomes more secure with every decision. To reverse it one would need a long run of contrary choices, since no single individual's private signal is strong enough to break the pattern. Cascades are in this respect like a heavy flywheel — hard to start and hard to stop.

(d) Reputational herding differs in its motive: the manager follows the crowd for career reasons rather than because she thinks the crowd is better informed. But the observable consequence is the same, and since we can see only the choices, the two cannot be distinguished from the data alone; both will present as a cascade. And indeed, because a reputational herd also leads people to suppress their private information, it is at bottom just a variety of informational cascade.
