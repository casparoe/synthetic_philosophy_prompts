Bit of a wierd one. I'm 34, I run a small landscaping business, and about two months ago I started giving away 10% of what I make. I assumed the hard part would be the giving. Turns out the hard part is picking where it goes. I've now spent the better part of three weekends in a spreadsheet comparing (a) a deworming charity and (b) a fund that does pandemic-preparedness work, and at some point I realised I wasn't doing a hard calculation, I was doing an unbounded one. Every number I pinned down had a sign that depended on something I have no handle on at all: whether the deworming money crowds out other donors, whether the health gains raise or lower future population and therefore future emissions, whether the pandemic money shifts some political equilibrium, and so on down. I know this worry has a name — something like cluelessness — and I know the standard replies (you're not clueless about everything, uncertainty is still uncertainty, act on what you've got), so please don't just walk me through the literature. What I want is a number plus the bones of it.

Here's the claim I want your credence in, stated as tightly as I can: when two altruistic options have direct effects within about a factor of two of each other, the true difference in expected value is more often than not set by indirect channels — equilibrium effects, effects on who exists later, knock-on political and economic effects — that the decision-maker can't estimate with any reliability, and that are correlated enough in sign that they don't simply cancel. The claim that the tie gets broken by the swamp, basically.

Give me your credence in that, and then the decomposition, a rough number and one line for each term:

- how often the indirect channels are big enough to matter at all, relative to the direct ones;
- given that they are, how likely they are to be sign-correlated, so the errors compound, rather than roughly symmetric, so they wash out;
- given all that, how likely it is that some decision procedure I could actually run — robust or ambiguity-averse choice rules, deliberately reasoning in a simplified toy model, treating the swamp as noise and going with the direct effects — restores a defensible ranking anyway;
- and whatever other term you think belongs.

Then the combination, and which single term the final number is most sensitive to.

One more thing, and I mean it: tell me what would change your mind. Which argument or piece of evidence would move your number the most, and where is your own reasoning here most likely to be wrong? I'd rather have a number with its weak joint marked than a hedge.
