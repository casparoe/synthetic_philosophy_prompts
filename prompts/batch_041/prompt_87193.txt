I have been trying for some time to put a name to a pattern of argument that keeps surfacing whenever people argue about what one ought to eat, and I have not been able to establish whether it already has one.

The shape of it is this. Someone maintains that a plant-based diet is better for animals than a diet that includes meat. The reply is that growing plants is not harmless either: harvesting a field kills mice, birds, and insects, and those deaths are as real as the deaths of the animals slaughtered for meat, so the comparison comes out roughly even. A stronger version of the reply says that a pasture-raised animal can be fed on land that is never harvested at all, so a diet containing some meat might kill fewer animals per calorie than a strictly plant-based one. A third version drops the arithmetic and says that since every way of feeding oneself kills something, no diet can be preferred to any other. The animals dying in the field, I note, are not raised for anyone's table; they live there, and the harvest comes to them.

What interests me is not whether the figures are right. Sometimes they are disputed on straightforward empirical grounds, and that dispute is ordinary. What interests me is a feature shared by the versions that are not ordinary. In those, the harm invoked is one that, by the arguer's own account, would have occurred just the same had the other option been chosen: the field would have been harvested, the mice would have died. Yet it is entered into the ledger against the option under attack, as though choosing it were what brought the harm about. An effect that is indifferent to the choice is treated as a cost of the choice, and the option that had been winning the comparison is declared to have lost it.

I meet the same shape outside this debate: the bicycle whose tyres kill insects, the wind turbine that kills birds, the ordinary purchase that somewhere supports something deplorable. I cannot tell whether this is one move or a family of them. I have been told it is merely whataboutism, but that seems to concern changing the subject, whereas here the subject is kept and the accounting is disputed. I have also been told it is the familiar distinction between what one aims at and what one merely foresees, but the arguers are rarely claiming that the deaths are unforeseen; they are claiming that the deaths belong in the same column. Nor is it simply the complaint that nothing I do will make a difference, since the arguer is not denying that choices have effects, only insisting that this effect belongs to one of them.

So: is there an established name for counting a harm that is fixed across the options as though it were a cost of one of them? If there is, I want to know how it is defined, how far it reaches, and which of the versions above fall under it. If there is not, I want a term coined for it, with a definition that keeps it apart from the neighbouring ideas it resembles, and some account of the boundary: when is citing a background harm a legitimate part of comparing two options, and when does it become this mistake? A bare label would leave me where I started.
