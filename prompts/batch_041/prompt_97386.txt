I hold the self-indication assumption: that I should reason as if I were a random observer drawn from the set of all possible observers, so that the bare fact of existing is itself evidence favoring worlds that contain more observers. I want a map of what would count against it, sorted from decisive to merely awkward, plus an honest verdict on whether the assumption can be defended without being qualified into unfalsifiability. Cover the familiar line of attack usually called the presumptuous philosopher -- that this reasoning licenses near-certainty about a vastly larger world on no empirical basis -- but spend at least as much effort on the pressure points I find less discussed. First, the reference class: if "observer" is read broadly enough to include animals, insects, or future machines, their numbers could swamp ours and reverse the assumption's practical verdicts, and I want to know whether that shows the principle is merely misapplied or genuinely ill-defined. Second, the unit of counting: the assumption speaks of observers, but if we count observer-moments instead -- or if a life can be sliced at a finer or coarser grain of experience -- then a world with the same people living the same span can be made more or less populous at will, and nothing in the principle fixes the grain. Tell me whether that renders the assumption not just counterintuitive but indeterminate. Which of these defeaters should move a committed self-indication theorist, which would they absorb by a qualification, and what, if anything, would remain of the position once the qualifications are made?
