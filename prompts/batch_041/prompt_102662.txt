I'm putting together a short position statement on self-deception for a seminar and I want a consistency check before I circulate it. Here is where I have landed.

First, self-deception is real, and the ordinary cases are genuine instances of it rather than figures of speech or plain error: the man who cannot bring himself to see that his business is failing, the parent who fails to register the plain signs of a child's addiction.

Second, deception is essentially a matter of intention. To deceive someone into believing that p is to intend to bring it about that they believe p while taking p to be false.

Third, in self-deception there is no second party and no unconscious homunculus. The deceiver and the deceived are one and the same person, acting at the same time.

Fourth, belief is not under direct voluntary control, and one cannot knowingly adopt a belief one regards as false. You cannot set out to make yourself believe something you take to be untrue.

Fifth, self-deception is continuous with the biases and motivated reasoning that psychology studies — wishful thinking, the reduction of dissonance, the search for confirming evidence — differing from them in degree rather than in kind.

Sixth, the self-deceived person is sincere. From the inside, self-deception feels like ordinary conviction; the man is not aware of having talked himself into anything, and he does not experience himself as choosing what to believe.

I suspect these mostly hang together, but I would rather find out now than in the room. Tell me which of them actually pull against each other and which only appear to; for each real conflict, rank it by severity, say what would have to give and what that costs, and tell me whether any single commitment is doing most of the damage. I am not attached to any one of them in particular, so do not shield any of them on my behalf.
