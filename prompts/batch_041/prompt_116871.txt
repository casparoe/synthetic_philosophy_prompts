An essay I read on responsibility inside organizations opens by restating Frankfurt's brain-implant case and then draws some large conclusions from it about free will, and I would like to know how much of that restatement I can trust before I cite it. As I understand the essay's version: a technician has implanted a device in an agent that will fire if and only if the agent is about to decide not to vote for a particular candidate, and if it fires it will make him decide to vote for that candidate; the agent in fact decides on his own to vote for the candidate, so the device never fires. Because the device would have produced the same decision, the agent could not have decided otherwise, yet we hold him responsible. So the ability to do otherwise is not a condition on moral responsibility, the principle of alternative possibilities is dead, and — since the case for incompatibilism rests on determinism excluding alternative possibilities — the case also shows that determinism is no threat to responsibility: compatibilism is vindicated, and the libertarian's demand for alternatives is exposed as having no bearing on blame. The essay adds that the only resistance comes from people who insist on a flicker of freedom, some tiny alternative such as being able to have begun to decide otherwise, and that this is a rearguard action, because the case was constructed precisely to close off every alternative, so an appeal to a flicker is just a refusal to accept the result. I am a compatibilist myself and would like to believe all of this, but I have also read somewhere that some incompatibilists use this very case themselves, which makes me suspicious of the essay's triumphant tone. What should I accept, what would a philosopher working on this wince at, and does the essay's conclusion survive once the restatement is corrected? Answer with exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no closing summary — each one or two sentences: the first should be the claim I can keep as stated; the second, a simplification that is defensible in itself but that the essay overreads; the third and fourth should each take up one claim that genuinely misrepresents the position, in descending order of how much it matters, saying in each case what a defender would object; and the fifth should say whether the essay's closing conclusion survives the corrections.
