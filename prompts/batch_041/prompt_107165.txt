Next month I present a paper at a small workshop on distributive justice, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis: the standard reply to the leveling-down objection — that prioritarianism ranks distributions by a concave transform of individuals' absolute levels and therefore never claims a world in which everyone is worse off and no one is better off is itself better — succeeds only by quietly giving up the idea that prioritarianism is a theory about the goodness of outcomes. I argue that once the view is relocated from axiology into the theory of choice (what I call the relocation move), the leveling-down objection mutates into a harder one: the view can no longer explain why the worse off warrant priority without covertly appealing to inequality, the very thing the reply was supposed to expunge. The audience is mixed. Most are welfare economists and social-choice theorists who care about axiomatic properties — Pareto, anonymity, separability, continuity — and will go after the formal machinery; several are egalitarian political philosophers who hold that equality is intrinsically valuable and will go after the moral story. Give me the objections I will actually get from these two camps, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased in that camp's own vocabulary, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Skip the stock objections from the literature unless this audience would actually raise them.
