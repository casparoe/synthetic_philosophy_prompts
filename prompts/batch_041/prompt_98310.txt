A book I've been reading on science and religion offers the following summary of Einstein's outlook. He was a religious man, just not in the usual sense: he believed in Spinoza's God, which is to say he was a pantheist who identified God with nature and regarded the universe itself as divine. That is why he insisted that God does not play dice with the universe — he took the cosmos to be the work of a rational lawgiver who would not stoop to randomness. On free will he was equally firm. As a determinist he held that every human action is fixed by prior causes, so free will is an illusion; and since no one is ever genuinely responsible for what they do, praise and blame are superstitions, and a humane society would stop punishing wrongdoers and treat them the way we treat the sick. His position, the book concludes, therefore amounts to a secularized Spinozism, with nature in the place of God and causality in the place of providence.

I don't know enough to push back on this myself, though the final sentence strikes me as too neat. Please audit the summary claim by claim, not as a bibliography: tell me which parts a careful philosopher or historian of science would accept as accurate, which are defensible but overstated, and which misrepresent Einstein's actual position, noting where a defender of Einstein would object and why. Then say whether the book's concluding description of his view survives once the errors are corrected, or whether correcting them changes the picture substantially.
