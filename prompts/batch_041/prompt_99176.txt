Two camps in the interpretation of Locke’s educational thought have been talking past each other. The first reads Locke as a proto-liberal theorist of autonomy: education is chiefly the cultivation of rational self-government, the child’s submission to the tutor is a temporary scaffold, and the goal is a person who can direct their own conduct by reason. The second reads him as a theorist of discipline and social formation: the child is governed from the start by habit, esteem, and shame; the tutor’s authority stands in for the community’s judgment; and the aim is to make virtue second nature rather than to make reason sovereign. Draft the joint statement that a committed member of each camp could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides accept about Locke’s aims, his account of human motivation, the roles of habit and reason, the place of esteem and shame, and the relation between the tutor’s authority and the child’s future freedom; (2) a disagreements table giving the precise remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording that the side holding it would accept; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each crux the argument or consideration both sides agree would bear on it, with each side’s prediction about what it would show; and (4) a note on which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal rather than substantive. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; refer to positions and arguments in plain terms, and do not invent quotations or page references. If the two-camp framing distorts Locke’s view — for instance, if the autonomy and discipline strands are too intertwined to separate, or if the camps are not genuinely opposed — say so plainly rather than manufacturing a joint statement. I would rather have the framing corrected than a polished document built on a confused premise.
