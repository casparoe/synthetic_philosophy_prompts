Two members of our village heritage committee fell out last week over a display panel in the village hall. The panel tells how, in the 1840s, eleven families were turned off their land and walked together to the coast and away; the story is the founding event of the village's idea of itself and is retold at the summer fête. A doctoral student working through the estate papers — the rentals, the bailiff's account book, the landowner's agent's letters — can find no trace of any such departure: the families appear to have gone one and two at a time over several years, some evicted, others simply following work. She has asked us to rewrite the panel. I am on the committee, said nothing, and cannot referee what followed.

Marta, a retired archivist, said we should rewrite it. The panel asserts who went, when, and together; those are claims answerable to evidence, and keeping the walk on the wall would be preferring a comfortable falsehood to our own history.

Dermot, who has lived here all his life and runs the fête, said she was treating memory as though it were bad history. The panel is not a hypothesis about the past but a record of what the village has made of the evictions — of who we take ourselves to be. The rentals record only what the agent troubled to write down; a family that walked away from a tied cottage need not appear in them at all, and the agent had every reason to set departures down as ordinary.

Marta said 'who we take ourselves to be' was doing the work of a licence. A story about eleven families walking to the coast together is either true or false, and those most damaged by it are the descendants of the people who actually left — some not evicted, none of them together — whose history is quietly replaced by a better one.

Dermot said a correction would be a second imposition. The panel was put up by the family at the big house, not by the village; the archive is itself a selection, made by people with an interest; and a student who arrived two years ago has no standing to tell a village what its own past was. A village that loses its story does not gain an accurate one in its place; it acquires hers.

Marta replied that standing has nothing to do with whether a claim is true, that 'the village' is not one voice and never was, and that the abuse of history elsewhere does not make this correction one more abuse.

Please go through the exchange point by point: who has the better of each move, which moves are real disagreements and which are the two of them talking past each other, and what both of them are missing. A definite verdict on each point would help me more than a general assurance that both sides have something to be said for them.
