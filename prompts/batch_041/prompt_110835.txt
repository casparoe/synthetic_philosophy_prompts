Suppose Charles Darwin were revived today, given a compressed but rigorous briefing on the biology, cognitive science, and moral philosophy that have developed since his death, and asked to write a short philosophical essay on the question: Does the scientific study of the origins of morality and religion vindicate or undermine the moral outlook he described and endorsed? The essay should address three developments that postdate him. First, the hypothesis, central to much cognitive science of religion, that religious belief is largely a by-product of ordinary cognitive biases, such as a tendency to detect agency, rather than a direct adaptation. Second, the proposal of moral bioenhancement: that biomedical interventions might be used to strengthen moral motivation and expand the circle of sympathy. Third, the metaethical debate over evolutionary debunking, in which it is argued that if our moral beliefs are products of natural selection for fitness rather than truth-tracking, then we have reason to doubt their objective validity. For each development, reconstruct the position Darwin would most likely take. Distinguish carefully between claims that follow from his settled views about the moral sense, sympathy, and the naturalistic study of mind, and claims that would require him to revise those views. Then identify the single point on which the historical record leaves his likely response most underdetermined, and explain why a responsible reconstruction should not pretend to settle it. Do not quote from Darwin's writings; paraphrase his views instead. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Write the essay in the third person, as a philosophical reconstruction rather than a pastiche.
