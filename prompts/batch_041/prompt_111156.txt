I sit on the art advisory committee at my university — we're the body that has to sign off on anything permanent that goes up on campus — and next Thursday we vote on installing a big weathering-steel spiral the donor has already paid for, on the lawn outside the library. Heres my problem: I've stood in front of the maquette three times now and it does nothing for me. I can see it's skillfully made, I can recite the artist's stated intent back to you, but I don't get it. Meanwhile the two people on the committee whose taste I actually trust (a curator, and a colleague who is never wrong about this stuff) both say it's the best piece we'll ever get to place, and most of the rest of the committee is just deferring to them.

Half of me says I trust experts about everything from vaccines to medieval history, so who am I to insist on first-hand experience before I'll vote? The other half says there's something off about voting yes on a piece I can't see the point of — that if I don't feel it, nobody else's feeling can be a substitute, and that our job is to exercise our own judgement, not ratify the curator's. One member keeps arguing that testimony can give me the facts about the work (materials, dimensions, that the artist welded it herself) but can never give me a reason for the verdict that it's good, and I honestly can't tell if thats a real distinction or a cop-out to make himself feel principled. (Our bylaws just say we certify "artistic excellence"; nothing says the excellence has to be visible to us personally.) I'm the member who's spent fifteen years reading rationalist blogs and telling everyone else to defer to people who know more than us, so I'd rather not embarrass myself here.

Write the short opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should land on a concrete recommendation — install, don't install, or install with conditions (if conditions, say what they are and when we'd review) — and it should give the strongest version of the side I'd be going against, not just my instinct dressed up. No citations or footnotes; this is going to deans. Structure it as: "Bottom line" — one paragraph, the recommendation and the date; "The case for going along with them" — about 300 words; "The case against" — about 300 words; "My call" — about 200 words, the actual reasoning and not just the verdict; "What I'm least sure about" — a short paragraph. Under two pages total.
