For the past several years, since retiring from the laboratory, I have been reading my way into the philosophy of mathematics, and I keep encountering a comparison that is asked to carry a great deal of weight — most recently in a popular book on the infinite, and before that from a mathematician friend who offered it as a deflationary remark. The continuum hypothesis, I am told, is independent of the standard axioms of set theory (Gödel showed that it cannot be refuted from them, Cohen that it cannot be proved), and this is said to be exactly the situation with Euclid's parallel postulate: once models of hyperbolic geometry had been constructed inside Euclidean geometry, so that the two stand or fall together as far as consistency is concerned, we are supposed to have stopped asking which of them was true and settled for choosing the geometry we found useful. By the same token, the argument runs, there is no fact of the matter about the continuum hypothesis, and set theorists who argue about it are either selecting an axiom system to suit themselves or deceiving themselves about what they are doing. I find the comparison seductive and I suspect it of being asked to do more than it can bear. Could you make the correspondence explicit — what plays the role of the axioms, of the parallel postulate, of the several models, and of the intended subject matter — and then say where the comparison breaks down, which of the disanalogies bear on the conclusion being drawn and which are incidental, and whether that conclusion survives once they are on the table? If the comparison cannot carry it, I would be grateful to know what you take the strongest available substitute to be. Assume only a physicist's background on my part, including a settled conviction that the geometry of the world is an empirical question.
