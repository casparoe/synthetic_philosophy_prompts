A friend of mine has been banging on about this argument for weeks and I've finaly caved and want a second opinion, because I can't find the join in it.

It's a version of Amartya Sen's liberal paradox, dressed up as a row between two neighbours, Anil and Bea. Each of them can either put a security light up in their own garden or leave it dark. So there are four outcomes: neither lights up (00), only Anil lights up (10), only Bea lights up (01), both light up (11).

Their preferences are odd but not mad:
- Anil likes the glow a light throws onto the shared path and would rather Bea had one; but he can't stand the glare or the wiring himself. So he ranks: 01 (Bea lights up, he doesn't) > 11 (both) > 00 (neither) > 10 (only he lights up).
- Bea is the mirror image: she wants Anil to light up, and given that, she'd rather not do hers. So she ranks: 10 > 11 > 00 > 01.

Then the arguement runs:
(1) A way of settling group questions is legitimate only if it satisfies two things: (U) if everyone prefers one outcome to another, the group treats the first as better; and (L) each person is decisive over at least one pair of outcomes -- for a liberal, over their own garden.
(2) To be legitimate in that sense, the group needs a single ranking of the outcomes that is complete and transitive, and that works whatever preferences people turn out to have.
(3) In the lights case no such ranking exists. (L) forces 00 above 10 (it's Anil's garden) and 10 above 11 (it's Bea's), while (U) forces 11 above 00 (they both prefer it). So 00 > 10 > 11 > 00, a loop.
(4) Nor is it just this case: whenever two or more people have views about each other's business, you can build preferences like these, so no ranking satisfies (U) and (L) together.
(5) So there's no legitimate way for the neighbours to settle the lights.
(6) And the same goes for any group, since there's always somebody with a view about somebody else's business.
(7) So political legitimacy is a fiction.

I'd like to keep (1) -- respecting clear unanimity and leaving people their own patch both feel non-negotiable to me. And I'm fairly sure the loop in (3) is real. But (6) and (7) feel like a mugging, and I'm certain something in the middle is doing more work than it lets on. One mate tried to rescue me by saying we simply shouldn't count preferences about other people's gardens at all, but that looks like it just guts (L) with extra steps and I'm not convinced.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine until now, and what does rejecting it cost me? Keep the settled bits separate from the bits that people who actually work on this still argue about -- don't hand me a contested fix as though it's the standard view. British English please, and keep it under 700 words.
