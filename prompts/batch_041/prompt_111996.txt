I am trying to pin down what the ordinary administration of income tax presupposes about the nature of the taxpayer's obligation, and what that presupposition implies for the old charge that taxation is theft.

Consider three features of the practice as it actually operates in the systems I know best. Withheld tax is not treated as a sum the employer merely owes the revenue: the withheld money is by statute a special fund held in trust for the state, and a failure to remit it is treated as a breach of trust rather than as an unpaid debt, with the individual responsible exposed to a personal liability that survives the employer's insolvency. The natural reading of that arrangement is that the money is the state's from the moment it is withheld, and the withholding agent a custodian who has misappropriated what was never his. Yet the same systems treat over-collection as a debt the state owes the taxpayer, repayable with interest, and under-collection as a debt the taxpayer owes the state, likewise bearing interest, with the ordinary incidents of the creditor-debtor relation: assessment, limitation periods, civil enforcement. On that reading the tax is not the state's money at all; it is something the state is owed, and the taxpayer is a debtor rather than a thief. And the deduction rules typically refuse a deduction for fines and penalties paid to the state while allowing a business to deduct, as a cost of doing business, the compensatory damages it pays to a private litigant — as though the payment to the state were the return of something that was never properly the taxpayer's, while the payment to the private party is a real cost.

What conception of the obligation does this practice presuppose? Does it commit itself to a proprietary conception — the state already owns the money, so non-payment is misappropriation, and the evader is indeed a thief — or only to an obligational one, on which the state is a creditor and non-payment is a breach of duty, no more a theft than failing to pay a supplier? Where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, in the sense that both conceptions would license exactly the same rules, so that the trust language and the criminalization of evasion are doing regulatory or rhetorical work rather than expressing a theory? And is the practice internally consistent, or does it need one theory to make sense of criminal enforcement and the personal liability of withholding agents and another to make sense of refunds, interest, and limitation periods?

Finally, what follows for the philosophical dispute? If the obligational conception supplies everything the practice needs, does that count against the claim that taxation is theft, or is the libertarian's theft a moralized notion — a wrongful taking of what is one's own by right — such that no administrative practice could confirm or refute it, and the apparent inconsistency is beside the point? If, on the other hand, the state does treat withheld money as its own property from the moment of withholding, does that concession do any work for the libertarian, or does it merely record what the law says, which is the very thing in dispute? And what would have to change in the practice for the proprietary conception to be operative rather than a convenient figure of speech — would the state have to stop paying interest on refunds, refuse them, or abandon the language of debt altogether?
