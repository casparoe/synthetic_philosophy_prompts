I am a junior at a mid-sized public high school and the only student member of the committee that administers our Student Opportunity Fund, a pool of roughly $1,400 that we raise each fall through a faculty talent show and a bake sale and that we are required to distribute in full before the last week of school. The fund exists to help students whose families cannot or will not pay for something school-related, and it is governed by three rules that students wrote years ago and that nobody has revisited since: awards must serve educational purposes, a phrase no one has ever defined; no single award may exceed $500; and no student may receive more than twice what any other student receives in the same year. I have to bring a recommendation to Thursday's meeting, and the four requests below are the only ones we received, so whatever I decide is what happens.

The requests are these. Maya, a sophomore whose family is comfortable, wants $260 for a robotics kit she says she will use every weekend, even though the school owns kits she can borrow whenever they are free. Devon, a junior who has been staying with relatives since his family lost their apartment, needs $180 for the graphing calculator required in his AP Calculus class, because every loaner in the library is checked out through June. Jonah, a freshman with dyslexia, needs a $1,100 reading device and software package that his special education team has recommended, which is more than the fund holds and more than twice what anyone else has asked for; his family has money but has refused to buy anything the school does not require. Amara, a senior, wants $120 toward the deposit for a summer journalism program at the state university that she says is the centerpiece of her college applications, and her family would have to choose between that deposit and her younger brother's dental work. Devon's grandmother, meanwhile, has offered to cover half his calculator.

When I asked my civics teacher about all this, she said the hard part is not deciding who deserves help but deciding what we are counting, and then the bell rang and she never finished the thought. I would like your advice on what I should actually recommend on Thursday, including whether I should follow our rules as written, reinterpret them, or ask the committee to change them, and what I should say to the students I end up turning down. If you bring in what people who study this kind of question argue, please be explicit about which parts are settled and which are live disagreements among them; I do not want a contested position presented to me as the obvious consensus, and I would like to know where you are least confident in your own advice.
