I write novels, and after twenty years at it I have noticed that the moral emotion I can make a reader feel and the moral emotion I can make a character feel are rarely the same one — and that the difference has consequences for a book that I did not choose and cannot always control. I would like an essay that settles a question I keep failing to settle myself.

The question is the old one about guilt and shame: which is the better moral emotion, and can a culture decide? I know the received answer. Guilt is act-directed, answers to a wronged party, and can be discharged by apology or restitution; shame is self-directed, answers to a witness, and can only be concealed or outgrown; therefore guilt is the more civilized emotion, and any culture that could trade shame for guilt should make the trade. I am not interested in a restatement of that answer. I want you to work through three difficulties that the received answer handles badly, and then reach a verdict anyway.

First, the audience problem. Guilt appears to require a wronged party; shame appears to require only a witness, and the witness can be internal. What follows for wrongs that have no face — complicity in harm whose victims are diffuse, distant, or already dead, or the quieter case of benefiting from arrangements one did not create? If guilt's demand for a determinate victim makes it go silent precisely where the wrong is largest, that is an argument for shame rather than against it, and I want to see it made properly.

Second, the disposition problem. Guilt is punctual: it attaches to an act and can be paid off. Shame is dispositional: it attaches to a self and cannot be paid off, only hidden or remade. Say plainly which of these makes a person likelier to act well the next time, and whether the answer changes when the wrongdoer is an institution rather than a person. Consider also that shame, unlike guilt, travels through groups — one can be ashamed of one's country, class, or family in a way that guilt resists — and tell me whether that contagion is a defect or a feature.

Third, the selection problem. Can a culture choose its moral emotions, or only its moral vocabulary? Take the instruments actually available — law, schooling, and, since it is my trade, the novel — and say what each can and cannot do. Be specific about which techniques of fiction (interior access, free indirect narration, the deliberate withholding of the victim's point of view, the long novel's habit of making us live with a character past the point where we would have forgiven them) tend to cultivate which emotion, and whether the form itself carries a bias.

Give me at least one concrete case in which guilt and shame disagree about whether anything was done wrong at all.

Then conclude. Name the better moral emotion, or name the conditions under which each is better and defend that as a verdict rather than a hedge. I would rather have a conclusion I can argue with than a survey. Where you find that the received answer survives my three difficulties, say so, and explain why my objections miss. I don't need names or a reading list; I need the argument.
