I am a doctoral student in a political philosophy seminar, and I am writing for help with a phrase that has been doing an enormous amount of work in our discussions and that I suspect is doing several different kinds of work at once. The phrase is "not everything is for sale." Our instructor used it last week to close off a proposal I had put forward, and I want to know what it could actually be claiming.

The proposal was a regulated market in transplantable kidneys. Under it the state would be the sole purchaser; the price would be a published schedule equal to the actuarially estimated lifetime risk premium for a healthy adult donor plus lost wages, revised annually; sellers would have to be adults who pass medical and psychological screening, including a screen for financial duress; brokers and private intermediaries would be prohibited; organs would be allocated by medical urgency and waiting time rather than by ability to pay, so that no one could buy priority; and every seller would receive lifetime follow-up care and insurance. I argued that this answers the exploitation objection (the price compensates rather than exploits), the coercion objection (the screening), the objection that the organs would go to the rich (allocation is not for sale), and the objection that the poor would be preyed upon (the duress screen). She said she would grant all of that for the sake of argument and oppose the proposal anyway, because not everything is for sale. When I asked what the objection was, if not harm, exploitation, coercion, or unfairness, she said that was precisely the point, and that anyone who has to ask has already missed it.

I do not want the phrase explained to me; I want it taken apart. Please set out the distinct claims it could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the very strong, and for each say what exactly it asserts, whether it is true, false, or true of some things and not others, what would be required to establish it, and whether, and how, it bears on my proposal in particular. I am especially interested in the differences among: an empirical claim about what people will in fact do; a claim about what the law will or should recognize and enforce; a claim that some things are not the kind of thing that can be transferred at all; a claim that the transfer is self-undermining, in that what is handed over is destroyed by the handing over; a claim that the sale is wrong even though nothing is destroyed and no one is harmed; a claim about what a sale expresses or signals, as opposed to what it causes; and any reading under which the sentence is not a claim at all but a way of declining to continue the argument.

One constraint on the readings: they must be selective. She is not a general opponent of markets — she defended surge pricing for ride-hailing in the same session — so any reading on which the sentence condemns markets as such cannot be what she means. The reading I most need is the one that does the work her remark was meant to do: to count against my proposal without appealing to any of the objections she has conceded are absent. If no single reading does that work, say so plainly, say which combination or weakened version comes closest, and say what it would have to add in order to succeed. If it helps to pin the readings down, test each against a second case that arose in the same session, a proposal to let voters sell their votes to one another; I care less about the verdict on that case than about which readings turn out to be doing independent work.

One last thing. The word "commodification" is thrown around in this seminar as though it named a single objection. Is it the name of the best reading on your list, a family name for several of them, or a name for something else entirely? I would rather have the distinctions than the vocabulary.

Please do not give me reading lists or citations, and I would distrust an answer that leaned on what some particular author is thought to have held; I want the claims themselves, sharp enough that I can tell which of them I accept.
