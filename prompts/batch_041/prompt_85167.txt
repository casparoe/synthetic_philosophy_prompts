Bit of background: I'm in a small reading group that's been chewing through Wittgenstein's stuff on religion for about six months now -- the lectures on religious belief first, then some of the people who've built on him -- and we've hit a wall that I don't think is going to move on its own. The two poles are Marcus and Priya. Marcus is a Quaker and takes the line that religious language is its own practice with it's own internal standards, so when an atheist asks whether 'God exists' the way you'd ask whether there's milk in the fridge, the question has already changed the subject; on his view the believer and the atheist aren't contradicting each other at all, they're doing different things with the same words. Priya grew up evangelical and left, and she thinks that's a cop-out dressed up as subtlety: if 'God exists' isn't answerable to anything, then it isn't a claim, and a church full of people using declarative sentences while meaning something else is equivocation, and slightly dishonest equivocation at that. Marcus says that's a category error. Priya says 'category error' is what you say when you've run out of arguements. There's also a third camp, basically one guy, who insists the whole thing is a verbal dispute about the word 'belief' -- that Priya means something like 'hypothesis' and Marcus means something like 'trust' or 'the shape of a life' -- and that everyone would agree if we stopped using one word for both. Marcus thinks that's a dodge; I half think it's true and half think it's a way of not answering, and the part of my brain that spent too long on LessWrong keeps whispering that Marcus is defending belief in belief rather than belief, which he does not appreciate.

Two things we tried that made it worse rather than better. Priya proposed a test case: take a believer who says 'God exists' with total sincerity, then over ten years reads too much Hume and stops; did the sentence change truth-value, or did it stop being the kind of sentence that has a truth-value at all? Marcus says the question is malformed, because the whole form of life changed and there's nothing stable left to compare -- and honestly I can't tell whether that's a deep point or a magic trick. Separately I floated the idea that religious utterances might be closer to promises than to assertions, in which case demanding evidence that someone believes is like demanding evidence that they've promised something, which is a confusion about what kind of thing a promise is; Priya said that's fine as far as it goes but it leaves out the bit where the promise is about a fact. Someone else keeps muttering about 'hinge' propositions, the ones that sit outside doubt and make doubting possible in the first place, and whether 'God exists' could be one of those for a believer -- which I find interesting but can't tell if it's load-bearing or just a redescription.

My own hypothesis, and I'd like you to tell me if it's wrong: that Marcus and Priya are answering two different questions under one heading -- Marcus is doing something descriptive (what is this person actually doing when they speak?) and Priya is doing something normative (is this warranted, and is it honest?) -- and that neither has a story about why the other's question doesn't count, which is why the same six arguments keep going round. If that's the diagnosis, what actually counts as progress here? A distinction, a decisive case, a formal result, or an argument that the dispute is ill-posed and we should stop? I don't want a survey of what other people have written -- I can't check any of it anyway, and I'm not after a reading list -- I want the structure of the disagreement, the reasons for your call, and an honest answer about whether anything could end this or whether it's one of those fights where the best available outcome is just knowing why it can't be settled. British English please, we're keeping group notes and half of us spell things the other way.
