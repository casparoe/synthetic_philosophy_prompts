Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications, then demonstrate that it satisfies them.

Setting and constraints. The case must be set in an ordinary, realistic deployment of an automated system: a hospital, a water or power utility, a transit or logistics operation, a building's safety systems, and so on. One identifiable person dies. No trolleys, no runaway trains, no science fiction, no superintelligent or deceptive system, and no reliance on the system's having intentions, beliefs, desires, or consciousness. The system is ordinary engineering: sensors, a control policy, an actuator, and a failsafe. The human operators are the same in every version of the case, know the same things, and do the same things.

The core specification. Present the case as a pair of systems that implement the same policy and produce an identical physical history, yet differ in whether the death is brought about by the system's issuing a command or by its withholding one. The cleanest construction, if you can make it work, holds fixed the sensed conditions, the thresholds, the actuator's effects, the timing of the death, and everything downstream of the actuator, and varies only the polarity of the failsafe — which state the actuator occupies when no command is present — with the control logic written accordingly. If you prefer another construction, it must meet the same bar: from the actuator onward the two systems' physical histories must be indistinguishable, and the sole difference must be whether the lethal event coincides with the presence or with the absence of a command.

Verdicts. Bring at least three accounts of the doing/allowing distinction into play, and at least two of them must disagree about this case. State each account in your own words before applying it. First, an account on which doing consists in the production of a positive act or instruction and allowing in its absence. Second, an account on which doing consists in the agent's being a difference-maker for an outcome that would not otherwise have occurred, so that intervening in a process already under way counts as doing even when the intervention takes the form of ceasing to act. Third, an account on which doing consists in moving the world below a baseline fixed independently of the agent, so that the verdict turns on how the baseline is drawn rather than on the agent's behavior. For each account, say what the system did in each member of the pair, and why. Where an account is ambiguous, or where it presupposes an agent with a bodily baseline and so cannot be applied to an artifact at all, say so rather than forcing a verdict; where its verdict depends on a choice — for instance, whether the baseline is the patient's condition on arrival or the course the world would have taken with no controller at all — give the verdict under each choice and say what the divergence shows.

What is doing the work. Identify the feature of the case on which the divergence turns. Then remove it, for instance by giving both systems the same failsafe polarity, and say what happens: does the case collapse into an ordinary doing/allowing case, and if so, which one? Then say whether any of your accounts is invariant across the pair, and what that invariance reveals about where the account locates the distinction — in the agent's output, in the world's causal structure, or in a baseline that must be stipulated. If an account is invariant, say whether an ordinary engineering team could implement it, and whether doing so would require the system to represent counterfactual baselines it cannot observe.

The strongest attack. Finally, state the best case for whichever account fares worst. In particular, if an account's verdict flips across the pair while the physical history does not, its defender owes an explanation of why the failsafe polarity is not an arbitrary engineering convention. Consider at least these replies and say which you find strongest: that the default state is the agent's rest state and rest is not action; that the account is correct and our sense that the two systems are morally equivalent is an artifact of attending to outcomes; that the account was never meant for artifacts, and the case shows a doing/allowing constraint cannot be implemented in one without its verdicts becoming hostage to a design decision. Say what would have to be true for the defender's reply to succeed.

Purpose, which fixes what counts as cheating. The case is meant to test whether a doing/allowing constraint can be built into an autonomous system without its verdicts becoming a function of an engineering decision about the failsafe. Verdicts must be generated by the accounts you state, not stipulated by the way you describe the two systems. If the pair classifies as doing and allowing only because you have labelled it so, the case fails.

Format. The scenario itself in no more than a page; the analysis after it, clearly separated. No citations, no appeals to authority, no attributions to named authors: the argument must stand on the case.
