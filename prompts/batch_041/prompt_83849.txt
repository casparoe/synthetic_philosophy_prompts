I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on media, democracy and the public sphere this term, and last week a fellow student tried to close down the whole discussion with four words: "the crowd is untruth." The occasion was an argument about citizens' assemblies and deliberative polling, but she extended the point immediately to opinion polling, to journalism, and to the very idea of a reading public, and the implication was that any judgement arrived at collectively is disqualified simply by being collective. I have a strong suspicion that she is smuggling a much narrower claim into a universal one, and that the slogan is being made to do work which at most one of its possible readings could licence. What I want from you is not a defence or a refutation of Kierkegaard, nor a survey of the secondary literature, but a disambiguation of the slogan itself: a menu of the distinct precise claims it could be making, arranged from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each.

Please make the readings genuinely distinct rather than restatements of one another, and press on the places where the sentence is ambiguous. What does "crowd" range over — any plurality whatsoever, any plurality that acquires decisive or voting or audible significance, any anonymous aggregate, any set of people united only by a common object of attention? What does "untruth" amount to in each case: a claim about knowledge (that groups cannot track truth as well as individuals), a claim about moral accountability (that numbers dilute responsibility until no one is answerable), a claim about ontology (that a collective is a fiction, an abstraction which has no hands), or a claim about the individual's relation to God or to the eternal? And whose untruth is at issue — the crowd's, or the cowardice of the individual who takes refuge in it? I would also like the readings sorted by how they stand to the original context, since the line comes from a note he wrote about his own authorship rather than from a treatise on group cognition, and since his diagnosis of the press and of "the public" in the 1846 review of a novel cuts in a particular direction: a public, on his account, is not a body of people who are genuinely present to one another but an abstraction, and the levelling he attributes to the present age is a matter of passionless reflection rather than of aggregation or head-counting. Some readings, I suspect, sit comfortably in that context; others are imports from contemporary social epistemology and have no business being dressed in his clothes.

So, for each reading: say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false; say whether it is supported, unsupported, or positively contradicted by the fact that he himself published books addressed to a wide readership in the hope of reaching that single individual, and that he was happy to have many readers provided each read as one; say which readings are really claims about legitimacy and responsibility rather than about truth-tracking; and say which readings would actually get my fellow student her two conclusions, namely that collective deliberation is disqualified as such and that the press is disqualified as such. Then tell me, in a closing paragraph, which reading she most probably meant, and whether her conclusions follow from it. Please write in British English, with British spelling and punctuation throughout, and keep the whole thing usable as a handout — headings or a numbered menu rather than an essay.
