Please write your comments in British English. Dont pull your punches, but don't invent problems either.

The question was:

Q3. "The gardener's not watering the plant caused it to die."

(a) Why is a sentence like this supposed to be in tension with the view that causation is a relation between events?

(b) The counterfactual analysis says that c is a cause of e just in case, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. Does that analysis remove the tension?

(c) The plant was also not watered by anyone else in the village, and the sun did not go nova. On the counterfactual analysis, do these count as causes of the plant's death as well? What does your answer suggest about the analysis?

The student wrote:

(a) There are two claims here that both look plausible. One is that causation is a relation between events: when we say that c caused e we are relating two happenings in the world. The other is that an omission is not an event. Nothing happens when the gardener fails to water the plant — there is just the absence of a watering. But a relation needs two relata, and the omission isn't among the things there are, so it can't be one of them. So either 'the not-watering caused the death' has to be rewritten so that the cause turns out to be an event after all, or we have to admit that it isn't literally true.

(b) The counterfactual analysis says that c causes e just in case, had c not happened, e wouldn't have happened. If the gardener had watered the plant, it wouldn't have died, so the not-watering comes out as a cause. This shows that the event-relation view is false: the analysis can relate the death to an absence, and absences are not events, so causation can't be a relation between events.

(c) The analysis is far too permissive. The gardener's not watering made a difference, but so did the fact that nobody else in the village watered the plant, and so did the sun's not going nova — had it gone nova, the plant wouldn't have died either. So they all come out as causes, which is absurd, because we want to say that the gardener's omission caused the death and that the others are just background conditions. The usual way out is to say that only some of these are salient: the gardener's omission is the one we pick out because it's the abnormal one, or the one we happen to be interested in. So the analysis doesn't tell us by itself which omission is the cause, but once we restrict it to the salient omissions it gives the right answer.

So the tension in (a) is real, and the counterfactual analysis is the right way to deal with it.
