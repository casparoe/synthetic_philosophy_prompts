I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and the term ends with the Dutch book theorem. My students can reproduce the proof on demand, but they treat it as a parlor trick — a recipe for fleecing a fool — rather than as an argument about what it is to hold a credence at all. I want to close the final meeting by reading a poem aloud, and I would like you to write it. All term I have been pressing on them the thought that the theorem lives two lives: the blunt, pragmatic one, on which the incoherent agent is simply someone a bookie can pick clean, and the depragmatized one, on which the betting talk is supposed to fall away and leave a claim about credence itself — all of it shadowed by the standing objection, put to me by a student who refuses to gamble on anything, that being the sort of person who would lose money is not the same as being the sort of person who believes something incoherent. We meet last period on Fridays, when nobody has anything left, so the poem needs to do its work quickly.

So: a poem of six quatrains and a closing couplet, in loose iambic pentameter, rhymed as you like, with a refrain that returns word for word at the end of every quatrain. The refrain should be the bettor's own statement of the odds — her credence spoken aloud in the first person — and it must not vary, because the whole point is that the credence stays exactly where it is while the book closes around it. Let each quatrain be one leg of the book: the bookie quoting a price, naming the stake, stating what the ticket pays and on what condition, and the bettor taking it because, one ticket at a time, the price looks fair to her. The couplet does the tally, and it must be a real tally, with numbers that add up rather than a gesture at probability in the abstract. I want the arithmetic visible on the page: the credences in question should fail to sum to one, and a reader should be able to see, without any commentary from the poem, that the total paid out exceeds the total that can possibly come back, whichever way the world falls.

Two further things matter more than the form. First, the poem has to leave room for the objection: somewhere the bettor, or a voice she is allowed to speak in, should say that she never asked to gamble, that she only ever said how likely she took something to be, and that a wallet is not a mind — and the bookie must not be handed a cheap answer to this. Second, a reader who has never heard of a Dutch book should be able to reconstruct the entire argument from the poem alone, while a reader who has heard of it should recognize the particular book you have built, so keep the mechanics exact and let the structure carry the reasoning. Please do not name any philosophers or cite any papers; I would rather the poem stand on its own feet.
