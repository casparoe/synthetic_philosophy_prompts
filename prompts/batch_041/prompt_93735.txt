I'm about 60 pages into a novel about a small zoning fight — a four-story apartment building proposed on a corner lot in a neighborhood of old Victorians, and my main character is a junior planner at the city who has to run the public process while her roommate is an organizer trying to get the thing actually built. I've been sitting in on hearings for research and I came out the other side with a question I can't shake, and it's not about the shouting, it's about the machinery. In a lot of American cities the real power doesn't live in the zoning code, it lives in the discretionary layer stacked on top of it: a design review board that can bounce a project over the color of the window trim; a variance process; a neighborhood association that gets a formal seat at the table and a mailing list, while the people who'd actually live in the building have no standing at all — they don't exist yet as a party, they're just the future tenants. And then there's the environmental review law, written for refineries and freeways, that a single neighbor can invoke to trigger a year or two of studies about traffic and parking and noise, on a building that would house more people on less land than the single-family house it's replacing. In plenty of cities the local council member has a de facto veto over anything built in their district — Chicago's the famous case, but it's not the only one — so a project lives or dies on one person's phone call. Meanwhile the same council that runs all of this has declared a housing emergency.

What I want to know is what theory of rights and standing that whole arrangement presupposes. My read is that it assumes the people who live next to a parcel hold something like a property right — not in the dirt, but in the neighborhood's future: its light, its parking, its 'character' — and that this right outweighs the interests of the people who'd live there. Is that a coherent thing to own? Is it a bundle of rights, or just an entitlement to veto change? And please separate what the practice actually needs from what it merely happens to fit. Could you justify design review and neighbor notice on procedural or deliberative-democracy grounds without ever committing to a neighbor's property-like claim? Or does the machinery, as built, only make sense if you already accept that present occupants have a stronger claim on a place than future ones — and maybe also that a neighborhood has an identity that has to be held onto, that it's the same neighborhood only so long as it looks more or less the same? Which I realize is it's own weird question, since nobody can say how much of a street you can replace before it stops being that street.

Then the payoff, which is really what I need for the book: if you took seriously the slogan about everyone having a right to the city — including the people who don't live there yet — what in that process would have to go? Would you have to give future residents standing, some kind of representation for the unborn tenants, or is that a fiction that would just get captured by whoever's loudest? My planner is trying to decide what she actually believes and I don't want to put a speech in her mouth that's just vibes, so I'd like the answer to be blunt about which parts of the status quo are load-bearing for some defensible theory of the city and which are just the residue of whoever showed up to the meetings in 1974.
