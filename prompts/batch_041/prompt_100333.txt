I need a prospectus section drafted for a two-quarter dissertation project in formal epistemology, and I would like it delivered as exactly five numbered paragraphs, with no introduction and no concluding summary. I will expand each one into a section later, so keep them to one or two sentences apiece.

The subject is the problem of logical omniscience, but I want the proposal built around a distinction I have not seen drawn cleanly, rather than around the usual catalogue of repairs to the knowledge operator. The distinction is between two ways an agent can fail to know what follows from what she already knows. In the diachronic case, the consequences are already implicit in what she knows and she has simply not performed the inference; nothing about her present state stands in the way except the expenditure of effort. In the synchronic case, no amount of effort would help, because the consequences cannot be represented within her present repertoire or derived by any procedure available to her. My working hunch, which the proposal should be designed to test rather than to assume, is that the synchronic case is either impossible or epistemically idle: that any defensible model of a finite reasoner treats omniscience as an idealization about resources rather than about content, and that formalisms which introduce non-standard contents in order to generate genuine synchronic closure failure end up describing states that are not recognisably knowledge at all. The lever I want to try is a recognition condition — a state is closed under a consequence only if the agent could be brought to see that consequence as following, so that closure is fixed by what she can come to recognise rather than by what holds at every world compatible with her information.

The five paragraphs should cover, in order: the question, stated so that it has a determinate answer and so that a reader could tell whether I had answered it; why the positions already on the table leave that question open, expressed as a gap in the arguments rather than as a survey of who holds what; the method — what cases, criteria, or constructions the argument turns on, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternative of comparing the existing formalisms feature by feature; what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing, including the finding that would make me give up the hunch; and a realistic division of the work across the two quarters, with an honest note on where it is most likely to overrun.

Please assume that I will supply the citations and the reading list myself; include neither.
