Athenian democracy's fifth-century record supplies three canonical failures, and I want them read through a single lens. The episodes are well known, so I fix the salient facts briefly. In 427 BCE the assembly, enraged by Mytilene's revolt, voted to put the entire adult male population to death and to enslave the women and children; the next day, after Cleon and Diodotus had argued the question again, it reversed the decision and sent a second ship to overtake the first, sparing all but the ringleaders. In 415 BCE it authorized the Sicilian expedition and then, over the following two years, reinforced a venture it repeatedly had the opportunity to abandon, until the force was destroyed at Syracuse. In 399 BCE a jury convicted Socrates of impiety and of corrupting the young and sentenced him to death, a verdict no later assembly could undo; the city's regret arrived too late to matter.

I want to read these through a construct I will call the reversibility illusion. Let a decision's reversal cost be the cost, at any moment after the choice, of undoing it. We naively treat that cost as fixed at the moment of decision; in fact it is non-decreasing, and it can climb steeply once downstream commitments accumulate — forces deployed, allies entangled, public positions staked, precedents set. A body that fails to forecast the growth of reversal cost will over-commit early, while reversal still looks cheap, and then persist when it should reverse, because reversal has by then become dear. The gap between perceived and actual reversal cost is the illusion.

Analyze the three episodes through this lens. For each, identify the deciding body, the perceived and actual reversal costs at each stage, and whether the illusion — rather than passion, ignorance, or the quality of the arguments on offer — is doing genuine explanatory work. Then run the analysis the other way, and be willing to find the lens wanting. At Mytilene the reversal in fact succeeded; if the illusion was small there, what does that do to the account, and what then explains the first vote? Which features of the cases resist the mapping — the role of the demagogues, the epistemic standing of the assembly, the moral content of the choices — and would a rival lens (collective weakness of will, escalation of commitment, groupthink, or the realist reading of the Mytilene and Melian debates) do better? Where is the construct silent rather than merely imprecise? Finally, say what it predicts that these episodes do not yet confirm, and what evidence from other cases — the amnesty of 403, for instance, where the Athenians deliberately made reconciliation irreversible by binding themselves by oath not to revisit past wrongs, or a modern case of your choosing — would count for or against it. End with an honest verdict on whether the construct earns its keep or merely redescribes the familiar, and if the latter, what minimal repair would turn it into a real theory.
