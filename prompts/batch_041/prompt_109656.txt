I'm a postdoc in computational psychiatry, and I've been handed next month's departmental journal club: about twelve people, mostly clinical psychology PhD students plus two psychiatrists, all of whom have read three or four papers invoking the free energy principle and none of whom, if I'm honest, could tell me what accepting it commits them to. I don't want you to explain the free energy principle to me — I want a plan for teaching it. What has to come first, and what can only be stated once the first thing is in place, and why. My own working diagnosis is that the trouble is that at least three quite different claims travel under one name: a normative claim about what any system that persists in a world must be doing, a computational proposal about how cortex might implement inference, and a modelling idiom that people then carry into interoception and psychopathology. If I put those in the wrong order, I think the room leaves believing the whole thing is predictive coding with extra algebra. Tell me whether that diagnosis is doing real work or whether I've manufactured a distinction the field doesn't actually respect, and if it is real, which of the three earns the first ten minutes.

Second, the carrying example. I want one case to hold the entire hour, and I'd rather you pressure-test my candidate than quietly substitute your own. I've been planning to use someone carrying a full mug of coffee up a staircase while talking on the phone: there's a generative model doing visible work, prediction error that is informative rather than merely irritating, action that changes the input, and a natural place for precision to matter. Say what such an example has to have and has to lack to bear that weight — I suspect it must not let anyone picture a small person inside the head reading off probabilities, and I suspect that any purely exteroceptive example, vision or reaching, smuggles in the idea that this is just control theory, so tell me whether the mug is enough of a body. I also want to know whether the dark-room objection (why wouldn't a system that minimizes prediction error just curl up in a dark quiet room and predict nothing?) belongs in the sequence at all, or whether raising it early simply hands the sceptics their objection before I've built anything for them to be sceptical about.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I most want. I can already predict some of what they'll form — that the principle is unfalsifiable and therefore empty; that minimizing prediction error means minimizing surprise itself, rather than something that bounds it from above; that active inference means we act only to confirm what we already expect; that the dark room is a reductio rather than a question about priors — and I want to know where in the sequence each of those gets headed off, not merely that it should be mentioned somewhere. One of the psychiatrists will ask, politely and about forty minutes in, why any of this is better than the formulation she already uses in clinic, and I'd like the plan to contain a place where that question gets an answer instead of a deferral. Finish with the single question I should put to the room at the end that would tell me whether the dependency order actually landed, and with what I should say out loud that I am not going to cover at this level; my instinct is the variational apparatus and the Markov blanket formalism, but tell me if leaving those out is precisely what will cost me the hour. Skip the reading list — they have read plenty. And I'd like the answer to be candid about its own footing: say which part of the plan you would bet against landing with this particular room, what I would see in the room that would tell me the ordering is wrong, and what evidence or argument would make you reorder it.
