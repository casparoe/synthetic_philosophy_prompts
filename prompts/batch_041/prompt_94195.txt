Suppose a small foundation has asked me to help it decide where to spend a modest budget — enough to support five or six researchers for three years, all of it for conceptual and theoretical work rather than experiments — on the study of one particular idealization of deliberation. The idealization, in bare form, runs as follows. An agent answers a question by decomposing it into subquestions, putting each subquestion to a fresh copy of itself, and combining the returned answers by a fixed and simple rule; the process iterates until the subquestions are trivial enough to be settled directly. A task is then said to be decomposable when it can be carved into subtasks such that a weak combiner recovers the answer to the whole from the parts. The picture is offered as an ideal — a reconstruction of what a sufficiently reflective agent would do — and is meant to illuminate both the reach and the limits of deliberation, and the safety properties one might expect from an agent that deliberates in this way.

My question is a prioritization question, not a request for a survey. Among the open conceptual questions this idealization raises, which should the foundation's researchers take up first, and why? I want the ranking defended, not merely asserted.

To make the task concrete, here are a few of the puzzles I have in mind, though I expect you to add to them and to tell me which of mine are less urgent than they look. First, the decomposition appears to be handed to the agent rather than produced by it; if finding the right decomposition is itself as hard as the original question, does the idealization explain deliberation or merely relocate the difficulty? Second, if the copies are genuinely independent, how does the aggregate avoid inconsistency among them, and does any requirement of consistency smuggle in a global coherence that the picture was supposed to generate rather than presuppose? Third, is this an idealization in the physicist's sense — false but tractable, useful for prediction — or a normative ideal of how one ought to reason, and does the answer change what the idealization is good for? Fourth, are there questions whose value lies precisely in resisting decomposition, so that the framework excludes something important about deliberation by construction? Fifth, the base case: settling a trivial subquestion directly still requires judgment, so where does the base level get its authority?

Please give me an ordering with reasons. For each item, say why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers and theorists rather than engineers, how neglected it currently is, and what argument or development over the next two or three years would move it up or down the list. Where you think a question is overrated — attractive to work on but unlikely to change anything downstream — say so and explain why. I am less interested in the final list than in the criteria you use to produce it and the trade-offs among them.
