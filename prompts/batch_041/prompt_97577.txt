I was at a dinner recently where a friend tried to settle Pascal's wager with what he took to be a knockout observation: the wager, he said, is nothing but the St. Petersburg game in disguise. Both, on his telling, dangle an infinite expected value; both manufacture it by multiplying an ever more extravagant payoff by an ever smaller probability; and since we all agree that the St. Petersburg game's infinite expectation is a mathematical artifact rather than a reason to hand over any finite stake to play, the wager's infinite expectation deserves the same treatment. He went further and claimed that the standard fixes for St. Petersburg — declining to maximize expected value when the expectation diverges, attending to the gamble's risk rather than its mean, and pointing out that no real casino could ever pay the prize — are in truth arguments against wagering on God. I suspect he has the analogy running backwards, but I cannot put my finger on precisely where, and I would like the comparison audited rather than a verdict on the wager itself.

Please begin with the correspondence table: tell me what on the wager's side plays the role of the entry fee, the doubling payoff, the halving probability, the casino's finite bankroll, and the decision rule, and mark any slot that has a counterpart on one side but not the other. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and be explicit about which of the disanalogies actually matter for the work my friend is asking the analogy to do, as opposed to which are merely decorative. I am especially interested in whether the familiar resolutions of St. Petersburg transfer one-for-one: in particular, whether a utility function that is concave but unbounded disposes of both problems or only one of them, and what that reveals about exactly which premise my friend must lean on in order to get from the claim that St. Petersburg's expectation is spurious to the claim that the wager's is. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion he wants — that the wager's infinite expectation is no more rationally compelling than the St. Petersburg game's — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, describe the analogy that would do the work he is after better than this one does.
