I keep a personal document on population axiology — I revise it every few months after another long argument with friends who take the opposite line — and before I circulate it I want a consistency audit. I suspect I'm holding commitments whose usual justifications pull against each other, and I can't tell which of them is actually load-bearing.

Here is where I've landed.

1. I accept the asymmetry in full: the presence of suffering is bad and the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of suffering is good even when there is no one to enjoy that good; but the absence of pleasure is bad only when there is someone who is thereby deprived.

2. From (1) I take the antinatalist conclusion — bringing a person into existence is never a benefit to that person, can be a harm, and is therefore at best not required and in practice usually wrong, even when the life would be worth living.

3. The death asymmetry: death is bad for the one who dies because it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have had. That is why extending an existing life can be a real benefit even though starting one cannot.

4. To state (1) precisely I use a rule I call the Ledger. For each candidate person, take their lifetime welfare if created, W. If created, they contribute W to the total. If not created, they contribute 0 when W is at least 0, and |W| when W is below 0 — credit for averting a bad life, nothing for forgoing a good one. The value of an outcome is the sum over all candidate people. I take the Ledger to be the asymmetry written in arithmetic.

5. I want betterness to be transitive, and to depend only on what the outcome is, not on which other outcomes I happen to be comparing it against.

6. For people who actually exist I am an unqualified totalist: more total welfare is better, and we should maximize it rather than average it.

Go through the list and tell me which items are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and which single revision would clean up the most. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say precisely what has to give and what it costs me. I don't know in advance which commitment is the odd one out, so don't presume; where a conflict is real but survivable, say so, and where something that looks contradictory is actually fine, explain why instead of filing it as a problem.
