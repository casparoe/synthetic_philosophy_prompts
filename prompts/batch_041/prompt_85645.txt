I am a retired experimental physicist, forty years in condensed matter, and since retiring I have been working my way into philosophy on my own — epistemology and logic mostly, with no formal training in either. Next month I am to lead a ninety-minute session of the monthly discussion group at my old institute: eight or nine retired scientists, all of them fluent in probability and statistics, none of whom has read any philosophy. My brief is the pair of puzzles usually called the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. I do not want the paradoxes explained to me; I can read. What I want is a plan for teaching them — the sequence, the example, the traps — and I would like you to treat my own proposed sequence as a hypothesis to be corrected rather than a brief to be executed.

Specifically:

First, the order. Tell me what has to be in place before either puzzle can even be felt as a puzzle — I assume something about the difference between holding a credence and accepting a proposition outright, and something about what follows from accepting several things at once — and tell me whether that ordering is right, and which of the two puzzles should come first.

Second, the carrying example. I want one case to bear the whole session, and I am suspicious of the two standard ones: the ticket-by-ticket lottery, because I fear it teaches the audience that this is a puzzle about arithmetic, and the author's preface, because I fear it teaches them that it is a puzzle about modesty. Tell me which case to use, and whether a single case can honestly do double duty for both puzzles; if it cannot, tell me what the two cases must differ in, and let that difference, not my worry, decide the matter.

Third, a hypothesis of my own that I would like you to confirm or refute. It seems to me that the two puzzles come apart over whether the agent has positive evidence against the conjunction of the things she accepts: in the lottery she knows outright that not every ticket loses, whereas the author of a preface has no direct evidence at all about the conjunction of her book's claims, only a general expectation of error. If that is right, say what follows for the teaching order and for which puzzle is the harder of the two; if it is wrong, say so plainly rather than accommodating it, and tell me what the real distinction is.

Fourth, the misconceptions, in the order this particular audience will actually produce them, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect at least these: that this is merely a threshold problem and some adjustment to the threshold dissolves it; that once belief is replaced by credence both puzzles evaporate; that the author is simply being humble; and that the puzzles show classical logic must be abandoned. Add the ones I have not anticipated.

Fifth, the check at the end — one question I can put to the room that would show me whether they have understood the difficulty rather than merely been shown it.

Sixth, what to leave out at this level, and what to say to the room about leaving it out. No readings, please; I have no wish to hand round a list.

Finally, tell me where this plan is most likely to fail, and what would change your mind about it — which consideration, if it turned out one way rather than another, would lead you to a different sequence or a different carrying example. If the session should end with a verdict rather than with the puzzles left open, describe the family of responses you would leave them with in your own terms, without relying on labels I would then have to look up.
