OK this is going to sound like a strange request, but bear with me.

I retired four years ago after thirty-odd years in sensory physiology — single-unit recordings in the moth antennal lobe, mostly — and I must have written "this cell represents the plume" or "this neuron codes for concentration" a few thousand times without ever asking myself what I meant by it. Now I've been reading philosophy of mind, and more recently the interpretability work people are doing on neural networks, and it's dawned on me that the sentence I spent a career writing is carrying a lot of unexamined freight. I'd like a map of the options, mostly so I can find out which one I've been holding all along.

So: build me a decision tree — flowchart, whatever draws easier — that takes a person from the plain observation ("this trained network does the task well, and here's a unit or layer that seems to track something in the input") down to a named position on whether it really represents that thing. The root should be the first commitment you have to make, and every internal node should be a single question that splits the remaining views, phrased as something I can actually answer without already knowing which label it leads to. Please don't make any node read like "are you a realist?" — I want to be sorted by what I say about cases, not by which word I find appealing.

Things that matter for my purposes:

- No citations, no paper titles, no author names. I don't want you guessing at attributions and I can't check them anyway. Just the positions and the questions that seperate them.

- The distinctions I care about most, roughly in the order I think they matter: (i) is the sentence even truth-apt, or is it a useful fiction / shorthand for a correlation? (ii) if it's truth-apt, is there one correct answer about what a given unit represents, or do several incompatible but equally predictive descriptions all count? (iii) what is doing the representing — the state carrying information about the thing, something downstream using it, training having selected it, or a structural/geometric relation to the thing? (iv) can the network's state be wrong, genuinely misrepresent rather than merely be noisy — and if not, does that sink the realist branch? (v) does representing require an interpreter, and if so is the interpreter constitutive or just an epistemic limitation on us?

- Use specific cases, not just abstractions, and put at least these somewhere in the tree or at the leaves: a network trained to fit completely random labels, which nails the training set and arguably represents nothing; a network that gets the task right through a spurious shortcut; a linear probe that decodes a clean direction from a frozen layer but whose direction does nothing when you ablate it; and a network with heavy feature superposition, so the units are polysemantic and any decomposition starts to look like a choice the interpreter made.

- Seven to nine leaves. Each one gets a name, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and — where it helps — the case above that the position handles worst. Flag any two leaves your tree can't seperate, and say whether that's because nothing could tell them apart or because I'd need a further question you haven't included.

- Where a combination of answers is empty, i.e. nobody sits in that box, say so and diagnose it: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Build the tree from the questions first and then tell me which named positions land in which corner, rather than starting from a list of positions and hanging questions off it.

- One last thing. I'd like the realism question to cash out for interpretability. If you end up in the realist part of the tree, what does that oblige you to want from an interpretability method — the network's actual algorithm, or merely a surrogate that predicts its behaviour? And which leaves think that question is confused to begin with?

Keep it readable by somebody who last sat in a philosophy classroom in 1978. No hurry.
