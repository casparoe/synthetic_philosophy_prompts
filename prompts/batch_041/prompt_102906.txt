I've been doing a run of interviews for a long piece on how scientists actually decide between rival explanations, and somewhere around the fourth transcribe-back it turned into me arguing with my own notes at 2am. So this is a plea for a philosopher rather than another scientist. What I want is a principle, and I'll trade you my gut verdicts for it, messy as they are.

Here's what I've got. (1) Two coupled ocean-atmosphere models that both reproduce the twentieth-century temperature record down to the noise but diverge wildly about what happens after 2100 — I don't think the historical record picks between them, yet I also don't think they're equally credible, because one of them has to violate what we know about cloud microphysics from the lab, and I'd want to count that lab work as evidence too, even though it isn't in the temperature record. (2) An anomaly in the orbit of a planet, explicable either by an unseen body or by a tweak to the inverse-square law — my instinct is that the observations available at the time didn't decide it, and that this is a fact about the evidence then, not about the world. (3) Two formulations of one physical theory, related by a transformation nobody could detect from the inside — here I think no evidence could ever decide, and it feels like a different kind of tie from (2). (4) A rival to a well-established theory that fits everything the incumbent does, but only by positing an extra field that nothing independently points to — I want to say the evidence doesn't decide, and yet I don't want to say the two are on a par, which seems to flatly contradict (2). (5) And the one I keep flip-flopping on: a theory built to fit a specific anomaly after the fact versus one that called it in advance, where both now fit everything equally well. I think the predictor is better supported, but I can't for the life of me say what evidence supports it, since by stipulation the evidence is the same.

Is there a principle that gets all five, or close? What I'd like back is a precise statement of it, the case that forces each clause, and what it predicts for one I haven't listed — say, two theories that are empirically equivalent given everything we could ever measure, but where one is quite alot simpler. And please be honest about which of my five verdicts the thing can't keep, because I suspect at least one of them has to go. Write it in British English, I'm filing for a UK magazine.
