I've been reading up on deontology (I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, so sorry if I mangle the vocabulary) and I keep hitting the same wall in the arguments about whether deontological constraints can be justified. The shape, as best I can tell: the constraint says you may not kill one to save five even though five deaths is worse than one, and the standard defence is that the constraint is agent-relative — it tells you what you may do, not what the world should look like — while the standard objection is that any reason you can give for having the constraint at all seems to be an agent-neutral reason about outcomes, so the constraint looks like it's sawing off the branch it sits on. Every defence I read seems to answer the objection by restating the agent-relativity; every objection I read seems to answer the defence by restating the demand for a rationale. They slide right past each other.

My hypothesis (probably wrong, tell me if it is): the two sides aren't disagreeing about any case. One is asking what justifies this rule from outside the agent's own perspective, the other is answering a different question — what am I to do — and the whole thing is either a verbal dispute dressed up as a substantive one, or a fight about whether a rule even needs an external justification, which is really a fight about what a moral theory is for rather than about morality.

If both sides say the same thing about that case, doesn't that support the verbal-dispute reading?
