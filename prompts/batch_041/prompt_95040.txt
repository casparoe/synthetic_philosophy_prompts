Ok so this is a weird request but I have a real problem and I think it's actually philosophical. I teach history at a community college, and my department is trying to write a one-page rule for when a student paper counts as legitimate historical revision and when it's denialism, and I keep getting the line wrong. It got personal last term when a student I like handed in a paper on Nanjing and I honestly didn't know whether to grade it or report it.

Here's the thing: I have pretty firm gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and they don't fit together. On rewriting numbers: someone redoes the parish records and argues the Black Death killed a smaller share of Europe than everyone says, fine, that's just history. Someone argues the inflated Dresden death toll that sat in old textbooks came from Nazi propaganda and the real figure is much lower, fine, more than fine. Someone argues the pre-Columbian population of the Americas was ten million rather than fifty, fine, even though that number basically decides how big you think the catastrophe was. Someone argues the 1943 Bengal famine killed a lot fewer than three million, fine, I'd read it.

Now flip it. Someone argues the Armenian death toll in 1915 was closer to 600,000 than 1.5 million, working from Ottoman census records, and I think that's denialism, flat. Same for arguing Nanjing is in the tens of thousands rather than the hundreds of thousands, or Srebrenica was a few hundred rather than 8,000, or that the Rwanda figure is padded. And I can't tell you what's different. The inflated Nanjing figure came from the other side's wartime propaganda too, so punching up at state propaganda doesn't sort it.

The pair that really breaks me: a serious historian argues the Irish famine wasn't a genocide because the deaths came out of policy and ideology without the specific intent, and I think that's a respectable argument. Someone argues the Armenian deportations killed people mostly through famine and disease, without an intent to destroy, and I think that's denialism in a lab coat. Same shape, opposite verdicts.

And then the stuff that makes me suspect my verdicts are just tracking whoever won. Katyn: for decades the official line was the Germans did it and saying otherwise was sedition, and the people saying otherwise were right. The Ukrainian famine being deliberately engineered went from fringe to textbook. In Japan, arguing the numbers are lower than the Chinese say is the calling card of a movement I find disgusting, while in Germany arguing there's no surviving document with Hitler's signature on the order, so the initiative probably came from below, is a real debate among real historians. Maybe that last one is the same move as the others and I just like the people making it. A relative keeps forwarding me this stuff and I can't explain what's wrong with it, which is humiliating.

My best guess at a principle, and I want you to break it: a claim is denialism when accepting it would let the perpetrators off the hook morally, and legitimate when it only changes the scale. But the Armenian numbers claim is only about scale and it doesn't feel like only scale, so that's probably wrong or needs a fix.

So: give me a principle, or two, stated as precisely as you can, tell me which of my verdicts each one saves and which it forces me to drop, and say what it predicts for a few cases I haven't listed. If you think it's really about the arguer's motives rather than the claim, say that, but then deal with the fact that I think a sincere lone person can be a denier. Continuous prose only please, no headings, bullets, bold or tables, just write it like an email. And don't give me a reading list, I want the principle itself.
