I turned forty this spring. I stopped drinking in my twenties, after a period I would rather not go into, and for years the decision seemed to explain itself; lately it has begun to look less like a conclusion than a habit, and I have been rereading the first two books of Plato's Laws to see whether the Athenian Stranger can help. What I keep returning to is the puppet: each of us a living creature strung on many cords, pulled this way and that by pleasure and pain and fear, with one golden cord of calculation that becomes law when the city makes it public (644d–645a); and then, a little further on, the proposal that a drinking party, rightly conducted, is a gymnasium for the soul — no wine before eighteen, moderation until thirty, and after forty the full invocation of Dionysus, whose gift the Athenian calls a medicine against the crabbedness of old age (666a–b). He also says that the drunkard, like the greybeard, may be in his second childhood (646a), and that a man who cannot hold his wine has shown you what he is. I am hosting a dinner next month at which I intend to drink, and I want to open it with a poem rather than a toast.

Write me a choral ode: a strophe and an antistrophe of eight to ten lines each in unrhymed iambic pentameter, followed by an epode of four to six shorter lines. Let the strophe voice the pull of the wine — the cords of pleasure, pain, and fear; the loosening of shame; the second childhood; the courage that is only boldness. Let the antistrophe answer it line for line, in the same order, as the golden cord answers the others: not a rebuttal but a counterpull, so that a reader feels the two forces in tension rather than one winning. Let the epode do what the Athenian actually does and hold them together in a single figure — the sober older man who presides over the drinking and lets the chorus sing — and leave me with the discomfort that the same wine which reveals what a man is also makes him worse, and that the Athenian's response is not to forbid the cup but to put someone in charge of it.

Accuracy matters more than atmosphere. The speaker of the Laws is the Athenian Stranger, not Socrates. The puppet is the whole living creature, not the body alone. The golden cord is calculation, and it becomes law only when the city ratifies it. The drinking party the Athenian defends is an institution for testing and training self-command, not a reward for the old, and the sober president is essential to it. If you use a Greek word, use it as he would. Someone who has read Laws I–II should recognize the passage; someone who has not should still find a poem. End on the question rather than the answer — I want something to argue about over the first glass, not a resolution that will make the evening polite. It will be read aloud, so it needs to work in the ear.
