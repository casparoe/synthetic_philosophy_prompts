I am a doctoral candidate in religious ethics, trying to pin down the rule my supervisor is actually running. She writes on Buddhist ethics, has spent her career resisting the label 'consequentialist', and insists that the precepts are not exceptionless — she calls them training rules, and says, in a phrase she uses constantly and never quite defines, that a training rule may be outrun but never outweighed. Over the years she has ruled on a scattered set of cases, and I have kept a list:

(i) A monk eats after noon so that he can sit with a grieving laywoman through the afternoon and keep her from despair: blameless.
(ii) A physician tells a patient that her illness is manageable when it is in fact terminal, chiefly because she cannot face the conversation: wrong.
(iii) A bystander shoots a gunman who is about to open fire on a crowd, in a moment of panic: wrong.
(iv) A householder breaks a vow to fast on a holy day in order to share a meal that reconciles two estranged brothers: blameless.
(v) A laywoman diverts money she had pledged to a temple fund in order to buy medicine for a neighbour's child: blameless.

I can fit at least two quite different rules to all five. Next month she must comment on a new case: a passer-by, who has calmly and deliberately weighed what to do, shoots a gunman about to open fire on a crowd, moved throughout by compassion for those who would otherwise die and, he says, for the gunman himself. Tell me which rules fit her record equally well and what each predicts about this case; give me your best guess at her verdict and the reasons for preferring that reading; and name the further case that would most cleanly show whether it is the character of the act or the state of the agent that she is really tracking.
