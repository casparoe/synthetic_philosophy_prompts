Last week a friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about embryo selection — she works in the counselling office of a fertility clinic, I've spent the past year reading the bioethics literature on it — and the fight was about whether the principle of procreative beneficence is a modest extension of ordinary parental love or the same old eugenics with a consent form stapled to it. Her line was that if you are already choosing, and you don't choose the child with the best chance of the best life, you owe an account of why, and "the other embryo felt more like me" is not an account. My line was that a principle which instructs you to rank your possible children along a single scale of expected well-being has already decided, in advance and without noticing, what a life is for — and that once such a ranking exists, the fact that nobody is compelled to consult it will turn out not to matter much. We didn't settle anything. What struck me afterwards was that both of us kept reaching for the same kind of example: the report, the score, the counselling session, the moment when a philosophical claim becomes a form you have to fill in.

So I would like a piece of deadpan satire that lives entirely inside that moment. Write the customer-review page of an online store — roughly 700 words — for a fictional service that sells prospective parents a ranked report on their embryos, complete with a single composite score, decimal places, percentiles, a confidence interval, a "traits" tab with sliders for height and temperament, and relentlessly friendly interface copy. I want eight or so reviews, each tagged as a verified purchase, plus two or three boilerplate replies from the company's customer-care team, under a "Most helpful" header and an aggregate star rating. The reviews must be sincere and entirely unfunny in tone — nobody in them may know they are in a satire — and the comedy has to come from the philosophy being exactly right. Include, at minimum: a reviewer agonising over having chosen the lower-scoring embryo for a reason she cannot state in the language the report gives her; one who wants to know whether the child she declined to select was harmed, and whether "harmed" is even the right word for a person who would never have existed; one from a Deaf couple who chose the embryo the report flagged as suboptimal and who is now being told by other users that they have insulted their own child by choosing them; one who is delighted with the service and horrified to realise that it has made her answerable for everything her son turns out to be, since she had the information and chose anyway; and, as the final review, one written by an adult who was himself selected as an embryo, who is attempting to file a complaint and cannot locate a harm, because the alternative to him was never anyone. The company's replies should be immaculate non-answers that keep insisting the decision rests entirely with the parents and that the service merely supplies information — that is the part I most want to be funny, because it is the part that is true.

Call the service something plausible and bland, in the register of a wellness startup — "Brightline Genetics," "Kindred," that sort of thing. Keep the eugenics history present throughout without any character in the piece ever using the word, and let the pressure come from other customers rather than from any authority. A bioethicist should find it fair rather than a caricature; a friend who has read none of this should still find it funny and slightly upsetting. No citations, no footnotes, no real people, companies, or studies.
