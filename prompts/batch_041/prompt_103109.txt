I am a novelist, and I have been trying to set down, for my own use, what I actually believe about blame, responsibility, and the stance one takes toward other people. Before I build anything on it, I would like an audit of whether the set hangs together.

(a) Following Strawson, I take it that holding someone responsible is not reporting a fact about their will but being disposed to react to them in the interpersonal register — resentment, gratitude, indignation, hurt, forgiveness — and that there is no further fact of the kind a metaphysics of free will might deliver that these attitudes answer to.

(b) I accept that the objective attitude — the stance in which one regards a person as a specimen to be explained, predicted, managed, or treated — is always available to us, in principle, toward anyone.

(c) I hold that a life in which that attitude were sustained toward everyone would not be a human life; the interpersonal attitudes are not a policy we could rationally elect to abandon, and abandoning them would be a kind of death of the person who did it.

(d) I believe the truth or falsity of determinism makes no difference here — that the quarrel between the optimist, who thinks our practices are safe whatever the physicists find, and the pessimist, who thinks they are not, is not one that determinism could settle.

(e) I think there is a real and important difference between the excuses that suspend a particular reactive attitude on a given occasion — that it was not meant, that it was not in the agent's power — and the exemptions that take a person out of the practice altogether, as we do with the very young, the severely damaged, the deranged.

(f) And here I speak as a novelist. The attitude I take toward my characters is precisely the objective attitude: I do not resent them, I explain them. I have come to think this is not merely a technical necessity of the craft but the source of whatever moral insight the novel has — that seeing people as products of circumstance, as specimens, is what lets me render them without the distortions of blame.

What I want is not a lecture on Strawson but an audit. Which of these six pull against one another, and how badly? I expect that some are in genuine conflict and some only appear to be. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be; for each apparent one, say what makes it dissolve. Order the genuine tensions by severity, and at the end name the single revision that would do the most work. I do not know in advance which of the six I would be least willing to surrender, so do not assume that one of them is the obvious casualty.
