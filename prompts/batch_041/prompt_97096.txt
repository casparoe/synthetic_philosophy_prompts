I'm a first-year assistant prof in the CS department at a mid-sized state school, and I've just been put on the qualifying-exam appeals subcommittee, which is funny timing because I'm teaching the theory of computation course next term and I've spent two weeks in a low-grade panic about how to present the Church–Turing thesis to students who will be examined on it. So this case has landed on me at a bad moment.

Here's what's in front of us. A student in last spring's quals got a question that read, "The Church–Turing thesis is a mathematical theorem. True or false." She wrote: false, and the question is malformed. Her reasoning, as far as I can reconstruct it from her appeal: the claim that every effectively calculable function is computable by a Turing machine can't be a theorem, because "effectively calculable" isn't a formal notion — it's the informal target, and the thesis is exactly what connects that informal target to a formal model. On her line, the convergence of Turing machines, lambda calculus, and recursive functions is evidence we've latched onto the right formal notion, not a proof of anything. She then said the interesting question is a seperate one — whether any physically realizable device can compute something no Turing machine can — and called that version a real empirical hypothesis, though probably not falsifiable in practice, since if someone announced a hypercomputer we'd suspect the physics first, then the idealization, then only at the very end our notion of what counts as a "procedure"; and she threw in something about analog networks with real-valued weights being able to do more than a Turing machine, and said even if that's right it's a fact about an idealization, not about effective calculability. The grader, who's taught the course for a decade, marked her wrong. His comment was that the thesis is the central empirical claim of computer science, on par with a law of nature, and that a student who says it's true by definition has just refused to answer. She's appealing.

I have to circulate a written opinion before Friday's meeting: uphold the grade, change it, or split it (credit on the first part, nothing on the second), plus whatever I can recommend about how we ask this in future exams. I need to be honest with myself that I half agree with the grader — there is something dodgy about "it's not a theorem, it's a definition" followed by a request for credit for having said something substantive, and I've watched the same move used to dodge any question about whether our model of computation is the right one. But I also can't shake the feeling that his rubric quietly fuses two or three different claims (one about what a human calculator could do given unlimited paper and patience, one about what any physical process can do, one about what our formal models happen to capture) and then treats the fused thing as a plain empirical fact, which is what makes the exam item basically unanswerable as written. Can you draft the memo? I want a clear bottom line with a real argument for it, the strongest version of the position I'm rejecting stated fairly enough that its defenders would recognize themselves in it, and something concrete about how the question should be worded next time so that a student who understands the distinction doesn't have to lose points to make it. No citations needed, I just need it to be right.
