I am applying for a two-year postdoctoral research fellowship in moral and political philosophy, and I need a proposal of roughly three pages. The area is the ethics of discounting the future, and I want to enter it from an angle that has not been worked to death. My half-formed idea is that two debates usually kept apart — the debate about future bias and near bias within a single life, and the debate about the social discount rate for long-term outcomes such as climate change — are in fact two halves of one problem: whether mere temporal location can by itself be a reason. Each has resources the other needs. The intra-personal literature has a fine-grained vocabulary for separating a preference that is about the direction of time from one that is about temporal distance, and for asking when such a preference is a rational defect in an agent versus a constitutive feature of having a perspective at all. The intergenerational literature has a sharper vocabulary for separating a discount that attaches to value from one that attaches to the reasons an agent has for promoting a value, and from one that merely reflects the causal openness and unsettled character of the future. Neither side imports the other's distinctions. My suspicion is that once these distinctions are in place, the supposedly irrational form of pure time preference — counting a benefit for less merely because it lies in the future — comes close to empty, so that most of what is actually defended under that name turns out to be something else, and the genuinely contested question migrates to whether the unavoidably located, agent-relative structure of concern can be shown to be rational rather than merely excused as a limitation.

Please turn this into a proposal. It should state the research question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions leave it open and why the two literatures have not already settled it; set out the method — what I will actually do, in phases across the two years, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives; say what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing, or turning out to be trivial or merely terminological; and give a candid assessment of scope, including which part of the ambition I may have to cut. The reviewers are philosophers rather than economists, so the proposal should not assume training in welfare economics. Part of its job is to earn my central distinction rather than presuppose it, so do not treat that distinction as established. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will supply those myself, and I would rather you not guess at specific sources or attribute particular views to named authors.
