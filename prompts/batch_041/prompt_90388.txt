I am a high school student trying to work out the rule that my ethics teacher actually applies when she decides whether an action has moral worth. She keeps insisting that the interesting question is not whether an act was right, but whether the agent was moved by what made it right, and that these two things can come apart. Her verdicts so far are these:

- A shopkeeper who deals honestly with customers because cheating would ruin his reputation: the act is right, but it has no moral worth.
- A frightened bystander who pulls a stranger out of the path of a car, terrified the whole time, because she thinks it would be monstrous to let him die: moral worth.
- A student who volunteers at a food bank only because a scholarship requires service hours: the act is right, but no moral worth.
- A woman who gives money to a family whose house has burned down, moved by their need and never giving duty a thought: moral worth.
- A student who declines to cheat only because he is afraid of being caught: no moral worth.
- A man who keeps a promise he regrets making, because he keeps thinking of how much the other person is counting on him: moral worth.

For an essay I have been assigned the following case. Maya, a classmate, steps in when a smaller boy is being mocked in the hallway. She says she did it because the boy reminded her of her little brother and she could not stand to watch. She never considered whether she had any duty to act, she doubts that bullying is wrong in any deep sense, and she says she only stepped in because the scene made her feel sick.

Please lead with your prediction of my teacher's verdict on Maya. Then set out the rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, stating what each of them predicts about Maya; tell me which rule I should treat as hers, and why; and name the single further case that would force a choice among those rules. If her verdicts are actually inconsistent, say where.

Keep the whole answer to 700 words or fewer.
