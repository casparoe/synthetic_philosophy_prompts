I'm doing a wierd double bill at the moment — working through the Zhuangzi for a Chinese philosophy reading group, and rewatching Inception with a couple of friends who are convinced the film is a puzzle with one right answer about whether the top falls. I'd like to read the film through the butterfly dream, and I want it done properly rather than as a bit of pop-philosophy garnish.

The bit of the butterfly passage I keep coming back to isn't the usual sceptical question about how you know you're not dreaming. What interests me is that Zhuangzi doesn't resolve it — no criterion, no malicious demon, no cogito — and the passage seems to end by saying that Zhou and the butterfly pass into one another, with an odd little pull-back where it still insists there must be a distinction between them (I might be misremembering the order of that, correct me if so). And the waking is always retrospective: you only call a dream a dream once you've woken, and there's no standpoint outside the whole series. So read Inception against that. The film is obviously Cartesian in its bones — levels, kicks, a private totem you carry down to test whether you're dreaming, a hero whose entire arc is getting back to a baseline reality and his kids. Does the totem survive a Zhuangzian objection? It's a token that's supposed to be infallible across worlds, but a dream can supply a token that behaves impeccably, and Cobb is using the top that belonged to Mal anyway, which the film's own rule says is meaningless. And what about Mal — she treats dying as the way to wake up, which is the Cartesian gamble played for keeps. Is Limbo just the state Zhuangzi gestures at when he suggests we might be unawakened from a great dream?

Then there's the part I actually care about: inception itself. The whole heist is about a thought the target will experience as his own — Fischer will believe an idea that isn't his and will never be able to tell from the inside. That seems to me to dramatise something the sceptical reading of the butterfly dream misses: not is this world real, but whose thought is this, and can you ever tell a borrowed content from an authored one? Zhuangzi's butterfly is pleased with itself and doesn't know it's Zhou; Fischer is pleased with an idea and doesn't know it's Cobb's. I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously, and then tells me where it breaks down — my suspicion is the film is finally anti-Zhuangzian, because it keeps a real world offstage and treats the whole thing as a retrieval problem, and the wobbling top at the end is a cheat that flatters us into thinking there's an answer waiting. Tell me where I'm wrong, and finish by saying what the film adds to the dream-doubt arguement that the argument on its own doesn't. Don't bother quoting the text at me or handing me a reading list — I just want the reading.
