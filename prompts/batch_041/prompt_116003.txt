I am a senior in high school, and this year I am taking a moral philosophy elective alongside a creative writing workshop, and the two have collided in a way I did not expect. In the philosophy class we spent two weeks on the idea that the rightness of an action depends on its consequences, and near the end my teacher introduced a problem she calls consequentialist cluelessness: if what makes an act right or wrong is the total difference it makes to how things go, then judging the act requires adding up everything it causes, and everything it causes never stops. A favour changes who a person becomes, which changes what she does, which changes people she has never met, and on and on past any horizon I can name. Her first point was that I cannot do this sum, which is bad enough. Her second point was stranger and is the one I keep thinking about: she said it may not be that the sum is unknown but that there is no definite sum to be unknown, because there is no principled place to stop counting and no way to add up consequences that never terminate. She also said the problem gets worse rather than better the wider your moral circle is — the moment you care about strangers, future people, or animals, the accounting stops being something anyone could ever finish. I have been assigned a sestina in the writing workshop, and I have started to suspect that the form and the problem might fit each other, so I would like to submit a poem to our class anthology that closes the unit.

I would like you to take the role of a poet who is also a philosopher working on consequentialism, and to think carefully before you write. Please begin by brainstorming out loud: propose six end-words and explain in a sentence why each one can survive six appearances in shifting positions without going stale, then choose the set you think is strongest and write the poem. The poem should be a sestina in the strict sense — six stanzas of six lines followed by a three-line envoi, with the end-words repeated exactly and rotating in the standard pattern, so that the last end-word of each stanza becomes the first end-word of the next — and the envoi should gather all six. The six end-words should stand for the links in the chain the problem turns on: the act, the intention behind it, the immediate effect, the distant effect, what can be known, and what cannot. The speaker should be someone looking back decades later at one small, ordinary act, something domestic rather than a trolley case; you may choose it, but I would suggest telling a friend an unwelcome truth, or lending money you could not really spare, or staying silent when speaking up would have been easy.

The reason I want the sestina specifically is that its rotation ought to do the philosophical work rather than merely decorate it. Every time an end-word returns it should mean something slightly different, because the world it returns into has changed — the same consequence arriving in a new position should feel like the same thing happening again and like something else entirely, which is exactly what I take the problem to be saying. The poem should not explain the problem in prose, and the words consequentialism and cluelessness should not appear in it; a reader who knows nothing about any of this should still find it a good poem, while a philosopher of ethics should recognize both layers of the problem — the sum I cannot compute and the sum that may not exist — and should not be able to say the poem has softened either one. Finally, the envoi has to attempt a verdict and fail at it: it should set side by side the fact that the act was done, or had to be done, and the fact that nothing settles whether it made things better, and it should leave those two things there rather than resolving them into either uplift or despair. Please do not end on a moral about doing one's best, and do not end on a shrug.
