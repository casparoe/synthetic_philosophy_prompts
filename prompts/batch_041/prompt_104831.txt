I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I'm writing the final. Every year the essay questions on consciousness draw the same answers: students summarize the Mary case, run through the ability hypothesis, the acquaintance hypothesis, and the phenomenal-concept move, and stop, without ever having to commit to anything. This year I want one essay question they cannot answer by reciting that catalogue, so I've built a variant of the case that I think forces a choice, and I'd like you to turn it into the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The variant has two parts, both about Mary's situation but pulling in opposite directions. In the first, while Mary is still in the room, a surgeon fits her with an implant that restores every downstream capacity associated with seeing red: she can now sort red from green, form and deploy the concept RED, imagine red, recall it, and reason about it exactly as an ordinary perceiver does — but the implant touches none of the phenomenal machinery, so she never has a reddish experience. Call her Implant Mary. In the second, her sister has the reverse condition: her visual system produces full, ordinary reddish experiences, but a lesion strips away all the downstream capacities, so she has the experience and can do nothing with it — she cannot sort, name, imagine, or recall. Call her Locked-In Sister. Both women already know every physical fact there is to know about color vision.

What I want the question to make students confront is this: does Implant Mary know what it's like to see red, and does Locked-In Sister? I want them to see that the ability hypothesis is committed to saying yes about Implant Mary and is thereby in trouble, and that the sister's case pressures the phenomenal-concept strategy in a different way, since she has the experience but arguably not a usable concept. Please write the essay question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words that works through what each of the standard replies predicts about both women and lands on a defended verdict about which reply handles the pair best, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain. I'd like the scheme to total 20 points, with a one-line note on how students typically get each point wrong, and I'd like the top-band criteria to require that the answer notices both asymmetries — the abilities without the experience, and the experience without the abilities — rather than merely listing the replies.
