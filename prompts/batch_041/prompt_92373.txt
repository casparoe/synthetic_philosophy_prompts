Write an employee handbook — roughly 800 words, in the deadpan register of corporate human-resources documentation, with headings, numbered clauses, a page of stated values, and a short FAQ — for a fictional news organization that has just completed an internal initiative called Project Leveling. The handbook is what a new hire receives on the first morning. It is written entirely from inside the institution's point of view, by people who believe every word of it, and the organization is invented: the target is the logic of the thing, not any real newspaper.

The satire should be aimed at the self-understanding of the modern press and at the public it claims to serve and to speak for. It should dramatize rather than assert the following diagnosis, which I take over from Kierkegaard's attack on the press: that the press manufactures an abstract readership — the public — which comprises everyone and is therefore no one, so that it can be invoked as an authority and never held to anything; that the characteristic product of the press is reflection and talk rather than action, so that an age can be exhaustively informed and still do nothing; that the leveling such an age undergoes cancels distinctions from below rather than raising anyone, producing not equality but an abstract individual who is nobody in particular; and that this leveling is driven less by a love of equality than by envy. The comedy should come from the institution sincerely embracing each of these as a good thing. Do not let the handbook explain its own irony, do not let it turn into a general lament about social media, and do not let anyone inside the document notice that anything is odd.

Project Leveling should be an exaggeration of a real tendency rather than a fantasy: the paper has abolished bylines, mastheads, and editorial titles on the grounds that every story belongs to the paper and the paper's voice is the voice of its readers. Four further requirements. The handbook's account of the public must include everyone and identify no one, and the document must then rely on that account — when it explains who is accountable for a story, or what to tell a subject who says the story ruined them. The paper reports but never acts, and the handbook must present this as a feature of the job and never concede that it is a limitation. Engagement is measured, promoted, and rewarded, but never directed at anything in particular; the handbook should describe this as the paper's public service. The handbook must never name Kierkegaard or quote anyone, and must not use his vocabulary as a technical apparatus; the word leveling may appear only as the name of the initiative.

Every claim must be one this institution could sincerely make given the diagnosis above, and the document may not quietly import an ordinary defense of a free press — holding power to account, giving the voiceless a voice — unless its own logic visibly hollows that defense out. This is a reductio, not a debate. Close with the answer new hires are instructed to give when a source asks who is responsible for a story.

The piece is for a graduate seminar on Kierkegaard and the public sphere: it should be funny to a reader who has never heard of him, and it should read as a fair exaggeration to one who has.
