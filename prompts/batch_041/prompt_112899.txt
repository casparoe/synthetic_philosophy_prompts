I need a teaching plan for a single session on a question in the philosophy of mind — the plan, not the content, and not an argument for a position. The question is whether symbols push the substrate around.

Here is the device I mean to build the session on. Picture a vast shallow arena, a billiard table the size of a football pitch, across which an enormous number of small magnetic marbles — call them simms — careen at random, each obeying a handful of simple mechanical and magnetic rules. Simms collide, cling, and separate; clumps grow, break apart, and re-form. Now pull back. At that distance you no longer see individual marbles, only larger and comparatively stable clusters — simmballs — and the simmballs exhibit regularities invisible at the level of the simms: certain kinds recur, certain kinds reliably follow certain others, and, if the walls of the arena change their bounce in step with events outside it, certain kinds of simmball come to track those events. The simmballs even seem to act on one another, one cluster's appearance seeming to summon or suppress another. Read the simmballs as symbols or concepts and the simms as a neural substrate, and the arena becomes a brain. The question the session exists to pose: when the simmball-level description is true and predictive, do the simmballs — the symbols — push the simms around, or is all the pushing done by the simms, with the symbols an idle gloss on the physics?

The audience is a graduate reading group in cognitive science: neuroscientists, machine-learning people, and philosophers of mind, none of whom works on causation. They are fluent in the idea that one system admits descriptions at more than one grain, and for that very reason they will treat causal talk as unproblematic. I have seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, and no assigned reading.

Give me the plan. Specifically:

1. The order. Establish the sequence in which the pieces must be introduced, and why each depends on the one before it — down to the assumption that has to be conceded before the question even becomes intelligible. I suspect the hard part is not the careenium but something that must first be settled about what makes a description true, or projectible, or explanatory, and I want you to tell me whether that is right, where it belongs, and where the session's real pivot falls.

2. The carrying example. The careenium is meant to carry the whole session; tell me what it must have and what it must lack for that to work. Then tell me whether it is in fact the right carrier, or whether some more mundane case — a chess engine, a market, an ant colony — should do the load-bearing work, with the careenium demoted to a closing flourish. If the careenium stays, say which of its features I must keep and which I can drop without loss.

3. The misconceptions. They will bring some and form others. My provisional list: that a higher-level description must be either a useful fiction or a mysterious addition, never simply another true description of the same thing; that if the micro-story is complete, the symbol-level story is idle; that causal talk at two levels has to be a competition, so exactly one level is the real cause; and that symbol-level causation would require a symbol to exert a force on a marble. Give me the full list in the order it will surface, and for each, the exact point in the sequence at which I should head it off — and, where it matters, the point at which I should deliberately let it stand for a while so that a later step dissolves it rather than a reminder pre-empting it.

4. The check. One question or task, at the end, that tells me whether they have understood the question rather than merely memorised a position — whether they now see what the two answers disagree about.

5. What to leave out, and what I should say aloud that I am leaving out, given this audience and this much time.

Do not give me readings, and do not simply answer the question on my behalf; I have a view of my own, and what I need is the plan for getting them to it.
