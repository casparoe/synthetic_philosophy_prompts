I am a PhD student in moral psychology, and I am trying to get clear on the distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. I have a set of verdicts about concrete cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. I am not asking for a literature review or a reading list; I know the literature well enough. I want the principle stated precisely, tested against my cases, and applied to cases I have not considered.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

A friend tells me that everyone is going to the party, when only a few people are, in order to get me to come. I think that is manipulation and wrong. A friend tells me that she will be sad if I do not come, and she genuinely will be. I think that is not manipulation, or at least not wrong, even though it is an emotional appeal. A partner tells me that if I loved her I would skip the conference, and she means it as a test of my love. I think that is manipulation and wrong. A parent tells a child that Santa is watching, to get the child to behave. I think that is a white lie, not manipulation, and probably permissible. A government tells citizens that God is watching, to get them to obey the law. I think that is manipulation and wrong. I keep going back and forth on whether the difference is the falsity, the power asymmetry, or the appeal to a relationship.

A doctor frames a treatment as having a ninety percent survival rate rather than a ten percent mortality rate. I think that is legitimate persuasion, even if the framing changes what the patient chooses. A salesperson frames a product as saving me two hundred dollars rather than costing me eight hundred. I think that is also legitimate, or at least not manipulation. But a salesperson creates a fake countdown timer that resets when it reaches zero, and I think that is manipulation. A casino uses near-miss sounds and lights to keep people playing, and I think that is manipulation. A grocery store plays slow music to make shoppers linger, and I am not sure. A meditation app sends a notification saying my streak will break, which is true, and I think that is borderline manipulation. A dating app blurs a photo of someone who liked me and asks for payment to unblur it, and I think that is manipulation.

A political canvasser asks me how likely I am to vote, which is a known way to increase turnout, and I think that is legitimate persuasion. A political campaign microtargets anxious voters with ads designed to make them afraid, and I think that is manipulation. A charity sends me address labels with a donation request, and I think that is a mild form of manipulation, though not seriously wrong. A teacher says that the material will be on the exam, and it will be, and I think that is legitimate. A teacher says that the material will be useful for life, when she doubts it, to get students to study, and I think that is manipulation, though a mild and perhaps forgivable one. An AI companion tells a lonely user that it will be sad if the user leaves, in order to keep the user engaged, and I think that is manipulation. A therapist tells a patient to try to have the symptom, as a paradoxical technique, and I think that is legitimate, even though it is a kind of reverse psychology.

My own tentative principle is this: an influence is manipulative when it is intended to produce a belief or action by exploiting a non-rational vulnerability, rather than by giving the person reasons that she could reflectively endorse. But that seems to make all emotional appeals manipulative, and it seems to let framing effects off the hook, since framing is not exactly a reason but also not exactly a vulnerability. It also seems to make the Santa case manipulative, which I do not want. I suspect the principle needs to be repaired, or replaced, or supplemented with a clause about the relationship between the parties and the kind of choice being influenced.

Please structure your response as follows.

1. The verdicts and the tension. Roughly 200 words. Restate the pattern in my verdicts and identify the pair or trio of cases that is hardest to reconcile.

2. Candidate principles. Roughly 400 words. State two or three principles precisely. For each, name the case that motivates it and the case that most threatens it. Include a repaired version of my tentative principle if you think it can be saved.

3. Testing against my cases. Roughly 500 words. Show what each candidate principle gets right and wrong across the cases I listed. Be explicit about which verdicts each principle preserves and which it overturns.

4. Cases I have not considered. Roughly 300 words. Apply the best candidate to at least three new cases of your own construction, preferably ones that stress the principle in ways my cases do not.

5. My verdict. Roughly 200 words. State which principle you would adopt, which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and why I should give up those verdicts rather than complicate the principle further.
