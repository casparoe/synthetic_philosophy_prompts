I'm writing a seminar paper on Kant's practical philosophy, and alongside that I've spent the past few months reading technical work on AI alignment, where I keep running into a contrast I can't get out of my head. The familiar picture of an artificial agent is goal-directed: it is handed an objective and selects actions that score well against it. A rival picture, which its proponents call approval-directed, drops the fixed objective and puts a human overseer in its place — at each step the agent predicts what that overseer would approve of and does it, so what the system tracks is a person's approval rather than a score. Its defenders argue that an agent built this way will be deferential, interruptible, and easy to correct, and so less likely to charge off toward a single objective in a way that no one can stop; that, they say, is safer than giving a powerful system a goal and hoping the goal was specified correctly.

I would like the paper to reconstruct, as carefully as Kant's texts permit, what he would have thought of these two designs. Would the approval-directed agent's dependence on someone else's approval make its will heteronomous in his sense, and if so, does that make it a worse candidate for genuine agency than the goal-directed maximizer, or a better one? Does his distinction between acting from duty and acting merely in conformity with duty do any work for a system that has no inclinations to resist and nothing to gain? The overseer's approval is empirical and contingent, whereas the moral law is supposed to bind every rational being alike, so does that gap matter — and is there any reading on which the overseer stands in for the law rather than for an external authority, which for Kant is precisely the error, since the law has to be self-legislated? He also holds that we can never be fully certain of our own motives, which would seem to make the overseer's approval a target even she cannot reliably report. And please take seriously the possibility that the question is ill-formed: if Kantian ethics presupposes a finite rational being with a sensuous nature, then neither design falls inside the theory at all, and I would rather have you say so plainly than force the analogy. Finish by telling me what the exercise shows about Kant's framework — vindicated, strained, or simply silent — and which of the two designs he would have chosen if he had to choose. Please write your answer in American English.
