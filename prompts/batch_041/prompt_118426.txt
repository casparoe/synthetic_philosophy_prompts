I teach a seminar on animal minds and I'm trying to write up a lecture on how the law actually draws the line on which animals have minds, and the more I stare at the line the less it looks like it tracks anything about minds. Here's the pattern.

In the US the Animal Welfare Act -- the main federal statute for animals used in research -- defines "animal" so as to leave out birds, purpose-bred lab rats and lab mice, farm animals, and every cold-blooded animal. So the mouse, by far the most numerous research mammal, isn't an animal as far as that statute is concerned, while a dog is. The federal humane slaughter statute names only cattle, calves, horses, mules, sheep and swine; poultry and fish aren't in it, so a chicken can be shackled and killed while still conscious, legally, while a cow can't. Over in Europe, the directive on animals used for scientific purposes was the first EU research law to reach an invertebrate at all, and the invertebrate it reaches is the octopus. And the UK's 2022 sentience act defines "animal" as any vertebrate other than us, plus any cephalopod mollusc and any decapod crustacean -- so octopus and lobster are in, and since shrimp and prawns are decapods they're in as well, while a honeybee or a fruit fly is out. Same act lets the relevant Secretary of State add further invertebrates by regulation later on, and requires the sentience committee's recommendations to respect religious rites, cultural traditions and regional heritage.

Meanwhile you can drop a live lobster into boiling water in a US kitchen; a mouse that is a protected research subject is, in most places, fair game for a glue trap in my neighbors kitchen; poisoning a dog is a crime while poisoning a rat is pest control; and nobody who drafted any of this thought they were doing metaphysics.

What I want from you: what view of consciousness does this patchwork presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful to seperate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit. Does it commit to consciousness requiring warm blood, or a backbone, or a centralized brain -- or is it using those as crude proxies for something else? Which rival accounts of animal consciousness does it quietly rule out, and does the octopus clause contradict the warm-blooded one? Is there really a theory here at all, or is it a bundle of unrelated decisions -- tractability, agricultural exemptions, whatever a minister can get through parliament -- that several incompatible theories of mind would each license equally well?

Then tell me what would have to change if we swapped the presupposed view out. Try (a) anything with a centralized nervous system and nociceptors counts, and (b) assume sentience wherever the evidence is at all plausible and give the doubt the benefit of it. What breaks first -- insects, farmed fish, the slaughter exemptions, pest control? And if you were the minister holding that delegated power to add invertebrates, what would you have to believe about a fruit fly to leave it out, and about a prawn to leave it in?
