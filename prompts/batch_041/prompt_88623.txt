I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of law seminar on vagueness, and I want to analyze the Supreme Court's obscenity jurisprudence as a case study. The Court has tried to draw a line between protected and unprotected sexual expression through the Miller test, with its appeal to contemporary community standards and its requirement that the work lack serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value, while earlier opinions admitted the line cannot be defined but is recognizable when seen. Read this episode through the debate between epistemicism and supervaluationism. Does the Court's practice presuppose a sharp line we are ignorant of, or does it treat borderline cases as lacking determinate legal status until a court precisifies? What does the law's need to decide every case do to supervaluationist truth-value gaps, and does persistent disagreement across courts and communities support epistemicism or undermine it? I am especially interested in whether higher-order vagueness — the vagueness of the borderline itself — makes the epistemicist's sharp but unknowable line untenable in the legal context, and in what this episode reveals about the two theories that the standard sorites examples do not. Please do not merely summarize the cases; I know the holdings.
