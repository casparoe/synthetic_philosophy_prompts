I've been teaching a final-year seminar on Scanlon and the aggregation problem, and I keep running into the same thing with my students: they can recite the complaint model back at me, but the moment you ask them to point at an actual institution and say what it's committed to, they freeze. So I want to give them a proper worked example, and I've picked the one I know best, from having sat on a couple of funding panels. I'd like you to write it up for me.

Here's the practice. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in England judges new treatments against a cost-per-quality-adjusted-life-year threshold — a QALY being roughly a year of life in good health — and the figure sits somewhere in the tens of thousands of pounds. Below the threshold a drug is funded more or less as a matter of course; above it, it is normally refused, even when the patient group is large and the benefit to each of them is real. Yet the same system is conspicuously more generous in two directions. A drug that extends life for people who are terminally ill gets through at a much higher cost per QALY than a drug that would improve the quality of life of a considerably larger group, and drugs for very rare conditions have their own, far more permissive route, so that a treatment costing an order of magnitude more per QALY than the one just turned down can sail through untouched. On top of that sits the individual funding request, where a single patient argues that their circumstances are exceptional and that the general rule was never meant to reach them. My students can't see how all of that hangs together, and honestly neither can I, which is rather the point.

So: reconstruct the view of value and of justification that this pattern presupposes, if it presupposes one at all. Does the aggregate threshold commit the system to numbers counting, in a way that the two exceptions quietly deny? Or can a single consistent anti-aggregative view — a contractualist one, where a principle is defensible only if no one could reasonably reject it, and complaints are weighed one by one rather than summed — generate the whole pattern, threshold and all? I'm after the distinction between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with, plus a judgement on whether the exceptions are principled or just the residue of political squeamishness about refusing dying people and rare-disease charities. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if you swapped the presupposed view for straightforward aggregative consequentialism, and again if you swapped it for a strict refusal to aggregate at all. Say where the practice is underdetermined — which rival theories would license exactly the same decisions — and give me your honest verdict on the awkward bit: whether an exceptional-case route built around one person's special circumstances can be squared with the very idea of a general policy that has to be justifiable to everyone it applies to. I dont want a reading list, I know the literature; I want the reconstruction. Finish with one line of the form \answer{...} containing a single sentence saying which view the practice most plausibly presupposes, or that it is underdetermined.
