You are an expert in the philosophy of the historical sciences with a detailed working knowledge of stratigraphic practice and of the politics of periodization. Before writing anything, think carefully about the strongest case each of the two people below could make, so that neither comes across as a strawman.

Write a dialogue between (A) a stratigrapher who has sat on the bodies that maintain the international geological time scale and who defends the 2024 decision not to recognize the Anthropocene as a formal epoch, and (B) an environmental historian who treats the Anthropocene as a period concept and holds that the geologists' veto settles nothing of importance. Their exchange should be a genuine back-and-forth in which each replies to the other's actual last point rather than restating a position.

Let them work through at least these questions: whether a unit whose upper boundary cannot yet be defined can count as a chronostratigraphic unit at all, and what follows if the Anthropocene is better described as an ongoing geological event than as an epoch; why the requirement that a boundary marker be globally isochronous pushes the choice toward plutonium fallout from atmospheric weapons tests and away from coal ash, the spread of agriculture, or the Columbian exchange; and who has standing to decide the question — a committee of stratigraphers, the fields that coined and use the term, or no one.

Present the dialogue as exactly five numbered exchanges, with one speech by each participant per exchange and a few sentences per speech. No scene-setting, no introduction, no concluding summary.
