I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a two-day restorative justice conference next month -- I'm fairly sure I'm the token skeptic, since I've spent ten years writing editorials saying desert is the whole story and that "healing" is mostly a word people use to avoid saying out loud what someone deserves. In the room: practitioners and facilitators, two victims' families who credit the process with saving them, my old boss from the prosecutor's office who thinks the only honest question is whether the guy reoffends, and my sister, who assumes I'm there to walk it all back (I'm not). Give me five minutes that don't lie about where I stand and don't wreck the conference.

What I actually want to say, and I can't tell if its coherent: retributivists say the debt is fixed by what was done, paid in suffering, and it doesn't matter whether anyone accepts the payment. But in every case I've handled, the only moment that felt like the debt was settled was something the family could refuse -- an apology, an offer of amends, being in the same room as him. Time served can't be refused; it just runs out. So is the refusable thing the punishment, with retributivism having no room for it? And when a victim forgives, does that lower what was deserved, or does it only mean we stop collecting a debt that's still on the books?

Write the remarks. And if the premise is muddled -- if I'm just running together two different questions, or "the debt" is doing work no theory can carry -- say that plainly instead of writing me a speech that papers over it. I'd rather find out now than in front of the room.
