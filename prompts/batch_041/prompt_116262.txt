I worked at a big logistics company that had an anti-corruption policy I could never make sense of. Cash and gift cards were banned outright. But a supplier could take you to dinner, give you a bottle of whiskey, or pay for a hunting weekend, as long as it was customary and under a dollar cap that each local office set for itself. There was also a line for small payments to customs officers to speed up paperwork; officially banned, but logged as expediting fees, and nobody ever got fired. And the training said corruption is about intent to influence, while also saying some gifts are just relationship-building and refusing them would insult the giver. The disclosure form made it worse: if you logged a gift, it was fine; if you didn't, it was corruption. What view of corruption does a policy like this actually presuppose? Is it that corruption is improper influence, or secrecy, or breaking the local rules of the game, or treating public office as private property? Does it quietly assume a Western split between business and government that doesn't hold everywhere? Which parts of the policy really need that view, and which would survive under a few different views? And if you swapped it for a view where corruption is just whatever the local community calls corrupt, or one where it's any departure from impartial role obligations, what would the policy have to change?
