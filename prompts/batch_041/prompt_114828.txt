I'm drafting a two-page prospectus for a one-semester honours thesis and want to try a half-baked idea on you. The line everyone repeat's is that honouring sunk costs is irrational, and the standard argument for it only really bites when the past is causally inert. But past spending can matter in at least two other ways. It's evidence — about me, or about the project's prospects — and sometimes it partly constitutes the thing I'm deciding about: a friendship, a career, a research programme, a marriage are the kinds of things that are what they are partly because of their history, so if I walk away there isn't a leftover thing that I could have had anyway. My hunch is that the fallacy is a real fallacy only in the first case, and that in the third the usual argument quietly assumes value is entirely forward-looking, which is a substantive assumption rather than a neutral one.

Write the prospectus: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer; why the psychology side and the decision-theory side both leave it open (they agree on the verdict but not on why, and I don't think either asks what happens when the good at stake is historical); the method, i.e. what I'd actually do over the semester and why that rather than just cataloguing cases; what a good result looks like and what would make me admit the project failed; and whether a semester is realistic. Skip the citations, I'll definately add those myself. And if the causal/evidential/constitutive split is just a mess, or the whole thing collapses back into the standard debate once you press it, say so plainly rather than dressing it up.
