I am a novelist at work on a book set in a plausible near-future publishing house, and I have run into a conceptual wall that I suspect Adam Smith could help me climb. In my invented firm — I have been calling it the Assembly — no one writes a first draft any more: a language model produces it, and the woman whose name goes on the cover spends her days selecting among generated versions, making small repairs, and, most importantly for my purposes, standing ready to be blamed. When a book turns out to be defamatory, or cruel, or simply bad, she is the one who must answer for it; the machine cannot be sued, shamed, or disappointed. A friend who read the draft said I had merely reinvented the pin factory, and that Smith would have recognised the deskilling at once. I am not satisfied with that reading, because my character is not only dulled — she is asked to feel responsible for work she did not do, and to take pride in it. What I want from you is a prediction rather than a summary: not a recitation of what Smith said about the division of labour, whose broad strokes I know, but your best reconstruction of how he would actually judge this arrangement, argued from the resources of his moral psychology rather than from a slogan about unintended social benefits.

Please work through three things in particular. First, whether Smith's account of the workman's relation to the product of his labour, and his associated worry that a life confined to a few simple operations starves the faculties, extends to a worker whose only remaining operation is to endorse an output she did not make — or whether the arrangement defeats the diagnosis by removing even the simple operations. Second, whether the machinery of the impartial spectator, and with it the desire for deserved praise and the fear of deserved blame, can be engaged at all by work one did not author, or whether that machinery would misfire, so that my character's sense of responsibility is either a hollow formality or something Smith would have found morally intelligible. Third, whether Smith would read her residual power to refuse a generated draft on ethical grounds as preserving the moral character of her labour, or as a cosmetic alibi draped over an assembly line. I am also curious whether he would extend sympathy, in his technical sense, to the reading public's admiration of the finished book: whether an impartial spectator could admire a work while knowing how it was made, or whether the sentiment would curdle into something else once the provenance was known. Where my case presses on a genuine ambiguity in Smith rather than on something he settled, say so plainly instead of papering it over; where his texts pull in two directions, tell me which pull you judge the stronger and why.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Throughout, say how confident you are in each claim, and single out the one point in your reconstruction that you are least sure of.
