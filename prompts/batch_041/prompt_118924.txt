Last night I lost an argument — or at least failed to win one — about Aristotle's theory of natural slavery, and I want to get it written up properly. My friend's position was that Politics Book I is an ideology in search of a philosophy: that the doctrine of the slave as a living tool is a rationalization of an inherited practice, and that the tell is Aristotle's inability to say what marks off the slave by nature from the slave by law. Mine was that the account has more structure than that, and that its real fault line is psychological rather than moral. If the slave has no deliberative faculty at all, while the woman's is merely unauthoritative and the child's merely immature, it is hard to see how he can be credited with the virtues Aristotle assigns him, or how he can apprehend a reason he is said not to possess.

So here is what I want from you. Read The Tempest through that theory. Prospero's title over Caliban has the unmistakable shape of the Aristotelian justification — rule for the benefit of the ruled, a creature serviceable as an instrument, a nature that instruction cannot improve — and the play is often said to demolish the justification from within. I am not convinced, and I want to know where the demolition actually happens. Which scenes give the theory its strongest case, including Caliban's eagerness to serve a new master? Where does the play break it open, and does it do so by exhibiting a rational capacity the natural slave is supposed not to have, or by some other route entirely — a claim of inheritance, say, or a claim about who took the island from whom? And what should we make of the fact that Caliban's servitude is punishment for a crime and seizure by force, which is exactly the case Aristotle concedes is often unjust and insists must be distinguished from the natural kind?

Two constraints. Do not reduce the play to an illustration of the theory, and do not treat the aporetic reading of Book I as a settled escape route; if those chapters are exploratory rather than doctrinal, say what survives of the theory. I do not want a survey of the commentary — I want the reading, and I want it to end with what the play shows about the doctrine that argument alone cannot.
