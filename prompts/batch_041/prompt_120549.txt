I've been reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections for something like fifteen years, which is long enough to have watched the same conversational move return in different costumes. Last week a thread under a book-review post turned into the usual argument about whether it is reasonable to act on a friend's recommendation of a novel. One commenter said that of course it is: there's no disputing about taste, so a recommendation is simply evidence about the recommender's dispositions rather than a claim about the book. Another said that precisely because there's no disputing about taste, a recommendation is worthless, since nobody can tell you what's good. Both wrote as though the slogan settled the matter, and they cannot both be right about what it settles.

What I want is a disambiguation rather than a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that 'there's no disputing about taste' could be making, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, and give me a verdict on each — platitude, substantive but defensible, or false. I would like the menu to include at least: a descriptive claim about what tends to happen when people argue about art; a claim about the norms governing when an aesthetic judgement may appropriately be asserted at all; a claim that aesthetic judgements cannot be supported by reasons that compel assent; the claim that such judgements can only be justified by first-hand experience of the object, so that testimony is not a legitimate route to them; and the range of anti-realist, expressivist, and relativist claims about whether aesthetic predicates express truth-apt propositions in the first place.

Then say, for each comment in the thread, which reading or combination of readings would actually license it, and which reading the people who reach for the slogan probably have in mind. One thing I would particularly like settled: I suspect someone can hold that aesthetic judgements are straightforwardly true or false and still insist that they cannot be taken on anyone's say-so, and I want to know whether that combination is coherent, and whether it is the standard position among people who work on this or a slightly eccentric one.
