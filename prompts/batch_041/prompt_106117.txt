I'm writing a feature about how we come to know what we know about other people's minds — I know, riveting — and I keep getting stuck on a passage in Locke's Essay, the chapter on perception (Book II, ch. 9). It's one of those bits where I understand every word and still feel like I'm missing the point. Here it is:

"Suppose a man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and the other, which is the cube, which the sphere. Suppose then the cube and sphere placed on a table, and the blind man be made to see: quaere, whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube?"

And then the answer, which Locke endorses: "Not. For, though he has obtained the experience of how a globe, how a cube affects his touch, yet he has not yet obtained the experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; or that a protuberant angle in the cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye as it does in the cube."

Three things I'd like help with, in rough order of how much they nag at me.

First, the "Not" — what is it actually denying? That the man couldn't recognize which is which, that he couldn't say which is which, or that he couldn't know it with certainty? Locke later says he wouldn't be able to say "with certainty," which feels weaker than Molyneux's flat "Not," and I can't tell if that's a real difference or just Locke being Locke. And is the reason given meant as a conceptual point about ideas, or just a prediction about what would happen in the first five seconds?

Second, why does Molyneux bother with the "same metal" and "nighly of the same bigness"? I suspect those clauses are doing real work — closing off some route by which the man could cheat — but I want to know exactly what cues they rule out and what would change if the sphere were gold and the cube were iron, or one were twice the size.

Third, and this is the one I actually need for the piece. I've been reading about gadgets that take a camera feed and turn it into a pattern of taps or vibrations on a person's skin, so someone blind from birth can feel the outline of a shape sitting in front of them. Say a man had used one of these for years and gotten very good at it — so he's learned a reliable correspondence between the patterns he feels and the layout of the objects in front of him, even though he's never seen anything. Now he gets his sight. Does the "Not" still hold? Or has he already got the experience Locke says he's missing, since the camera-to-skin device is basically sight routed through touch? What I'm really after is whether the passage is about the gap between touch and sight specifically, or about the gap between any two signals that haven't been correlated yet. My hunch is its the second, but I've got no idea if that's a fair reading or if I'm smuggling in something the text won't support. Tell me which way to read it and where the passage is genuinely ambiguous rather than just old-fashioned.
