I've spent the last few months reporting on the politics of self-binding — debt brakes, balanced-budget amendments, statutory spending caps, advance directives, the whole family of devices by which people and polities try to tie their own hands — and the column I'm drafting has forced me to state my own position, so I'd like an audit before I file. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) Democratic legitimacy requires that the living demos retain the power to revise any law by ordinary majoritarian means; no generation may bind a later one on questions of basic policy. (2) Nonetheless I support entrenched constitutional rights — guarantees that courts enforce against passing majorities, and, to take a concrete mechanism, provisions that a bare majority may repeal only by re-enacting the repeal after an intervening election — and I think a polity is better, not worse, for having them. (3) I take individual precommitment seriously: a person who locks away the key, signs an enforceable advance directive, or posts a bond forfeited on backsliding is acting autonomously, not less than autonomously, and I would want my own past arrangements to bind me. (4) I think constitutional entrenchment is best understood as the demos doing just what such an individual does — binding itself against its own anticipated weakness of will — so the same justification carries straight over. (5) What makes a precommitment binding, as I understand it, is that the agent bound is the agent who bound: the authority comes from its being one and the same self at both ends of the arrangement.

What I can't tell is whether these five actually hang together or whether I'm quietly telling two incompatible stories and papering over the seam. Please audit the set: tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and how badly, ordered by severity rather than presented as a flat list, and separate the real conflicts from any pair that merely looks like a conflict but dissolves once the relevant distinction is drawn. For each genuine conflict, say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set — I'd rather see the trade-offs than a verdict. I'm not sure my confidence in the individual case and my confidence in the democratic case can survive each other, and I have a nagging worry that the move from one to the other is doing work it isn't entitled to, but I don't trust my own sense of where the fault line actually runs, so don't just confirm my hunch. I want the internal audit, not a tour of what the standard literature says.
