I run a small research group that studies subjective experience — chronic pain, tinnitus, the felt character of mood — and we keep hitting the same methodological wall. Participants report features of their experience that they insist are ineffable, intrinsic, private, and known to them directly: the particular quality of their tinnitus, the distinctive texture of their pain, the felt tone of a depressed afternoon. Our clinicians and statisticians want these features turned into measurable constructs; the participants say any number we attach to them misses what they are. I have been reading about Daniel Dennett's proposal that qualia, understood as properties carrying exactly those four marks, may be an incoherent posit — that the more carefully we try to pin them down, the more the alleged properties turn out to do no explanatory or predictive work, and that the disputes they generate (did the taste itself change, or only my attitude toward it?) cannot be settled by any possible evidence. I want to take that proposal seriously as a practical matter rather than as a debating position.

Write me a decision procedure my group can actually run, in about twenty minutes per candidate construct, whenever a participant report or a clinician's proposal introduces a purported quale. The procedure should do the following.

1. State the four marks a purported quale must have if it is to be the thick kind Dennett targets — ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly or immediately apprehensible — and give a research assistant a way to tell, from the report alone, whether the construct is being offered with all four marks or only some of them.

2. For each mark, give one question that tests whether the mark is doing any work in the study: whether the alleged feature could ever be detected, compared across occasions or persons, or falsified independently of the participant's say-so.

3. Include a step for what I will call the two-tasters pattern: two participants whose behavior and dispositions agree in every observable respect, but who disagree about whether the experience itself changed or only their attitude toward it. Say what the procedure concludes when no possible observation would adjudicate the dispute, and what it recommends in place of the disputed construct.

4. End with a verdict in one of three forms: keep the construct as an ordinary measurable feature, and say what has been dropped in doing so; replace it with a dispositional or report-based redescription; or flag it as a claim the study cannot use as a dependent variable and must record verbatim without reification.

5. Mark the points where the procedure cannot decide, and say who should decide — the participant, the clinician, or the principal investigator — and on what grounds.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a tinnitus study in which the participant reports an ineffable quality of the sound distinct from its loudness and its distressfulness; (b) a wine-tasting study in which a change of label changes reported pleasantness, but participants insist the taste itself is unchanged; and (c) a proposal from a psychiatrist colleague to measure the felt quality of depression as a primary outcome. At least one of the three should come out in a way my group will not like; say which one, and why.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, what it will wrongly discard, and whether there is anything the participants are right about that the procedure has no way to record.
