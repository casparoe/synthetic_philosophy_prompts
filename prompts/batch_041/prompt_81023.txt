I am preparing a paper on whether religion makes people better, and one analogy keeps doing the heavy lifting in the material I am reading; a colleague restated it for me in a seminar as follows. Religion is to morality what scaffolding is to a building. It was erected alongside the moral life while that life was still under construction, it supplied the external bracing that kept the structure upright, and now that the structure stands on its own — secular law, general prosperity, mutual sympathy, the welfare state — the scaffolding can be dismantled without any loss to the building. The moral drawn is that religion did genuinely load-bearing work in earlier centuries, that contemporary secular societies are at least as law-abiding and trusting as religious ones, and hence that religion was a temporary support rather than a foundation, so secular societies can sustain trust unaided.

I want an audit of that analogy rather than a survey of the literature.

First, set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — saying what plays the role of the scaffolding, the building, the foundation, the builders, the construction schedule, and any inspection that would certify the building is now self-supporting, and what each mapping commits the user of the analogy to.

Second, notice that both terms are ambiguous in ways that matter here. Religion can mean a body of beliefs, a community, a repertoire of rituals, a system of supernatural sanctions, or an identity; morality can mean moral motivation, moral knowledge, moral habits, or the mutual trust that makes cooperation possible. Tell me which disambiguations the analogy needs, whether it works equally well across them, and whether the argument slides quietly between them. Then locate the breaks in the analogy and separate the ones that matter for the conclusion from the merely picturesque ones — in particular, whether anything in the source corresponds to the fact that scaffolding is temporary by design and removed on purpose, and whether we have any analogue of an inspection certifying that the building can bear its own load.

Third, say what more moral would have to mean for any of this to be assessable at all — in-group versus out-group prosociality, self-report versus behaviour, virtue versus rule-compliance, private conduct versus public — and whether the analogy depends on an equivocation there.

Finally, give me a verdict: does the analogy support the conclusion it is being used to support, or is the conclusion riding on the picture rather than on anything the picture establishes? If a better analogy is available, supply it and say what it does better.

Take a definite position rather than laying out options for me to choose among, and begin your reply with your bottom line in the first sentence, with the reasoning after — no preamble and no restating of my question.
