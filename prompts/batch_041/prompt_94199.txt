I'm building the take-home section of my grad seminar final on consequentialism and I need one long essay question on cluelessness. The problem: every year half the class just reheats the standard "we can't know the remote consequences so the theory demands impossible knowledge" move, and I get thirty versions of the same essay. I want a question that forces them to actually distinguish versions of the problem and then defend a position, and that they can't answer by reciting the famous framing.

Here's the crux I want it built around. Make them pull apart (a) the weak/epistemic reading — we simply don't know the far consequences — from (b) a stronger reading where the total consequences of an act are open-ended or incommensurable in a way that makes "the total value of the act" not even well-defined. Then make them say which of these is the real threat to act-consequentialism's *criterion of rightness* as opposed to just its *decision procedure*, because that distinction is where they usually hand-wave.

Then the twist. Give them a proposed fix — I'm calling it the Local Fix, invented for the exam, don't worry about who's defended it — which says: maximize expected value over the foreseeable consequences, and treat the unforeseeable tail as symmetric noise that washes out over enough cases. They have to evaluate the Local Fix. A good answer should notice it quietly presupposes a probability distribution over an open-ended consequence space, and that "symmetric noise washes out" is false the moment the tail is fat or a single speculative consequence (a distant catastrophe, some butterfly-effect utopia) can dominate the sum. I'd like a decent answer to also bring in the Pascalian worry that any act might get dominated by some vanishing-probability astronomically-large outcome, and say whether that's a genuine parallel or a cheap one.

For the deliverable: write me the exam question itself (aimed at ~90 minutes, closed book, no citations required — they should show the moves, not the bibliography), a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme that lists the specific must-hit points with marks out of 25, plus for each point a one-liner on how candidates typically blow it. The difficulty should come entirely from getting the distinctions and the evaluation of the Local Fix right, so a student who's memorized the topic but never thought about it should land around a C.
