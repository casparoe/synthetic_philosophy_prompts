I teach a 300-level seminar on decision theory and risk — 18 students, one 75-minute session — and I want to blow the whole hour on Pascal's mugging. Not explain it; make them feel it. The structure of the thing is easy to state (Yudkowsky coined the name on his blog, Bostrom later wrote it up as a little dialogue, but don't bother citing either — I just want the skeleton), so the whole problem is that stating it takes four minutes and then everyone nods and nothing lands. Design me a classroom game instead.

The one thing I care about them hitting: any nonzero credence, however tiny, plus a mugger who can scale the stakes, swamps everything — so there's no probability small enough to justify saying no, and the only way to hold the line is a rule that just ignores either probabilities or stakes. I want that to happen to them in the room, not get asserted at them afterwards.

I half-designed something and ran out of time on it; steal it, fix it, or throw it out if you've got better mechanics:

1. Credence first. Before anyone hears the numbers, each student writes their own subjective probability that "the threat is real" on an index card and seals it in an envelope. Then the numbers get revealed. Then they have to actually act. I want them to discover that their own sealed number, whatever it was, doesn't rescue them.

2. Escalation. Open with a trivial mugging (hand over a point or lose ten), then escalate the stakes round by round, including at least one round where the payoff is something they can't put a finite number on. At some point, stop and have them each write down the probability at which they'd stop paying — then have the mugger scale the stakes to whatever number makes the expected value cross their cost. They should end up unable to defend their line except by something that either ignores probability entirely or caps the stakes.

3. Many muggers. Hand out mugger cards so all 18 of them can make demands of each other, with a fixed point budget per person. The demands won't be satisfiable at once. I'd rather they feel the incoherence than hear about it.

4. A reward variant, not only threats, so the Pascal's-wager shadow shows up and they notice it's the structure doing the work, not the menacing.

Keep it a point/candy economy — no real money, but the points should be worth something small and real enough that people get annoyed (last slice of pizza, a couple bucks, something pooled). I'm fine with the mugger being me, or rotating, or a scripted role.

Give me the full thing: rules, materials, the exact numbers for the escalation, a payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and roles. Then the debrief — map what actually happened in the room onto the positions people take on this (the committed expected-value maximizer, the one who sets a probability floor and can't say where it comes from, the bounded-utility move, the person who refuses to assign a number at all, and the pragmatic "I don't care, I'm not paying" response), but don't make me hand them a reading list to do it. Tell me what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it and what to do when it does. And be honest with me about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself — I'm already suspicious that play-points-in-a-room won't produce the real vertigo, and that the exploitability/Dutch-book angle only shows up if the many-muggers round works.
