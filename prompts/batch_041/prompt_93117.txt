Im in a long-running argument with a friend about whether Camus's novel and Camus's anti-death-penalty essay can both be right, and I want a real critical reading rather than the usual absurdism-101 version. Here's the setup. The essay — the one against capital punishment that ended up in Resistance, Rebellion, and Death — runs roughly like this: the state killing a man it has already chained up is worse than the crime it is answering, because it is done cold, in advance, with the whole apparatus of deliberation behind it; the condemned man dies twice, once when the sentence is pronounced and again for real; the guillotine was sold to the public as a clean, clinical, humane machine, and that sales pitch is itself part of the horror; and no man is in a position to pass an absolute judgement on another. Now put that beside the novel. Meursault is tried for a murder he did commit, but what actually gets him killed is that he did not weep at his mother's funeral, went to a movie with a girl the next day, and will not say the words the court wants him to say. Then in the last pages, after the chaplain, he opens to the indifference of the world, calls himself happy, and wants a crowd at his execution to greet him with howls of hate.

I have been going round in circles on this alot, so please actually commit to some positions. The essay is against a practice — an institution, a procedure, something a state does on a schedule to a person — while the novel is about a consciousness. Do those two even speak to each other, or is Camus the novelist quietly answering a different question than Camus the essayist? And does the ending sabotage the essay: if Meursault is genuinely happy and the world is genuinely indifferent, what exactly is the harm the essay is complaining about? A hostile reader could say the novel concedes the executioner's premise, that some people are already outside the human community — Meursault, and the Arab who never gets a name — so killing them costs nothing. I want you to either defend Camus against that or admit it lands. Related: the essay says nobody can judge another absolutely, but the narrator judges the court, the chaplain and the spectators with total cold certainty. Is that a contradiction in Camus or is it the point? And is the trial scene the essay's argument in dramatic form, or does the novel make the death penalty feel like fate and aesthetic closure in a way the essay would hate?

Then tell me which single scene I should put in front of someone who thinks the book is only about the absurd, to show them it is also a book about the machinery of killing, and finish with what the novel shows about the death penalty that the argument on its own cannot.
