I serve on the clinical ethics committee at a teaching hospital, and I am trying to predict how the committee will decide a case next week, or at least what it should decide if it wants to remain consistent with its own record. Our minutes record outcomes and brief rationales, not a settled rule. Over the past three years we have ruled on five advance-directive cases.

First, a patient with early Alzheimer's disease, still legally competent, executed a directive refusing intubation and antibiotics once she no longer recognized her family. Two years later, in moderate dementia and no longer recognizing them, she repeatedly asked for everything to be done. The committee authorized comfort measures only, reasoning that the earlier competent self had authority over the later self and that the later request was not an authentic expression of her values because the dementia had altered them.

Second, a patient with severe treatment-resistant depression, competent, signed a Ulysses-style directive refusing electroconvulsive therapy if he later refused it while depressed. When he later refused, the committee overrode the directive and permitted treatment. It reasoned that the later refusal was a product of the very illness the directive was meant to guard against, and that honoring the directive would defeat the earlier self's deeper purpose.

Third, a patient with progressive neuromuscular disease, competent, wrote a directive refusing mechanical ventilation once he lost the ability to speak. Later, still able to communicate by eye gaze, he asked to be ventilated. The committee honored the later request. It said the directive's scope was unclear as to whether it applied when he could still communicate, and that his later preference was sufficiently settled to count.

Fourth, a patient who had signed a directive refusing blood products later underwent a religious conversion, remained competent, and requested transfusion. The committee honored the later request, reasoning that a competent later self may revoke an advance directive, and that the directive was not irrevocable.

Fifth, a patient with bipolar disorder, competent, signed a directive refusing hospitalization during future manic episodes. During a later manic episode he refused hospitalization. The committee honored the directive, reasoning that the refusal was a symptom of the episode and that the earlier self had anticipated exactly this situation.

Next week's case is a patient with early Huntington's disease who, while still competent, signed a directive refusing artificial nutrition and hydration once he could no longer recognize his family or carry on a conversation. He is now in advanced disease, no longer recognizes them, and cannot converse, but in several lucid intervals he says he wants to live and asks for food and water. He is not competent to revoke the directive. The committee must decide whether to withhold artificial nutrition and hydration as the directive instructs, or to provide it because of his repeated lucid-interval requests.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) identify the candidate rules that fit our earlier rulings; (2) state what each rule predicts for the new case; (3) name the closest analog among the precedents and explain why; (4) give your recommended decision, with the reasons that support it; (5) name a further case that would distinguish the rules, and say what principle we would be adopting if we decide either way.
