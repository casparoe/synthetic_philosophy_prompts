A familiar move in discussions of self-deception is to model it on the deception of one person by another: one party induces a false belief in a second, and a paradox is then supposed to fall out at once, since in the self-directed case the deceiver and the deceived would have to be one and the same, and so would have to know the truth and be ignorant of it simultaneously. I have seen this analogy pressed into service to argue that self-deception, strictly speaking, cannot occur, and that whatever we call by that name must really be something else, such as weakness of will, wishful thinking, or a merely causal drift of belief that no one intends. I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a survey of the literature.

Please lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the deceiver, the deceived, the proposition the deception targets, the evidence, the intention, and the payoff; and mark the slots that the self-directed case leaves empty, if any. Then say where the mapping breaks, and, crucially, which breaks matter for the impossibility conclusion, separating the disanalogies that merely restate the obvious fact that there is only one person from those that genuinely block the transfer of the argument. In particular, consider whether interpersonal deception requires two distinct agents at all, or only a certain structure of informational and motivational asymmetry that a single mind might reproduce; whether deception names a success or merely a project; and whether the deceiver in the ordinary case must occupy a vantage point that the self-deceiver cannot.

Finally, tell me whether the impossibility claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole way. One suggestion I have heard is that self-deception is better likened to a government suppressing one of its own intelligence reports than to one person lying to another. Assess that candidate: does it preserve the asymmetry without importing two agents, and if so, what does it license and what does it still leave unexplained? If you think a better analogy is available, propose it and state plainly what it can and cannot support.
