I'm a second-year student and one of my modules this term is on religion and politics, and our seminar has been stuck on the same arguement for about five weeks now. I honestly can't tell anymore whether people disagree or are just talking past each other, so I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on which country has it right.

The setup: we're comparing how England, Germany, France and the US handle religion and the state. One group reckons France is the only one that's actually done it properly — the state doesn't recognise or fund any religion, religion gets pushed out of public institutions, and everyone's equal because everyone's private. On their view England is a fudge (an established church, bishops sitting in the Lords by right, church schools paid for out of the public purse) and the US is a fudge in the other direction (official non-establishment, but religion all over public life, and it clearly suits the sort of religion that's cheap to run and needs no buildings).

Then there's a group who say laïcité isn't neutral at all. It's definately just one particular settlement — a country with a Catholic history deciding that the properly religious person is the one who keeps it to themselves — and that this quietly penalises people whose religion isn't a private Sunday thing (headscarves being the obvious flashpoint). On their view the American approach is the neutral one, because it just says the state won't pick a winner, rather than the state deciding in advance what real religion looks like.

Then someone always brings up Germany, where the state collects a tax for the churches off the back of a register of who's a member, and says this blows up the whole debate: church tax is neither establishment nor separation, the state is basically the churches' tax collector, and your religion is a line on your payslip. I actually think that's the most interesting thing anyone's said, but it just gets absorbed into the same two camps.

Here's what's odd. Nobody in the room disputes the facts. Nobody thinks France secretly funds mosques or that England has no church schools. Everyone agrees the four countries treat religion differently. And yet five weeks in there's zero movement, and the same four or five people say the same four or five things.

So: is this a verbal dispute, where secularism and neutrality just mean different things to each side and they'd stop fighting if they defined their terms? Is it a real disagreement about values — roughly, whether the aim is to privatise religion or to treat all religions equally, which are two different aims? Is there a hidden empirical question underneath it (does laïcité actually make life worse for minorities, does establishment actually corrupt religion)? Or are the camps answering different questions — one about what's just, one about what keeps the peace? My own hunch, and I'd rather you test it than just agree with it, is that the real split is about whether a state can be neutral about religion at all, and that the two camps differ on that before they differ on anything about France or England.

Given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a different question — or whether we should just accept that it can't be settled, and why.
