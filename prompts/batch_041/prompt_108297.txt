I am a novelist at work on a book that tells one death four times over, and for some months now Kurosawa's Rashomon has been my companion. I would like a reading of the film through Nyāya epistemology, specifically through the pramāṇas — and a reading rather than a summary, faithful in both directions: the film should be allowed to complicate the theory, and the theory to expose what the film is doing.

Let me say at once what I do not want. The film is usually read as a parable of the relativity of truth, and I have come to think that reading is lazy — it converts a precise failure into a vague one. My interest is in the frame story: the woodcutter and the monk sheltering from the rain at the gate, recalling a trial for a stranger. They are not testifying at all. On a strict Nyāya account memory, smṛti, is not a pramāṇa, and the counterfactual reasoning by which the two men try to reconcile the accounts, tarka, is not one either. I want the reading to say what, if anything, is still generating cognition in that frame, and whether the answer is that nothing is.

Here is a hypothesis I would rather have tested than endorsed. I suspect the four accounts do not fail because they contradict one another; they fail because each narrator misclassifies his or her own evidence — reporting as perception, pratyakṣa, what could only have been inference, anumāna, or reporting a wish as a cognition. If that is right, the correct diagnosis is not conflicting testimony but a set of faults of the kind the tradition catalogues under hetvābhāsa: a reason unestablished, a reason that deviates, a reason contradicted by what the court has already accepted. Tell me whether that diagnosis survives contact with the film, and where it does not.

Two further things I would find genuinely useful. First, verbal testimony, śabda, is admitted only from an āpta — a speaker who knows the matter directly and is free of the wish to deceive — and I want to know what the film does to that condition, particularly in the case of the dead samurai speaking through a medium, and whether Nyāya has anything to offer about a speaker who is sincere, well placed, and still wrong. Second, on the Nyāya view the validity of a cognition is established extrinsically, from outside, while each of these narrators treats his own account as self-certifying. Is that the deepest thing the film shows, or am I making the film carry a thesis of mine? I am also curious whether upamāna does any work in the woodcutter's testimony or is simply idle there; I would rather be told it is idle than be handed a place to put it.

Keep the Nyāya recognizably Nyāya rather than dissolving it into a general theory of perspective, and where the film resists the reading, say so plainly instead of smoothing it over. Finally, tell me what would change your verdict: which scene, or which piece of the Nyāya apparatus, would make you abandon the misclassification hypothesis — and where in your own reasoning you would expect that reasoning to fail first.
