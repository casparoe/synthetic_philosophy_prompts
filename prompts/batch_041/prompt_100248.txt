Bit of an odd one, but here goes. I'm giving a little speech around the fire on the last night of a trip next month and I want help writing it.

The trip: four of us drive up to the same lake in Vermont every August, four nights of camping, and we've done it twenty-one years running. This is the last one, because my friend Dev is moving to Melbourne in the fall. I'm the one talking. There'll be six or seven people there — the four of us, Dev's wife, my girlfriend, Marcus's brother — so half the room has never done the drive and has no idea what any of this is about.

Here's the thing I actually want the speech to get right. There are a few parts of this trip that aren't rules and aren't really habits either. We always stop at the same diner outside Rutland on the way up — the Bluebird, which is honestly not good. Nobody ever decided that. Year one we were hungry and it was there, year two it was "the diner," and by year six driving past it would've felt like a crime. Nobody's in charge of it, nobody would be angry if you skipped it, there's no punishment of any kind, but the one time Ryan skipped it for a work call the whole trip felt like it had started in the wrong place. Same with the order of the evening: tarp up, fire, eat, then the same three songs in the same order, badly. And when Dev brought his wife the first time, we all just kind of added a stop at the overlook — nobody agreed to that, it just became part of the trip, and now it's as fixed as the diner.

I half-remember something from a podcast about all this. Some philosopher in the sixties — Lewis? the book might be called Convention, which is such a boring title that I might be making the whole thing up — supposedly said that a convention isn't a rule and isn't a promise. It's just a regularity that everyone follows because everyone expects everyone else to follow it, and it could just as easily have been some other regularity instead. Any diner would've done. And it stays alive only for as long as everyone keeps expecting it — the minute that stops, it's not that someone broke a rule, it's just that the thing isn't there anymore. I don't know if I've got that exactly right, but it's close to what the Bluebird is, and it's what I want the speech to be honest about.

So: write me the speech. Four minutes or so when read aloud, funny in a couple of places, and it has to land both for the three of us who've done every year and for Dev's wife and my girlfriend, who think the diner thing is a bit much — not a lecture at them, not a sentimental blur at us. The hard part is what I can and can't say. I can't tell everyone we'll keep doing this, because I don't think that's true and I don't want to lie at the fire. I can't say the Bluebird matters, because it doesn't, and saying it would be the wrong kind of true. And I don't want to say the trip was sacred or that we owe it to each other to keep it going, because I don't think we do, and pretending otherwise is exactly the thing I'm trying to avoid. Whatever you write has to hold together as something a person would actually say out loud with a beer in their hand, not an essay that happens to have a fire in front of it.

Then, so I can check it before I stand up: after the speech, give me a table, one row for each claim the speech makes about the trip or about the four of us, with these columns, named exactly like this:

- Line: the sentence or phrase from the speech that carries the claim, quoted word for word.
- Claim: what it's saying about the trip, in plain language.
- Kind: exactly one of Convention (only true because everyone expects everyone else to keep it up), Rule (would hold even if nobody expected it), Prediction, or Feeling (not a claim about how things are at all).
- Safe: Yes / No / Only because of the hedge in the line, plus one short clause saying why.

At least six rows, and I want the rows to cover the riskiest lines, not just the easy ones.
