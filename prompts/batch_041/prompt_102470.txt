I recently began using a large language model as a research assistant in my graduate program, mainly to check proofs and to draft code for statistical simulations, and I have been unsettled by how often I can follow, use, and even defend an output whose derivation I could not reconstruct on my own. At a dinner last week, two classmates—neither of whom I agree with consistently—got into an argument about exactly this, and I have been unable to adjudicate it. Ana began with what she called the reconstruction condition: you understand a result only if you can generate or at least independently check the steps that lead to it; otherwise your relation to it is mere conditioned assent. Ben replied that this conflates understanding with derivation. On his view, understanding is a cluster of abilities—using a result in new cases, knowing its scope and limits, recognizing when it has been misapplied—and none of those requires being able to reproduce the proof. He added that most of what any researcher knows is held through a division of cognitive labor; if Ana’s condition were applied consistently, it would disqualify most of science. Ana answered that the comparison fails because a textbook or a colleague can give reasons and can be held answerable for error, whereas the model’s output is opaque and the model itself is not an epistemic agent who can be questioned. Ben said accountability is a social fact, not a condition of understanding, and that we cannot inspect the reasoning of human experts either; we rely on track records and institutions, and the model can be tested in the same way.

Ana then proposed a distinction between reliance and deference: reliance is dependence on a source you can monitor or independently verify, as with a calculator whose arithmetic you can redo; deference is dependence on a source you cannot monitor, as with an oracle. She claimed the erosion of understanding happens specifically when AI shifts us from reliance to deference. Ben replied that the distinction is unstable—you cannot independently verify the calculator’s chip, and for the model you can ask for intermediate steps, run consistency checks, and compare outputs across tools—and that the real question is whether the overall practice is reliable, not whether every step is reconstructible. Ana’s final move was that reconstruction is what enables dissent: if you can only see the model’s fluent output, you lose the capacity to recognize when the consensus it reflects is wrong. Ben said that is an empirical prediction about cognitive effects, not an analysis of understanding, and that the remedy is better checking rather than less dependence.

I have reproduced their exchange as fairly as I can. Please adjudicate it move by move, not just overall: for each of the numbered claims below, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating briefly. (1) Ana’s reconstruction condition; (2) Ben’s ability-cluster account of understanding; (3) Ana’s reason-giving and accountability disanalogy; (4) Ben’s reply that accountability is social and human experts are equally opaque; (5) Ana’s reliance/deference distinction; (6) Ben’s instability objection and his reliability criterion; (7) Ana’s dissent argument; (8) Ben’s empirical-effects reply. Then say what each of them is missing, and give a final verdict on who is more nearly right about the specific question whether AI dependence erodes understanding, and under what conditions. I am not asking which of them I should side with personally; I want an adjudication of the arguments as actually made.
