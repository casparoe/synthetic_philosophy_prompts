I am a sixth-form student and I run the prediction league at my school. I am reasonably comfortable with the mathematics involved: probabilities, expected value, calibration over a run of forecasts, and the idea of a scoring rule that is proper, in the sense that you maximise your expected score by stating your genuine probability rather than hedging towards one half. We score each forecast by squaring the gap between the probability given and what actually happened, though I have also seen a rule that takes minus the logarithm of the probability assigned to the outcome, which punishes confident mistakes far more savagely, and I have never been sure whether the choice between them matters.

I have been reading about a position in epistemology which holds that the only thing that makes a degree of belief good, considered purely as a degree of belief, is how accurate it is, and which then tries to squeeze the standard rules of rationality out of that single thought: that your credences should obey the probability calculus, that you should update by conditionalisation, and so on, on the grounds that any set of credences violating those rules is accuracy-dominated, in that some rival set would score better however the world turns out.

Please restate that whole programme in the vocabulary I actually use — forecasters, questions, outcomes, stated probabilities, scoring rules, expected score, dominance, calibration, hedging, a league table — as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the epistemologists' argument come across cleanly as claims about which score to maximise, and which come across only if you distort them? Which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I am particularly suspicious of the following: in the league the questions and the possible outcomes are fixed by someone else, the score is awarded by me, and a stated probability is a number written on a form rather than a state of mind the forecaster is in. I should like to know whether those differences are incidental or whether they are exactly where the philosophical content lives. Finally, tell me whether the residue is a defect of my forecasting vocabulary — something it cannot express but ought to — or a sign that the original position was less clear than it looked. Please do not treat a claim as translated merely because it can be dressed in scoring language.
