Some years ago I left the law for the writing of historical fiction, and I have now published three novels set in the decades between the wars. I have been asked to write a preface on method, and before I commit anything to print I want my working convictions audited. They are these.

(1) Analogical reasoning about politics and war has no probative force. For any two episodes one cares to compare — Munich and the present crisis, Vietnam and some new intervention, the rising and ruling powers of the so-called Thucydides trap — the shared features and the unshared features are both inexhaustible, and nothing in the episodes themselves marks which set is the one that matters. The selection is made by the analogist, and made in advance of the evidence.

(2) All the same, I hold that historical understanding, and certainly the historical novel, is impossible without such analogies, and that a large part of the novelist's craft consists in finding the earlier episode that will illuminate the later one. I keep a working ledger of such pairings and consult it daily.

(3) The lessons of history are moralizing impositions of hindsight. The same archive yields opposite lessons: the July Crisis of 1914 can be read as teaching that mobilizations and threats spiral past anyone's control, while the 1930s can be read as teaching that an early threat is precisely what averts war. Nothing in the record adjudicates between the two readings.

(4) I am a structuralist about large outcomes. Demographic pressure, geography, and the diffusion of technologies settle the great questions, and the statesmen whose decisions I narrate are, at the scale that matters, along for the ride.

(5) I am committed, in the books themselves, to the moral seriousness of individual choice. My characters' decisions are the entire point of the enterprise, and a novel that treated those decisions as determined would be worthless to me — worse than worthless, a lie about what it is to be a person.

(6) The historical novel's first duty is fidelity to the documentary record: I may invent freely in the gaps, but I may not contradict what is established.

Please audit this set. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what it would cost me to give it. Then tell me which single revision would clear up the most, and what that revision would leave untouched.
