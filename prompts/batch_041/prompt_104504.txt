I have been following the sortition revival on rationalist blogs and have arrived at a set of verdicts I cannot fully square. A criminal jury chosen by lot, with adversarial testing and a judge, seems to me legitimate even though it decides guilt and liberty. A citizens' assembly chosen by stratified lot, paid, given expert testimony, deliberating for a month and then proposing electoral reform that goes to a referendum, also seems legitimate. A constitutional convention selected by lot, with its proposals subject to ratification, likewise seems legitimate. A university selecting among applicants who clear a published academic threshold by lottery seems fair and better than the current arms race. But a legislature selected by lot to pass ordinary legislation and administer the state seems to me a bad idea. A central bank board selected by lot from qualified economists seems worse. A hospital selecting among surgeons who meet a competence threshold by lottery seems unacceptable. A city council selected by lot to run a city for a term seems doubtful, though I waver. My tentative principle is that lot is appropriate when the decision is bounded, one-off or ratifiable, and the required competence is general rather than specialist; it is inappropriate when the task requires sustained accountability or specialised expertise. This seems to save the jury and the assembly, but I cannot get it to deliver both the admissions lottery and the surgeon lottery, since both select among the qualified. It also seems to condemn the city council while I am not sure I want that. Can you state the best principle, or small set of principles, behind these verdicts, say which verdicts it forces me to abandon, and apply it to two further cases: a randomly selected body with power to set the agenda of an elected legislature, and a randomly selected review panel that can veto regulations but not initiate them. Please answer in British English.
