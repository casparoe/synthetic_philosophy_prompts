I'm a TA for an intro philosophy of mind course and the extended mind stuff is up next week (Clark and Chalmers, the notebook guy Otto, the parity principle, plus the usual objections about coupling vs constitution and cognitive bloat, and Clark's later add-ons about the resource being reliably there and endorsed without deliberation). Every year the section goes the same way: half the room insists the notebook is just a tool, the other half insists its part of his memory, and we never get past it. So I want to open with a piece of fiction to make the disagreement sharper. I'm using His Dark Materials, the Pullman trilogy (some of them will only know the TV version, thats fine).

In case you're hazy on it: in that world every person has a dæmon, an animal that's part of them, that talks back and argues with them. Kids' dæmons change shape and then settle into one form at puberty. Touching someone else's dæmon is a taboo; witches can send theirs off alone for miles. And the awful bit, intercision, is where the General Oblation Board cuts the bond between a child and their dæmon, and the kid comes back hollowed out -- alive, but not really there.

What I want is a reading of the books through the extended mind thesis, not a plot summary with some philosophy sprinkled on top. Decide things rather than survey them:

- Is a dæmon an extended mind in Clark and Chalmers's sense, or does the case actually break the thesis? If it breaks it, which version does it break?
- Intercision looks like a natural experiment we can't run on ourselves: sever the external part and see what's left. Does it show the dæmon was constitutive of the mind, or only that it was part of the body? And does that distinction survive contact with the books?
- Does the thesis need some condition about the coupling being contingent? Otto could have used a different notebook, or none; a dæmon is not optional and not replaceable. Difference in kind, or only in degree?
- Clark's picture is of minds leaking out invisibly into notebooks and phones. Pullman makes the boundary of the person visible and public, and makes touching it a violation. Does that taboo track anything real about the ethics of other people's extensions? There's an argument floating around that if your mind extends to your phone then grabbing your phone is more like grabbing your brain than your property -- does Pullman dramatize that intuition, or something else?
- Be honest about where the reading fails. Dæmons are metaphysical souls, so importing them into a naturalistic thesis may just be cheating. If you think the comparison is a category error, say so, and say what survives anyway.
- Finish by saying what the books add here that the arguments don't have on their own.

Then, after the reading, give me a table with these columns: Feature or scene (name it); What the extended mind thesis predicts about it; What Pullman actually shows; Verdict (supports / complicates / undercuts); Why, in one sentence. Six to eight rows, no more. And if I've got a detail of the books wrong, flag it and carry on with the corrected version.
