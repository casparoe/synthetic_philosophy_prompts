My opening move. Stipulate everything the theorem needs: we share a prior over the question at issue, we are both Bayesians who condition correctly on our private information, we are both rational, and we each know all of this. What remains is common knowledge of our posteriors. So write them up: I put 0.3 on the board, you put 0.7, we each read both, we each watch the other read both, and we keep going as long as you like. No finite run of that yields common knowledge; the hierarchy never closes. It follows that the antecedent is never actually satisfied by two people, so the theorem seems to have no bearing on the disagreement we are having at this moment — and the retreat to nearly-common knowledge looks worse, since it is often said that arbitrarily small gaps in common knowledge can flip outcomes outright. Where, then, does your theorem bite on the case in front of us? If the answer is that it is an idealization like any other, say so plainly, and tell me what an idealization that no rational agent can occupy is supposed to constrain.
