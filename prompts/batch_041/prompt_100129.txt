Ok, I need help building something and I'm not even sure it's the right shape. I run a tiny deep-uncertainty desk for a government department - three of us, and we get pulled in maybe once every couple months when a decision is too big or too weird for the normal cost-benefit people. The pattern is always the same: the decision is basically one-shot (we can't run a pilot and learn), the stakes are high and some outcomes are hard or impossible to undo, and when we ask the expert panel for probabilities they won't give us numbers. They give ranges, or a handful of scenarios with weights, or they just refuse. So I end up holding a big set of probability distributions instead of one, and then everyone argues about what to do with it.

I don't have a stats PhD and neither does anyone who has to sign off on this, so what I actually want from you is a procedure my team can run - a checklist, a flowchart in words, a set of tests in order - for turning a set of probabilites into a defensible recommendation. Not a lecture on the theory, a thing I can hand a new hire on their first day.

Some constraints I think matter:

- It has to seperate two things I keep treating as the same: a set that's wide because the experts genuinely don't know, vs. a set that's wide because two experts flatly disagree with each other. I suspect those want different handling and I've been merging them.

- It has to deal with pooling. I've got 5-7 overlapping ranges and I know averaging them shrinks the spread in a way that feels like cheating. The procedure needs to tell me when I'm allowed to narrow the set and when narrowing it is the sin.

- It has to handle the case where a variable that should be irrelevant to my decision nonetheless makes my answer about the thing I care about less precise. I saw this happen in a toy model and it broke my brain - adding information made me more uncertain about the target - and I want a step that flags it if it's a real phenomenon and not just me being confused.

- It should use the actual decision rules that exist for this kind of thing (maximin, the regret-based one, the one where an option counts as fine if it's best under at least one distribution in the set, and whatever else is in the toolbox) and be honest that they can disagree and rank the options differently. Don't hide that behind picking one rule for me. I want the procedure to say which rule to use when, or else to output a set of defensible options instead of a single pick.

- Related: our decisions are one-shot but they recur. I've read that if you just keep choosing whatever's admissible from the set, someone can string your choices together into a sequence that loses you money for sure. If that's real, the procedure should say what it means for a team that keeps making these calls, and whether it's a reason to narrow to a single rule.

- It has to mark clearly where it stops being a calculation and starts being a value judgement I should escalate to the minister rather than decide myself. I'd rather the procedure have a big honest hole in it than paper that over.

Then run it, start to finish, on two cases:

(a) pre-emptive release of a stockpiled countermeasure. The panel broadly agrees it works, but the range on the rate of serious side effects is enormous, and it's mostly two camps disagreeing, not noise.

(b) a cheap, reversible mitigation that almost certainly does nothing but has a small chance of helping alot. I have a suspicion the procedure is going to tell me to do (b), and I already hate that, so if that's where it lands don't soften it - tell me straight, and tell me what it reveals about the rule I picked.

Also tell me what the procedure can't do, and name the failure mode I'm most likely to hit in practice.
