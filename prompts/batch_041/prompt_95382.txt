I'm a second-year PhD student in ML, and for the last month or so I've been trying to actually understand what's supposed to be *proved* about Occam's razor, because my advisor keeps saying the prior in Solomonoff induction is 'forced' and not a matter of taste, and I definately don't buy it yet. Two people in my lab got into it about this after a reading group and I've been chewing on it since. Ravi said: the whole point of the setup is that you don't choose a prior, it falls out — you enumerate programs, weight each one by 2 to the minus its length, and the Kraft inequality guarantees the weights sum to something you can normalize. So 'prefer short hypotheses' isn't a heuristic or a norm, it's just measure: short programs automatically carry most of the probability mass. Jess said that's sleight of hand. She said the weights depend on a choice of universal machine, and the invariance theorem only tells you that two machines' complexities differ by a constant — but that constant is unbounded as you vary the pair, so you can't say the choice doesn't matter, you can only say it doesn't matter asymptotically. Ravi said that's exactly the point, asymptotically is the whole game: the convergence theorem says the mixture converges to the true distribution and does so essentially as fast as any computable predictor can, so whatever you lose to the constant gets eaten. Jess said 'asymptotically' is doing all the work there. For one thing the convergence is only guaranteed if the truth is computable, which is exactly the thing in dispute, so using it to justify induction in the real world begs the question. For another, the prior is uncomputable — nobody can actually run it, and every real approximation is a different measure, so the theorems are about an object no agent can have. And she added: even setting that aside, the 'objective' content is just that you have to pick *some* computable measure, which is an extremely weak constraint — you could pick one that favors long programs and the machinery would be just as happy, with a different constant.

Ravi came back: fine, any universal measure works, but the point is that *any* of them gives you the same asymptotics, so the theory is robust in the way that matters; the alternative is pretending there's no principled reason to prefer simple explanations, which is worse. Jess said 'robust' is a word for 'the constant never washes out on the problems we actually do,' since all human science is small-n and the constant can dominate every comparison you'll ever make. Ravi said that's true but it's not an argument against the framework, it's an argument that finite agents have finite problems. Jess said it's an argument that the framework has been quietly redefined from a theory of induction into a theory of what an infinite ideal agent would do, and the Occam's razor people cite is not that.

I honestly can't tell who's right, and I don't want a summary of the literature or a reading list — I want you to go through the exchange move by move. For each claim (the Kraft-inequality/measure point, the invariance theorem plus the unbounded constant, the asymptotic-convergence defense, the computability-of-the-truth circularity charge, the uncomputability of the prior, the 'any universal measure works' reply, and the small-n objection) tell me whether it's right, wrong, or right-with-a-caveat, and explain why. Then say what both of them are missing, and who has the stronger overall position.
