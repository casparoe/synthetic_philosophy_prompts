I am a novelist, and I am close to finishing a book whose fourth chapter is nothing but documents — a letter, a timetable, a bill of sale, and, the piece I still lack, the prospectus of a small preparatory school to which, in the autumn of 1952, a family entrusts its only son. The school is a strange one, though the document must never say so. Its charter is the paper its founder read out to a learned society, the one from 1950 that proposes replacing one question with another and suggests that instead of programming an adult mind one might build a child's and then educate it. I want the prospectus to take that suggestion literally and institutionally: a school whose entire existence is the literal-minded pursuit of an argument, written up in the flat, self-satisfied, faintly unctuous register of British institutional prose of the period. Nothing in it may sound like science fiction; no jokes about robots, no winks at the reader, no editorial voice, no epigraph, no footnotes. The school must never once notice that anything about it is odd.

Write me roughly nine hundred words, set out under prospectus headings: a headmaster's letter to parents; an admissions note; the curriculum; a section on what the school calls originality; a short and reassuring page on the pupil's interior life; the fees; and the final examination, which the school must describe in its own bland words and never by the founder's name for it, though a reader who knows the paper should recognise the game at once. I want the intake to arrive with nothing in them but wiring and appetite, and the school to speak of its task as organising them; I want a standing rule that a teacher is not to inquire into what a pupil has become, presented as a matter of tact rather than ignorance; and I should like a register of the occasions on which a pupil has surprised its teacher, kept in a bound book and read aloud at prize-giving. The humour has to come from fidelity rather than exaggeration: every absurdity in the prospectus should answer to something the founder actually argued, and a specialist reader ought to be able to point to the sentence in the paper that each policy is descended from. Do not quote the founder and do not put words in his mouth — let the school paraphrase him in its own institutional voice, and let the paraphrase be a little flattering to the school, as institutional paraphrases are. What I am satirising is the habit, which I take to be very much alive, of describing a machine's training as its education and then withholding from the pupil everything an education is supposed to produce — originality, an inner life, the capacity to surprise its teacher — while treating a thought experiment as though it were a syllabus. End on the headmaster's closing paragraph to parents: cheerful, confident, and, on second reading, appalling.
