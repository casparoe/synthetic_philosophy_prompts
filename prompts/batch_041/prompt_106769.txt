For the better part of a month now a friend and I have been arguing about whether historical analogies are instruments of judgment or traps dressed up as arguments, and I have decided I want to end it by getting the specification straight rather than by trading examples. She is a career diplomat and insists that no serious decision is ever taken without one: she reaches for Munich whenever someone floats a settlement with a hostile power, and for Vietnam whenever someone floats an intervention, and she regards the two lessons as equally sound. What bothers me is not that she is wrong about either episode but that the same habit of mind appears to license opposite prescriptions, which makes me suspect that the analogy is doing the persuading and the reasoning is reverse-engineered afterward. The current fashion for reading every great-power rivalry through the Thucydides trap has only sharpened the worry, since that framing presents itself as a structural regularity rather than a rhetorical move. So before either of us cites another precedent, I want the criteria on the table.

Write me the desiderata for a theory of historical analogy — what any adequate account of when an analogy to the past is a legitimate basis for a decision or a claim would have to deliver. I want the cases it must get right, the puzzles it must dissolve or at least own, and the distinctions it cannot collapse, each with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a mere preference. At a minimum I would expect it to say something about how the precedent gets selected in the first place, rather than taking the salient one as given; about how an analogy can serve as a hypothesis-generator without being smuggled in as evidence for the conclusion; about what makes a resemblance structural rather than superficial; about the fact that Munich and Vietnam are each pressed into service for opposite conclusions; and about what happens when a lesson, once learned and acted upon, alters the very base rates it was drawn from, so that the historical sample can no longer validate the projection it was supposed to support.

Flag which of the requirements are non-negotiable and which are really commitments of one camp rather than genuine conditions of adequacy, and — this is the part I most want — name any pair you suspect cannot be satisfied together, above all the pair that would make the whole enterprise look self-defeating. Then say, briefly, which single requirement does the most to rule out the accounts of analogy that people actually hold. Skip the scorecard of named theorists; I will fill that in myself.
