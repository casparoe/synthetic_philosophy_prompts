I'm in a small reading group that's been working through a chapter on natural selection and meaning, and we've ground to a halt on this idea of a free-floating rationale -- a reason for some trait or behaviour that isn't anybody's reason, because nobody's doing the reasoning (I'm fairly sure its Dennett's phrase, from the Darwin book, but our discussion has floated a long way free of the text). The illustration we keep falling back on is the bird that fakes a broken wing to draw a predator off the nest: there's a perfectly good reason for the display, the bird doesn't have that reason, and it still feels like the reason is doing real explanatory work. What I want is a proper explanation of what the view is actually committing itself to, not a restatement of the slogan -- and I need it to settle something, because two people in the group have landed on opposite readings and I suspect they can't both be right.

The first reading says the rationale is just the trait's function in a nicer suit: once you've said the display functions to distract predators, there's nothing left over for the word 'rationale' to add, and all the reason-talk is a façon de parler that's useful for generating hypotheses. The second says no, the whole point is that a rationale is normative in a way a plain function isn't. You can't sensibly say a heart got its job wrong by not beating, but you can say the bird made a mistake if it drops the broken-wing act too early or misreads a grazing cow as a predator -- there's a standard the behaviour can fall short of, and that standard, with no reasoner anywhere in sight, is precisely what the word is for. So is the intended difference between a free-floating rationale and a mere function a difference in normativity, or in something else entirely -- being about something, being inferential, being assessable as means-to-end? And whichever way that comes out, is the rationale supposed to be a feature of the world our descriptions latch onto, or a grid we project? Those same two people also disagree about whether it's legitimate to describe a river's meanders as a free-floating solution to the problem of getting to the sea, and I don't have a principled way to adjudicate.

Then please do something with a case of mine instead of just re-running the usual zoo. A plant whose leaves get chewed starts releasing a volatile blend that summons parasitic wasps, which lay their eggs in the caterpillars. I want to know whether that counts as a free-floating rationale, what exactly is supposed to be floating free, whether the plant can be wrong about it (it puts out the same volatile after a mechanical wound, or when the wind breaks a stem), and what the answers imply about whether the rationale is real or just our description. Somewhere in there I'd also like to know what stops the notion from collapsing into "anything has a rationale for anything," and whether a rationale survives once the benefit is gone -- a trait that's now vestigial but still hanging around.
