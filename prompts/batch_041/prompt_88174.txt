I've just been appointed to the steering committee of my city's first citizens' assembly: a body of roughly a hundred residents, chosen by lot, meeting over four weekends on one question — the order in which we close two of our three river crossings for rebuilding — whose recommendations go to a binding referendum. I have a year, a modest budget, and probably one chance to get the council to approve a design that isn't decorative. Because the format is explicitly borrowed from classical Athens, I've spent the last month with the standard cautionary tales: the assembly of 427 that voted to kill every adult man in Mytilene and enslave the rest, then reconvened the next morning and reversed itself; the Sicilian expedition of 415, authorized by an assembly that had already expanded a modest twenty-ship force into an armada, which ended two years later with the fleet destroyed and the expeditionary force killed or enslaved; and the trial of Socrates in 399, a few years after the restored democracy had sworn an amnesty covering acts done under the oligarchic regime. I've also noticed, reading more closely, that the Socrates case cuts against the usual moral: on the one day he held a seat on the presiding committee he refused to put an illegal motion to a vote, and he later refused an order from the oligarchy to arrest a resident — so the same episode gets cited both for "majority tyranny" and for the value of procedural dissent.

The lessons I keep being offered are these: (1) mass deliberation is impulsive, so build in delay and revote; (2) the real problem was rhetoric and demagogues, so constrain speech or structure it; (3) the real problem was amateurism, so weight expert input; (4) the real problem was that no entrenched law bound the assembly, so add judicial review or supermajority thresholds; (5) the real problem was that decision-makers bore no cost, so align cost-bearing with voting power; (6) the real problem was distance and slow feedback, so restrict the assembly to questions whose consequences it can observe; (7) the real problem was the boundary of the demos — the people deciding were not the people affected — so widen the franchise for the question at hand.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Which of these failure modes should my design actually be built around, ranked, and why? Tell me which of the three Athenian episodes is genuine evidence for which diagnosis, and which of the seven lessons are overrated or simply don't follow from the history they're hung on. I care most about the criteria you're using — upstreamness, tractability for a committee with one year, whether the failure is in the participants or in the surrounding institutions, and whether fixing it would even change outcomes — plus the trade-offs among them, and which single feature you'd spend my one shot on. Finish by saying what evidence over the next year would move something up or down your ranking.
