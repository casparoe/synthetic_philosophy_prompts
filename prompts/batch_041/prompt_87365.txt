I'm in a grad seminar on Bayesian epistemology and I've ended up in an arguement with a friend that neither of us can settle. The thing we disagree about is whether the standard rule for uncertain evidence is actually correct as a general account of rational updating. The rule as I understand it (I can never remember what it's called): sometimes your evidence doesn't make you certain of anything, it just moves your credences over some set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities — say from fifty-fifty to 0.7/0.3 — and in that case you're supposed to take those new numbers as given, hold fixed your conditional probabilities inside each of the possibilities, and recompute everything else from them. My friend thinks that rule just is what rationality is, full stop. I think it's a rule that gets the obvious answers right in the easy cases and then quietly falls apart, and here's the case that made me think so. I'm trying to work out whether my upstairs neighbor is home and I hear a noise through the ceiling; my credence that he's home goes from about 0.5 to about 0.65. But the noise is also evidence about my hearing tonight (the pipes in this building can sound exactly like a door closing), so the very act of hearing it seems to change the conditional probabilities the rule tells me to hold fixed. My friend says that just means I carved the evidence up wrong — put "my hearing is unreliable tonight" into the set of possibilities, recarve, and the rule works fine. I say that's circular: the rule then only ever does the easy half of the job, after you've already guessed the right carving, and different carvings give different answers, so the rule isn't the whole story about rationality, it's a piece of one.

So: what's your credence that the rule, applied to whatever carving the total evidence really determines, is the complete and correct account of what it's rational to do when your evidence is uncertain? I want the number and the machinery behind it. Give me exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up: (1) your credence, as a number between 0 and 1, with the one-line reason; (2)–(4) the three sub-questions your number leans on hardest, each with its own rough probabilty and a line of justification — things like whether an agent can rationally shift credences without becoming certain of anything, whether the carving is uniquely fixed by the evidence, whether the hold-fixed condition is really a requirement of rationality or just a convenience, or whatever else you think belongs; (5) which of those three you're least confident in and what would move it most. Don't hand me a reading list — I'm offline right now and I want your reasoning, not a survey — and don't be polite about it: if you think my neighbor case is just a carving mistake on my part, price that in and give me the real number.
