ok so I've been voluntold to lead next week's session in our consciousness reading group — mixed room, about eight people, half psych/neuro PhD students and half philosophers, nobody who actually works on split-brain stuff, and it's 5pm on a Thursday so half of them will be half asleep and the projector doesn't work reliably. I'm not asking you to explain the topic to me, I've read enough of this literature to have opinions. What I need is a plan for how to actually get it across in 90 minutes. The thing I'm most worried about is the order. What has to land before the question — is the split-brain patient one subject of experience or two — even sounds like a real question to them rather than a spooky one, and what only makes sense once something else is already in place? I have a hunch the session should be built around the fact that 'unity' is doing at least two seperate jobs in these discussions: there's the question whether there is one bearer of the experiences, and there's the question whether the experiences themselves form one stream or field, and I think the empirical results push on those two in quite different ways. If that's the right spine, tell me what the order has to be; if it's the wrong spine, say so and give me the right one.

Then the example. Which single case should carry the whole 90 minutes — and say what it has to have and what it must not have. I was going to use the one where you flash a diffrent picture to each half of the visual field and the patient's hand picks the object that only fits the picture the speaking half didn't see, and then when you ask why, the left hemisphere smoothly makes up a reason that fits the chicken rather than the snow. It's a great demo, but it's almost too neat — it makes the whole thing look like a parlour trick and I worry people walk away thinking the science has settled the metaphysics. I'd also like to bring in the contrast with people who were born without a corpus callosum at all, who as far as I understand mostly don't show the dramatic disconnection picture — if cutting the bridge splits the mind, why doesn't never having a bridge? (Correct me if I've got that wrong, I might be misremembering the clinical picture.) Does that contrast belong in this session, and if so where, or does it just muddy things at this level?

Misconceptions — here's my list, and I want to know which I've got wrong and which I've missed: (a) two minds means two persons, so the patient is literally two people sharing a body; (b) the speaking hemisphere is the real person and the other one is more or less a machine, so this is just a horror story about losing half your self; (c) unity just means being one organism with one brain, so the whole question is verbal — this is the one that killed last year's session, everyone went home satisfied that 'unity' was a word and not a phenomenon; (d) the hemispheres disagreeing is just ordinary inner conflict, like one part of you wanting to stay in bed, so there's nothing special to explain. Where in the sequence do I head each of these off, and are there any I'm creating myself by the way I've set the thing up? Also, what should I openly refuse to go into at this level and just say so out loud? And give me one question to ask them at the end — one that would actually tell me whether they've got it, i.e. whether they can see that the unity question is a real question, rather than just whether they can recite the findings. I don't need readings, the syllabus already has them.
