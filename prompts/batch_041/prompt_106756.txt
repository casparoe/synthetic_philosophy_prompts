I have been asked to lead a single sixty-minute session on the identity of indiscernibles for a mixed group of undergraduates drawn from every faculty — no prior philosophy, no reading, and no second session in which to repair anything. What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

Begin with the order of ideas. Which pieces must be in place before which, so that nothing is asserted before the audience has the equipment to see why it is not trivial — the two Leibnizian principles and the fact that they run in opposite directions, the distinction between a thing's intrinsic features and the relations it bears, and the shift from asking what is true of things in this world to asking what could be true of things in a world we merely describe? Say at which point each piece becomes unavoidable rather than merely useful, and which of them is better postponed to the end as a payoff than smuggled in early.

Then the carrying example. I am tempted to build the session not on Black's two spheres but on something the audience already handles every day: two files on a computer that are byte-for-byte identical, so that any property one has the other has, and yet there are manifestly two of them. Tell me whether that case can bear the weight the spheres were built to bear, what it must have and what it must be stripped of for the parallel to hold — plainly the two copies cannot differ in name, timestamp, or folder if the indiscernibility is to be strict — and where it breaks down. The sharpest place, I think, is this: sitting in this folder, or occupying this block of the disk, looks like a feature of the file, and if it is, the files are discernible and the example proves nothing; if it is only a relation the files stand in, say whether the same move rescues Black's spheres or whether it is available there in a way it is not here. If the spheres should carry the session after all, tell me why, and tell me what the file case is then good for.

Finally, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface, each with the exact point in the sequence at which it should be headed off: that the two principles are one claim read in two directions; that the spheres are two, hence discernible; that a claim whose denial is barely imaginable must be a truth of logic; and that the question is merely whether two things can resemble each other exactly, which everyone already grants, rather than whether such a pair would have to be one thing. Close with the single question I should put to the room that would distinguish a group that has grasped what is at stake from a group that has merely followed an example, and tell me plainly what I should refuse to cover at this level — the quantum case, the apparatus of possible worlds, the substratum and bundle-theory alternatives — and what I lose by refusing.
