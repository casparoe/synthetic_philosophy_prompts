I've been trying to get my head around the fanaticism objection to expected value maximization and I think I've found a way to defuse it, but I want a sanity check before I believe it. As I understand it, the whole problem comes from allowing utility to be unbounded — once you do, some lotteries have infinite or undefined expected value, nothing can be ranked, and the whole apparatus jams. Which makes it look like a technical glitch in the formalism rather than a real objection to the view. So why not just cap utility at something huge but finite, say 10^100 utilons? Then every option has a well-defined expected value, I can compare them and maximize in the ordinary way, and the fanaticism worry dissapears. (Same trick for probabilities, right — I could just ignore anything below 1 in 10^60, and then no tiny-probability case can ever drive a decision.) Does that settle it, or am I missing something? And if capping is the wrong move, how would I even pick the cap — is there a principled way to choose it, or is it just whatever makes the math come out nice? For context I'm a data scientist, not a philosopher, so I'm probably missing something obvious.
