I am a high school student taking a philosophy elective, and I have also been working on a history project about Mostar. In the elective we read the standard Ship of Theseus puzzle, and it struck me that the bridge from my history project is a real, recent, non-hypothetical instance of it, or at least something close enough that the differences matter. The Old Bridge, the Stari Most, was built in the sixteenth century, destroyed in 1993 during the Croat-Bosniak war, and rebuilt between 2001 and 2004, reopening in July of that year. The reconstruction used the same local fine-grained limestone, the same site, and Ottoman construction methods; divers recovered stones from the original bridge out of the riverbed, though most of what they brought up was too damaged to be reused. The area was inscribed on the World Heritage list in 2005 as the Old Bridge Area of the Old City of Mostar. So we have a case where material continuity is partial, formal continuity is nearly complete, functional continuity is complete, and locational continuity is complete.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions, and I would rather have it disciplined than sweeping. First, take the Ship of Theseus puzzle seriously as a theory of persistence and say what it implies about this bridge. Who exactly is the claimant of identity here, the residents, the state, the international heritage body, the historical record, and does the answer change with the claimant, given that the puzzle is usually told with a single owner and a single artifact? Then work through the standard solutions and say which ones actually get traction: spatiotemporal continuity, material continuity, four-dimensionalism, constitution views, sortal-relative identity, closest continuer. Be explicit about what counts as the material, the form, the function, the craft knowledge, and the community's memory of the bridge, and say which of these the puzzle can represent and which it silently ignores. I also want the honest part: where the theory is silent rather than illuminating. And there is a wrinkle I would like handled rather than smoothed over. Heritage conservation has its own doctrine about authenticity and reconstruction, and it changed during exactly this period. The 1964 Venice Charter looked on reconstruction with disfavor, while the 1994 Nara Document on Authenticity, drafted in Japan, insisted that authenticity must be assessed within a culture's own terms, and it was motivated in part by the Japanese practice of periodically dismantling and rebuilding wooden shrine structures. So the conservation world was arguing about the same question, in the same decade, under a different name.

Second, run the analysis the other way: what does this episode show about the Ship of Theseus? I have a suspicion I want tested rather than confirmed. The standard puzzle assumes that the answer is either the continuously repaired object or the reassembled original planks, and that the interesting question is which of the two it is. Mostar is neither. The recovered planks, so to speak, exist but are mostly unusable, and the replacement is deliberately made to be as indistinguishable as possible in form and technique. As a contrast case, take the Ise Shrine in Japan, where the main shrine buildings are rebuilt on an adjacent site roughly every twenty years as a matter of prescribed practice and the deity is transferred in a rite, and where nobody appears to experience this as a puzzle about identity at all. My question is whether Mostar together with Ise shows that the puzzle is underdescribed, that it trades on a parochial assumption that persistence must be traced through matter or shape, or whether it shows something more specific, such as the relativity of identity to a sortal and to a community's interests. Tell me which features of the episode the puzzle genuinely captures, which it misdescribes, and what the case is evidence for. If the best answer is that different solutions win for different claimants, say that explicitly and show the divisions rather than giving me one verdict.
