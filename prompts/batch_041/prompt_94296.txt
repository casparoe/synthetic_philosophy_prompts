I'm a doctoral student in philosophy who has recently taken a job doing cause prioritization for a small grantmaking organization, which means that most of what I now work on has its payoff — if any — decades or centuries out. That has turned the problem of "consequentialist cluelessness" from a seminar curiosity into something personally pressing, and I keep running into it in arguments between two people in my cohort, whom I'll call Dana and Marcus. They went at it again last week over coffee and I genuinely couldn't referee, so I'd like you to do it for me. Please take the role of an expert in both normative ethics and decision theory — someone fluent in the formal literature on choice under uncertainty as well as the practical debates about long-term impact — and adjudicate their exchange move by move. Before you commit to a verdict, brainstorm to yourself what the strongest version of each position is, so that you aren't grading either of them on a weak reading; then tell me, for each move, whether it is right, wrong, or right-but-doesn't-establish-what-it's-used-for, with a short justification. Finish with what, if anything, both of them are missing. I don't want a declaration of an overall winner so much as a careful audit of where each of them is correct.

Here is how it went. Dana opened: consequentialism says to do the act with the best overall consequences, but the causal consequences of any act fan out indefinitely, and a century or two out you can't even specify the relevant state space, let alone assign probabilities. So for any two options the long-run difference isn't merely unknown but unknowable, while the near-term difference is trivial against the total; therefore the theory can't rank acts at all, and any consequentialist who acts is quietly substituting a short horizon, which is arbitrary. Marcus replied with two points: first, that she's sliding between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure, since there can be a fact of the matter about which act has the best consequences even if nobody can compute it, and epistemic limits don't touch the truth of the criterion; second, that the practical difficulty isn't special to consequentialism, because ruling out clueless action would rule out most long-horizon prudence too. Dana answered that the criterion/procedure distinction buys nothing practical and, taken seriously, makes the theory useless as a guide, which was the whole point of having one; and that the standard patch — maximizing expected value — doesn't rescue it, because expected value is only as good as the distribution you feed it, and where we're clueless we have a placeholder rather than a distribution. Multiplying a placeholder by a value and summing over a century yields a number, she said, but the number is an artifact; worse, since the future is enormous and the tail is exactly where we're blindest, the expectation is dominated by the terms we're least entitled to. Marcus countered that this proves too much: by that argument nobody could ever act on long-horizon considerations — not a parent, not a physician, not a pension fund — and we act under acknowledged model error constantly, with paralysis as the only alternative. He added that cluelessness comes in degrees: often the sign of an effect is robust even when its magnitude isn't, so we can identify acts that are better across a wide range of plausible models without saying how much better; and that there's a difference between having no information and having a little, whereas she was treating "we can't be sure" as "we have nothing." Dana came back that the robustness claim is usually an illusion, that the history of interventions is a graveyard of sign flips, and that when a sign really is robust it's typically because one has quietly narrowed to a near-term, local metric — which is just to abandon the total-consequences view for common-sense morality wearing a consequentialist label; so either you're clueless and have no guidance, or you've smuggled in a restriction you can't justify from inside the theory. Marcus closed by saying the smuggling charge assumes the only options are the total view or an arbitrary restriction, when there's a third: reason about what's choice-worthy given our actual evidence and accept a modest, corrigible ranking rather than a divine one, since a criterion doesn't owe us a decision procedure that settles every case, and demanding that it do so imports a requirement from outside. Please work through this carefully and tell me who is right about what.
