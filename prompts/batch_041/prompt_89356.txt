I'm a fifth-year PhD student in political theory with a formal-methods side, and in three weeks I have to present at a joint session of our social choice workshop and the political theory seminar — roughly half economists who work on preference aggregation, half normative theorists who have never proved a theorem, and me in the middle trying to hold both. My paper defends a deflationary reading of Sen's liberal paradox: the theorem is not evidence of a deep tension between liberty and the Pareto principle, I argue, but evidence that a framework which can represent a right only as a pattern of social preference over pairs of alternatives cannot state the liberal claim without distorting it, and that every version of the alleged conflict — including the familiar two-person illustration with the prude and the libertine — is an artifact of that representational choice. My constructive proposal is that rights be modeled as constraints on the agenda, or as moves in a game, rather than as decisiveness over pairs: the theorem survives, but its normative bite does not. I am fairly confident about the diagnosis and much less confident about the proposal, which is why I want a question-period preparation sheet rather than reassurance.

Here is the room I have to survive. Two of the economists work on the theorem itself: one will say, amiably, that my theorem is fine and my interpretation is doing all the work; the other thinks the only live question is whether the liberal claim can be weakened to self-regarding preferences without losing the content of liberty, and will treat my representation-and-agenda line as a way of changing the subject. A third works on game-theoretic representations of rights and will press me on whether my reformulation escapes the impossibility or merely restates it one level up. On the theorists' side: a Rawlsian who holds that rights are trumps and will say that any framework in which a right appears as a constraint on a social ranking has already conceded too much; someone working on republican accounts of non-domination who will ask what my view implies for the legitimacy of a state that must choose among alternatives it cannot rank; and a skeptic about formal methods who will ask whether the paradox is anything more than an artifact of a bad model and whether my paper amounts to saying so at length. A graduate student in the workshop has been reading behavioral work on preference reversals and will ask what work the word "preference" is doing in my argument at all.

What I want from you is that sheet: the eight to ten objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get from a law school or a philosophy of law audience — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does if I fumble it, each phrased the way the person who will raise it would phrase it, with a two-minute reply, a candid note on where that reply is soft and what a good opponent says next, and an explicit note on which objections I should concede preemptively in the talk rather than be forced into conceding under questioning. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, the single objection I should restructure the talk to preempt, and one thing the theorists in the room will be thinking but will not say out loud. Please skip the citations and the advice to engage with the literature — I know the literature, and what I need is argumentative content in the vocabulary this room will actually use. One last thing: a friend in the economics department told me last week that I have simply rediscovered the old complaint about the informational basis of social choice and dressed it up in new clothes, and it stung more than I let on, so I would rather meet that charge head-on than leave it for the theorists to raise at the end.
