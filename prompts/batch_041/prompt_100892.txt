I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and there is a pattern in my own reasoning that I have never seen named, though I suspect it is not peculiar to me.

I try to keep my uncertainty honest. When I do not know whether something will happen — whether a friend will show up, whether a project will land on time, whether a measure will pass — I try to hold a range rather than a point. I tell myself the chance is somewhere between, say, forty and seventy percent, and I try to update the whole range as evidence arrives rather than commit prematurely to a single number.

Then I have to act, and acting wants a number. So I choose one: the low end when the stakes are lopsided, the midpoint when I am being lazy. This is the part I care about. Months later, in an unrelated conversation, I will assert a point estimate with complete confidence — fifty-five percent, say — and I will have no memory of the range I began with or of the arbitrary way I picked the point. The number that my decision rule manufactured has quietly become a belief I take myself to have measured. It gets worse: when new evidence arrives, I update the manufactured point rather than the original range, so the imprecision I started with never comes back, even when the evidence would have warranted it.

I see the same move at the level of groups. Two people who hold the same range still need one number to act together; the number they settle on — by negotiation, or by deferring to whoever sounds most certain — becomes the group's credence and enters the record (minutes, forecasts, dashboards) looking exactly like a measurement, with the range and the negotiation nowhere in it. And I notice the mirror image too: sometimes a belief I had thought was sharp turns out to have been widened by this very awareness, as if I could only ever hold one of precision or honesty about my imprecision, never both at once.

I know the neighborhood of concepts, and none of them is quite this. Dilation is about an interval widening when you learn something that looks irrelevant; that is a change in the interval, driven by evidence, not its replacement by a point driven by a decision. Ambiguity aversion and the Ellsberg-style setup are about preferring not to take the imprecise gamble at all. Anchoring is about an external number tugging at an estimate; here the number is not imported but manufactured by the decision rule and then mistaken for a measurement. The collapse of a set of probabilities to a single value under a maximin-style rule is the mechanism, but the accounts I have found treat that as a property of the rule, not as something that then happens to your beliefs and sticks.

So: is there an established name for this? If there is, I want to know how exactly it differs from the nearest concepts above and which of them fits best. If there is not, coin one, define it, and tell me where its edges are. In particular: does it require that a decision actually be forced, or does merely reporting a summary do the same work? Does it require forgetting, or can I watch myself do it and do it anyway? Is the group version the same phenomenon, or a different one wearing the same costume? And is there a principled way to act on a range that does not manufacture a belief — or is the manufacture unavoidable, so that the only real question is whether I keep the receipt?
