Ok so I teach a seminar on adaptationism and the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique, and every year I get the same thing: students trot out the chin, the male nipple, the panda's thumb, and figure they've cracked it. So for this year's problem set I invented a fictional animal, and I want you to work through it first — partly so I can check the case is actually discriminating, and partly because I'm curious where a model lands.

Here's the creature. Vexilus thalassina, a made-up deep-water crustacean-ish thing. Take it as real for the purposes of the question, and take it as a small population that's been isolated in the same cave system for a few million years. Six features, below. The descriptions are all I'm giving you — some quietly point at a developmental or genetic link, some at history, some at nothing much at all. That's the point.

A. A pair of huge forward-facing eyes, packed with rods, tuned to the blue-green wavelengths its bioluminescent prey emit.
B. A vivid indigo pigment in the carapace. The same enzymatic pathway that makes the pigment is also the only route this animal has for breaking down a toxin in its main food source; the pigment itself appears to do nothing.
C. A thin, whiplike appendage on the head that glows and is waved around to draw prey in. Developmentally it buds off the same tissue that, seperately, becomes the third walking leg.
D. A small stub on the underside that in every individual examined has no nerves, no muscles, and no measurable metabolic cost.
E. A ridge along the back whose height tracks body size tightly across the whole population, but which doesn't seem to do anything on its own. It sits right where two body segments fuse during development.
F. A set of fine hairs on the feeding limbs that filter food particles — cheap to build, clearly used every feeding cycle, and present in the same form across the whole genus.

What I want: sort these six into tiers by how confident you'd be that each is an adaptation (i.e., maintained by selection for its current role) rather than a spandrel, an exaptation, a developmental or phylogenetic byproduct, or drift. Three or four tiers is fine, each feature in one of them, one sentence per feature explaining the placement. If a feature is genuinely ambiguous in a way that matters, say so instead of forcing it into a tier.

And since this is going into a dataset and I don't want to grade you on vibe: tell me how confident you are in the overall sorting, and flag the single placement you're least sure about, with your reason.
