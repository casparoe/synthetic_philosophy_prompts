I teach a seminar on the concept of collapse, cross-listed between philosophy and archaeology, and next week we turn to fiction — partly because the scholarly literature has made the word "collapse" so contested that I want a case where the ambiguity is dramatized rather than argued. We have spent the term on the Late Bronze Age systems collapse, where a network of palace economies, long-distance exchange, and literacy in several scripts came apart at once; on the Maya, where what failed was a particular political and ideological order — divine kingship, the royal courts, the monumental program — while the population, the languages, and much of the agricultural regime persisted; and on Easter Island, where the ecocide narrative is now seriously disputed and it remains unclear how much of the demographic catastrophe belongs to the pre-contact period and how much to European arrival. The recurring lesson has been that "collapse" names at least four different things: demographic decline, loss of political centralization, the failure of an ideological order, and the loss of a system's capacity to reproduce its own complexity — writing, exchange, specialist production. A society can suffer one and not the others, and much of the disagreement in the field is really about which sense is doing the work.

I want to hand my students Emily St. John Mandel's Station Eleven and ask them to read it as a theory of collapse rather than an illustration of one. The novel gives us a pandemic that kills nearly everyone, then refuses the genre's usual satisfactions: we never learn how or why the flu emerged, the collapse itself is largely skipped over, and the book's center of gravity is twenty years later, in a world of small settlements, a travelling Shakespeare company, and a Museum of Civilization assembled in a stranded airport out of the old world's detritus. What I want from you is a reading that takes both the novel and the collapse literature seriously. Which of those four senses does Mandel actually depict, and which does she quietly deny? If the post-pandemic world keeps literacy, Shakespeare, orchestral music, and the memory of credit cards, but loses the networks that made any of them meaningful, is that a collapse or merely a simplification — and does the novel have the conceptual resources to tell the difference, or does it trade on our sentimentality about art? I am also interested in the Museum and the Symphony as rival implicit theories of what a civilization is: one says a civilization is a collection of objects worth preserving, the other that it is a practice that must be performed to go on existing. And I would like you to say where the novel resists the reading. The Prophet's cult suggests that new ideological orders form quickly, which complicates the elegiac tone; the pre-pandemic chapters about Arthur Leander are given equal weight to the post-pandemic ones, which suggests the rupture is less total than the premise implies; and the phrase the book keeps returning to, that survival is insufficient, is one it lifts from an old television episode, which is itself a claim about how culture travels through catastrophe. End by saying what the novel adds to the collapse debate that the archaeological cases cannot — whether it shows us something about continuity and meaning that the resilience literature can only assert. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — as though you were writing me a long letter.
