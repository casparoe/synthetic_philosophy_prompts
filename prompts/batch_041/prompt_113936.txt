Write a mock referee's report, about 500–600 words, on a manuscript submitted to a seventeenth-century learned society. The author, already celebrated for a theory of sovereignty and obedience, claims to have squared the circle and insists that the very same demonstrative method, proceeding from definitions, secures his political conclusions with the certainty of geometry. Take the occasion to be Thomas Hobbes's De corpore (1655), which contained just such a claimed proof of the squaring of the circle—retracted, then replaced by fresh attempts—and the pamphlet war it touched off with John Wallis, Oxford's Savilian Professor of Geometry, a quarrel that ran into the 1670s and drew in the Royal Society, which Hobbes suspected of siding with his adversary and whose experimental philosophy he opposed on principle.

The report should be deadpan and procedural: a weary assessor's numbered findings ending in a recommendation. The humour must come from getting the episode right rather than from sneering at the man. Let the exasperation be institutional—a method advertised as ending all dispute has now produced two decades of it; "demonstration" is being used as a rank one claims rather than a result one earns; each refutation is met by redrawing the definitions; and the author's eminence in politics keeps a settled mathematical question artificially alive. Keep in view what is genuinely at stake: Hobbes wanted to end civil war by making politics demonstrable, so the failure of the demonstrative method is not incidental to the joke but its substance. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the Hobbes–Wallis affair and legible to a reader who has never heard of it, and it should keep the irony stable rather than letting the referee convert or the author repent.
