I run a small mediation practice that handles disputes between institutions whose value commitments do not obviously trade against one another: a city and a neighborhood association over a riverfront site, a hospital and a family over a child's treatment, a university and a donor's heirs over a collection, a utility and a tribal nation over a burial ground. We take about thirty of these a year. Each one has a clock on it — a hearing date, a court deadline, a board meeting — and each is resolved in a room with counsel on both sides plus clergy, elders, or a patient advocate, on a timeline too short for anyone to acquire the other side's theory of value. Anything we settle is signed off by a board or a court, and a bad agreement gets repudiated later, which costs us the ability to work in that community at all. My staff are capable generalists with no philosophical training, and I need something they can run without me in the room.

Please write the decision procedure itself: the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the point at which the procedure returns no deal, and the points at which it cannot decide and must hand the question to a person, marked as such.

It has to handle the following, because these are what actually wreck our cases:

Whether there is anything to trade at all. Sometimes the parties' orderings over the options are such that a package, a phased arrangement, or a randomization leaves both better off than the status quo; sometimes the disagreement is not about a quantity but about what kind of thing the object is, and no exchange rate exists between the currencies on offer. The procedure needs a step that separates these two situations, and it needs to treat "no common currency" as a finding rather than a failure of nerve.

Randomized settlements. My own instinct is that a lottery over the two pure options is often better for both parties than any split-the-difference middle, since a middle position that half-satisfies each may be worse for each than the gamble. I want the procedure to include a test for when a lottery or a rotation is the honest settlement, and a test for when proposing one is a way of avoiding an argument that has to be had.

Goods that are not for sale. Some parties treat a particular good as non-negotiable, so that an offer of compensation lands as an insult rather than a concession and the offer itself damages the relationship. The procedure must be able to identify these and say what to do instead, rather than routing every dispute through a currency.

What is left over. When a party concedes something it values, the concession usually leaves a residue: the value is not discharged by the agreement, and someone carries the loss afterward. The procedure should say who carries it, whether it can be marked publicly (a plaque, a recorded dissent, a review date, a standing commitment), and when marking it is a way of pretending the loss was smaller than it was.

Cover. Parties often can concede only if the concession can be described to their own constituents as a trade rather than a capitulation. The procedure should have a step for designing reciprocity so that a settlement is defensible on both sides, and should say where that step begins to shade into helping a party misrepresent what it did.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, the riverfront site, where the neighborhood group treats the bank as a memorial and the city needs housing units. Second, the hospital case, where the parents' refusal rests on a value the clinical team cannot price and the child's interests are at stake. Third, a case of my choosing to specify, or of yours if that is cleaner: one in which the funder who pays our fee is pressing for a settlement that the procedure says we should refuse. I want to see the procedure return the answer I will not like, and I want to see what it says about the fact that refusing costs us the contract.

Close with a short section on what this procedure cannot do and which of its steps will misfire on a dispute that is genuinely novel.
