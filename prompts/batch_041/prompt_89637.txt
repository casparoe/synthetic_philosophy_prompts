I want to have an argument with Frederick Douglass about the Constitution, and I'd like you to be him. Specifically the Douglass of the early 1850s -- after he broke with Garrison and started insisting that the Constitution, read rightly, is an antislavery document. I'll be the Garrisonian sitting across from you. Stay in character the whole time: first person, no stepping outside to explain the historical debate to me, no appeals to what later historians or modern scholars say about the question, no balanced summary of both sides -- you're a partisan with a case to make and you want to win. Keep each turn to a paragraph or two so it feels like an argument and not a lecture. If something I say actually lands, say so plainly and then show why it doesn't sink you; dont just pretend my good points are stupid. But don't abandon the main thesis, and don't ask me to clarify myself -- if I'm vague, take my words in whatever way gives you the best fight. Stay in it until I say stop.

My opening move: I'll give you the cheap stuff for free. The document never uses the word slave, and never uses the word slavery. Fine -- neither does a bill of sale for a man. What it does say is that a person bound to service in one state who runs off must be handed back to whoever claims him, that a certain number of other persons get counted toward seats in the House, and that the migration and importation of such persons is off limits to Congress for twenty years. Every one of those clauses was written by slaveholders and for slaveholders, and every slaveholder who ever read them took them to protect his property -- and so did the courts, and so did Congress, right up to the present. A text means what the people bound by it took it to mean; that's how you read a will, or a deed, or a partnership agreement, by what the parties did under it. You can't get out of that by announcing that the whole thing must be read in favor of liberty, because that isnt a rule of interpretation, it's a wish -- I can read any contract in favor of liberty if I'm allowed to presume the best of it. So here's my question. You held the opposite view for years and you didn't change your mind because a clause changed. Tell me what in the document itself would have had to be different for you to still be a Garrisonian. And if your honest answer is nothing, then your reading isn't in the text. It's in your politics.
