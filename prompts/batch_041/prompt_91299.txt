I am assembling the conceptual apparatus for a paper on what reliance on AI systems does to a person's understanding, and I have concluded that I cannot adjudicate the accounts on offer until I possess a defensible statement of what an account in this area must do. Please supply that statement: the desiderata a theory of the epistemic consequences of AI reliance must meet, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, together with a clear verdict on which of the requirements are genuine and which are merely the commitments of a camp.

Fix the target first. What I need is an account that can take a single episode — one occasion on which an agent accepts and acts on an output from an AI system — and say whether that episode diminished, preserved, or improved her understanding of the matter, and why any diminution counts as a loss. The account must return a verdict on that episode as it stands, without surveying the agent's entire intellectual biography or waiting on outcomes.

The requirements should at minimum be answerable to these cases, which I take to be the ones any account in the area will be judged by:

1. The radiologist who accepts the system's flag, cannot reconstruct the system's grounds for it, and could not have produced the flag unaided, yet can articulate for herself the features that make the flagged region suspicious.
2. The accountant who, after two years of letting a model draft returns, can no longer catch the class of errors she once caught unaided, although her clients' returns are now more accurate.
3. The mathematician who asks the model for candidate conjectures, discards nearly all of them, and proves the survivor herself.
4. The student who can restate the submitted argument but cannot defend it under questioning, and who believes she understands it.
5. The layperson who relies on the system for a judgment she could not in principle check, and who has good grounds for thinking it more reliable than any alternative available to her.
6. The expert who continues to defer because she has checked the system often enough to have calibrated it, and who would be epistemically worse off trusting her own unaided judgment.
7. A laboratory in which no single member understands the pipeline, though the group's results are sound.

State the desiderata one by one, each with its motivating case, and mark each as a demand on the account's verdicts (a case it must get right) or a constraint on its machinery (a distinction it must draw, a fact it must not contradict). I expect the list to include, at a minimum, something about the difference between an agent's grasp of the answer and her grasp of the dependency structure — why the answer is the answer, and what would have made it otherwise; something about not treating delegation as such as a defect; something about the case in which the agent is simply the worse judge; something about reliance that is unverifiable in principle; something that does not rest on the agent's own introspective report of her understanding; and something that keeps the verdict from collapsing into an assessment of the output's quality. Engage these directly rather than restating them, and add whatever I have omitted.

Two working notions of my own — my labels, not anyone's — should be given a place if they can have one. The first is the erasure test: imagine the system struck from the agent's history and ask what she still has. The second is the credit problem: agents routinely take credit for competences they have not acquired, and others extend that credit to them. Say whether an account can satisfy the rest of the list while accommodating these two, and whether they pull against each other.

Then the part I most want. Flag any pair of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. I have a suspicion about one such pair — the demand that delegation not be treated as a defect, set against the demand that reliance on a model be distinguished from reliance on a colleague, an instrument, or a textbook — but I would rather be told the suspicion is misplaced than have it confirmed. If the pair is genuinely inconsistent, say which member should be weakened and how; if it only looks inconsistent, say what dissolves the appearance. Indicate which single requirement, if granted, excludes the most otherwise plausible accounts, and which requirement is doing the least work and could be dropped without loss. Finally, note where a different purpose for the account would change the list, and give the list as it would stand on the other side of that fork.

No citations, no literature survey, and no attributions by name; if you invoke a recognizable position, characterise it in your own words and mark it as a reconstruction. You may close with a line each on how two or three recognizable families of view fare against the list, described generically. A table is acceptable for the closing comparison, but the weight should fall on the requirements and their interactions.
