The Nuremberg trial of the major German war criminals is usually contested under one of two headings: the founding act of international criminal law, or victors' justice. I want it read instead through Pascal's account of justice and force — the claim that each is worthless without the other, since justice without force is impotent and force without justice tyrannical; the recommendation that the two be joined; and the darker suggestion that, because men could not make what is strong just, they settled for making what is just strong, so that much of what passes for justice is established custom that no one examines precisely because it is established.

Here is the episode, so we need not quibble over facts. The Allies agreed in London in the summer of 1945 on a charter that created the tribunal and fixed its four counts. The charge of crimes against peace — waging aggressive war — had no antecedent statute; the prosecution answered the retroactivity objection by pointing to the interwar pact in which the signatories had renounced war as an instrument of national policy; the defence replied with tu quoque, and with the observation that the court's authority came only from the men who had won. The judgments were followed within a few years by the Genocide Convention and the Universal Declaration, and eventually by a permanent international criminal court; the prohibition on aggression has meanwhile been violated repeatedly, with enforcement blocked by the very body charged with enforcing it.

Now the analysis, in both directions. Fix the roles: who is the just and who the strong; what exactly counts as the force in play (the armies, the occupation, the courtroom itself) and what as the justice (the norm against aggression, the norm against atrocities). Say where the joining of the two succeeds and where it merely appears to — is the retroactivity objection fatal on Pascal's own terms, or is the appeal to the earlier pact exactly the move he recommends, making what is just strong after the fact? Then the recursion problem: a rule forbidding force needs force behind it, and whoever commands that force stands above the rule. Decide whether Nuremberg and its aftermath show this to be fatal to Pascal's pairing or a defect institutions can partly repair, and say what would have to be true for the verdict to go the other way. Finally, what the episode does to the theory: does it confirm the cynical Pascal, for whom law is the victor's self-portrait dressed as principle, or embarrass him by yielding a force-made norm that outlived the force that made it and later bound its makers? And is his doctrine really two doctrines — a descriptive claim about the origin of legal authority and a normative recommendation about joining justice to force — that this episode pulls apart? If some other account of law's authority, Hobbesian or positivist or the natural-law reading the prosecution itself pressed, handles the case better, say which, and why.

I know the history and the jurisprudence; spare me both. What I want is the mapping, the points at which Pascal's framework falls silent, and a verdict on whether it survives the case.
