I am a professor of philosophy preparing a graduate seminar on translation, interpretation, and the identity of works, and I want to sharpen my objections to a view that I find both attractive and difficult. I would like you to play the part of its most resourceful advocate. The view is this: when a literary work is constituted by a formal constraint, such as a lipogram, an acrostic, a fixed syllable count, a rhyme scheme, or a palindrome, a translation that does not preserve that constraint is not a translation of that work but at best a paraphrase or an adaptation. You are to be a committed proponent of that view, not a caricature: you may grant that some semantic drift is inevitable, and you may distinguish constraints that are constitutive of the work from those that are merely ornamental, but you must not concede the central claim. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph of continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Begin directly with your response to my opening move, in character, and do not ask me to clarify the role first.

Here is my opening move. I have invented a short poem for the seminar. It is a lipogram: it avoids the letter e entirely. It is also an acrostic: the first letters of its fourteen lines spell out the name of the poet's dead brother. Now suppose I translate it into a language whose resources make it impossible to satisfy both constraints at once: any rendering that keeps the acrostic must use a word containing e, and any rendering that keeps the lipogram must break the acrostic. I can preserve the acrostic and lose the lipogram, or preserve the lipogram and lose the acrostic, or preserve neither and keep what I take to be the poem's emotional core. You say the constraint is constitutive. But if the constraint is tied to the source language's contingent resources, then preserving it in the target language may require inventing a constraint that the original poet never faced; and if I drop it, I seem to have abandoned the very thing you say makes the work what it is. So which is the translation? Or is the very idea of the same poem across languages incoherent?
