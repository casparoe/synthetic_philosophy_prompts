For the past two years I have taught the second-year undergraduate metaphysics course here, and next Thursday I have ninety minutes on origin essentialism — the claim that a particular person could not have come into existence from any sperm and egg other than the ones from which she in fact came. My thirty students will arrive having read the excerpt in which the claim is defended; they will know what a possible world is, they will have had a few weeks of modal logic, and they will be, as second-year students generally are, suspicious of anything that smells like essentialism. The room has a whiteboard and no projector, and I would rather not spend the hour writing definitions on it. What I want from you is emphatically not an explanation of the thesis; I have taught it before and I can state it in a sentence. What I want is a plan for teaching it: the order in which the components have to be introduced and why that order is forced rather than merely tidy — what must already be in place before the claim even registers as a claim, what can safely be postponed, and what will go wrong if I take the pieces in the order that comes most naturally to me.

I also need the single case that will carry the whole session, and I need to know what that case must have and must lack. My own inclination is to skip the case everyone uses and to build the hour on one I have devised: the very same sperm and the very same egg, but meeting in a dish rather than in a body, at a different hour and in a different country, with a second version in which a single gene is corrected before the fusion takes place. My reasoning is that this will force the students to ask whether the essential origin is the pair of gametes or the entire causal route by which the person was produced, which strikes me as the interesting question. But I suspect the case may already presuppose the distinction I am trying to get them to see, and may quietly hand them the answer. Tell me whether to use it, whether to use it in both versions or only one, and what the alternative should be if it fails — and if you think the standard case is better, say why.

As for the misconceptions, I have my own list and I want yours organized against it. I expect them to conclude that the thesis is genetic — that what is essential to you is your DNA — and to be unable to say why that is a different claim. I expect them to hear it as an epistemic thesis, something about how anyone could ever know which gametes were theirs, rather than a metaphysical one. I expect the regress objection to arrive in some form: if the gametes are essential, why not the meeting of the parents, the migration of the grandparents, and everything else upstream, so that the entire causal history of the world becomes essential to you? And I expect the worry that the thesis makes the past necessary and therefore makes a hash of ordinary claims about contingency and freedom. The one I am most worried about, because I think it is the deepest and the hardest to dislodge once it takes hold, is that they will treat the whole question as one about how we would describe or label a person — that coming from a different sperm and egg will strike them as a difference in our words rather than a difference in the world. For each of these, tell me at which point in the sequence it should be headed off, whether it is better met head-on or dissolved by a distinction I introduce earlier, and which single one, if it survives to the end of the hour, means the session has failed. Then give me one question to put to them in the last ten minutes that would show whether the thesis has actually landed rather than whether they can recite it, and tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level — I am inclined to keep transworld identity, counterpart theory, and the parallel debates about artifacts and natural kinds out of the room entirely, but I want to know whether any of them is load-bearing for the rest. Please write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to a colleague — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold or italicized labels, no tables — and in American English.
