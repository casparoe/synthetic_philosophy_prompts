I'm a third-year philosophy student, and this term I'm taking a seminar on anthropic reasoning and the philosophy of probability. We've just finished a unit on the doomsday argument, and one of the assignments is to write a piece of satire for the seminar's public blog, which runs student papers alongside lighter material. My roommate and I have spent two weeks arguing about whether the argument is even an argument, so I'd like my satire to be fair rather than a hit piece — the kind of thing a specialist reads and thinks, yes, that's exactly the move people make. What I want to write is a self-help column: a deadpan advice column in which a fictional life coach teaches readers to apply the doomsday argument to their own lives — to their careers, their marriages, their five-year plans, their calendars. I want the coach to be entirely sincere and the reasoning to be entirely his own; the comedy should come from watching a real argument get extended, without a single wink, into a program for living. Please take on the role of a philosopher who works on anthropic reasoning and who also writes comic prose, and before you draft anything, spend some time brainstorming the specific technical points you want the piece to land, because I care far more about getting the philosophy right than about the jokes.

The coach's method should be a genuine, teachable procedure with numbered steps, built out of the machinery of the argument as it is actually formulated: you count yourself as a random member of some class of observers — a sample drawn from the set of all humans who will ever live, or all humans who have lived so far, or something narrower — you note your position in that set, and you read off a probability. Two things should be visible to anyone who knows the literature. First, the choice of reference class is doing all the work, and the coach should choose his own class for his own convenience: a broad, flattering class when he wants to reassure the reader, a narrow, unflattering one when he wants to sell something, and he should never notice that he has switched. Second, the argument's force depends on an assumption about what it means to find yourself as one observer among many — the pessimistic assumption that you are a random sample from the observers who exist, and the cheerful rival assumption that your very existence is likelier in a world with more observers to be born — and the coach should use whichever of the two gives him the answer he wants, in the same column, without acknowledging the conflict. Somewhere in there I'd like a passing mention of the objection that the argument, if it worked, would have absurd consequences for other questions — the objection that it proves too much — and the coach should dismiss this objection with a line that accidentally restates it.

For the form: a title, a chirpy italicized tagline, the numbered steps as subheadings, a short testimonial from a satisfied client, and a closing sidebar in which a rival columnist briefly objects and the coach replies. Deadpan throughout — no irony markers, no asides informing the reader that this is absurd, no winking at the audience. The piece should be legible to someone who has never heard of the argument, who ought to be able to follow the logic and see why the coach's advice really does follow from it, and satisfying to someone who has read the literature, who should recognize exactly where he cheats. About 800 words. The columnist, his client, and the rival should all be invented; no real people, no citations, no references to actual books or papers, and no quotations from anything — this is satire, not a literature review. End the column by turning the argument back on the reader, since the coach's method, honestly applied, applies to everyone who reads it.
