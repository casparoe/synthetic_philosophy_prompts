Marcus: A rent cap is just a price ceiling set below the market-clearing rent. Hold the price under where supply meets demand and you get a shortage — fewer units offered than people who want them at that price. Textbook. So it doesn't even help the people it's meant to help.

Dana: That only follows if the market was already sitting at the competitive equilibrium. In most big cities the number of apartments barely moves with the rent — it's zoning and land that limit supply, not the rent level — and a few landlords in a tight market have plenty of pricing power. If supply is basically fixed and landlords were already charging all the traffic would bear, then capping rent mostly moves money from landlords to tenants instead of destroying units. Calling that a "shortage" is smuggling in an assumption.

Marcus: Even with fixed supply it still misallocates. The cheap apartment doesn't go to whoever values it most, it goes to whoever got there first; people hang onto places they'd otherwise leave, like a couple rattling around a three-bedroom after the kids move out; the queue just replaces the price. And anyway it's not right to stop two adults from agreeing on a price for a place to live.

Dana: "Values it most" just means "would pay the most," which isn't how much the home matters to someone — it's how much money they have. Also the scary numbers people quote are usually from the old laws where the rent stuck to the unit forever; the newer ones let it reset once someone moves out.

Marcus: Then you're not really defending rent control, you're defending housing justice and using rent control as the tool. Fine, but you can at least admit it's blunt — condo conversions, black markets, landlords letting buildings rot.
