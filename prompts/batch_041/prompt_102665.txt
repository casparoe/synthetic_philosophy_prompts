I am a novelist at work on a book set in a small, underfunded biosecurity institute — the kind of place where a handful of people with the right credentials and a little lab access could, in principle, do something irreversible before anyone outside noticed. I want to write a scene in which two of my characters, both senior and both entirely competent, argue about a principle rather than about a plan, and I would like you to draft that dialogue for me.

The principle is one my protagonist has arrived at on her own, and it runs roughly as follows: whenever a number of people each have the ability to undertake some consequential action on their own initiative, and each is left to judge that action for herself, the world will tend to get the actions that the most reckless or most extreme among them happens to favor — because the cautious hold still while the heedless move, and so the set of actions actually taken is a biased sample of the judgments on offer. From this she draws a rule of restraint: on matters that are grave and hard to undo, one should not act on one's own assessment at all, but should instead try to conform to what a well-informed group would decide, even when one is privately convinced the group is wrong. Her interlocutor, a colleague who has spent his life around exactly these decisions, thinks the rule is a beautiful piece of reasoning put to a disastrous use. He will want to press her on whether the argument proves too much — whether the same logic condemns the prudent lone actor's restraint just as readily as the reckless one's action — on whether it quietly assumes that everyone's judgment is equally unreliable, and on what becomes of the one person in the room who is actually right.

What I need from you is a genuine exchange, not two monologues taking turns. Let each character take up the other's strongest point and answer it; let the argument move, so that by the end neither of them is standing exactly where she or he began, even if neither concedes. Both should be intelligent, and neither should be a fool or a villain — I want a reader to be able to take either side. Please write the dialogue in American English.
