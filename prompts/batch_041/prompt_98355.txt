Draft the joint statement that two philosophers of probability could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. One party — call her the skeptic — holds that the paradox is a genuine reductio: the principle of indifference is not a principle at all but a license to manufacture whatever answer you like, and the fact that it survives only when the "chance mechanism" is stipulated in advance shows that it is doing no work. The other — call him the invariance theorist — holds that the paradox refutes only the naive formulation; that the correct regimentation of indifference is a requirement of invariance under the symmetries the problem actually exhibits; and that once stated this way the classic cases have determinate answers. Neither is a caricature, and neither is to be attributed to a named historical author; let the arguments speak.

Produce the document the collaboration would yield:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties accept. These should include the setup of the chord problem and the three standard selection methods with their answers (choosing two random endpoints on the circumference, giving 1/3; choosing a random point on a radius and erecting the perpendicular chord, giving 1/2; choosing a random point in the disk as midpoint, giving 1/4), the standard "cube factory" case in which uniform ignorance over a cube's edge length and uniform ignorance over its volume give incompatible probabilities for the same event, and the finite contrast cases where indifference is uncontroversial. Also include the claims they can jointly make about what has gone wrong — for example that bare ignorance does not by itself fix a distribution over a continuum, that a probability assignment presupposes a model of the chance setup, that the paradox is not a dispute about the arithmetic of any one case, and that the existence of the paradox does not by itself show that symmetry reasoning is ever illegitimate. Be careful to separate what both sides really accept from what would be vacuous or one-sided.

2. A precise statement of each remaining disagreement, in wording that the party holding that position would accept as a fair rendering of its own view. At minimum these should cover: whether the paradox is a reductio of the principle or only a demand that it be supplemented; whether a unique, non-arbitrary invariance group can be identified in each of the classic cases, or whether selecting the group is itself an unprincipled choice; and whether two agents who share the same total evidence but parametrize the unknown quantity differently (say, by edge length rather than by volume, or by a ratio rather than its reciprocal) are in the same epistemic situation or genuinely different ones.

3. A list of cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — each with the argument, case, or test both parties agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Include at least these candidates, and add others you think are genuine: (a) whether the scale- and translation-invariance argument that is supposed to single out the 1/2 answer in the chord problem really is unique, or whether a differently but equally plausible physical picture of how the chord is laid down yields a different invariant answer; (b) the cube factory, where the question is whether any structural or physical fact privileges one parametrization over the other; (c) a case in which the chord is generated by an explicit physical device with a known symmetry, where the parties disagree over whether the "right" answer then comes from the physics or from indifference applied to it; (d) the finite contrast, where the question is what it shows that the principle behaves well for finite partitions and badly for continua; and (e) whether a maximum-entropy or reference-prior construction counts as a vindication of the principle or merely relabels the choice it was supposed to make.

4. For each party, a short statement of what it would count as evidence or argument against its own position, and — if you judge it — whether any part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, in which case say exactly which part and why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table whose columns are the crux, each side's predicted verdict, and the observation that would settle it. Do not cite works or authors; give the arguments in your own words.
