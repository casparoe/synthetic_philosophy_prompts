Ok this is a wierd question to be asking a model but here's where I'm at. I was never a politics person — voted, that's it, never put a sign on my lawn. Then last spring my neighbourhood got into a fight over a rezoning (developer wanted to put up a six-storey building on the lot where the old bowling alley was) and I ended up on the steering committee of the group opposing it, and honestly the people in that room did not fit together at all. Two retired guys who listen to talk radio all day. An anarchist kid who wants to abolish the police. A woman who once ran for the Greens. And me, who mostly just didn't want my street torn up. Somehow we were all "the same side," and the people on the other side — the ones who showed up to tell us we were NIMBYs strangling the housing supply — were also a total mess: grad students, a libertarian developer, and a city councillor I'm pretty sure is a communist. The whole thing left me with a half-baked view that I've been chewing on for months, and I want you to try to break it.

The view is this. Left and right is a real distinction, but not because the two sides share beliefs — they don't. It's real the way the difference between sweet and savoury is real: no essence, no sharp line, but it predicts things. What actually holds each side together is (1) a shared set of opponents and (2) a shared sense of what you're allowed to say in the room. And the *function* of the labels is coordination — they're a Schelling point, a way for strangers to find each other fast, which is why they survive even though nobody can define them. On top of that I think the two sides are held together *asymmetrically*: one side is more like a movement with a doctrine, the other more like a coalition of groups who happen to need each other right now. So the mirror-image picture — two comparable blocs, each with its own coherent philosophy — is just wrong.

Things I already suspect count against me, so tell me if I'm right to be worried. (a) You factor-analyze people's attitudes and one big dimension falls out; doesn't that suggest there's a single underlying thing — some general comfort with hierarchy, say — rather than a coordination convention? (b) Ordinary people's issue positions don't cohere much at all; the alignment seems to be something elites do *to* the public, which would make the whole distinction an artifact of who's doing the talking. (c) Maybe it's all downstream of party systems, so in a country with differently-shaped parties the "left" and "right" would carve up completely differently and the labels are just a name for my team.

What I want from you is not "it's complicated, both things are true." Specifically:

- What would show the distinction is *fake* — a projection, a team jersey with nothing inside it? And what would show it's real but *thin*, like sweet vs savoury, as opposed to real and *thick*, like the difference between a mammal and a bird? Those feel like different claims to me and I keep sliding between them.
- Is there any test that separates "one latent factor, e.g. hierarchy-acceptance" from "a coordination convention that happens to generate correlated answers"? From survey data alone I can't see how you'd tell those apart. If they're observationally equivalent, say so, and then tell me whether that's fatal to my view or just makes it untestable — and whether untestable is actually a problem here.
- The asymmetry claim: what would disconfirm it? If it turned out both sides are equally doctrine-driven, or equally coalitional, is my view dead or just dented?
- Does my view predict anything a rival doesn't? Take a genuinely new issue nobody has sorted on yet — selling your own organs, or whether AI should be making planning decisions, something like that. If the sides sort the same way they always do, is that evidence for me or against me? I genuinely can't tell.
- And the one that actually scares me: if "left" and "right" just mean "us" and "them" relative to whoever's speaking — the way "here" and "there" only mean something relative to where you're standing — then maybe the distinction is real in the trivial sense that any us/them is real, and my view quietly collapses into "people form in-groups." How would I tell a real ideological division from plain tribalism, either from the inside or from outside?

Rank these from fatal-if-true down to mildly awkward, and tell me which ones a defender of my view would have to actually concede versus which they'd wave away. Be honest about whether I've built something nothing could refute; I'd rather find that out now than after another year of arguing about it. And I don't want a reading list or a pile of names — I want to know what the smoking gun would look like, what kind of case would settle it, so I'd know it if I saw it.
