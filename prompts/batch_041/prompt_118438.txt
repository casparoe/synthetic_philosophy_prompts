I've definately talked myself into a position I'm not sure I can defend, and I'd like you to try to knock it down. I've been reading about the Great Divergence — the whole 'why did Britain industrialize and not China' thing — and I've ended up thinking the question isn't well posed at all. Roughly: it treats Britain as the baseline and China as the deviation, and the 'and not China' half presupposes China was on the same road and just stalled, when alot of the better work says the two were far more alike than the old story admitted. If they were alike, there's no contrast left to explain. But here's the part I actually care about, because I think it's the real reason: I've got a criterion. I think a contrastive 'why A and not B' is only well posed if you can say, non-circularly, which features of A and B are the relevant ones to hold fixed and which are up for grabs. If you can't, the question is just an invitation to narrate whatever you already found interesting about A. And I don't think anyone can do that here — pick a different set of features to hold fixed and you get a different answer, and nothing in the question tells you which set is the right one.

So play it straight: what would count against me? Not 'historians disagree' — I want the actual defeaters, sorted from the ones that should really move me to the ones that are merely awkward. Where would the question's being well posed actually show up? Is there something about the two economies around 1750-1800 I should expect if it's a genuine contrast and not if it isn't? What does my view predict that the rival view doesn't? Which bits would a defender of the question just flatly deny, and which would they have to concede? And be honest about whether I've made the thing unfalsifiable — if 'well posed' has quietly come to mean 'has an answer I'd like', and I've defined the real answers out of existence, say so, and then tell me whether that's fatal or just the normal cost of a meta-level claim. I'd also like to know how much of this is specific to China and Europe and how much would apply to any 'why here and not there' in history; if I'm just re-describing the problem of the reference class, or of contrast classes generally, tell me that instead of humoring me. The thing that'd move me most, I think, is a case where the contrastive question actually earned its keep — produced a prediction someone could check — so if there is one, lead with it. And if the honest verdict is 'well posed but only in a weaker sense than people want' or 'ill posed, and that's fine, the interesting work is somewhere else', I'd much rather have that than a split-the-difference answer.
