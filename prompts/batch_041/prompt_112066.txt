I am a novelist, and I am three drafts into a book about a hospital that is ranked publicly on patient satisfaction for the first time; the plot turns on what the staff do once the ranking exists. Reading around the subject, I have come to understand Goodhart's law as the claim that any measure, once it becomes a target, will inevitably be corrupted — which is precisely the engine of my story, since the satisfaction scores are gamed, the numbers rise, and the care does not. I had thought this gave my administrators, who are meant to be the cleverest people in the book, a clean solution: choose a measure that no one can manipulate. So in the final act they abandon the surveys and adopt the county's overall mortality rate, an index no single hospital can move, and I had intended to write this as the wise, unsentimental fix. I would like to be told whether that reasoning is sound, because I have no wish to put a piece of counterfeit social science into the mouth of a character who is supposed to be right.

What nags at me is whether "cannot be gamed" and "cannot be distorted" are the same property at all, and whether a measure that nobody can influence is a measure that can still do any work; I suspect my administrators may be solving the wrong problem, and that the more interesting failure is not fraud but something quieter — a sincere reallocation of effort, an indicator that gradually stops tracking the thing it was chosen to track. I have also been using "specification gaming" as though it were simply another name for the same phenomenon, and a friend who works in policy tells me that several quite distinct mechanisms are habitually filed under the one heading and that mine is the crudest of them; she would not elaborate, which is why I am asking. Tell me where my understanding goes wrong, and what my administrators should in fact be worrying about — I would rather my novel's central conceit be defensible than merely quotable.
