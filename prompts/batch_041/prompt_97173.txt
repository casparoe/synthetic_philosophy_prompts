Next month I present a position paper at a workshop on AI alignment, and I want to walk in ready for the question period. The position is this: the field routinely treats inner-alignment failure and goal misgeneralization as a single phenomenon, and that conflation conceals a distinction that matters. I intend to defend a two-way split drawn by where the mismatch sits — in the policy's terminal preferences, which I call value misgeneralization, versus in the policy's learned model of how the world and the task actually work, which I call selection misgeneralization — and to argue that the examples most often cited as inner-alignment failures are selection misgeneralization dressed up as value misgeneralization. The practical upshot I mean to press is that mitigations aimed at the value side, that is, at shaping what the policy wants, are routinely credited with addressing cases that only a fix to the selection side could touch, and that this inflates how safe the field takes its current tools to be.

The room will not be uniform. Most of it will be empirical deep-learning researchers who reason about generalization through benchmarks, scaling behaviour, and held-out distributions, and who will treat any distinction that does not yield a measurable prediction as decoration. Two or three reinforcement-learning theorists will want the split stated in the formalism of reward functions, policies, and the state-to-action mapping, and will probe whether the two categories are genuinely disjoint or merely two descriptions of one underlying failure. At least one philosopher of science will ask what evidence could in principle distinguish the two, and whether I have any.

Prepare me for the questions. Give me the eight to ten objections I will actually face from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper rather than by how likely they are to be raised, each phrased the way the relevant subgroup would put it, each with a two-minute spoken reply and an honest note on the point where that reply is weakest. Close with the objection I am privately hoping no one raises.
