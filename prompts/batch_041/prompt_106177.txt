I'm recently retired — 34 years as a geologist, mostly deep-time stuff — and since I stopped working I've been working my way through philosophy, which has honestly been more fun than the last decade of my career. Anyway, through a chain of favors I've ended up on the board of a small charitable foundation (we give away maybe $2M a year). We just got a big bequest and now we have to decide what to do with it, and the board has split into two camps. One camp wants to fund a program that helps people right now. The other wants to sink the money into something whose payoff is mostly centuries out — think reforesting, or an endowment for a research project that won't bear fruit for 150–200 years.

To even compare those two options we need some kind of discount rate, and I've noticed something that unsettles me: the whole decision basically comes down to whatever number we pick. At 0% the long project wins easily; at 3% it loses and it isn't close. And the number is doing work nobody has really justified — the economist on our board just says "use the market rate," which feels like it's sneaking in an answer to a moral question. I've read enough now to know there's a whole debate about "pure time preference" — whether it's even coherent to care less about someones welfare just because they happen to be born later — and I don't think the board realizes it's making that choice.

We meet in three weeks and I have to circulate a written recommendation before then. Write it for me: recommend a concrete policy for what discount rate — or range, or rule for picking one — the foundation should use when comparing projects with very different time horizons, specific enough that we could actually apply it to this decision and the next ones. Give the reasoning, and give the strongest case for the side you come out against; the person who'll be arguing with me is smart and I don't want to get caught flat. One more thing: I'm not a naif about compounding, so please don't just hand me a rate. I want to know what would have to be true for that rate to be the right one, and where the whole argument is really a value judgment wearing a number's clothes.
