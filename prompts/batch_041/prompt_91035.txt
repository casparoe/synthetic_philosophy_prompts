I am a novelist — the last book I finished was set largely in a relief depot, which is how I ended up as the least qualified person on the board of trustees of a small family foundation that funds famine relief and famine history — and on the fourteenth I have to vote on something the board has been circling for a year: whether the foundation adopts a formal position on what famines are. The draft that will be tabled says, in effect, that famines are political crimes and never natural disasters, and that our grantmaking and our annual appeal should say so. Three of us object, for three different reasons. The emergency physicians and the agronomist on the board insist that weather, blight, and soil really do start these catastrophes, and that a doctrine denying it will make us look like ideologues to the people who run the warehouses. Our solicitor notes that a foundation whose charter is relief and research rather than advocacy, and which has partners inside countries that would take exception to being called criminal, cannot simply print such a sentence in an appeal letter. And one trustee argues that we should have no doctrine at all and should just fund the boring things — early warning, cash transfers, storage. I do not want a survey of the literature; I have read more of it than is good for me, and in any case footnotes do not get votes. I want a memo I can circulate.

The board's confusion, I think, is that we keep collapsing three questions into one. There is the causal question (what actually stopped food from reaching people), the imputational question (who, if anyone, is answerable, and for what), and the remedial question (what our answer should change about what we fund and what we say in public). Please keep them apart, and apply to the imputational question the four-part test I have been arguing for in these meetings, because I want to see whether it survives contact with the cases: foreseeability (was the trajectory of the crisis knowable in time, from information the responsible authority actually had?); capacity (did that authority have the food, money, transport, and administrative reach to blunt it?); substitution (was there a specific alternative course open to the same actors at the same time, not merely a world in which better people held office?); and persistence (once the dying was manifest and known, did they stop, or keep doing it?). My proposal is that a finding of political crime requires all four, while a finding of political failure requires only foreseeability and capacity — and that most of what we loosely call famine crimes are failures, which is a distinction worth the trouble. Then run the three cases we have been shouting about. Bengal in 1943: wartime inflation and the collapse of rural wages, the removal of rice and boats from the coastal districts to deny them to an invader, trade barriers between provinces, priority rations for war workers, a provincial government that never declared a famine — alongside a cyclone, flooding, and rice disease, and a harvest that was not, in the aggregate, catastrophic. Ireland from 1845 to 1852: blight as the trigger; food continuing to leave the country while relief was first withdrawn and then redesigned so that the workhouse was closed to anyone holding more than a quarter acre of land; roughly a million dead and about as many gone. China from 1959 to 1961: procurement quotas set against harvests that local cadres had inflated, labour pulled into backyard steel and irrigation works, communes that forbade private plots, grain still being exported, and what was long officially called a natural calamity, with deaths now estimated in the tens of millions. If you think one of those premises is wrong, say so in a note rather than quietly building on it.

Give me the memo in six titled sections, in this order and no others. Recommendation (about 150 words), opening with the decision itself — adopt, adopt with conditions, or decline — which version of the doctrine you favour, whether it governs our internal analysis, our public language, or both, and the date it takes effect. The test (about 250 words), restating the three questions and the four-part test, sharpened if you can sharpen them. The three cases (about 500 words), a paragraph each, ending with the verdict the test yields — crime, failure, or neither — and one sentence on the weakest joint in your own reasoning. The case against (about 250 words), the strongest version of the objection, written as if by the trustee who holds it rather than as a straw man. Reply (about 200 words), an honest answer to the best of it, conceding whatever must be conceded. And Implementation (about 150 words): what changes in the grant guidelines and the appeal letter, what stays the same, and who does what by the date of the vote. Close with one sentence — a doctrine in a single line that could go on the inside cover of the annual report — and then tell me, in one further sentence, what would have to be true for you to change your recommendation. The two-track question, doctrine for us and different language for the public, is the part I most want your judgment on, so do not leave it to the implementation section to resolve. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list; if a claim cannot be made without one, either drop it or mark it plainly as a premise I should go and check.
