I have been defending a view I call perceptualism about aesthetic value: the aesthetic value of an artwork is entirely determined by its perceptible properties. On this view, a perfect forgery — one indistinguishable from the original in every perceptible respect, under any possible sensory modality or scientific analysis — is aesthetically just as valuable as the original. Its provenance may have historical, sentimental, or monetary value, but not aesthetic value. I am a graduate student in philosophy of art, and I have been arguing for this in a seminar; my interlocutors keep appealing to the "authenticity" intuition, but I want to know what would actually count against the view, not just what makes people uncomfortable.

Please give me a map of the view's exposure. What observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or possible cases would count against perceptualism, and what would have to be true for it to be false? I am especially interested in the following: (a) the case where a perfect forgery is created and then the original is destroyed, so that the forgery is now the only remaining object with those perceptible properties — does its aesthetic value change, and if so, is that a defeater? (b) the case where a work believed to be an original is later discovered to be a forgery — if our aesthetic evaluation changes upon discovery, does that show that aesthetic value is not perceptible-only, or only that our experience of the work changes? (c) the two-worlds case: in world A, a work is painted by a famous artist; in world B, it is a perfect forgery created by a different artist, with identical perceptible properties — if we think the work in world A is more aesthetically valuable, is that a defeater, and can the perceptualist reply that the difference is not aesthetic but historical without trivializing the view? (d) the possibility of aesthetic properties that are not perceptible but are nonetheless aesthetic, such as being original, inventive, expressive of a particular historical moment, or a solution to a problem in the medium — are these aesthetic properties, and if so, does perceptualism collapse? (e) a forgery that is not a copy of any existing work but is an entirely new work created in the style of a famous artist, later discovered to be a forgery, where the forger's intention was not to deceive but to honor the artist — does the intention matter, and what does that show? Also, what does perceptualism predict that its rivals do not, and are those predictions anything more than conceptual?

I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an explicit distinction between evidence that would be decisive and evidence that would merely be awkward. I also want to know what the view's defenders would themselves concede as counting against them, not just what critics say, and which of these defeaters are live rather than merely logically possible. Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the view risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualifications — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Please answer in American English.
