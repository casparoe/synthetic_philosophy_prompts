My closest friend and I have just spent most of an evening arguing about lying, and we ended somewhere I can no longer reconstruct, so I want an outside judgment about what her position actually commits her to. She calls herself a Kantian and is the most consistent one I know: she holds that lying is never permitted, not even to the murderer at the door, and she did not budge when I pressed her with the obvious consequences. What I could not get out of her, and what I need from you, is the principle that generates her verdicts — she kept insisting there is a real difference between a lie and a mere untruth, and whenever I asked her to draw the line she changed the subject.

Her verdicts, as best I can recover them:

- If a murderer asks where his intended victim is hiding, you must not lie to him — though you may refuse to answer, stay silent, or walk away.
- You must not make a promise you intend to break in order to get a loan.
- You must not testify falsely in order to save someone you believe to be innocent.
- You must not tell a friend that a manuscript you think is unpublishable is good.
- You must not deceive a thief about where your money is hidden.
- A doctor who tells a patient that a sugar pill is a powerful drug has lied, even if it helps him.
- Silence, refusal to answer, and changing the subject are permissible in every one of these cases.

The new case: a man who intends your friend harm comes to your door and asks whether she is inside. You know she is upstairs. You do not say anything false to him. You tell him, truthfully, that you have not seen her leave. He concludes that she is not in the house and goes away satisfied.

Give me a definite answer rather than a survey of possibilities. Lead with your verdict on the new case in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. In particular: (1) your best guess at her verdict on the new case, and whether she will say you did something wrong; (2) the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, stated precisely enough that they can be told apart; (3) which of those rules you would bet she is actually running, and why; and (4) the further case, ideally one I can put to her over coffee, that would decisively separate whichever rules you think are still live. Commit to a reading of her — I would rather be told what she thinks and be wrong than be handed a menu.
