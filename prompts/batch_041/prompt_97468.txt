Okay so I'm running a reading group on AI alignment next month — mixed room, a few ML folks, a couple of philosophers, and the running joke is that nobody can actually say what 'the alignment problem' refers to. Half of them insist it's one problem; the other half think 'alignment' is one word doing six jobs at once. I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts people on exactly that question, and I'd like it to be good enough that it doesn't just recapitulate the usual camps.

Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of schools: work out which questions have to be answered before you can even state a position, order them so each branch node is answerable by someone who doesn't already know where it leads, and only then place the named positions on the leaves. Each leaf should carry the position name (or 'unnamed' if nobody actually holds it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Six to eight branch nodes feels about right for a handout.

The distinctions I care most about, since it's my handout:
- whether the position treats the hard part as epistemic (we can't specify the target) or agential (something is optimizing against us);
- whether 'aligned' is a property of a system or of a relationship/history between the system, its operator, and the rest of the world;
- whether the difficulty scales away as capability grows, stays roughly flat, or gets worse;
- whether the position is about a single system you control or about a whole ecosystem of interacting systems and their incentives — this is the one I think people collapse when they shouldn't.

One more thing I keep telling them and want the tree to actually capture: the 'one problem or several' question secretly splits into three — do the difficulties share a root cause, do they share a solution, or do they just share a name? A good tree should be able to tell those apart rather than treating them as the same disagreement.

Please make sure these leaves show up somewhere: the 'it's basically a specification/engineering problem' view; the value-loading view; the containment/control view; the multi-agent coordination view; the 'alignment is really about who owns the training signal and the deployment incentives' structural view; and the deflationary 'there's no single target so the question is ill-posed' view. Also, if the tree naturally forces the outer-vs-inner alignment distinction, seperate them properly; don't just staple inner alignment on as an extra leaf if the branches don't require it.

Use one running case to keep the nodes concrete — say a system that faithfully does exactly what its operator's stated objective literally says, where that objective turned out to be a bad stand-in for what the operator actually wanted — and vary it across the nodes as needed. At the end, list any pairs of leaves the tree can't really seperate and say why, and flag any branch combinations nobody occupies, with a one-line guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Just start with the tree itself — no intro, no 'here's a decision tree' preamble.
