I chair the ethics committee of a community savings and loan cooperative — roughly six thousand members, four branches, a small lending book. I was co-opted onto the committee in the spring after fifteen years in consumer credit, and took the chair last month when my predecessor retired; the subject has become personally pressing because I am now the one who signs the minutes recording our reasons. The board has told us to stop deciding case by case and adopt a written procedure for screening anything the cooperative proposes to offer or to partner on. Four or five proposals reach us each month, each with a rate, a term and a marketing deck, and we have ninety minutes to dispose of them. My committee is largely lay — a retired teacher, a shop steward, a local GP — and there is no economist among us. The regulator expects a documented rationale for anything we decline as well as for anything we approve, so whatever we adopt has to be runnable by people who will never read Aristotle.

That is why I want the procedure built on Aristotle rather than on modern finance ethics. I have read the fifth chapter of Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics and the early chapters of Book I of the Politics closely enough to know what he says: that a shoe has two uses, wearing it and exchanging it, and that the second is not its proper use; that money exists by convention rather than by nature, as a stand-in for need, so that unlike the things it measures it can be abolished by agreement; that acquisition for the household has a natural limit set by the need it serves, whereas acquisition for its own sake has none; that the equality in an exchange is a genuine problem, since things so unlike cannot be commensurate, and money is only the conventional measure of the need that makes them so; and that lending at interest he treats as the clearest case of gain contrary to nature, since money is not meant to breed — though the word for interest in his language is also the word for offspring.

What I need from you is a decision procedure my committee can actually run: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a refusal, and where it must hand the question back to the committee because the issue is one of values rather than of facts. Keep what Aristotle actually holds and drop what will not survive being turned into a form; tell me which of his distinctions must be preserved intact and which I can safely lose; and mark clearly any step you add that he did not supply, because I shall have to defend the whole thing to a board that will ask. I do not need citations or a reading list — I need the procedure.

Run it on three proposals. First, a time-bank pilot: hours exchanged between members with no money changing hands, plus a small administrative fee to the cooperative. Second, a rent-to-own arrangement with a national retailer for washing machines and cookers, which I am uneasy about because it is uncomfortably close to what I used to sell. Third, the case I do not want you to be kind about: our core business, members' savings on deposit lent out at a rate that pays them their annual dividend. If the procedure condemns that, say so plainly, and say what follows for us. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do — the cases it will get wrong, and the points at which it will merely launder a decision that is really ours.

Please write it in British English, as I shall be circulating it to the committee and the board.
