I'm a junior and the data editor of my school newspaper, and before we print any story that compares two groups our faculty adviser wants the comparison to have passed some kind of check; at the moment the check is just her reading it the night before we go to press. Two submissions came in this month: one argues that our accelerated math track is harming students, since the district's own figures show accelerated students passing the AP Calculus exam at a lower rate than students in the regular track, and another argues that a new peer-tutoring program is useless, since students who attended tutoring scored lower on the final than students who did not. I have a spreadsheet, an hour, and one introductory statistics course behind me. I have heard reversals like this called Simpson's paradox, but I do not know what that name is supposed to tell me to do. Please write a procedure I can actually run on a comparison like these: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and which comparisons I may print as they stand, which I must report split into subgroups, and which I must decline to print in either form. It should be honest about the point where the arithmetic stops and a causal assumption begins, and hand that decision to my adviser instead of pretending to settle it. Then run it on both submissions, including whichever verdict we will not want, and address the case it will get wrong: last year we printed a reversal story and the district's analyst said our split was invalid because we had split on something that was itself a result of the thing we were comparing. Also say plainly what the procedure cannot do.
