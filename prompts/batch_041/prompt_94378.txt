Here is the position I want restated. There's a family of arguments that goes roughly like this. Some harms — climate change, factory farming, the outcome of an election — are produced by very large numbers of people each doing something small. Take any single person: their individual action makes no difference to whether the harm occurs. If I skip this one drive, or this one burger, or this one vote, the aggregate outcome is unchanged; the emissions total, the number of animals raised, the election result all come out the same. So it looks as though my individual action is not a cause of the harm, and if being wrong requires causing harm, my action isn't wrong. Call that the inefficacy objection.

The responses I've encountered: (a) even if my action isn't necessary, it's part of a set of actions that is jointly sufficient, so I'm still a cause in some weaker sense; (b) I'm not a cause of the harm, but I'm complicit in it, or I share responsibility as a participant in a practice rather than because of my marginal effect; (c) the right question is what would happen if everyone, or enough people, did as I do; (d) the harm is imperceptible at the scale of one person, and treating imperceptible effects as no effects is a mistake; (e) what's wrong is the pattern or rule I'm acting on, or the expressive meaning of the act, not its causal upshot; (f) my responsibility should be some share of the total, proportional to my contribution. I've also heard that some of these are really answers to different questions — one about causation, one about blame, one about permissibility — and that people talk past each other because of that.
