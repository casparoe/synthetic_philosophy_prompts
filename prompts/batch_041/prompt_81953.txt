In a graduate seminar on Nietzsche's essay on history — the second of the Untimely Meditations — a classmate kept returning to the claim that you can't judge the past by present standards. He produced it whenever anyone praised Nietzsche's "critical" mode of history, on the grounds that critical history drags the past before a tribunal and condemns it, which he said was anachronism dressed up as philosophy. He was using the line as a conversation-stopper, and I should like to reopen the conversation without first having to concede that he is obviously right.

I am not asking whether he is right in general. I am asking what that one sentence could actually be claiming. It seems to slide between several quite different things: a point about how we can understand past agents at all; a rule about what historians ought to do as a matter of professional method; a claim about whether past agents can be blamed for what they could not have known; a moral-relativist claim that values are simply indexed to their epoch; and perhaps a political warning about instrumentalising the past for present purposes. Please set these out as a menu of distinct readings, running from the near-tautological through the genuinely contentious to the ones that are, I suspect, simply false; say for each whether it is plausible on its own terms; and then say which of them, if any, would actually deliver my classmate his conclusion that Nietzsche's critical history is illegitimate. Where a reading is doing real work, be precise about what kind of judgement it forbids, and about who is forbidden from making it — the past judging itself, us judging the past, or the discipline of history as such.

Finally, say whether Nietzsche's critical history is even the sort of thing the slogan is aimed at, given that it appears addressed to a community's relation to its own inherited past rather than to the moral scorekeeping of long-dead strangers.

Please write the answer in American English.
