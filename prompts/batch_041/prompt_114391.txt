I am a novelist, and I am building a book around a character whose motives must remain genuinely undecided for two hundred pages and then be settled, for the reader, in a single late scene. I would rather engineer that case than arrive at it by feel, so I have been reading about inverse reinforcement learning, the project of recovering an agent's preferences from her behavior alone, and I want a thought experiment of exactly that shape: two preference hypotheses that fit every event I have written so far and come apart in one scene still to come. What the case is for is deciding whether such a scene can be an honest revelation or whether it will read as a cheat, so the specifications are the exercise.

1. Contemporary and realistic, domestic or small in scale: a marriage, a household, a family business, a long friendship. Four or five people at most. No institutions doing the work, no technology, and no one who is a philosopher or an economist.
2. The hypotheses must concern what she wants, not what she knows or believes. Hold her information, her beliefs, and her circumstances fixed across both, and demonstrate that you have. If the case needs her to know something under one hypothesis and not the other, it has failed.
3. They must differ in kind, not degree: different features of the world carrying the weight, not one concern weighted more or less.
4. Both must be live readings of a person, the sort of thing an attentive friend might believe of her. If one of them is monstrous, the case is dead.
5. I want a particular structure in the difference between them. The two preference structures should assign value to situations in a way that cancels out along any single course of action, so that the difference is invisible in what she does so long as she does it well. My guess is that this makes the two observationally equivalent for a perfectly optimizing agent and that the equivalence breaks only when she falls short of perfect optimization. If that is not the right way to get what I want, say so and give me the structure that is; I care about the effect, not the route.
6. Accordingly the scene must turn on the shape of her situation and the limits of her deliberation, never on what she is offered. No escalation of stakes, no larger sum, no greater sacrifice, no new information, and no one drunk, ill, coerced, or out of character. Change the shape of her circumstances, not their severity.
7. The two hypotheses must share a single account of her imperfection: the same noise, the same lapses, the same ceiling on her deliberation. If the divergence requires the two to be imperfect in different ways, the case fails.
8. Every earlier event must be predicted by both, and I want that checked event by event, including the ones that look most diagnostic: the renunciation, the sacrifice, the moment of apparent cruelty.
9. In the final scene the two must predict different observable behavior, different actions rather than different readings of one action. If both can accommodate whatever she does, the scene settles nothing.
10. The scene must be writable as ordinary prose, a page or less, with no confession, no diary, no omniscience, and no one explaining her to the reader. It should be a scene the reader could not have seen coming and, on a second reading, finds fair.

Build the case, the scenario in a single paragraph, and then walk through it: state the two preference structures plainly enough to be compared; list the observed behavior and show that it is exactly what both predict; state what each predicts in the final scene and why; say which structural feature is doing the work and what happens to the divergence when it is removed; and tell me how a defender of the hypothesis that comes out worse would attack the case, and what would remain. If the specifications cannot all be met at once, tell me which one is doing the impossible work rather than quietly dropping it.
