OK so I'm a backend enginer by trade — no philosophy degree, I just read a lot on the side — and I've somehow been roped into TA-ing an upper-level undergrad course on rationality and decision theory. The prof wants me to write one of the final-exam essay questions, and since I'm also the one grading it I need to hand in a model answer plus a marking scheme with it. The topic she assigned me is money pumps and diachronic rationality, and I've noticed that every single time I ask somebody about this they just say "intransitive preferences are irrational because you can be money-pumped into giving away all your money" and then stop, which seems way too quick to me and is definately not something I want to reward on an exam.

What I actually want the question to do is make students confront the sophisticated-chooser case, because that's the part I genuinely can't get my head around. Set it up so there's an agent with a real preference cycle over three things — say they prefer apples to bananas to cherries and back to apples — but who is fully aware of their own preferences and can see the entire sequence of trades a pump would run against them coming from a mile away. The question should force them to say whether such an agent is irrational at all, whether the money-pump argument is even aimed at them, and what has to be true about the agent — about foresight, about being able to commit or stick to a plan, about whether their preferences stay stable from one moment to the next — for the pump to actually bite. I also want it to press on the difference between showing that a preference structure is exploitable and showing that it's irrational, because I strongly suspect those two come apart and I want to see whether the students can say exactly how. And if a student wants to say the sophisticated agent can just refuse the very first trade, the question should make them defend that move instead of just asserting it — something about deliberately acting against your own current preference, or binding your future self, and whether that's even allowed to count as the rational thing to do. Please don't build the question around any particular named philosopher's paper; I don't know the literature well enough to trust myself on attributions, and I'd much rather the marks come from the reasoning than from name-dropping.

Format-wise: start the response with the exam question exactly as it would be handed to the students, then give the model answer (somewhere around 700 words is fine), then a marking scheme worth 20 marks that lists the must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, and one line per move about how students typically blow it. Just dive straight in — no preamble, no "here's the question I came up with," and no restating what I asked for.
