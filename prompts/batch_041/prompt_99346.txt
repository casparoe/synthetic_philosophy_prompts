I am a fourth-year PhD student in distributed systems, working on replication and consistency models; that vocabulary is native to me, whereas I am only an interested amateur in social choice. I would like you to take the discursive dilemma as List and Pettit present it — a group judging a set of logically interconnected propositions, each member's judgment set internally consistent, and the discovery that proposition-wise majority aggregation can yield a collectively inconsistent set — and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in the terms I actually use: replicas, states that satisfy integrity constraints, merge and reconciliation operators, consistency models, state-machine replication, quorum, and impossibility results in the style of CAP. In particular, recast the choice between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation as a choice about where to reconcile — on the base facts, recomputing the derived ones, or on the derived output directly — and tell me which of the social-choice desiderata have clean counterparts as properties of a merge operator (determinism, convergence, symmetry or anonymity, and independence of irrelevant alternatives as some locality or context-independence condition). Then give me the residue report: which parts of the dilemma translate without distortion, which translate only by fudging, and which have no counterpart at all in my framework. I expect the interesting residue to be that a replicated log has an external specification of correctness while the group's judgment set does not, and that the claim the group is a single agent that can be held responsible, together with the normative question of which procedure is legitimate rather than merely which satisfies more desiderata, may have nowhere to sit. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it marks a genuine gap in my framework or a point where the original claim was doing less work than it appears to.
