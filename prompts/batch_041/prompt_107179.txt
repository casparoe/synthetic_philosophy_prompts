I am grading a take-home exam in a graduate decision theory course. The question was:

State Good's theorem on the value of information, prove it for the finite case, and identify two conditions that are essential to the theorem and two that are not. Explain why the theorem is sometimes said to fail for non-Bayesian agents.

Here is the student's answer:

Good's theorem says that the expected value of information is nonnegative. Let S be the set of states, A the set of actions, p(s) the prior, and u(a,s) the utility. An agent who acts without information chooses a* = argmax_a Σ_s p(s)u(a,s). An agent who observes a signal E with realizations e updates to p(s|e) and chooses a_e = argmax_a Σ_s p(s|e)u(a,s). The value of information is V = Σ_e p(e) Σ_s p(s|e)u(a_e,s) - Σ_s p(s)u(a*,s). For each e, Σ_s p(s|e)u(a_e,s) ≥ Σ_s p(s|e)u(a*,s) because a_e is optimal given e. Taking expectations over e gives V ≥ 0. So a Bayesian agent can never be worse off from costless information.

Two essential conditions: the information must be costless, and the agent must be Bayesian. Two conditions that are not essential: the information need not be perfect, and the agent need not be risk neutral, since expected utility already encodes risk attitude. The theorem fails for non-Bayesian agents because they may update in ways that do not maximize expected utility, and it also fails if the agent is risk averse and cannot commit to ignoring the information, since the value of information can then be negative. A common mistake is to think the theorem fails when the information is bad news, but that is wrong: even bad news can be valuable because it allows the agent to choose a better action. The theorem also shows that the expected value of perfect information is always at least as great as the expected value of sample information, since perfect information is just a limiting case.
