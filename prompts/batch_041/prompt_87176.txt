A foundation can support four researchers for three years to work on the theory of performance legitimacy — the claim that a government earns its standing by delivering prosperity, order, and public goods rather than by being elected — with China and Singapore as the standard cases, though the Gulf monarchies and Vietnam press the same claim through very different institutions. They want conceptual and comparative work, not new country studies or econometrics. Which open questions in this area should they prioritize, and in what order? I care more about the reasoning than the list: for each, why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for people working from texts and cases rather than data, and what over the next few years would move it up or down. Add questions I have missed, and say which familiar ones you would drop.

Treat three things as live rather than settled. First, legitimacy that comes from who decides and legitimacy that comes from what gets delivered are usually posed as rivals; say whether they really are, and whether the second can be specified without borrowing standards from the first. Second, a regime claiming to have performed, its subjects believing that it has, and its having performed are three different facts, and I suspect the interesting questions live in the gaps between them. Third, performance is always judged against a counterfactual — what the alternative would have delivered — and the regime has some say over which counterfactual is salient; say whether that makes the notion circular or merely hard to measure.
