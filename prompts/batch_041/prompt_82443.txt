I'm in a metaphysics reading group that's spent most of this term going round and round the statue-and-the-clay case, and I've started to suspect we're not really having one argument. The set-up we keep coming back to is the standard one: a sculptor buys a lump of clay on Monday, sculpts it into a figure on Tuesday — call the lump Lump and the figure David. One faction says there's just one object on the plinth: Lump is David, and the impression of two is an artefact of how we describe and count things, so the question 'how many objects are there here?' has no answer the world is hiding, only answers relative to a way of talking. The other faction says there are genuinely two objects sharing all their matter: David and Lump have different histories (only Lump existed on Monday) and different futures (squash it and Lump survives while David doesn't), so by Leibniz's law they can't be one and the same — and that, they insist, is a fact about what exists, not about our vocabulary. A third person keeps muttering that 'exists' and 'object' can simply be read two ways and both readings are legitimate, which is either the deepest point in the room or a way of changing the subject, and I honestly can't tell which.

We've tried to break the deadlock with variants and it hasn't worked. A figure cast in a single pour from a mould, so there's no pre-existing lump that predates it. A rock that wind and rain have shaped into exactly the same figure — everyone agrees it isn't a statue, but it seems to raise the same counting question. And a case where you squash David and later sculpt the same clay into a completely different figure: one thing with a strange career, or two things that never overlapped? My hunch, which I'd like you to test rather than just adopt, is that this is three disagrements wearing one coat — a real one about the modal and historical facts, a verbal one about how to count and what 'object' is doing, and a third that's really about whether these questions have determinate answers at all — and that our group keeps sliding between them without noticing.

So what I want isn't a survey. Commit to a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement is this actually, what is each side really committed to once you press on it, and is one of my three strands doing all the work while the others are noise? Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a criterion for counting, a proper treatment of the constitution relation, or just conceding it's a fight about words and asking which way of talking earns its keep. Don't end by telling me it's complicated and leaving me to pick; take a position and give me your reasons.
