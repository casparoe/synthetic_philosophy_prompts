Im putting together a session for a reading group on psychiatric classification, and I want the ground rules sorted before anyone starts shouting about whether some diagnosis is "real". So: give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of psychiatric classification has to meet. The cases it has to get right, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, the empirical findings it has to sit comfortably with, and the self-referential stuff it has to handle. Lead with the whole list in one go, then the justification for each item after it — no preamble, no restating my question.

Things I'd want in there, though don't let me constrain you too much:

- the cases. Homosexuality being taken out of the DSM, and older race-based diagnoses like drapetomania that were straightforwardly ideology; hysteria dissolving as a category; PTSD as something arguably built partly by its own criteria and by advocacy; the multiple-personality/DID epidemic that rose and then fell with diagnostic fashion; kids' bipolar doing the same; culture-bound syndromes that look real in one place and don't travel; the same person collecting different labels depending on which edition of the manual you're holding.
- the distinctions. Useful vs valid. Socially constructed vs arbitrary — constructed things (money, borders) can be perfectly real, so a theory can't just use "constructed" as a synonym for "fake". Fuzzy boundaries vs no boundaries. Reliability vs validity: something can be measured very consistently and still be the wrong thing to measure. A kind changing because of the label vs a kind merely being re-described as fashions change.
- the findings. Comorbidity being the rule rather than the exception, i.e. alot of people meet criteria for several things at once. Modest heritability with no clean biomarkers. Treatment response cutting across categories (the same drug working for depression, anxiety and OCD is awkward for anyone selling these as distinct natural kinds). Thresholds being set where they are for pragmatic reasons, and mechanically moving prevalence when they move.
- Hacking's looping thing. Some categories have members who know they're being classified, take up the label, and get reshaped by it — and the classifiers get reshaped too. So the target moves while you're measuring it. A theory needs to say what validity even amounts to for a kind like that, and how it differs from the kinds in chemistry where nobody in the sample reads the paper.

Two things I especially want: flag which pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied (I suspect it's something around the homosexuality case demanding a real notion of getting it wrong while the DID case makes validity a moving target — tell me if that's the right fault line), and say which single requirement does the most to rule out the going candidates. A quick line on how the symptom-checklist approach, the dimensional/biological research programme, and the "whatever helps the patient" pragmatist line do against the list is welcome, but the list is the main thing.
