I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and I would like a careful audit of a passage I have so far been taking at face value. A popular science book I am reading offers the following gloss of Eugene Wigner's 1960 essay on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, and I want to know how much of it a philosopher of mathematics or someone working on the foundations of physics would stand behind.

The book's summary runs roughly as follows. Wigner's observation, it says, is that physics gets more out of its mathematics than it puts in. The explanation, according to the book, is that physicists are unusually adept at constructing mathematical tools tailor-made to fit the phenomena under study — its examples being Newton's law of gravitation, fitted first to falling bodies and then extended to the planets, and the matrix mechanics that Heisenberg and Born devised for atomic spectra and that then accounted for the hydrogen spectrum. Since no merely human contrivance could go on matching nature in this way, the book concludes, Wigner's essay amounts to a demonstration that the world is mathematical through and through: that mathematics is discovered rather than invented, and that the universe is at bottom a mathematical structure. From this the book extracts a methodological moral — that a physicist is entitled to trust a beautiful equation before there is any data for it.

I would like you to go through this account claim by claim and tell me what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what Wigner himself would have rejected. In particular: is the book right that the effectiveness is explained by physicists building tools to fit the data, or has it inverted Wigner's emphasis? Does Wigner actually conclude that the world is mathematical, or does he leave the matter otherwise? And does the book's closing methodological moral follow from any part of the summary that survives correction? I would also value your assessment of a suspicion of my own. After forty years of curve-fitting, I am inclined to think the whole mystery is an artifact of survivorship — that we simply forget the mathematics that failed to apply and remember the mathematics that succeeded — but I doubt a philosopher would let me off so easily, and I want to know whether that reply genuinely dissolves Wigner's puzzle or merely relocates it. Please lead with your bottom line in your first sentence — whether the book's gloss is fair — and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
