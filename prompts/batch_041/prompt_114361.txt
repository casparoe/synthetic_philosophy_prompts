For the past three years I've been reporting on international anti-corruption programs, and I've arrived at a position I can no longer shake: that the line between corruption and legitimate gift-giving is drawn by power rather than principle. What the development industry files under bribery, clientelism, and patronage, I've come to read as an alternative system of obligation and redistribution — resources circulating through kinship and reciprocity networks in places where formal welfare and impersonal markets are thin. On this view the Western anti-corruption framework is a cultural export dressed as universal ethics, and the practices it stigmatizes are functional, meaningful, and often locally legitimate. A colleague sharpens the point with a distinction I've found useful: between dyadic corruption, a private payment for a private favor, and distributive corruption, a flow of resources that sustains a whole network of dependents — and she argues that only the first is unambiguously a wrong.

I want you to tell me what would count against this. Not the objections that merely restate the consensus, but the cases, arguments, and comparisons that should genuinely move me, ranked from those that would be fatal to those that are merely awkward. Include what a defender of my position would themselves have to concede, and be honest about whether the view can be stated so that nothing counts against it — and whether that would be a defect or, for a claim about meaning and legitimacy, simply a feature of the subject matter. Two things I would personally find most persuasive: a case in which a practice that wears the face of gift-giving produces exactly the harms of outright graft whatever the locals say it means, and an argument that the appeal to local meaning is itself a way of overriding the people inside those societies who bear the costs.

Give me exactly five items, each a sentence or two, as a numbered list ordered from most to least decisive, with no introduction or conclusion.
