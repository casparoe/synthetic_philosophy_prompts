I serve on the ethics committee of a small rural hospital, and we are trying to decide how to divide a limited fund between two programs: power wheelchairs for patients with advanced ALS and hearing aids for patients with moderate hearing loss. Our chair has proposed what she calls the common threshold rule, under which we should distribute the money so that the last dollar spent on each program produces the same increase in what she calls patient functioning, while also guaranteeing that the ALS program receives at least forty percent of the fund because those patients are worse off. A colleague argues that the whole exercise is incoherent, since there is no interpersonal unit of functioning and any comparison we make is simply a disguised value judgment. I find myself persuaded by both of them, and I am not sure what to do when the committee votes next week. Please describe the most important implications of the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons for how I should think about the chair's rule, for whether I can consistently vote for it, and for what I should say to my colleague. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
