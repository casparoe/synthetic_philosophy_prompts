I am a software engineer with no training in logic, and I sit on the technical advisory committee of the body that accredits formal-methods safety cases for railway signalling and medical-device firmware. We dispose of half a dozen submissions a year, and at next month's plenary we have to deal with one from a supplier whose specification of the device is written as a first-order axiom system. The axioms admit only structures with infinitely many states, and the specification puts the device's admissible sensor readings in one-to-one correspondence with the real numbers, so the theory proves that the state space is uncountable. The supplier's technical annex concludes from this that the specification is categorical: that any structure satisfying the axioms is isomorphic to the reference device, and that conformance can therefore be established by proof, with no testing obligation.

One of our members, a mathematician, has circulated a note saying that claim cannot be true. Her argument is that a first-order axiom system in a countable language with one infinite model has models of every infinite cardinality, and in particular a countable model, so there are structures satisfying the supplier's axioms that the supplier would not accept as its device. She calls this Skolem's paradox, and adds that the same axioms appear to establish both that the state space is uncountable and that a countable model of them exists. I am not equipped to adjudicate this, and I have to circulate our committee's written opinion before the plenary.

Please draft that opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action — what we should and should not certify, the wording the certificate should carry, and a deadline for the supplier's response — and it has to be intelligible to members with no background in logic. It should engage the mathematician's argument on its merits rather than dismiss it as a technicality, while giving the supplier's best reply equal weight. My own suspicion is that the reply runs roughly like this: that the paradox is an artefact of describing the specification from outside any particular model, that being countable is not an absolute property of a structure but one relative to the surrounding theory, and that what certification needs is agreement in observable behaviour at the interface rather than identity of structure. Please say whether that reply works, and if it does, adjust the recommendation accordingly; if only part of it works, say which part survives and what the certificate must still withhold. If the point is one we cannot settle internally, say so and propose what the certificate should say in the meantime. The supplier's engineers will be in the room when this is tabled, so the opinion has to be one we can defend to them.
