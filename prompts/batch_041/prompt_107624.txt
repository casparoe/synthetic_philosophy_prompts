Kant's 1797 reply to Constant on whether one may lie to save a friend turns not on the wrong done to the murderer but on the claim that any lie corrupts the very source of right and thereby harms humanity in general; commentators still dispute whether the murderer-at-the-door example is the heart of his view or a misreading of it, and the case is commonly reframed as lying to a Nazi officer about the Jews hidden in one's house. I want to teach Adventures of Huckleberry Finn beside that debate. Huck lies to the men hunting runaway slaves who come upon the raft, and he resolves to help Jim escape even though he believes the act damns him — so the duty he thinks he is betraying is one installed by a corrupt society, while the claim he actually acts on is the humanitarian one. Give me a reading that takes both the novel and the Kant–Constant argument seriously: does Huck's practice of lying amount to a Constantian ethic, on which truth is owed only to those with a right to it, to a Kantian one, or to neither? Where does the novel complicate Kant — does it show, for instance, that truthful speech can be the more destructive act, or that a lie's remote consequences are not the liar's to own? Which scenes would you point to, and what does the novel add to the debate that the argument alone does not? Please answer in American English, and do not reduce the novel to an illustration of the theory; say where it resists the reading.
