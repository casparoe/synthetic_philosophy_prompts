I'm in this scrappy little reading group that meets above a laundromat, and last week we spent like two hours rehashing the cooperate-in-a-one-shot-prisoner's-dilemma-if-you're-sure-the-other-guy-is-basically-you thing — the Hofstadter move where if you and the other player are running the same reasoning, defecting against them is just defecting against yourself, so you both cooperate and the usual "defect is dominant" logic looks kind of dumb. My roommate says the whole group is a cult and maybe she's right, but anyway: I want to write a short story that actually dramatizes this instead of having two characters sit around explaining it to each other, and I'd like you to write it for me.

Here's the setting I've been kicking around, tweak the surface details if you want but keep the skeleton. Near-future coastal town, a flood-insurance co-op that only pays out if enough households voluntarily cap their own claims. Every household secretly writes a single number on a slip — basically how much of their own claim they're willing to eat — and the payout formula is rigged so that if everyone writes the generous number everyone gets a decent payout, if everyone writes the selfish number everyone gets scraps, and any single household that writes the selfish number while everyone else is generous walks away with a jackpot. The twist I want to hang the story on: the co-op's old founder handed out a mimeographed "reasoning guide" back in the day, and the guide tells you to decide by imagining that every household which read the same guide is deciding along with you, i.e. treat the other guide-readers as copies of yourself. But the guide went through two printings and the second one quietly changed a single line, so a guide-reader can't actually be sure whether the neighbor they're supposedly "sharing a mind" with got the first or the second version — and the two versions push you toward different numbers. That's the part I care about, the slippage between "they're me, so I pick what I'd want both of us to pick" and "but I don't know which version of me they are."

So: POV of someone who's just figured out the two printings exist, standing at the kitchen table at 11pm with a pen, knowing their best friend in town has the other printing. Make it genuinely about the reasoning — let us feel both intuitions pulling, don't resolve it with a tidy lecture at the end. Like 1500 words, and please don't force it to be uplifting or even conclusive; ending on the vertigo of the decision is fine, maybe better. Also I'm worried it'll come out sounding like a textbook, so no characters who exist just to say "ah, but consider the following objection."
