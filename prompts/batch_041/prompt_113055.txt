I've come to accept a fairly strong version of the discursive-dilemma claim: that whenever a group must reach a verdict on a question that decomposes into logically connected sub-questions, and each member is individually consistent, aggregating the members' judgments issue-by-issue can yield a collectively inconsistent set, so that no aggregation rule can be both responsive to individual judgments and collectively consistent. I take this to be not a formal curiosity but a standing obstacle to any group's claiming to speak with a single rational voice. What would count against that? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they should be to me. In particular: which relaxations of the usual conditions restore the possibility of consistency, and are any of them realistic rather than formal tricks; whether a procedure that drives a wedge between the group's beliefs and its decisions, for instance aggregating on the premises and letting the conclusion follow or the reverse, genuinely escapes the dilemma or merely relocates it; and whether the real mistake is demanding collective consistency in the first place. Then evaluate a proposal I've been handed by someone who thinks the whole thing is overblown: that the group's official position should simply be whatever conclusion a majority would endorse after reasoning from the aggregated premises. Tell me what that proposal gets right, where it breaks down, and whether it is a solution or an evasion. I'd like to know what a committed defender of the dilemma would themselves concede counts against them, not just what critics say, and an honest verdict on whether the strong version I've adopted is well insulated from refutation and whether that insulation is a defect. I'd be most persuaded by an actual procedure that manages both consistency and responsiveness, if one exists. Write it as continuous prose, the way you'd explain it in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
