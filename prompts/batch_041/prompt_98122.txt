Right, Ive been going back over Hume's essays on money and trade — the ones where he treats money as little more than a stand-in for real goods, and where a trade surplus is meant to sort itself out because the gold flows in, prices rise, and the country's exports get priced out of foreign markets. Heres what I want from you: what would Hume actually have made of a modern economy where money isn't gold at all but fiat, and most of it is conjured into existence by commercial banks when they hand out loans rather than by any central authority? Does his price-specie-flow story survive any of that, or does it just fall apart once money stops being a commodity? And would he draw a real distinction between a country with its own currency and one locked into something like the euro, where the money is issued by a central bank that answers to no single government? Reason it out from his own commitments rather than just labelling him a quantity theorist, and be honest about where you're extrapolating beyond what he actually argued versus where he had something fairly definite to say.
