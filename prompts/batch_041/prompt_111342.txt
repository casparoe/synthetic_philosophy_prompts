I work on the ML platform team at a company that sells risk-scoring models to lenders and insurers, and over the past year I've somehow become the informal fairness reviewer for everything we ship. That has forced me to actually settle what I think instead of gesturing at it, and since I have no formal philosophy background I've assembled my position out of papers I've read and arguments I've had with colleagues. Before I put it in a design document that three other teams will be held to, I'd like a consistency audit. Here is where I've landed. First, I think the core of fairness is individual: a model is fair when it treats people who are alike in the respects that actually matter alike, and I take the group-level statistics to be downstream of that — get the individual treatment right and the group numbers should take care of themselves. Second, I think a model is only fair if it makes its mistakes at the same rate across groups: if it flags one population more often when those people are not in fact a risk, or misses the genuinely risky cases in another population more often, that is discrimination, whatever the aggregate accuracy looks like. Third, whatever else is true, the score has to be calibrated within each group — a 30% chance of default needs to mean 30% for everyone, otherwise the human underwriters downstream are handed a number that means different things depending on who it is about, which just relocates the unfairness to a place I can't inspect. Fourth, I think fairness is a property of the world rather than of the model: if the labels record who got caught rather than who did wrong, no amount of post-processing at the model layer repairs that, and most fairness fixes I've seen are laundering a broken target variable. And fifth, I've come to think that the impossibility results in this area — the ones showing you cannot simultaneously have within-group calibration and equal error rates across groups unless the groups have identical base rates, or the predictor is trivially perfect — show that there is no fact of the matter about which fairness criterion is the correct one, so any choice among them is a political judgment, and the honest engineering move is to say so out loud rather than pretend a cleverer objective function will dissolve the problem.

What I want is an audit rather than a tutorial. Tell me which of these five actually pull against each other and how hard, which ones only appear to conflict but turn out to be about different things once you're careful about what each is claiming, and for the genuine conflicts which one I should drop or weaken and what that costs me. I'd also like to know whether any of the five are really the same commitment stated twice, or one quietly presupposing another, because it feels to me like at least two of them are doing the same work and I can't tell which. Please order the problems by severity instead of handing me five bullets that all say "in tension," and distinguish the ones where the package is strictly inconsistent from the ones where I've merely drawn an inference that doesn't follow. Close by telling me the single change that would clear up the most, and be blunt about which commitment is doing the most damage, since I'd rather find out now than after it's in a design doc. I don't need a reading list or citations — I want to know where I'm cheating.
