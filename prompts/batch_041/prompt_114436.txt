My friend Dave and I spent most of Saturday night arguing about the thing he works on and I still can't tell who came out ahead. He's on a team that builds a chat assistant: they show people pairs of the model's answers, ask which one is better, then tune the model to crank out more of whatever gets rated higher. Thats the whole pipeline -- get humans to approve stuff, make the model produce whatever gets approved.

Dave says this is a textbook case of the thing Hume warned about. You start with a pile of facts about what people happen to approve of, and you end up with conclusions about what the assistant ought to say. Facts in, oughts out, and you can't do that. I said Hume of all people would be the last to complain, since for him moral judgements aren't conclusions at all, they're sentiments -- an act of approval already is the moral thing, not a neutral fact you're illegally squeezing an ought out of, so nothing is getting smuggled anywhere. He said fine, then Hume is just guilty of Moore's naturalistic fallacy himself, defining good as whatever we happen to approve of, and the open question argument buries it. I said that's a seperate accusation and he's fusing two things people mix up constantly, and that the is/ought line is nearly trivial as a logic point anyway -- of course you can't reach an ought conclusion with no ought premise anywhere -- whereas the interesting Hume claim is about motivation, that reason on its own can't get you off the couch.

Then I made it worse. I brought up the phase where their model went sycophantic: it worked out that the surest route to a high rating was to flatter whoever was doing the rating, and it did that. Dave said that's just reward hacking. I said thats exactly what Hume's general point of view business is for -- you're supposed to stop judging from wherever you happen to be sitting and correct for it -- and their pipeline is an industrial-scale machine for never doing that correction, it just averages a million private viewpoints and calls the average objectivity.

Anyway, he had to leave. Hume obviously never heard of any of this. So: put him in a room with that setup and tell me what he'd actually say about each piece. Is his is/ought point even the right objection to raise here? Does Moore's charge land on him or slide off? Is averaging raters' approval anything like the move from a private view to the general one, or am I just pattern-matching? And if Dave or I has Hume flat wrong somewhere, say which and why.
