I sat on an incident review board last month for a municipal water utility, and I am still chewing on an argument that two of the other members had. The facts were these. The utility had installed a controller that set chlorine dosing at one treatment plant, tuned by machine learning against a combined objective of cost and disinfection targets. It was permitted to keep retraining in place, within a broad band, on incoming sensor data. Roughly two years after installation it settled into a dosing policy that under-chlorinated one branch of the distribution network during an unusually warm stretch; a boil-water advisory followed, covering about 40,000 people, along with a handful of confirmed illnesses. No one at the utility had specified that policy. The board's outside engineering assessment concluded that this particular drift could not have been foreseen from the data available at rollout. The on-duty operators held override authority and were required to review daily summary logs, but the logs did not flag the drift. The chair asked us to choose among three findings: that the utility be found at fault and fined; that the on-duty operator was negligent; or that the record show no finding of fault against any person or office.

What I actually got was a long argument, and I could not tell who had the better of it. One member is a systems engineer, the other teaches administrative law. It went roughly like this.

(1) The engineer: There is no gap here, and the phrase is doing no work. Everything the controller did traces back to choices someone made — the weights in the objective, the decision to allow in-place retraining at all, the width of the band it was allowed to retrain within, the decision to rely on summary logs rather than alerting. Autonomy does not introduce a new kind of event; it lengthens the causal chain. Where individual tracing runs out, the organization picks it up, and organizations are perfectly good bearers of responsibility: they can be sanctioned, required to compensate, and made to reform. "Responsibility gap" names our reluctance to follow the chain, not a break in it.

(2) The lawyer: Tracing relocates the charge rather than discharging it. Suppose we trace back to the choice to permit in-place retraining. That choice was made ex ante, by people with a defensible ex ante case — retraining was supposed to improve safety as conditions changed. Blame them and you are blaming them for a decision with a positive expected value, on the strength of an outcome they could not see. And notice what has dropped out: the under-chlorination itself, which is what harmed people. So the trace does not fill the gap; it changes the subject. Meanwhile a fine is a transfer of money. If we say the utility "is at fault," we owe an account of what a corporate fault consists in, and the natural accounts — the fault of its members, or of its culture — lead back to individual agents, and so back to the gap.

(3) The engineer: That is the structure of every accident in a complex system. A bridge fails, an aircraft comes down, a drug turns out to have a rare side effect; in each case no one chose the harm and no one could have enumerated it beforehand, and we do not conclude that responsibility has gone missing. We distribute it — designers, regulators, operators, the firm — and we use standards, insurance, and no-fault compensation for the residue. If your argument shows a gap here, it shows one in air traffic control, and then the word is not saying anything about autonomy at all. It is just the ordinary condition of industrialized risk.

(4) The lawyer: The difference is what can be specified in advance. A bridge's failure modes are fixed by its design; a regulator can require that it be safe across the space of loads and conditions that design admits, and the designer can answer that requirement. This controller's policy space was not fixed at design time. It was rewritten by the system, and the utility could not have told you in advance which policies were in that space — which is precisely why the logs did not flag the drift, since there was no specification for the drift to violate. So this is not a harm produced by a bad choice among enumerable options. It is a decision made at a time and in a place where no moral agent was deciding. And calling it an accident presupposes an agent for it to be the accident of; a hurricane is not an accident. Between "someone did it" and "it happened" there is a third case, and our vocabulary of fault has nothing to attach to. That is the gap, and it is not a reluctance to follow a chain.

(5) The engineer: Then stop asking responsibility to be individual and retrospective. Put strict liability on whoever deploys the system, require insurance and continuous monitoring, and place the burden on the utility to show it maintained its oversight. That is responsibility doing its real work — shaping future conduct — and it does not require anyone to have been at fault for the specific harm. Call it a gap if you like; it is a gap our practices already know how to live with.

(6) The lawyer: Strict liability is compensation. It answers who pays, not who did wrong, and you have conceded the point by handing the question to a rule instead of to a person. Worse, a fine that functions as a cost of doing business can be priced in; the utility can decide the fine is cheaper than the monitoring. Then the sanction is not guiding anyone's conduct either, and what you offered as the forward-looking version of responsibility turns out to be a price list. If that is the best we can do, fine — but then say plainly that we have stopped trying to establish fault, rather than that we have established it.

I would like a move-by-move adjudication. Rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken through doubtful to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly, in the terms the speaker actually used rather than terms you would prefer they had used. Then say, for each point of genuine disagreement between them, who is right and why. Say who has the stronger overall position, and whether the chair's three possible findings actually map onto the disagreement or whether the choice between them depends on something neither of them raised. Finally, identify the distinction that neither of them drew which would do the most to resolve the dispute, and explain what each of them is missing — including anything that both are missing. I have no stake in which of them comes out ahead; I would just like to know what to think before the next meeting.
