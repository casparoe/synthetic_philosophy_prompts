I teach early modern philosophy, and there's a pattern I keep running into in seminar, in student papers, and honestly in my own writing, and I've never found a name for it, which is starting to bug me. So here's the shape of it, and I want to know if it's a real thing or just something I keep noticing because I've decided to notice it.

Someone defends a thing — a policy, a person, a rule, a distribution — by arguing that every alternative is worse. That part's fine, it's an ordinary comparative argument. But then they slide from "the alternatives are worse" to "the thing is good," and they treat the comparison as having done the work. And here's the part that really gets me: the slide works *better* the worse the alternatives are. When the alternatives are only a little worse, everyone in the room can see that least-bad isn't the same as good. When the alternatives are catastrophic, nobody notices the slide at all, and pointing it out sounds pedantic.

Two instances from my own life, then the one that got me thinking about it. First, our grading curve: every alternative arrangement screws some student over more, so the curve gets called fair, even by people who think it's arbitrary. Second, a hiring process that nobody will defend on the merits — "any other process would be gamed harder," and that's supposed to settle it. Then the real one: Leibniz on the best of all possible worlds. His version seems to have an extra piece the everyday versions don't. It isn't only that this world beats the alternatives; it's that a wise and good chooser picked it, and that on his account the choice was certain — morally necessary, he says, not absolutely necessary — because a God who is wise and good can't prefer the worse. So the goodness seems to come from the character of the chooser, not from the comparison at all. Which makes me suspect the everyday versions are quietly borrowing a premise they don't have: some stand-in for "the chooser is good," like "the process," or "nature," or "history." In his ethics the same structure shows up, since for him the goodness of a thing is bound up with its place in the perfection of the whole, which is why I think the slide is so easy in his system and in ours.

So does this have an established name? The nearest things I can think of don't fit. I know the "appeal to worse problems" bit in informal logic, but that's about how much a problem matters, not about whether something is good. I know there's something in the philosophy of science about the best explanation on offer maybe being the best of a bad lot, but that's about inference, not value. And "lesser of two evils" and "second best" are relatives, obviously, but they seem to name the option rather than the inference.

What I don't want is just a label. If there's a concept, tell me which one it is and exactly where my description overshoots or undershoots it. If there isn't, coin something and define it so I can use it on students: what it covers, what it doesn't, whether it's a fallacy at all or just an argument with a suppressed premise, and whether the inference can ever be valid — if so, what has to be added. Boundary cases please: does it apply when the alternatives really are all terrible and the thing really is good? Does it apply to the retroactive versions, where someone argues from a bad outcome that there was no better option, and then treats that as vindication? And a separate question, since I'm teaching him in the spring: does Leibniz actually license this move, or do people just take his conclusion and drop the theology that was carrying it?

Answer in American English, please — I'm going to paste it into a handout.
