My closest friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a puzzle that neither of us could put down, and I'd like you to turn it into a dialogue between the two of us. Please write it as a fair-minded reconstruction rather than a transcript: give each of us the strongest version of our position, let us actually answer each other's points instead of talking past each other, and don't let either of us become a strawman. We both understand the basics, so the whole interest is in the specific moves.

The puzzle is this. We were comparing a spectrum of possible lives. At one end, call it L1, is a single day of the most intense pleasure either of us can imagine, with nothing after it. At the other end, L100, is a life of about eighty years in which every day is mildly pleasant but thin — nothing painful, nothing ecstatic. We filled in the ninety-eight lives between them so that moving from one to the next adds a little more life, while the pleasure of any single day drops by an amount too small to notice. Here is the trouble. Taken one pair at a time, each of us kept judging the shorter life worse than the longer one: you get more life and you cannot feel the drop in quality, so how could the longer one not be better? Run that through the whole series and it implies L100 is best. But when we looked at the two ends directly, we both wanted to say the opposite — that L1 is better than L100, that one perfect day beats eighty years of mild pleasantness. So the pairwise judgments and the direct judgment point in opposite directions. My friend says this shows that "better than" is not transitive and that philosophers have simply been wrong to assume it is. I say it shows that one of our pairwise judgments has to be mistaken, because betterness must be transitive — otherwise the whole practice of comparing anything falls apart.

I want the dialogue to take this seriously and to fight over the right response. In particular, I want both of us to press the moves that actually matter. My side should consider, and not merely wave at, the possibility that some of the pairwise comparisons are vague or indeterminate rather than straightforwardly true, and the possibility that if betterness were intransitive then someone could take money from me in a cycle of trades I would accept at every step — I suspect that is the real reason transitivity matters, and I want to see whether the argument survives being spelled out. My friend's side should be free to answer that the money-pump argument assumes what it set out to prove, and to argue that the honest description of the spectrum is that some of these lives are neither better nor worse nor equal, but comparable in a way that simply isn't a ranking. Let the dialogue end without a tidy resolution if a tidy resolution would be dishonest, but have each of us actually concede something or sharpen a point under pressure.

One request about how to handle me. If the way I have set this up is confused — if, for instance, the case only looks like a paradox because I am quietly assuming that every pair of lives must be comparable, or because "better than" is being stretched across two different senses — I would much rather you say so plainly than stage a debate around a dichotomy that doesn't hold. Don't manufacture agreement or disagreement to be polite; if my framing is doing illicit work, tell me where.

Please write in American English.
