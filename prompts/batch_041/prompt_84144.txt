I edit the behaviour section of an ecology journal that receives a steady stream of papers whose central claim is that some trait is an adaptation for some function. My referees are capable biologists but not evolutionary theorists, they have about an hour per manuscript, and I make the final call, so I need something they can run rather than a lecture on adaptationism. Please turn the spandrels critique — Gould and Lewontin's arguments that current utility is not evidence of past selection, and that a trait may be a byproduct of something else entirely — into a decision procedure for evaluating a claim of the form "trait T is an adaptation for function F." It should run in order: what exactly is being claimed (current function, historical origin, or both); which non-adaptation alternatives are live for this trait (drift, developmental or phylogenetic constraint, byproduct, an exaptation since co-opted to a new role); what evidence would discriminate among them and what the manuscript actually offers; and how much of the remaining disagreement is about research strategy — how heavily to weight selection as a default — rather than about data. Say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure should stop with a verdict of "not supported," and where it must hand the judgment back to me because the question is about what the journal should publish rather than about what is true. It must not let referees escape in either direction: neither treating "it works well" as proof of selection, nor disposing of an inconvenient trait by calling it a spandrel and moving on. Then run it on three claims: the female orgasm as a byproduct of the male orgasm rather than an adaptation, the human chin as a growth byproduct, and a third of your choosing — an adaptationist claim that is widely accepted and that I would prefer to accept, where the procedure nonetheless tells me I cannot. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do.
