Ok so this is a slightly odd request but bear with me. I'm a philosophy professor — philosophy of mind, mostly consciousness — but before I went back to grad school I spent about ten years as a distributed-systems engineer, and I still think in those terms when I'm tired. I teach the split-brain material every year and I keep having the same itch.

Here's the itch. In distributed systems, "split brain" is already a term of art: you get a network partition, both halves keep serving, each side thinks it's the only primary, and you end up with two divergent histories nobody can reconcile. There's a whole toolkit for it — quorums, leader election, consistency models, the CAP tradeoff, single-system-image guarantees, Byzantine stuff. And in philosophy of mind, split brain means comissurotomy patients and the question of whether there's one subject of experience in there or two. I want you to take the philosophical debate — the two-streams reading, the unity-of-consciousness questions, the business about whether the patient is one agent and two experiencers, the arguments over whether phenomenal unity is a real relation or a deflationary gloss — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the distributed-systems vocabulary. Not as a cute analogy, as an actual translation. Pick your mapping explicitly (what's a node, what's the network, what's the partition, what plays the role of a consistency guarantee, what's the client, what's the quorum) and then stick to it.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue. What didn't make it across? Which claims translate cleanly, which only translate if I fudge something, and which have no counterpart at all in the distributed-systems picture. And when something doesn't translate, I want your read on whether that's a defect of my engineering vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it sounded. Be honest — if the mapping is doing violence to the philosophy, say where.

Some things I'm already suspicious about and want you to adjudicate rather than just echo back at me: whether one-subject-or-two is even the right question (a cluster with two primaries isn't a subject of anything, so maybe the metaphor begs the thing), whether the unity that matters is phenomenal or just functional/access unity (consistency is a functional notion — is phenomenal unity anything like it, or is that exactly where the translation breaks), and whether the patient's insistence that the left hand did something on its own is evidence about subjects or just about one reporting system with partial access to the whole.

Format I want, roughly:
- "The mapping" — spell out the correspondence in prose, maybe 400-500 words.
- "What carries over" — the claims that survive, with the mapping stated for each, ~300 words.
- "The residue" — what doesn't translate, seperated into (a) fudged and (b) inexpressible, ~400-500 words.
- "Defect or fog?" — your verdict on whether the residue indicts the engineering vocabulary, the philosophy, both, or neither, ~200-300 words.

Don't give me a reading list, and don't hedge by telling me the two vocabularies are just different — I know they're different, that's the whole point. I want to know exactly where the joints don't line up.
