The individualism–holism dispute in social science has now run for more than a century, and what I want from you is not a verdict but a diagnosis: why does it never resolve? On one side, the methodological individualists hold that an acceptable social explanation must ultimately be cashed out in terms of the beliefs, dispositions and actions of identifiable individuals, and that talk of 'society', 'structures' or 'institutions' doing things under their own steam is at best shorthand and at worst a category mistake. On the other side, the holists hold that social facts are *sui generis*: that collective properties — a norm, a price level, a class position, a bank run gathering pace — have causal powers irreducible to any individual's states, and that the demand for individual-level microfoundations either distorts the phenomenon or simply changes the subject. What I find striking is that both camps now routinely concede something like a supervenience claim — no difference in social facts without some difference in individual facts — and yet the argument carries on as though nothing had been conceded.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that this is the multiple-realisability dispute from the philosophy of mind wearing sociological clothes. On that reading the individualist is a kind of type-identity theorist who thinks the only genuine causal relata are individual states, the holist is a non-reductive physicalist who thinks higher-level kinds are real precisely because they are multiply realised and sustain their own generalisations, and the apparent stalemate is the same stalemate over whether higher-level causal talk is autonomous or merely convenient. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, or whether it papers over something the two debates do not actually share — for instance, whether social kinds are *socially constructed* in a way mental kinds are not, which might make the analogy break at exactly the interesting point.

Then give me your own diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says rather than by a survey of positions. In particular, is the residual disagreement (a) a verbal one about the word 'explanation' and whether it must bottom out in mechanisms; (b) a hidden empirical or formal question — say, whether there exist robust higher-level generalisations that survive every permissible redistribution of individual-level detail; (c) a disagreement about which cases are the paradigm of a social explanation, with each side quietly selecting examples that favour it; or (d) two groups answering different questions, one about what there is and one about how to explain, under a single heading? I suspect the honest answer is a mixture, so I would like you to say which ingredient is doing the load-bearing work and which is merely rhetorical.

Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves a pseudo-problem, a formal result about the existence of autonomous higher-level generalisations, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. If you conclude that the dispute is a genuine one that merely happens to be hard, say so and tell me what the hardest surviving core of it is.
