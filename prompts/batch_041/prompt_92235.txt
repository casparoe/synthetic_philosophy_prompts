Last month I spent an evening arguing with a close friend — a legal historian — about Pascal, and I lost badly enough that the argument is still with me. That matters because in three weeks I am to give the speech at the investiture of another friend, who is being sworn in as a trial judge, and everything the first friend said is exactly what I cannot say out loud in that room.

Here is the disagreement, as fairly as I can put it. My friend's Pascal is a cold realist about law. On that reading, the celebrated maxim that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical is not a pious slogan but a diagnosis: it tells us that the authority of law rests in the end on custom, on the imagination, and on force, not on justice; that the just man obeys the law not because it is just but because it is law, and would not be much disturbed if the law changed under him; and that the peace we credit to justice is really the peace of settled habit backed by the bailiff. My reply was that this is too quick, that force in Pascal is doing at least three different jobs at once — the brute coercion of the man with the keys and the warrant, the standing institutional machinery of the courts, and the imaginative force of habit and custom — and that pulling these apart makes Pascal less cynical and more interesting than my friend allows. We did not settle it, and I suspect we cannot.

Write me the speech. Eight minutes, delivered aloud. The room will hold judges and lawyers who have read the Pensées and will hear every allusion, the appointee's family, who have not and should not be made to feel they are at a seminar, and a few clergy who will notice if I make Pascal say something he would not. The Pascal material has to do structural work rather than decorate: the occasion is a ceremony that asserts precisely the union of justice and force that we were fighting about, so the speech has to decide what is actually being celebrated, and whether it can be celebrated honestly by someone who half-believes my friend. I would like the speech to take a side, or at least to make the stakes of the side-taking visible, and to refuse the platitude that dissolves it. One image I keep returning to, which you may use or discard: a judgment is only words until someone with a key and a warrant appears at the door — the sheriff's knock is where the whole thing either holds or does not.

No citations, no reading list, and do not put words in Pascal's mouth: if you want his phrasing, paraphrase it rather than manufacture a quotation.
