I do failure analysis on industrial equipment, and there is a pattern in how my colleagues and I talk that I have never found a name for. While a hypothesis is still open, I will say something like "if the pump had run dry, the seals would have shown exactly this scoring" — I am not asserting that the pump ran dry, and I am not denying it either; I am putting forward a hypothesis that would explain the evidence I am looking at. Weeks later, once testing has ruled the dry run out, I use the same "had … would have" construction, and now it plainly does mean the antecedent did not happen. The words do not change; only what I know changes. A physician I work with describes symptoms the same way while she is still working toward a diagnosis, and a friend who investigates fires does it while narrowing down a cause. Is there a name for this — a conditional of the counterfactual form that is used to reason toward an antecedent that may well be true, and that settles into an ordinary counterfactual only after the antecedent has been ruled out? I am aware of the familiar distinction between the "if" you use when you do not yet know whether something happened and the "if" you use when you know it did not, but that does not seem to capture what I am describing, since the form here is the counterfactual one from the start. If there is no established term, I would like one, along with a definition that says where the line falls: in particular, whether the "it did not happen" component is part of what such a sentence means or only something hearers assume and can withdraw, and how one and the same sentence can do inferential work at one moment and describe a lost possibility at another.
