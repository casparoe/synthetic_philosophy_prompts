I'm a novelist, and my old college has asked me to give this year's commencement address — it's the last one they'll ever have, because in the fall they're being folded into the state university system and that campus as we knew it is done. So its a strange room: a few hundred kids in gowns who mostly want to get to lunch, their parents, a faculty who are losing their jobs, and a president who would very much prefer I not make it worse.

Here's my problem. I write endings for a living, and I know that where you put the last page is a choice — you can turn the same events into a tragedy or a beginning just by deciding when to stop talking. And I've been reading alot about collapse lately, the real thing, Bronze Age, the Maya, Easter Island, and the more I read the more it seems like the word "collapse" is doing something sneaky in most of those stories. The palace archives stop, so we say the civilization fell — but the archives stopping is evidence about scribes, not about farmers. Usually the dirt can't even tell you "everyone died" apart from "everyone moved forty miles and quit making the pots we know how to date." And the famous cases are contested: the Maya story is at least as much a political reshuffle as a disappearance, and the Easter Island ecocide story is being fought over hard by the people who actually dig there. Then the other side of it, which is the part that scares me: what looks like resilience can just be the same failure moving slower, a society buying more complexity than the complexity is worth, because the people who pay for it and the people who decide to buy it are never the same people.

What I want from you is the speech. Five minutes, spoken, no footnotes. It has to do two things that are pulling against each other. It has to be warm enough for the parents who've never heard any of this and just want their kid to walk out feeling the day was real. And it has to be straight enough that the two archaeologists on the faculty don't feel condescended to — which means no loose talk of collapse, and no pretending this is secretly a rebirth, because that's the kind of thing that sounds profound from a podium and like a lie in the parking lot. The idea that an ending is partly a matter of where you stop telling the story should be doing real structural work in it, shaping what I can and can't say — not name-dropped in the middle like a fortune cookie. And I'd rather not blame anyone from the stage, including the trustees, even though part of me would like to.

Just give me the speech. Start right in with it, no preamble, no "here's your commencement address." Then two or three lines at the end about what you decided you couldn't put in it, and why.
