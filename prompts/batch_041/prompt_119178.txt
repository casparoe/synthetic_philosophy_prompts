My friend Maya has been going to a bunch of the abolitionist reading groups and we got into it for like three hours on Friday, and I came away with a pattern I can't stop seeing and don't have a word for. Here it is. Every replacement for police that we could come up with seems to land in one of two buckets. Bucket one: it has no authority to make anybody do anything — the mental health crisis van, the person who handles school discipline, the traffic safety people — and then the second someone won't cooperate, the answer is call the police, so the cops are still the thing holding the whole arrangement up, just further back in the frame where you don't notice them. Bucket two: you give it teeth. The traffic agency can detain you, the crisis responders can hold you against your will, the new body gets a uniform, a radio, a use of force policy and a complaint process, and congratulations, that's a police force with a different name, and within a year you're hearing the exact same complaints about it, except now nobody's watching because they're in vans instead of cruisers and (at first) unarmed. So the coercion doesn't shrink when you defund, it gets moved sideways and renamed, and the renaming is what makes it invisible. My question is whether there's an established name for this. The nearest things I've got are net-widening and mission creep, but neither is really it — net-widening is about diversion programs pulling more people into the system, and this is about where the capacity for force ends up living after you've moved the furniture around. Same with the whole Weber thing about the state claiming a monopoly on legitimate violence — that names the claim, not the recycling.

What I actually want isn't a label, it's the carving, and I don't need a reading list, I need to know where the edges are. Does this only bite when the teeth are physical force, or does it also apply when they're fines, court orders, evictions, cutting off benefits — because if it applies there too, then the thing being monopolized is way bigger than force and the whole framing shifts. Does it cover private security, campus cops, HOAs, the guy at the store who detains shoplifters, and if so what's actually different about them, since they clearly aren't the state? And here's the part that was really the fight: is there a version of this that says the pattern is a conceptual necessity — any rule that's enforced at all needs a backstop that can eventually do something physical to somebody — versus a contingent fact about how we happen to have built these particular institutions, because I genuinely cannot tell which one Maya and I were disagreeing about. Also I'd love a counterexample if one exists: an alternative that stayed in bucket one and never had to call anyone, or one that got teeth and stayed recognizably something other than police. And tell me how confident you are in all of this, and flag the single claim you're least sure about — I'm way more sure the pattern is real than I am about what it means, and I'd rather you say so than paper over it.
