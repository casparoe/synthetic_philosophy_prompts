I'm a freelance journo who writes about ideas — long features for magazines that are usually, and often unsuccessfully, trying to make the culture-war beat a little less stupid — and I'm working up a piece on honor, dignity and victimhood cultures. The hook is that I keep having the same argument with a friend who teaches at a small liberal arts college in Ohio. He thinks the rise of victimhood culture is a straightforward moral regression; I think a lot of what he's calling victimhood culture is just dignity culture applied consistently, and neither of us can put our finger on exactly where we part ways, which makes me suspect we're not really disagreeing about the sociology at all but about what makes a moral culture good in the first place. My editor wants a sidebar quiz to run with the feature, and I'd rather not do the lazy version where the quiz just asks readers whether they believe in honor, dignity or victimhood and then tells them what they already said.

So: write me a proper sorting instrument. Eight to ten questions, each one a concrete situation rather than an abstraction — someone insults your mother across a bar, a colleague quietly takes credit for your work in front of the boss, a stranger posts a video mocking you and it goes round, your kid's school asks you to weigh in on a bullying dispute, a friend asks you to publicly vouch for her against an accusation, a relative says something vile at a wedding, a superior belittles you in front of juniors, an ex posts something defamatory — with two or three answer options each, and a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto positions rather than onto labels. Build the thing from the logical space first: what the culture treats as being at stake (standing in the eyes of others, equal worth that needs no defending, or protection from harm that others owe you), who is the rightful responder (you, nobody, or third parties and institutions), whether a culture gets assessed by its own internal logic, by its consequences, or by the commitments it embodies, and whether the three labels name distinct kinds at all or just points on a continuum. Then put the actual positions in that space — the progressivist who thinks victimhood culture is dignity culture finally taking harm seriously, the declinist, the pluralist who says each fits a different social structure and the ranking question is malformed, the hybrid or continuity thesis, the neo-honor revivalist who thinks honor is superior because it's about accountability, the grim pragmatist who says it's just a question of which pathologies you can live with, and the deflationary view that the whole typology is bad sociology — and tell me which combinations of answers are empty and why, i.e. is nobody there because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any positions the quiz can't seperate however I score it, and tell me where the scoring key is doing work the questions can't really back up. Don't send me reading lists or citations, I'm on deadline and it all has to stand on its own.
