I am a graduate student in philosophy of economics, and I have been trying to name a pattern that I first noticed in a departmental prediction market about whether a colleague would get tenure. The market was public, and everyone knew that anyone with genuine inside information was barred from trading. Everyone also knew that everyone knew this. So nobody traded. The price sat at the prior. Then one afternoon a small trade went through, and the price jumped sharply, not because the trade was large but because its existence was taken as evidence that the trader knew something the rest of us did not. It turned out the trade was a joke. But the joke moved the market more than any permissible piece of information could have, and for a while it made people update in a direction that the joke itself did not support.

What I cannot find a name for is the structure behind that episode. The usual result, as I understand it, is that when everyone shares a prior and knows that everyone is rational, no one will trade on private information, because the mere willingness to trade at a given price reveals that the other side knows something unfavorable. That much I can follow. The second-order effect is that once the no-trade result is common knowledge, the absence of trade stops being informative—it is simply what the theory predicts—but any observed trade becomes disproportionately informative, because it signals that someone is either irrational, informed, or manipulating. The more people trust the no-trade result, the more weight a single trade carries, and the more profitable it becomes to trade for no reason other than to move that second-order signal. The equilibrium in which no one trades therefore seems to undermine itself: its credibility makes deviations more meaningful, and meaningful deviations attract people who have no private information at all.

Is there an established name for this phenomenon? I do not mean the original no-trade theorem, nor the general idea that information can destroy insurance or that costly information can make markets less efficient. I am after the specific reflexive loop in which the public credibility of a no-trade prediction turns the first trade into a disproportionately strong signal and thereby creates an incentive to manufacture that signal. If there is a term, I would like to know what it is and exactly what it covers. If there is not, please coin one and define it, with attention to the boundaries: how does it differ from adverse selection, signaling, manipulation, self-defeating prophecy, and the paradox of informationally efficient markets? I would also like a concrete example, ideally from a prediction market or an insurance exchange, that shows where the phenomenon starts and where it stops. Please answer in American English.
