My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about rent control and minimum wages and I suspect we never disagreed about the same question: I kept making claims about who may exclude whom from what, and she kept making claims about what happens to supply, and each of us treated the other's point as beside the point. I'd like a decision tree I can send her — pitched at two reasonably literate non-economists — that sorts positions on the ethics of price controls by what they actually claim rather than by the labels they wear. Each branch node should be a question either of us could answer without knowing where it leads: whether the baseline of comparison is the market as it actually runs or an idealized competitive one; whether a price is being treated as an allocation rule or as a message; whether the objection to a control is that it wrongs the regulated party or that it makes things worse overall; whether the control comes with an explicit substitute rationing rule (queues, waiting lists, lotteries, connections, bureaucratic discretion) or is simply imposed on top of one. Leaves should be named positions where a name exists and marked 'unnamed' where none does, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment; make sure the tree contains the leaf holding that the ethical case and the economic case come apart entirely, and the leaf holding that a control defensible as a transfer is still objectionable because of who ends up doing the rationing. Keep it to about eight to twelve nodes, handle a ceiling (rent control) and a floor (minimum wage) and say where the same structure carries over and where it breaks, and don't pad it with textbook deadweight-loss material — I know that argument and so does she. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and whether that is because they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Tell me how confident you are in the cuts you've made, and single out the one branch point you are least sure marks a genuine disagreement rather than a verbal one.
