I'm a graduate teaching assistant for a philosophy of biology unit on the concept of biological function, and what I want to hand my students is a one-page diagnostic instrument rather than another summary of the debate. The recurring trouble is that they use the word in two incompatible registers inside a single paragraph: first as whatever a structure is doing here and now that keeps the organism alive, and then, without noticing the shift, as what the structure was selected for — which is how they end up saying both that a vestigial organ still has a function and that a healthy heart can fail to do its job. I'd like a decision tree, roughly ten to fourteen branch nodes and eight to ten leaves, that takes a student from their verdicts on concrete cases to a position on what makes it the case that X is the function of a trait rather than one of its mere effects. Every branch node must be phrased as a question about a case, answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the positions, and no node may be a disguised invitation to pick a label; build the tree out of the logical space of answers first, then locate the positions in it, rather than working from a roster of views.

Please make sure the cases include at least these: a trait that was selected for one effect but whose current contribution is a different one (the noise a heart makes, or a structure co-opted for a new use); a trait that now contributes to survival and reproduction but arose by drift and was never favoured by selection; a vestigial structure that contributes nothing at all; a trait that raises reproductive success while shortening life; a heart in an organism that has just died, and a token organ that has never once performed its job; a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an organism that comes into existence by accident with no ancestors and therefore no selection history; a gene inserted into a laboratory-built minimal cell that does nothing whatsoever; and a unit in a trained neural network that we casually describe as detecting edges, where nothing selected it in the biological sense although something did shape it. I want the leaves to include the etiological family, split by whether actual selection history is required or a standing disposition to be favoured will do; the family that treats a function as a contribution to some capacity of a containing system, where the choice of system is partly the analyst's; the family that relativizes functions to goals and therefore gets malfunction cheaply; the accounts that generalize selection so as to cover learning and deliberate design in one stroke; the pluralist view that two legitimate concepts are doing different work; and the deflationary view that the dispute is largely verbal. For each leaf, give one line on its most awkward commitment.

Two further things. Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, say whether that is because the position would be incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and where two leaves cannot be pulled apart by any question you can honestly phrase, flag them as a pair the tree fails to separate rather than inventing a case to prise them apart. Finally, append a short list of the nodes whose phrasing quietly presupposes one side of the dispute — I suspect any question about what a trait is *for* does — together with a neutral rewording of each. I don't need author names, dates, or a reading list: label a leaf by its name only where the name is standard, describe it in a sentence otherwise, and mark it unnamed if nobody in particular defends it.
