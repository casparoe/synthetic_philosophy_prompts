Next month its my turn to run the library philosophy group and I've picked the donkey — the one that starves between two identical piles of hay because it has no reason to prefer either. Forty-five minutes, nine or ten people, no philosophy background, but they will argue with me about anything. Don't explain the puzzle to me, plan the session for me.

What I need: the order the ideas have to come in so that it lands as a genuine problem rather than a joke about a stupid animal, and why that order and not another — what depends on what. The single example that should carry the whole thing. I dont want to use the donkey itself as the running case, it's too easy to laugh at, so pick me something else and tell me what it needs to have and what it must not have. The misconceptions they'll arrive with or form and exactly where in the sequence I head each one off — I'm guessing one of them says "it'd just pick one, so what", one says "this is really about free will", one says "the donkey's just thick" — and one question to ask at the end that would show me whether they got the structure of the problem rather than just the punchline.

Four things I want built into the sequence, becuase they're the bits I find genuinely interesting and I'll make a mess of them if I improvise:

- The gap between "the two options are exactly as good as each other" and "the two options can't be compared at all". I think my group will slide between those two without noticing, and I want the order to force the distinction rather than patch it up afterwards.
- Breaking the tie with a policy — "I always take the left one", or a coin — and whether that counts as a reason. I want them to feel the pull of "but deciding to flip is itself a choice", and then see why the regress stops there. This is the bit I most want to get right.
- That a tie stops being a tie the moment you've picked, since the one you picked is now the one you're getting. I can't tell whether that's a real point or a trick, so say which.
- That a real donkey wouldn't stay stuck for long — something noisy in the wiring would settle it. Why that's true and still not an answer to the puzzle.

Also: not having a preference versus not knowing which is better. I've watched people collapse those and I need to know where in the session to stop them doing it. And I need to know what to leave out, and to say out loud that I'm leaving it out.

I'll be talking the whole time, no slides, though there's a whiteboard.
