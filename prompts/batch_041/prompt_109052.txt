The Archivist's Dilemma: What the Frame Problem Is, and What Learned Systems Merely Borrow

1. The frame problem is usually introduced as a difficulty in the logic of action. To write axioms for what an action changes, one must also say what it leaves unchanged, and the list of non-effects is unbounded: opening a door does not alter the colour of the walls, the position of the furniture, or the truth of the Treaty of Westphalia. The standard remedy is to write only the positive effects and let inertia be assumed by default, but the Yale shooting problem showed that this manoeuvre does not settle which assumptions survive when several events interleave. Philosophers drew a broader lesson from the episode: a creature that had to represent all and only the relevant consequences of its actions would be paralysed, so some restriction on what is considered must be built in somewhere.

2. I want to distinguish two readings of the problem. On the representational reading, it is an engineering question about how to specify inertia compactly in a formal language, and it belongs to the nonmonotonic reasoning literature that McCarthy and Hayes inaugurated. On the deliberative reading, it is a question about how an agent determines what is relevant to a practical question at all — a question about the frame of attention rather than about frame axioms. These are not the same problem, and I claim the first is a special case of the second.

3. Consider the archivist's dilemma. An archivist is instructed to preserve everything that will be relevant to a future historian's question. Because the question has not yet been asked, "relevant" has no extension: there is no fact of the matter about what to keep. The archivist can keep everything, in which case the archive explodes and becomes useless; or the archivist can keep what present questions make salient, in which case the archive becomes a mirror of current obsessions. Every frame is either no frame or a parochial one. The dilemma is structural rather than practical, because it follows from relevance being a relation between a body of information and a question, not a property of the information itself.

4. It follows that frames are not solved in general, only within closed tasks. What looks like a solution in chess, in the blocks world, or in theorem proving is the fact that the question is fixed in advance by the rules of the domain: the relevance relation is supplied by the task rather than discovered by the agent. On this view the frame problem is not fundamentally a problem about agents; it is a problem about tasks, and it dissolves exactly to the extent that a task is closed.

5. Contemporary learned systems appear to circumvent the problem, since they carry a prior over what typically matters, induced from a training distribution. I want to argue that this is not a solution but a loan. The frame is borrowed from the distribution, and the loan is called in whenever the task leaves the distribution. Brittleness, on this reading, is not an incidental engineering wart; it is the frame problem reappearing in the only place it can appear — at the boundary of the distribution. A system that seems to know what not to consider only knows it for questions it has effectively been asked before, in the form in which it has been asked.

6. An obvious objection: the dilemma is false, because relevance is constituted by the agent's interests, and an agent with purposes thereby has a frame; there is no further fact about relevance for it to miss. I think this is right about agents and wrong about the dilemma. It does not dissolve the problem but relocates it into the question of where purposes come from — a training distribution in one case, natural selection and development in another. That may be the honest answer, but it makes the frame problem continuous with the problem of the origin of norms, and it entails that any system whose purposes are given from outside has a frame given from outside.

7. So the useful question is not whether a system has solved the frame problem, but whose frame it is using, and what happens when the question changes.

Second, the distinction in section 2 between the representational and the deliberative reading — is it a genuine distinction, or does the first simply collapse into the second once stated carefully? Third, whether the archivist's dilemma is a real dilemma or an equivocation on "relevant"; if the latter, say precisely where the equivocation lies, and whether a better formulation could rescue the point. Fourth, the borrowed-frame claim in section 5: is it doing conceptual work, or is it a dressed-up statement of distribution shift? Is it falsifiable, and what would count against it?
