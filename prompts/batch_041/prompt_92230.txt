I teach a senior seminar on reference and modality and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori that a student can't get through just by reciting the Hesperus/Phosphorus and water/H2O material. So build the question around cases they definately won't have seen. Here are two I made up:

(1) A spectroscopy team explains a set of unexplained lines by positing a new element, and they name it 'novium', meaning by that "the element responsible for these lines." Years later it turns out the lines come from two seperate elements and there's no single cause.

(2) A different team names a substance 'glorp' just by pointing at a beaker -- "this stuff, whatever it turns out to be, is glorp." The beaker later turns out to be a mixture, with one component doing all the work everyone actually cared about.

I want the question to make them say, for each case, whether the term rigidly designates and whether any identity statement involving it comes out as a necessary a posteriori truth -- and to be explicit about what's doing the work: reference-fixing versus meaning, uniqueness of the description, outright failure of reference, or the possibility that the introducers built something they took to be essential into what they meant. Include one part that forces them to say what would have to be true of a description for a term whose reference is fixed by it to still count as rigid, in a way that doesn't just collapse into "descriptions aren't rigid."

Give me the question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks -- things like treating reference-fixing and meaning-giving as the same thing, or assuming failed uniqueness automatically kills rigidity. No citations needed, they won't have web access.
