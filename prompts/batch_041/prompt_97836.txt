I write a column on economics and ideas, and I want a joke to open a piece on what the concept of revealed preference can and cannot deliver. The joke has to land for readers who already know the concept, so its punchline should turn on something genuinely true of it rather than on generic economist humor: that a choice reveals a preference only relative to the alternatives the chooser actually faced, that what is recovered is at most an ordering and not a quantity of satisfaction, and that the real content of the idea lies in the consistency conditions it imposes across menus, which is also why a sufficiently flexible imputed preference can be made to fit almost any pattern of choices after the fact. Do not build the joke around a particular paper, author, or textbook example, and do not explain the concept inside the joke; it should presuppose the reader's familiarity. A few lines is plenty.
