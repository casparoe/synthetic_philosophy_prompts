I volunteer two days a week at a drop-in advice centre in a town that lost its main employer in the nineties, and for the past year I have also sat on the small panel that administers the council's discretionary crisis fund. We see people whose benefits have been sanctioned, or who are waiting weeks for a payment that should have arrived, and the form we fill in after each hearing asks us to record whether the claimant's difficulty was "reasonably avoidable." Those two words have quietly become the whole of my moral life, and I have started to notice that my gut verdicts on the cases we hear are confident and, I think, mostly sensible, but that they do not add up to anything I could state out loud. I would rather find out what they do add up to than keep trusting the gut.

Here are the ones I keep coming back to, with what I actually think. (1) Nadia, 52, gave up her job as a school dinner lady to look after her mother, who has advanced dementia and cannot safely be left; she claims carer's allowance and a top-up, and she turned down a night job at a distribution depot because nobody else can do the overnight care. She should be supported without question, and a sanction here would be monstrous. (2) Tom, 34, left a well-paid job in insurance two years ago to play in a band; he earns about £400 a month and claims a top-up. I resent it. He chose this, and the rest of us should not be paying for his records. (3) Priya, 29, works twenty-four hours a week as a care assistant, takes home less than the benefit floor for a family of four, and receives a top-up. Entirely fine — hard, useful work that still does not pay enough to live on. (4) Dermot, 45, has been signed off with a back injury for nine years and has been filmed carrying a fridge up two flights of stairs for his brother. Sanction, and I do not lose any sleep over it. (5) Kevin, 23, third generation on benefits, no qualifications, missed two appointments with his work coach because he overslept. I think the sanction is right in principle and wrong in size — a month's money for oversleeping — and I hate that his two children lose the money rather than him. (6) Aisha, 38, cleans houses for cash about twelve hours a week and does not declare it. That is fraud, and I know it is the same offence as Dermot's, but I feel almost nothing about it; it is the only work she can fit around school hours. (7) Marcus, 41, is offered a job ninety minutes away; after the commute and the childcare he would be £40 a week worse off than staying on benefits, and he turns it down. He should not be punished for doing the arithmetic — the taper is the problem, not him. (8) Sam, 33, has sole custody of a six-year-old and is offered a zero-hours job with two days' notice and shifts that change every week; he turns it down. Not sanctionable; nobody could arrange childcare on those terms. (9) Bridget, 61, was made redundant after thirty years at the same firm, has applied for everything going for eighteen months and gets interviews, and is sanctioned for not applying outside a ninety-minute radius — she does not drive and her husband is disabled. Outrageous; she did everything that was asked of her.

I have a tentative principle, and I would like you to test it rather than admire it: what you deserve depends on whether you could have avoided needing help without giving up something we would never ask an ordinary taxpayer to give up; support is owed in proportion to how unavoidable your shortfall was, sanctions in proportion to how avoidable it was, and the cost of a sanction should fall on whoever could have avoided the shortfall. It seems to get Nadia, Marcus, Sam and Bridget right, and Dermot too, but I suspect it is quietly cheating on Tom against Priya, that it cannot say anything useful about the size of Kevin's sanction or about the children in cases five and ten, and that it makes a mess of Dermot against Aisha, since both are the same offence and I react to them completely differently. Please state the best principle or small set of principles that captures these verdicts, saying precisely which case forces each clause; apply it to cases I have not listed — the man with relapsing MS sanctioned for appointments he missed in a bad month, the woman who takes a job, is harassed, and quits after three weeks, the man offered only thirty-hour posts when he can manage eight, the woman whose payment is cut because a partner moved in and controls the money, the man who could double his income by moving two hundred miles but has a child in a specialist school — and then tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts the principle cannot keep, and whether I should give up the verdict or add a clause.
