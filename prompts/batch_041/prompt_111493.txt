I have recently been made chair of a hiring committee whose decisions are taken by majority vote, and the standard defence of majority rule — the Condorcet jury theorem, under which a group of deciders each better than chance converges on the truth as the group grows — has turned from a textbook curiosity into a live problem for me, because I suspect our procedure leaves us less reliable than any of us would be alone. I would like you to design the case that shows how that can happen, rather than summarise the literature about it.

The setting must be ordinary: a committee, a selection board, a clinical panel, with no exotic machinery and no more than a short paragraph of scene-setting. There must be at least nine deciders and a binary question with a fact of the matter. Every decider, taken individually, must be more likely right than wrong, and I do not want the result to come from smuggling in someone who is individually worse than a coin flip. All of the theorem's standard assumptions should hold — equal competence across deciders, sincere rather than strategic voting, a shared interest in getting it right, and conditional independence given each decider's private information — with exactly one exception: the independence of the deciders' errors from one another, which you will break by giving them a single shared item of evidence that each treats as telling and that is misleading with a stated probability.

What I want back is the case and then the arithmetic. Give me each decider's competence, the majority competence for the whole panel, and the competence that same majority would have in the counterfactual where the shared item is replaced by nine independent private items of comparable diagnostic strength, and show the calculation. I want the majority of nine to come out no more reliable than a single decider; tell me honestly how extreme the numbers have to be for that, and which quantity governs whether it happens at all. Then tell me what a defender of the theorem should say in reply, and take the standard cures one at a time — enlarging the panel, raising each decider's individual accuracy, and letting the panel deliberate before voting — saying which of them repairs the case, which leaves it untouched, and which makes matters worse, and in what sense of worse. Finish by telling me what would change your verdict: which single feature of the construction, if I altered it, would restore the usual convergence, and where in your own reasoning you would most expect an error to be hiding.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to a colleague. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
