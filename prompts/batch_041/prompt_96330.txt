I sit on a review panel that has to decide whether to renew a large multi-lab programme built around the free energy principle, and the panel has agreed to fix its standard of adequacy in advance, before we see any further results — otherwise we will simply rationalise whatever the programme has already produced. I have been asked to draft that standard, and to draft it in a form that two hostile camps could both sign: the camp that treats the framework as the long-sought unifying theory of brain and behaviour, and the camp that suspects it of being unfalsifiable, or true but empty.

Write the standard. I want the full specification of what a theory in this territory — predictive processing, active inference, and the free energy principle as the unifying claim above them — would have to deliver in order to count as an adequate theory of perception, action, and learning. Organise it as a list of requirements, and for each one give the case, result, or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a matter of taste.

Cover at least:

- the phenomena the theory has to explain, including those any account in this area is expected to cover and those that have historically been taken to distinguish it from its rivals;
- the distinctions it must not collapse: between the free energy principle and predictive coding, between a normative principle and an empirical hypothesis, between the computational and the algorithmic level, and between a system that actually minimises something and one that can merely be redescribed as if it did;
- the results and objections it has to survive, among them the charge that it cannot be falsified or is trivially satisfied, the dark-room problem, and the worry that its explanations generalise so far that they predict nothing a rival could not predict;
- the constraints imposed by its being offered as a theory of living systems rather than of a single subsystem.

For every requirement, say whether it is a genuine demand on the theory or the prejudice of one camp, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are negotiable.

Then flag the pairs that concern me most: requirements that appear jointly unsatisfiable, or that pull against each other — falsifiability against the breadth that makes the framework unifying is my first guess, but tell me whether I am right and whether there are worse ones. If some subset of the desiderata cannot be met together, that is the most useful thing you can tell me, so put it plainly.

Close with a short section — a few sentences per view, no more — assessing predictive coding, active inference, and the free energy principle in its broadest form against your list. I will do the detailed scorecard at the panel; I want the criteria from you, and only a rough sense of how the contenders fare.
