An interpretability group I advise wants to make mechanistic anomaly detection operational, and they have run into a conceptual wall before any empirical one. The intended workflow is this: during an audit they identify a mechanism responsible for some capability; later, under a shifted input distribution or after further training, they want to flag the cases in which the system is not running that same mechanism. The obstacle is that nobody can say what makes two episodes of computation instances of the same mechanism rather than two mechanisms that merely agree on the data, or one mechanism that has been subtly rewritten. They have five candidate criteria on the table — behavioral equivalence on a reference distribution; isomorphism of the causal graph recovered by circuit analysis; sameness of computational role with the implementation free to vary; sameness of the set of causally relevant features; and matching counterfactual profiles under intervention — and the criteria disagree precisely on the cases that matter, while each is cheap to state and expensive to apply.

I want a defended agenda, not a survey. Suppose the group has about three researcher-years and a theory-minded team that cannot lean on consulting the surrounding literature. Which open questions about mechanism individuation must be settled before an anomaly can even be defined, and in what order should they be taken up? For each, explain why it is upstream of the others — which downstream commitments it fixes — how tractable it is for such a team, and what concrete example or result within a year would move it up or down the ranking. Then tell me which of the five criteria you would discard outright, whether any single criterion could ever be sufficient and under what conditions, and how the ranking would change if the group cared only about catching deception-like failures rather than about mechanism identity in general. The ordering and the reasoning behind it matter far more to me than completeness.
