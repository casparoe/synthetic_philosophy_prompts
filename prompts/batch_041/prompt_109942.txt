I am a novelist, and for the past three years I have sat on the governing council of our guild — the association most working writers in this country belong to, which runs a legal-aid service for authors in contract disputes, a hardship fund, a translation prize, and a shared office that almost nobody uses.

We vote next month on whether to register the guild as a political party. Two years ago the electoral law changed, and registered parties now receive public funding, a seat on the broadcasting council, and a place on the national cultural commission; the registry closes on the thirtieth of next month, and there is no second window before the next general election. The faction in favour, led by a poet who has spent a decade on the legal-aid committee, argues that we have been petitioning for the public lending right for eleven years and that the only writers anyone hears are the ones with a party behind them. The faction against argues that a guild that becomes a party stops being a guild.

I am caught in the middle, and I keep returning to Simone Weil's short piece arguing that political parties should be abolished: her claim that a party is first of all an organisation with a will to survive and grow, that it must therefore hold a position on everything, that belonging to it obliges a person to be insincere about questions on which they have no knowledge and no business having an opinion, and that this habit corrodes the patient attention on which any honest work depends. I find the argument hard to answer. I also notice that the electoral commission requires a party to answer fifteen fixed questions before it can register — three about copyright, translation and the lending right, and twelve about energy prices, a border dispute, and the retirement age — and that our guild would have to answer all fifteen within the month.

Please write the written opinion I will circulate before the vote and read aloud at the meeting. Roughly a thousand words, continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; I will paste it into an email, and it should read the way a letter reads. Address it to the council, recommend a definite course of action with a date attached, and give the losing side its strongest argument rather than a strawman. I would like it to work out, rather than assert, whether the corruption Weil describes belongs to the form (registering, answering the fifteen questions, putting our name on a ballot) or to the sociology (a mass base, the pursuit of office, the appetite for winning), and whether the workaround our lawyer has proposed — a separate legal entity, a different name on the ballot, guild funds ring-fenced, members free to decline — actually answers her objection or merely relocates it, given that the same people would be doing the subscribing. It should also face squarely the thing I cannot dismiss: that the hardship fund and the legal-aid service are what the guild is for, that the broadcasting seat is worth a great deal to translators and midlist novelists, and that it is easy for a novelist whose books are published abroad to prefer purity on behalf of colleagues who cannot afford it. Weil herself never withdrew from the world, and I do not want her used as an alibi for doing nothing.

Argue with her; do not summarise her. Where you depart from her, say so and say why. No footnotes, no reading list, no citations — I have no way to check them and the council will not read them. Rely on the account of her position I have given above, and if the opinion needs details about our guild that I have not supplied, invent them and keep them consistent, without pretending to know more about our situation than I have told you.
