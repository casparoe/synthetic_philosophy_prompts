Every year at Christmas my family watches Its a Wonderful Life and every year my brother and I end up arguing about whether the ending actually proves anything. Then a few weeks ago I listened to a podcast about Leibniz, the guy who said God had to pick the best world out of all the ones he could have made, and that the evil and suffering in this one are worth it because of how good the whole thing is. The way it was explained, God isn't totting up happiness like a scoreboard. The best world is the one with the most harmony and variety, and some things just can't exist alongside each other, so the goods you get come with a particular set of costs and no other. There was also something about a happy fault, where the sin and the falling are supposed to be what makes room for something better, and about nothing happening without a reason why it's so and not otherwise.

So what I want is a proper reading of the movie through that. Does Bedford Falls count as a best possible world? Because all the film shows us is one alternative, Pottersville, where George was never born and Harry drowned as a kid and Mary never married, and saying things could have been worse isn't the same as saying this is the best there could be. The counterfactual also feels like cheating to me, since it keeps every single person the same and just deletes George, and I have a feeling Leibniz wouldn't let you slide people in and out of a world like that.

Things I can't work out on my own. Is George's misery at the bridge the happy fault, the film telling us his despair is somehow load-bearing? Or is that just a plot device. And what is Potter doing in the best of all possible worlds, he never gets punished and the movie just walks away from him. Also the ending, where everyone shows up with the money, feels like its paying George back for his troubles, and I don't think that's Leibniz's claim at all, I think his claim is about the whole and not about individuals getting squared up.

I'd like the reading to take both the film and Leibniz seriously, tell me which scenes really do dramatize the argument and which ones cheat, and finish with what the movie shows that the philosophy on its own doesn't. No need for a reading list, I just want the argument.
