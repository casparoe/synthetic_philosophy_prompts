I teach an upper-level seminar that pairs the philosophy of law with the logic of self-reference, and I am setting the take-home final. I want one essay question built around the miniature legal order below, which I have invented precisely so that it cannot be answered by reciting a stock account of how legal systems rest on some ultimate, unvalidated rule.

Rule A. This order may be changed only in the manner set out in Rule B.
Rule B. A change is adopted when two-thirds of those voting approve it.
Rule C. Rule A may be altered only by a unanimous vote, and Rule C itself may not be altered in any way.

A member proposes P: "Rule A is struck out and replaced by 'This order may be changed by simple majority'; and Rule C is repealed." P is put to the vote and carries with eighty per cent of those voting.

The question should require students to decide whether P is validly adopted, and to say what the answer reveals about whether an order can contain a genuine rule for changing its own rules. It should force them to separate the question of whether P satisfies the stated procedures from the question of whether those procedures can validate changes to themselves, and to diagnose the resulting difficulty as a contradiction, an infinite regress, or a gap that some ultimate rule must close. It should also draw out whether the two limbs of P can be severed, and whether Rule C's self-entrenchment is doing any work that Rule A does not already do.

Please give me the question as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific confusions that should cost marks. No citations or reading lists are needed.
