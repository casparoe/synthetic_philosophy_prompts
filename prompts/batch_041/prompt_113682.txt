**Q3 (10 points).** The argument from illusion is often presented as a threat to the view that in perception we are directly aware of ordinary material objects. (i) Set out the argument. (ii) Explain the disjunctivist response to it. In your answer, distinguish (a) the claim that a veridical perception and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination are experiences of the same fundamental kind from (b) the claim that the subject in the good case and the subject in the bad case begin from the same epistemic position. Say which of (a) and (b) the disjunctivist denies, and whether the response succeeds.

**Student answer.** The argument from illusion takes a case like a straight stick that looks bent when it is half under water. In that case the stick is not bent, but it looks bent, so what we are immediately aware of in having the experience cannot be the stick; it must be something else that really does have the property of being bent -- a sense-datum, or a mere appearance. The illusory experience and the ordinary perception of a straight stick are subjectively indistinguishable, and nothing in the experience itself marks the difference between them, so whatever is true of the one must be true of the other. It follows that in the veridical case too we are aware of a sense-datum rather than a material object, and direct realism is refuted.

Disjunctivism rejects the generalisation step. Its central claim is that a veridical perception and a hallucination are not experiences of the same fundamental kind even when they are subjectively indistinguishable: the veridical case is a genuine relation to an object, the hallucinatory case is not. So the disjunctivist denies (a). He also denies that the two cases are indistinguishable -- since on his view hallucination is not even a genuine perceptual experience, the subject in the bad case is in a position to know that no object is before him -- and that is what blocks the slide from the illusory case to the veridical one.

Notice that the same reply is available in the illusory case itself: you really do see the stick, and it merely looks bent because of the refraction at the water's surface, so the argument never gets going there. The hallucination case is just a more extreme version of the same situation, so the argument fails across the board and disjunctivism is vindicated.

As for (b): because disjunctivism is a thesis about the nature of experience, it entails that the good-case subject has something the bad-case subject lacks -- the object itself, as a factive reason -- so it denies (b) as well, and this is what makes it an answer to the sceptic rather than a restatement of common sense.
