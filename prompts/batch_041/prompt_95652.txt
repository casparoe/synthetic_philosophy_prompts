Germany's constitution obliges the state to protect animals — the clause was added in 2002 and is not confined to species useful to us — yet it is drafted as a state objective rather than a right: it binds the legislature, the administration and the courts, but no animal, and no one acting on an animal's behalf, can enforce it, and it is weighed against other constitutional goods rather than overriding them. The same legal order criminalises cruelty to animals, and the protection at issue is explicitly protection of the individual animal from avoidable suffering, pain and harm.

What view of animals' moral status does this arrangement presuppose? Kant's position — that we have no duties to animals, only duties concerning them, so that cruelty is forbidden because of what it does to human character — looks like it fits the "objective, not a right" form, but the insistence on the animals' own avoidable suffering seems to point somewhere else. Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say what would have to change in the clause, in the criminal law, and in ordinary farming and slaughter practice if animals were held to be owed duties directly rather than merely to be the objects of them.
