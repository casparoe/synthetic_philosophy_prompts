Role-wise: think of yourself as a formal epistemologist who's sat through a hundred of these and knows the difference between the objections that actually get made in a room and the ones that only exist in the literature.

The talk: I'm arguing that the standard way everyone frames "uncertain evidence" is confused. The usual story goes that sometimes experience doesn't hand you a proposition you're certain of, it just nudges your credences around, and Jeffrey conditionalization is the rule for that case. My line is that there's no such case. What actually happens when you catch a glimpse of something in bad light is that you come to be *certain* of something -- namely, of a fact about your own credences. You become certain that your credence in E is now q. And then Jeffrey conditionalization isn't a special rule at all, its just ordinary conditionalization on that proposition, carried out in an enriched space where propositions about your own credences live alongside propositions about the world. I claim that once you see it this way, a bunch of the standard complaints either dissolve or turn out to be complaints about something else entirely.
