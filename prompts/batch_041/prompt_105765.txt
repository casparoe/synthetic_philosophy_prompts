I'm not a philosopher, never took a course, and I work on the operations side of a company that scores insurance claims, so a chunk of my job is watching a rule engine make decisions that are moral decisions — who gets paid, who gets flagged, who gets a letter saying no — without anyone in the building calling them that. My buddy and I argue about this constantly. He says you can't program ethics, full stop. I say we already have, badly, and the real question is what a good version would even look like. So I dont want a tour of famous theories. I want the spec. Take "a theory of machine ethics" to mean a theory of how to actually get a system to do the right thing in the particular situation it's in — not a theory of what the right thing is in general — and write me the list of things such a theory has to deliver before it's worth anything, with the case or consideration behind each item.

Some things I keep bumping into that I suspect belong on the list, but I can't tell if they're real requirements or just my hang-ups, so sort them for me and add what I've missed: the system has to do something sensible when two of its own rules collide and nobody's awake to ask; it will meet situations its designers never imagined, and it has to not fall apart when it does; it has to be able to be wrong in a way that can be caught and fixed without the fix breaking ten other things; it has to cope with not knowing which consideration should win, not just with not knowing the facts; it comes in fleets, thousands of copies making the same judgment, so anything slightly off gets multiplied into something big; someone has to be able to read off afterward why it did what it did — I call that the auditor's requirement, and I honestly can't tell whether the explanation has to be true to how the machine actually worked or just useful to the person reading it; and it has to decide fast, faster than any deliberation I'd recognize as deliberation. Where I really want your thinking is on which of these are genuine requirements and which are just the habits of one camp, and whether some bunch of them can't all be true at once — if two of them pull against each other, that's the part I care about most, more than any scorecard. If a requirement knocks out a whole family of approaches, name the family in half a sentence, but skip the reading list and the citations, I won't chase them down.
