My decision theory and ethics reading group is mine for the full 90 minutes next month -- 14 people, mostly second-year PhD students, and I want to spend it on expected value fanaticism. Not a lecture on it. I want them to feel the pull of it in thier bones and then feel it go wierd.

The thing I want them to run into: if you maximize expected value, a 1-in-10 shot at 1000 points and a 1-in-10^100 shot at 10^102 points are exactly as good, and so is the sure thing worth 100. There's no principled rung on that ladder to get off at. So design a game -- chips or points, no real money -- that walks them up the ladder and makes them want to get off.

Some things I specifically want in it. They have to commit to a rule in writing before the ladder starts, something like "we'll take any bet with a chance above such-and-such," and then hit the rung where thier own rule makes them turn down the best option on the table. At least one rung should get resolved for real with an actual random draw, so somebody wins and everyone who refused looks like an idiot, and then a second draw where the winner looks like an idiot instead. I want the aggregate picture next to the one-shot picture too: give them a round where they're setting a policy for a thousand people over a thousand repetitions, where the absurd rung is obviously the right call, right alongside the one-shot case where it's obviously silly, and let them notice they can't hold both. And finish with the mugging thing -- someone offers a huge payoff for a small payment, you pay, and he just keeps upping it.

No names until the debrief. The debrief should map each round onto a piece of the problem, be honest that the game can't reproduce the infinite-payoff version (that part I'll do on the board), and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- especially the ways they'll try to weasel out, like insisting the big payoffs aren't real or just refusing to engage with the ladder, and what I should do when that happens.
