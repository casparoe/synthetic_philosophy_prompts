I would like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in social choice theory who have spent decades talking past each other about the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and the meaning of strategy-proofness.

The first camp — call it the robust-impossibility camp — holds that the theorem is a deep and durable result: strategy-proofness is essentially incompatible with reasonable preference aggregation, and every proposed escape (lotteries, transfers, restricting the domain of admissible preferences, weakening dominant-strategy incentive compatibility to Bayesian incentive compatibility, moving to Nash implementation) buys strategy-proofness only by sacrificing something else — ex-post efficiency, budget balance, individual rationality, or the generality that made the problem worth posing. On this reading the theorem behaves like a conservation law, and the constructive response is to stop hunting for a manipulation-proof rule and start designing institutions that manage manipulation.

The second camp — call it the parameter-regime camp — holds that the theorem is a precise statement about a narrow configuration: deterministic social choice functions, ordinal preferences, unrestricted domain, onto ranges, and dominant-strategy incentive compatibility. Change any of those parameters and the impossibility either disappears or turns into a tradeoff you can price and design around. On this reading the robust-impossibility reading is an over-generalization that inflates a theorem about one formalization of manipulation into a verdict on the possibility of good aggregation.

Write the document the two camps could actually co-sign. Organize it as follows.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign without either feeling it has conceded ground: about the theorem's formal content and its assumptions; about what it does and does not rule out; and about which adjacent results both accept as correct, including Arrow's theorem, the result that strategy-proof randomized schemes must be lotteries over dictatorial or otherwise degenerate schemes, the bilateral-trade impossibility for efficient, budget-balanced, individually rational trade under incomplete information, and the existence of non-dictatorial strategy-proof rules on restricted domains such as single-peaked preferences. Keep every claim substantive enough that a reader could deny it.

2. Disagreements. A table in which each row states one remaining disagreement, with one column giving the formulation the first camp would accept as its own view and the next column doing the same for the second camp, in language neither side would call a caricature. Flag any row you believe is verbal rather than substantive.

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each, state the crux in neutral wording, say which camp's position would move and in which direction, and name a concrete argument, example, or formal result that both camps would agree bears on it — with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Candidates you should consider, though you may add or replace: whether the familiar pattern of tradeoffs is one theorem or a family of unrelated ones; whether dominant-strategy incentive compatibility or Bayesian incentive compatibility is the right formalization of non-manipulability; whether randomization is a genuine escape or a retreat; whether unrestricted domain is doing normative work given that real electorates have structured preferences; and whether manipulation is even the right thing to be pessimistic about.

4. Escapology scorecard. For each escape route — lotteries, transfers, restricted domains, Bayesian incentive compatibility, Nash implementation — a row stating what it buys, what it costs, and whether the first camp can classify the cost as the same kind of cost the theorem warned about or the second camp can classify it as a different problem altogether.

I care about one concrete case and want both camps to commit in advance. The case is allocating a fixed pool of public-good funding among proposals when participants can express intensity of preference through a limited budget of voice credits — a quadratic-voting-style mechanism that is deliberately not strategy-proof in the dominant-strategy sense but is designed so that the price of influence disciplines misreporting. Have both camps state what they expect to happen to strategic behaviour under such a mechanism, what each would count as evidence for its own reading, and what each would count as evidence against it.

Close with a short section headed What would change my mind, in which you identify the argument or consideration that would lead you to a different verdict on which cruxes are the real ones, say where the residual disagreement between the two camps is most likely to be verbal rather than substantive, and point to the places in this document where your drafting is most likely to have failed — a formulation only one side would actually sign, or a crux that is really vacuous — saying which parts you would revise first if you had to defend the document.
