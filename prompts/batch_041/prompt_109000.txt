I recently moved into a mechanistic interpretability group, and within the month I have to decide whether a chapter of my dissertation can keep using claims of the form "the model represents X," or whether that vocabulary has to go. At our last meeting a colleague put an argument to me that I still can't answer, and I would rather not settle it by attrition.

(1) A representation claim is supposed to be a claim about the network — about what is going on inside it — not merely a report of what a researcher finds it convenient to say.
(2) The activations of a trained network carry a great deal of information about the input. Nearly any property of the input, including properties the network was never trained on and properties that are artefacts of the dataset, can be recovered from a mid-layer activation vector by a decoder: linear probes do this for many properties, and inversion procedures recover the input itself.
(3) So if decodability is what it takes to be represented, the network represents an open-ended set of features, including disjunctions and idiosyncratic properties no one would call part of its picture of the world.
(4) A claim that is true of every sufficiently wide trained network, and true of features no one cares about, says nothing about any particular network. So decodability alone cannot be what representation amounts to.
(5) The natural repair is to require causal role: a feature is represented only if behaviour depends on it, as shown by intervening — patching, ablating, swapping activations — and seeing the output move.
(6) But an intervention is itself just an experiment on activations, and what it shows is again that information present in the activations predicts the output. The verdict is still read off the activations by a decoder of ours; the causal test is the decodability test with more steps.
(7) Hence representation talk is either trivial, by (3)–(4), or circular, by (5)–(6). No reading makes "the model represents X" both substantive and about the network. The vocabulary is ours, not the model's, and should be dropped.

I want to keep the claim that representation talk is substantive and about the network rather than about us — interpretability seems worthless without it — but not merely because the alternative is unpleasant. Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If the answer is (6), I need to know what would make an intervention more than a decoder in disguise; if it is (2) or (3), I need to know what the evidence actually licenses.
