I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, logic, or game theory, and I would like help settling an argument that I overheard rather than took part in. Please take on the role of an expert in epistemic logic and the theory of common knowledge — someone who can reason rigorously about what agents know about what other agents know — and think the problem through step by step before committing to a verdict. I would rather be told that my framing is confused, or that part of what I am asking cannot be answered as posed, than receive a smooth non-answer, so please say so plainly if that is the case.

Background. My team has a weekly puzzle hour. Last week's puzzle was this. A group of children sits in a circle; each child has either a clean or a muddy forehead. Each child can see every other child's forehead but not their own. No one speaks except a parent, who announces once, publicly, that at least one of the children has a muddy forehead, and who then repeatedly asks whether anyone now knows their own forehead is muddy, with each child raising a hand if and only if they do. All the children are perfectly logical and hear every announcement. The answer to the puzzle is that if exactly k children are muddy, those k children raise their hands at the k-th round of asking, and no one raises a hand before then.

Two colleagues, Marcus and Dana, kept arguing about it after everyone else had drifted off. As best I can reproduce the exchange:

1. Dana said the parent's announcement is doing no work at all. Every child can already see the others' foreheads, so whenever more than one child is muddy, every child already knows that at least one child is muddy. The announcement therefore tells them nothing they did not already know, and if the hands go up only after it, the puzzle must be cheating — smuggling in information through the back door.

2. Marcus said the announcement does not add any fact about anyone's forehead; it converts a fact that everyone already knew into something that everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on. He kept using the term common knowledge and insisted the distinction is technical rather than verbal. Without it, he said, each child's reasoning about what the others would have concluded in earlier rounds cannot even begin, so the announcement is precisely what makes the induction run. He added that in the case of a single muddy child the announcement is even new information of the ordinary kind, so Dana's claim that it adds nothing at all cannot be right as stated.

3. Dana said that if the puzzle works only because everyone is assumed to reason perfectly, and to know that everyone else reasons perfectly, and to know that they know that, then the load is being carried by that assumption rather than by the mud. Real people do not share that kind of mutual certainty about one another's reasoning, she said, so the puzzle describes an impossible world and tells us nothing about any real group. Marcus replied that the assumption is real and that its failure is the interesting part; Dana answered that calling an assumption the interesting part is what one says when the assumption is doing all the work.

4. Marcus then made a claim I do not have the background to evaluate: that in games, a public announcement of something everyone already knew can change what people do — can even settle which of several equally attractive outcomes everyone ends up in — precisely because it changes what everyone knows about everyone else's knowledge, without changing anyone's knowledge of the facts. Dana said this is incoherent: if nothing about the situation and no one's information about the situation changed, nothing is left that could change behavior, and Marcus is merely redescribing a change of information as a change in knowledge about knowledge. Marcus then raised a distributed-systems problem that I do know: two parties trying to agree on a time over a channel that can silently drop messages, where no finite back-and-forth of acknowledgments can guarantee that both sides are certain the other is committed. He said this shows how hard it is to obtain the thing the puzzle hands out for free, and that the puzzle's clean public announcement is a fiction; Dana said that if the announcement is a fiction then so is the puzzle, and Marcus said no, the fiction is the point, because it isolates something that is normally unavailable.

Please adjudicate this the way you would referee a formal dispute. First, set out in your own words what a child knows at each round and how that changes, so I can see the machinery. Then, for each of the four moves above, say what is right in it and what is wrong or overstated, and justify the rating — do not just tell me who won. Then answer the three questions that seem to me to be underneath the argument: (a) is Dana right that the announcement adds no information, or is Marcus right that the distinction he is drawing is real — and if it is real, say precisely what changes when the announcement is made and what does not; (b) does the children's reasoning really depend on an assumption about everyone's rationality, and if so exactly how strong that assumption has to be — as strong as Dana says, or weaker; and (c) is Marcus's claim that a public announcement can change behavior without changing anyone's knowledge of the facts true, and if so, give me a concrete case small enough to check by hand, or if it is false, explain what he is confusing. Finally, tell me which of the two is closer to right overall, and what both are missing — the consideration that neither raised but that a careful treatment would.
