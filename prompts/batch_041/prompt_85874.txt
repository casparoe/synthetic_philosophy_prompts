I've come round to the occasionalist account of causation associated with al-Ghazālī's attack on the philosophers' necessary connection: no created thing has any causal power of its own, God is the sole agent of every event, and what we call causal necessity is only God's settled habit of producing one thing after another — a habit that can be broken at will, which is what a miracle is. Tell me what would count against it. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and mark which of them an occasionalist would themselves concede as counting against the view rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding of it. Three things I'm especially unsure about. First, whether the position can survive the loss of the background picture it seems to rest on — discrete moments of time, substances carrying no intrinsic powers, an atomism of bodies — and whether, if that picture goes, the view loses only its motivation or its truth as well. Second, whether it explains too much: any regularity, and any suspension of one, is equally consistent with God's habit, so the view seems to predict nothing about which regularities we should find, and I want to know whether that is a genuine cost or a feature of any theistic account of causation. Third, whether the standard move of distinguishing God's creating an act from the creature's acquiring (kasb) it rescues human agency or merely renames the difficulty — and, relatedly, whether the position is finally distinguishable from mere conservationism, which leaves creatures real powers and keeps God only as the source of their being, or whether the difference between the two is verbal. Close by saying what would change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
