I am preparing a graduate seminar on Fear and Trembling and I would like you to construct an argument, not a survey. Here is the thesis I want defended: that the teleological suspension of the ethical, as Johannes de Silentio presents it, suspends only the universal — the shared and publicly articulable moral order, of the broadly Hegelian kind the book has in view — and not ethical requirement as such; and that once this distinction is drawn with sufficient care, the standard objection that Kierkegaard is merely a divine-command irrationalist in disguise loses most of its force. I want you to argue for that reading, and to argue for it in a way that a reader who already knows the book well would find genuinely contestable.

To make the argument bite, I want you to do several things at once. Distinguish, as precisely as you can, between suspending the universal and suspending the ethical; explain why the absolute relation to God, on this reading, functions not as a licence but as a demand of a more exacting kind; and confront the strongest version of the opposing view, namely that the reading I have proposed simply relocates the problem rather than solving it, since an obligation that cannot in principle be made intelligible to anyone else is no obligation at all. Bring to bear what we might call a publicity condition on moral justification — the thought that a moral reason must be shareable in principle — and then either show that the suspension can satisfy that condition or argue that the condition itself must be relaxed for the case at hand, saying clearly which you think is correct and why. Say something about the role of Abraham's silence, and about whether it is essential to the structure of the case or merely a feature of the narrative. Finally, consider the objection that the position makes Abraham's act unteachable, and explain what work, if any, the exemplar is still doing once the universal has been set aside.

Write the response as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would set out a philosophical argument in a letter to a colleague.
