I've been in a metaphysics reading group and I'm stuck on this. I've become conviced that grounding is a single, unified, non-causal relation of metaphysical explanation—the kind of thing that holds between the fact that the table is solid and the fact that its molecules are arranged thus and so—and that fundamentality is just the limit case of it, where the chain of grounding stops. I also think it's irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, and well-founded: nothing grounds itself, the grounded never grounds its ground, if A grounds B and B grounds C then A grounds C, and every chain bottoms out in something fundamental. I want to know what would count against this package. Not just the usual it's obscure or it's not causation complaint; I want the specific cases, arguments, or formal results that should make me give up or at least qualify it.

Some things I've heard that worry me: cases where two facts seem to ground each other, like maybe the existence of a whole and the existence of its parts, or the fact that a is identical to b and the fact that b is identical to a; cases where a fact seems to ground itself, like the fact that something is self-identical; cases where grounding looks like it fails transitivity, so A grounds B and B grounds C but A doesn't ground C; and the possibility of an infinitely descending chain of grounds with no fundamental level, which would kill well-foundedness. Are these real defeators or are they just puzzles that a defender can absorb? I'd like them ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed grounding theorist would themselves concede as counting against the view. Also, is there a version of the view that survives all of them, or does saving it require so many qualifications that it stops being the original claim? I'm especially interested in whether the unity of grounding—the idea that it's one relation rather than a family of different dependence relations—is the part that's most exposed. I don't need names or citations, just the arguments.
