I'm writing an essay on The Handmaid's Tale and I keep circling the epilogue. Here's the lens I want to use, and its a bit homemade, so let me spell it out: silences don't only get imposed on the historical record at the end, by censorship. They enter at four points. When the thing happens and someone decides it's worth noting, or not. When the surviving traces get assembled into a story. When a later reader retrieves them. And when we decide, in hindsight, what it all meant. Power sits at all four, and the last one is the sneakiest because it wears the costume of scholarship. The record is never the past anyway, it's what made it through the filter, and the filter is shaped by who's allowed to write, keep, and hand things down.

Read the novel through that. The epilogue is the obvious target: the future symposium, the professors working from a bundle of cassette tapes, guessing at her name, her motives, whether she got out at all. What is Atwood doing there, is it a satire of the archive's confidence, or is she also implicating the reader, who likewise has nothing but her voice and wants to know how it ends? But I don't want a reading that stops at the epilogue. Gilead controls what gets written down and kept, and yet the whole book exists because somebody talked into a recorder, so the novel seems to say the unofficial channel is where the truth goes. Push back on that if it's too neat. And her narration is itself a reconstruction, she admits she's rearranging and filling gaps, talking to a 'you' who may not exist, so some of the silences may be memory's doing rather than power's, and I want the essay honest about where my framework over-explains.

Which scenes should I go to, where does the novel complicate the four-stage picture instead of illustrating it, and does it actually add something to how we think about who gets recorded that an argument alone wouldn't? Don't quote at me, I have the book. Last line, one sentence, starting 'Bottom line:': does the novel hold out any way out of archival silence, or is the point that there isn't one?
