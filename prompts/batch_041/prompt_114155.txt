I run a monthly discussion evening for adults — twelve or so people, mixed backgrounds, no philosophy training, ninety minutes in a hired room with a whiteboard, a stack of index cards, and a tin of tokens I use for keeping score in other games. Next month I want to do the puzzle I keep circling back to: the vampire case, where someone is offered a change so total that she cannot know in advance what it would be like to want what vampires want, and so cannot work out whether it is a good deal. I understand why the case is famous, but it is rigged for a room like mine — nobody there will ever face a vampire, so the puzzle stays a puzzle and everyone nods and goes home. What I want instead is an evening in which the twelve of them actually choose, with something small but real riding on the outcome, and discover for themselves that the ordinary procedure — work out what you want, estimate how each option serves it, take the best — has no grip here. The thing I want them to feel is not that we cannot predict the future, which they already believe, but something stranger: that one of the options would change the person doing the choosing, so the preferences they would use to weigh it are the very thing at issue. I have read that the standard repair is to value the discovery itself, deciding that what you want is to find out what it is like, and I can see that this works by changing the subject; it certainly does not help twelve people in a room on a Tuesday. For what it is worth, my interest is not purely theoretical — a friend of mine has spent a year trying to decide whether to leave a job he has held for eleven years, and keeps asking me what he wants, which I have come to think is the wrong question asked in the right words.

Design that evening for me. Think it through properly before you commit to a mechanism, then give me the whole thing: the setting and materials, the exact rules, the payoff scheme (points or tokens, no real money), a timeline for the ninety minutes, and the roles and constraints that do the work. The transformation has to happen inside the activity rather than be explained to them: whatever the transformed participants come to value must be genuinely closed to everyone else, and the closure should follow from what that value is rather than from a gag order I impose — I would rather the untransformed be unable to tell whether you would have to be there is evasion or a real limit. No vampires, no pills that grant new senses, and please do not rebuild it around the standard cases (a child, a cochlear implant, a war); I want a mechanism that fits ordinary adults who have no idea they are inside a thought experiment. It also should not be a mystery box with unknown contents — that is just risk, which they understand perfectly well — and it should not be a trust exercise in me, or anything that rewards them for guessing what I am thinking.

Then give me the debrief: moment by moment, what happened and which part of the problem each moment corresponds to, with the vocabulary and the names introduced only at the end, since I do not want the terminology doing work the game should have done. Two further things I need. First, a blunt list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what to do about each. Second, an honest account of what this activity cannot reproduce about the real phenomenon and what I therefore have to supply myself in the debrief. Be straight with me on that last point: if what I am asking for cannot survive being turned into a ninety-minute game with twelve strangers — if it is bound to collapse into a trick, or into a lecture with cards — say so plainly rather than quietly designing around it. You know this literature well enough to be exact about it, and you have run rooms like this before; approach it as a designer first and a philosopher second. Start with one sentence naming the mechanism you would build, then give me the design, with no preamble and without restating my question.
