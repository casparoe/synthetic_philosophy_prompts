I run a weekly reading group on AI safety and alignment for about sixteen graduate students, split roughly evenly between machine learning and philosophy. A session runs ninety minutes; I have a whiteboard, a projector, index cards, and a pair of dice, and no budget. Design a single in-class game for one session whose rules, roles, and scoring make the participants collide with the difference between an average-case guarantee and a worst-case guarantee before I name it. Most of them can recite the distinction; almost none of them have felt it. The target intuition is sharper than robustness is hard. I want them to feel, in the order I have listed:

1. that a system tuned to look good on the data it has seen can be made arbitrarily bad by a single input;
2. that a guarantee is only ever a guarantee relative to a stated class of inputs or adversary, and that as that class swells toward everything, the only surviving guarantee is vacuous;
3. that patching against a known adversary is cheap and buys almost nothing against a fresh one;
4. that randomization changes what can be guaranteed at all.

The session should end on the alignment-specific point, which I want them to arrive at themselves rather than hear from me: that the trouble is not that the average is miscomputed, but that the thing being deployed will itself be an optimizer, or will sit in a world that selects for the tail, so that the average case was the wrong quantity to have optimized in the first place.

Requirements:

- Give me the full ruleset: the roles, what each team can see and when, the exact sequence of rounds, and a payoff and scoring scheme that uses no money and no computers, only paper, cards, and dice.
- The activity must be playable by people who know only basic probability. Do not require them to know the minimax theorem, Yao's principle, the no-free-lunch theorems, or any other named result. If randomization is one of your rounds, the participants have to be able to discover its effect from the scores alone.
- Do not build it on the well-worn example of adversarial perturbations of an image classifier, and do not use the framing of a malicious red team poisoning a single labeled input. The mechanism should be one they are unlikely to have seen, and the adversary should be as impersonal as the logic permits.
- Include a timeline for the ninety minutes, the materials list, and the exact wording of the two or three instructions I read aloud. Keep those minimal; the rules should carry the lesson, not my voice.
- Include a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the corresponding part of the distinction and states plainly where each of points 1 through 4 showed up in the scores.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, including the objection I expect to hear most often, that the opposing team only won because it knew our rule, and how to turn that objection into the point rather than defusing it.
- Be honest about what the game cannot reproduce. I want a short section on which feature of the average-case and worst-case gap in real alignment work the activity necessarily leaves out, and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief.

Please do not include citations, reading lists, or pointers to papers. I want the design to stand on its own.
