You are a political philosopher who also writes literary fiction, and you are good at making an abstract institutional question matter to one particular person on one particular afternoon. I want you to write a short story about selection by lot — the idea, argued in various forms by various people, that offices filled by election might better be filled by drawing names at random from the whole population: because a random sample mirrors the citizenry in a way no electorate ever does, because it strips out campaigning and fundraising, and because it spreads the burden and the honor of governing evenly across everyone.

To keep this from becoming a story anyone could have written from general knowledge, I am handing you an invented institution, and you should treat its details as fixed.

The legislature of the city is filled by lot. Every adult resident is in the pool. Terms run three years. Service is compulsory: if your name comes up, you serve, unless you are dead or incapacitated. Some decades ago the city added one clause to the lottery statute — a seat won by lot may be transferred, for any price, to any other adult resident, provided the transfer is registered with the clerk before the first session. The buyer serves the full term and holds every power of the office. The official rationale was compassion: service can wreck a small business, a career, a marriage; if someone would rather sell the seat than serve it, who is the state to forbid a mutually beneficial trade? In practice the market has grown teeth. Brokers now write contracts before the draw, paying residents a modest sum in exchange for the right to the seat if their name should come up. And the city statisticians still publish, every year, the comforting sentence that the chamber is a random sample of the citizenry — a sentence that is true of the draw and false of the chamber, which now consists mostly of the comfortably off, with a thin trickle of the poor passing through on their way to a sale.

Before you write a word of the story, spend a page thinking on the page. What is sortition supposed to accomplish, and which of those goals survive the transfer rule? Who is helped by the market, who is harmed, and where do the market's defenders have a point that a critic ought to concede? What would a person in this city actually feel at the moment the envelope arrives? Do not show me this thinking as the story; it is scaffolding, and the story must stand without it.

Then write the story, 1,500 to 2,500 words, under these constraints:

- Center it on one concrete decision made by one character — someone whose name has just been drawn, or who has just signed a pre-draw contract, or who is weighing whether to buy. The philosophy must be lodged in the decision, not narrated around it.
- Include at least one character who defends the market sincerely and well, someone who would not be embarrassed to make the argument in public, and at least one who is honestly uncertain.
- Nobody delivers an essay on democratic theory. No lecture, no Socratic dialogue, no character who exists only to be corrected. If a character makes an argument, let it come out bent by self-interest, or half-formed, or in the middle of doing something else.
- Do not resolve it. End in the middle of the moral weather, not with a verdict.
- Avoid the two obvious moves: a thinly disguised ancient Athens, and a dystopia in which the lottery turns out to be a death sentence.
- Keep the prose concrete — money, weather, the clerk's office, the smell of the room. Resist allegory.

After the story, add a short afterword of 150 to 250 words, plainly written and clearly separate from the fiction, naming the tension the story was built around, saying which way if either you think it should be resolved, and explaining why the story itself resists that resolution. Write the afterword in your own words; no citations or reading lists are needed.

Take your time. Think carefully about what the lottery is for before you decide what it costs.
