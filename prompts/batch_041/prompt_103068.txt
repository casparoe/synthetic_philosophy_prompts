Three affirmative-action regimes are routinely filed under a single heading, though on inspection they appear to presuppose rather different things.

(i) Norway requires that the board of every public limited company be composed of at least forty per cent of each sex, with dissolution of the company as the sanction for failure. The requirement bites on the composition of the board itself, not on any individual appointment; no woman need show that she personally suffered disadvantage, and no particular woman need have been passed over.

(ii) India reserves a fixed share of places in higher education and public employment for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, roughly in proportion to their share of the population, and a further share for Other Backward Classes. The beneficiary categories are hereditary and defined by caste; the traditional justification is reparation for a long history of exclusion; and yet within the OBC category, and increasingly in discussion of the others, the relatively advantaged are screened out as a creamy layer.

(iii) Until recently, selective American universities practised race-conscious admissions, justified not as compensation to anyone but by the educational value of a diverse student body: no fixed numbers, no individual reparation, race weighed as one factor among many in a holistic comparison of applicants, and the benefit described as accruing to the whole class rather than to the minority student admitted. That practice has now been struck down, while preferences for the children of alumni survive untouched.

Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of each. What does each presuppose about who may count as a proper beneficiary — that a group must be disadvantaged, or merely underrepresented? Does it presuppose that the good distributed is a benefit to the recipient, a positional good whose value consists in its distribution, or a benefit to third parties? Does it treat the relevant injustice as historical, as ongoing, or as neither? Does the Norwegian scheme presuppose anything at all about individual desert, or only about the composition of a body?

Then do four things. First, separate what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with. Second, say whether the three are mutually consistent, or whether they harbour incompatible views about what makes a group a fitting beneficiary. Third, identify where each practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same arrangement. Fourth, state what would have to change if the implicit view were replaced by a rival — if the boardroom quota were rebuilt as compensation, say, or the caste reservation as a diversity measure.

Two particular puzzles to address along the way. Does the means-testing inside the Indian scheme commit it to ongoing rather than historical disadvantage, and is that consistent with a reparative rationale? And if board seats are largely positional goods, does a forty per cent requirement deliver any absolute gain to women, or does it only redistribute relative standing?

Finish by sorting the apparent inconsistencies into those that are genuinely philosophical and those that are residue of law, administration, or political compromise.
