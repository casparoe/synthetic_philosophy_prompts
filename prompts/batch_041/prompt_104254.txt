Consider a practical pattern that recurs across animal-welfare advocacy, reducetarian guidance, and institutional dining policy. To reduce animal suffering, guides and institutions replace beef with chicken, pork, or eggs, on the reasoning that a beef cow is a larger, longer-lived, and plausibly more cognitively complex animal than a broiler chicken, so that a kilogram of beef embodies more suffering than a kilogram of chicken. Yet the same guides, when arguing that plant-based diets reduce harm, frequently quantify harm as animal deaths per calorie or per kilogram of protein, and observe that a single cow yields hundreds of kilograms of edible meat while a single chicken yields one or two, so that beef involves far fewer deaths per calorie than chicken. And these same actors typically leave out of the ledger the field animals — mice, birds, and above all insects — killed by the combines, pesticides, and habitat clearing that crop agriculture requires; those deaths are treated as either not counting or not worth counting.

I would like an analysis of the philosophical commitments this cluster of practices presupposes. Address the following:

First, what must be true for two metrics — suffering per animal or per unit of meat, and deaths per calorie — to function as live options within the same argument, and what does the readiness to move between them, or to hold both at once, reveal about the underlying theory of welfare? Does the substitution advice presuppose that suffering scales with the number or the mass of lives, or only with the duration and intensity of experience, and are those two pictures compatible?

Second, what theory of moral status does the exclusion of crop-cultivation deaths presuppose? Distinguish at least three possible bases: that field and wild animals have lesser standing; that incidental, unintended deaths matter less than deaths that are the point of the practice; and that the deaths are genuine but not tractable to reduce. Say which of these the practice actually requires, and which are merely convenient.

Third, is the overall pattern internally consistent? If a single consistent theory can underwrite it, state that theory; if not, identify precisely where the inconsistency lies and what would have to change in the advice or in the metric for the pattern to be defensible.

Throughout, be explicit about what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits well with, and flag the points at which it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several incompatible theories would each license the same practical guidance.
