I am assembling an internal handbook for a small two-party mediation practice — divorcing spouses dividing assets, two founders splitting equity, two departments dividing a shared budget — and I want the handbook to commit to one explicit allocation rule rather than leave the facilitator to improvise, and to be candid about what that commitment costs. The rule I have settled on is the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. As I understand it: given a convex set of feasible utility pairs and a disagreement point describing what happens if talks fail, one finds each side's ideal point — the highest utility that side can obtain from any feasible agreement — draws the segment from the disagreement point to that ideal point, and takes the point where the segment crosses the Pareto frontier. It is the solution characterized by Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance under independently rescaling either side's utility, and a monotonicity axiom (roughly, a side never does worse when its own prospects improve and the other side's do not); it does not satisfy independence of irrelevant alternatives.

Please describe the most important implications of adopting this rule for how we should run our mediations. I want the practical consequences drawn out rather than a recitation of the rule's formal properties, organized around the decisions a facilitator actually makes. In particular, I need to know what follows for:

1. the menu of settlements we write on the board, and whether a facilitator who adds or removes an option is thereby taking a side;
2. how we elicit and record each side's aspiration, given that the ideal point enters the rule directly;
3. whether we may reassure both sides that widening the range of possible settlements cannot make either of them worse off;
4. whether the rule obliges us to compare the two sides' utilities on a common scale, or whether each side's own scale suffices;
5. what we should do when the feasible set is uncertain or disputed, and what changes when a third party joins the table.

A case we have argued about: Alice's and Bob's utilities are each measured on their own scale, the disagreement point is zero for both, and the currently feasible settlements are those giving each at most 1, with the two utilities summing to no more than 1.5. Under the rule, each side receives 0.75. A new settlement has now been proposed that gives Alice nothing and Bob 1.5. One of my colleagues says this cannot matter, since the rule will not select that settlement. Work out what the rule actually does with it, and then say what that shows about how a facilitator should treat the agenda.

No references needed — I want the reasoning and its practical consequences, in a memo of roughly 800 words.
