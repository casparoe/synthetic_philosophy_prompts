I am giving a paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop next month in defence of a relational conception of personhood — roughly, the view that being a person is not a matter of possessing certain intrinsic capacities but of standing in relations of mutual answerability with others, so that personhood is sustained, and not merely recognised, by the continuing address of those around one. The centrepiece is a case I have been developing. A woman in late-stage dementia no longer recognises her daughter; the daughter nevertheless continues to address her as 'you', keeps promises she made to her, asks her forgiveness for old wrongs, and holds her to agreements they once made. My claim is that this continued address does not merely express a belief about the woman's personhood but holds her in it — and, crucially, that it is not therefore arbitrary, because it answers to the woman's own history of participation in that practice of answerability, so the sustaining relation is answerable to something rather than conjured at will. I am aware this is doing a lot of work with one case and that I have not fully said what happens when the sustaining relations run out.

The audience will be three groups, and I need to be ready for each of them in its own vocabulary. There will be clinical ethicists, who will want to know what any of this changes at the bedside, whether a view that locates personhood in others' address gives caregivers a power I have not acknowledged, and whether I am romanticising relationships that are in practice coercive, resentful or simply exhausted. There will be liberal political philosophers, for whom the sentence that personhood is sustained by others will sound like personhood being conferred at will — a threat to the moral equality of persons and to the very idea of rights that do not depend on being loved or remembered. And there will be two or three people who work on African relational thought, who will be alert to the possibility that I am borrowing a communal conception of the person and quietly stripping out what makes it communal, leaving a picture in which a lone caregiver's attitude, rather than a community's practice, does the metaphysical work.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from these three groups, rather than the generic ones any audience would raise, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do if I fumble it, each phrased the way the group that would raise it would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than defend, and which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly why I am hoping it. Keep the whole thing usable at a lectern — no essays, no literature surveys, and nothing that presumes I can look anything up mid-session. End with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' naming the one objection most likely to sink the paper if I have no answer to it.
