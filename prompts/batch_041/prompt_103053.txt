I have been told to strike the phrase 'national character' from a manuscript, and before I concede the point I want to know exactly what I would be giving up. Treat this as a specification exercise, not an essay. Give me two lists of desiderata: first, what any adequate account of national character as a descriptive notion would have to deliver; second, what any adequate account of the permissibility of generalising about peoples would have to deliver. Fix the purpose narrowly and hold me to it: I care about national character only insofar as it might explain why formally similar institutions behave differently in different countries, so adequacy is relative to that explanatory job, and whatever that job does not require I will treat as optional. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, say whether it is non-negotiable for my purpose or a preference of one camp, and then flag any subset of the requirements that you think cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want.

For the descriptive list, I expect at least: an account of the bearer (population, culture, state, institutions) and of why the nation is the right reference class rather than region, class, cohort, language or religion; a decision about whether the trait is a distribution over individuals or a property of a collective not reducible to its members; a statement of the quantifier and the comparison class in any character claim, since such claims are comparative and cannot be refuted by counterexamples alone; a rule for when an aggregate correlation licenses an inference about an individual, and what to do when the correlation reverses under partitioning; an account of transmission and stability that survives mass immigration, generational turnover and institutional reform without either evaporating or becoming unfalsifiable; consistency with the fact that most variation lies within groups, that borders are recent and partly arbitrary, and that traits are multidimensional and only loosely correlated; and a statement of what would count as evidence against a given character claim. Do not let the list collapse into a licence for a single general factor unless you can defend one.

For the normative list, I expect the distinction between the question whether a generalisation is warranted and the question whether one may assert or act on it; an account of what makes the wrong cases wrong (treating a tendency as an inner nature, self-fulfilling effects, the dignitary harm of being pre-judged, who bears the cost of error); a line that forbids the bad uses without forbidding the uses that medicine, insurance and translation depend on; and attention to context and speaker (private belief versus public assertion, insider versus outsider, generalising about the powerful versus the powerless).

Then say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and give me three or four sentences, no more, on how the main families of view fare against the list — the trait-at-a-distance cultural-psychology view, the eliminativist view that the notion is a myth, the thin view that it is only a label for institutional and social facts, and the view that it is a matter of how a people interprets itself. Keep the weight on the requirements; the scorecard I can do myself.
