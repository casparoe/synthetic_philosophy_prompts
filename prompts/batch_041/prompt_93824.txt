Take what I'll call a pre-emptive directive. Not the ordinary kind, where someone writes down 'no feeding tube if I'm in a persistent vegetative state.' The interesting kind is where the person is aiming straight at her future self: she writes that she knows that later on she'll be cheerful, will say she's happy, will insist on staying alive, and that none of that is to be counted. She's predicting that her later self will repudiate her values, and she's trying to make the repudiation irrelevant in advance. Argue that this kind of directive is self-undermining: its content concedes that the later self is a locus of judgement in its own right (thats what makes the prediction possible at all), and once you've conceded that, you can't get the authority back by writing 'and don't listen to him' into the document. So a pre-emptive directive has no more authority than an ordinary one, and probably less.

Do the work properly though. First say what the authority of a directive is supposed to consist in anyway — sameness of person, sameness of will, a proxy for a will that can no longer speak — and show what the pre-emptive case adds or takes away on each of those accounts. Then deal with the obvious objection, which is that predicting someone will disagree with you isn't the same as conceding that their disagreement counts: parents predict that their kids will scream about the medicine, and nobody thinks that makes the kids' screaming authoritative. Why isn't this just that? And be honest about where your argument is weakest, including whether it proves too much, since arguably every advance directive is a bet that the later self won't end up seeing things the way you do now.
