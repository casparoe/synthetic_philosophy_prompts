I teach a political philosophy seminar on Rawls, about 16 undergrads, one 75-minute session, and every year the veil of ignorance ends up being something they nod at rather than something they actually feel. I want to fix that with an activity instead of another lecture.

Heres the angle I want to build it on, which I think is the real engine of the argument and always gets skipped: the difference principle is only supposed to win if the parties are choosing under the maximin rule, and maximin is only rational for them because they don't know the odds. Rawls is pretty explicit that if you knew your chances this would just be a lottery you'd evaluate by expected value, not by the worst case. So I want the students to run the choice twice, once where the veil is thin enough that they know their odds and once where it's genuinely opaque, and see whether they actually switch principles. And I want the second round to carry the strains-of-commitment wrinkle: after they settle on principles, they get assigned to positions, and whoever lands badly off has to say whether they can still affirm the scheme they picked, with no renegotiating.

Design me the activity. How to stage the two rounds so the students build the candidate principles themselves rather than me handing them a menu, the payoff and probability scheme for each round (no real money), how the assignment works, and the debrief that maps what they did onto the maximin rule, the epistemic condition on the veil, and the strains of commitment. Tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm already expecting at least one group to just refuse to play the second round, and someone to argue the whole setup begs the question against utilitarianism by stacking the deck, so tell me how to handle those and whatever else you see coming. Also be honest about what the activity can't get across, I assume it can't reproduce the actual derivation of the two principles in their lexical order, or the fact that the parties aren't self-interested bargainers but moral persons with a sense of justice, but tell me if theres more.

No lecture up front, and I'd rather they never hear the words maximin or difference principle until the debrief.
