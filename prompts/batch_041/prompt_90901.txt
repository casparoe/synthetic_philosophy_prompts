I've been going back and forth with a friend who does welfare economics and we've been totally stuck for like a year on interpersonal utility comparisons. I don't want a verdict on who's right, I want you to tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is.

My line: 'utility' is just a bookkeeping device for representing a person's preferences over lotteries. Each person's utility is only pinned down up to any increasing relabeling — you can stretch or bend the numbers any monotone way and it represents the exact same choices. So claims like 'A is better off than B' or 'moving a unit of stuff from B to A gains more than B loses' aren't hard to verify, they're not even well-formed. There's no fact they could be tracking. Comparisons across people aren't difficult, they're empty.

His line: thats a trick of your formalism. We compare welfare all the time and you can't do distributive justice or cost-benefit or even 'who has it worse' without it. If your representation can't express the comparison, the representation is too thin, not the comparison fake. And he adds a jab I can't shake: you already treat a single person's utility as cardinal the moment you do expected utility, so why does cardinality turn into gibberish the second it crosses a skin boundary?

The case that keeps us circling is this: two worlds, in one everyone is equally miserable, in the other some people are better off and nobody is worse off. He says obviously the second is better. I say better for whom, under what description — show me the fact. We each think the other is making a howler and neither of us budges.

Here's my hypothesis, test it: we're using 'utility' for two different things. He means a measure of well-being, a real quantity of how good a life is going, that exists whether or not anyone chooses anything. I mean a representation of choice, a construct defined entirely by its job of explaining what someone picks. If that's the whole story then the 'debate' is two questions wearing one word and there's no fact of the matter about who wins because we aren't even disagreeing. But I suspect it's not that clean — maybe there's a hidden formal question (something about exactly when cross-person comparisons are recoverable from choice data and when they aren't), or maybe it's bedrock: he has an intuition that well-being is a real magnitude and I just don't, and no argument will touch that.

So diagnose it. Which is it — equivocation, a hidden empirical or formal question, or clashing bedrock intuitions? Ground the diagnosis in what each side is actually committed to, i.e. what has to be true of 'utility' for each of our claims to come out true, not in who sounds more reasonable. And tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or a reason to think nothing will ever move this. If you land on the two-questions reading, say which question each of us is really answering, and whether either of us is secretly answering the other's without noticing.
