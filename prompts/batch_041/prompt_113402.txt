I'm a retired natural scientist (geology) who's gotten into philosophy late in life. I've been reading alot of popular history and philosophy of history, and a book I just finished gives this summary of the Ranke-Carr-White thing. I'd like to know if it's fair.

Ranke is the father of scientific history. He thought the historian should just go into the archives, get the documents, and report the facts without adding anything of himself -- his famous motto was that history should show how it actually was. Carr and Hayden White came along and blew that up. Carr said there are no historical facts, only facts of the past that the historian turns into history by selecting them, and since selection is always from a present-day standpoint, objectivity is impossible and every generation writes its own history. White went further: he said historians are basically novelists. They take a bunch of real events and emplot them into a story with a beginning, middle, and end, and the story form is a literary imposition, not something in the events. So for White, a historical narrative is a kind of verbal fiction, and there's no way to say one narrative is more true than another -- they're just different stories you can tell about the same data. The upshot is that the old ideal of objective history is dead, and history is just another form of fiction, though it uses real events as raw material.

How much of this is right? Go sentence by sentence and seperate what a careful historian of historiography would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong. And does the final conclusion follow from anything true in the summary? I'm not looking for citations or a reading list -- just the audit.
