I run a small theory group — myself and two postdocs, three years of funding — inside an organisation that produces quantitative long-run risk estimates for policy. Our work is currently Bayesian, and we are deciding whether to move the group's core formalism over to imprecise probabilities. I want an agenda rather than a survey: which open questions in imprecise probability theory most deserve the group's three years, ranked, with the ranking defended.

Three constraints fix what "deserves" means here. First, upstreamness: we would rather settle something that other work depends on than add one more application. Second, tractability for a small theory team with no empirical apparatus of its own — we can prove things and build toy decision problems, but we cannot run experiments. Third, practical consequence: we care about questions whose answer would change what a risk assessment actually reports or recommends.

Please treat the following as candidate items, but feel free to add, merge or dismiss them: (a) dilation — whether it is a defect of the formalism or a correct verdict on evidence that genuinely says nothing about a target quantity; (b) how far imprecision washes out under learning, and whether there are conditions under which learning reliably widens a credal set rather than narrowing it; (c) the choice among decision rules — maximality, E-admissibility, Γ-maximin and the family around it — and whether that choice is a substantive normative commitment or an accounting device; (d) sequential decision making, where imprecision combined with conditioning appears to make dynamic consistency hard to preserve; (e) the computational cost of representing and updating credal sets, and whether the interesting cases are tractable at all.

Two ideas I would like you to engage with, both mine and both rough. The first is what I call imprecision laundering: converting an imprecise model into a decision by a maximin-style rule may smuggle in a sharp and arbitrary prior over worst cases, so that imprecision buys honesty in the modelling and loses it again at the point of decision. Is that a real problem, and is it the same problem as (c) or a different one? The second is the value of imprecision, by analogy with the value of information: are there conditions under which widening a credal set — deliberately, beyond what the evidence forces — yields better decisions, or is imprecision always a cost one pays for honesty? If there is a result to be had here, I should like to know what shape it would take.

Finally, say which of these questions are genuinely open and which only look open because the field has quietly moved on, and tell me what result or development over the next two years ought to move each item up or down the ranking. No reading list, please; I want the argument.
