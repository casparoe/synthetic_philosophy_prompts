I'm a philosopher of biology by training, and for the last three years I've had a semi-official consulting arrangement with the exhibit team at a mid-sized natural history museum. They pulled me in originally because a label in the evolution hall said that the function of the panda's extra wrist bone was to strip bamboo, a visiting biologist wrote them a furious four-page letter about it, and it turned out that nobody on staff could say what would make that sentence true or false. Now I sit in on their label reviews maybe once a month, and the pattern never changes: a writer has a sentence of the form "the function of X is Y" — sometimes X is a trait, sometimes it's a whole species in an ecosystem ("the function of prairie dogs in the grassland is..."), which I keep telling them is a seperate conceptual animal and they keep not believing me — and the room can't decide whether the sentence is an overclaim, an underclaim, or perfectly fine, so it usually gets softened into mush by committee. They've finally asked me for something usable, and I'd rather hand them a real procedure than another lecture, so I'm asking you to write it.

Here's what I need. A decision procedure that a label writer with no philosophy background can run on a draft sentence in about twenty minutes, one page, no jargon: the questions to ask in order (and why that order), what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values or of editorial taste rather than fact. I want a short battery of concrete tests they can actually apply to a sentence — something like, does the trait currently contribute to some capacity of the organism, and is there reason to think it was selected for that contribution — since the two accounts of function (the etiological/selected-effect family and the causal-role family) give different verdicts on the same trait, and the procedure has to make that disagreement usable rather than hide it. It should also say what to do when the strong claim fails: what weaker sentence to print instead, and how to tell a genuine "we don't know" from a face-saving hedge. Then run it on three of their stuck labels: the panda's radial sesamoid, goosebumps in humans, and the giraffe's long neck (the current label implies the neck is for reaching high foliage, and there's a long-running dispute about that which I won't rehearse here). At least one of those should come out as an answer the exhibit team will hate, and you should say so rather than softening it. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do, including whether the ecosystem-function sentences can really be handled by the same machinery with different nouns or whether that's a different problem wearing the same word. Please dont give me reading lists or citations — the writers won't use them, and half of them I couldn't check from the museum anyway.

One more thing, and I mean this: if the honest answer is that there is no single procedure here, because the two accounts of function are answering different questions and any usable procedure has to begin by asking the writer which question they're asking, then say that plainly instead of pretending the two can be fused into one flowchart — and then still give me the best thing you can. And flag which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, because the writers will follow your checklist literally and I'll be the one getting the letters.
