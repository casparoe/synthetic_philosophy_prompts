I'm one of the members of the review panel that has to approve every assisted death in our jurisdiction before it happens — two doctors, a nurse, a lawyer and three lay members, seven of us, majority rules. The law only covers people who have a medical condition, so that's a filter we all accept going in, and every case passes the same formal checks anyway (competent, informed, no pressure from anyone, 90-day cooling off, yadda yadda). The fights are never about the formal checks. Here's the last three years in miniature, the ones I think are representative:

— 64, pancreatic cancer, maybe six weeks left, pain nobody could get on top of, asked three seperate times over four months, picked the date herself. 7-0 approve.
— 52, ALS, on a ventilator at night, could plausibly live another five years, completely lucid, said the loss of his hands and the dependence was worse than dying, had been asking for 14 months. 5-2 approve, the two dissenters saying he wasn't actually dying.
— 34, severe anorexia, in and out of hospital since she was 15, two psychiatrists said she was competent, had been asking for three years, her parents agreed with her. 6-1 reject. We said the illness was doing the talking, and that she hadn't exausted treatment.
— 78, metastatic cancer, clearly dying, in a lot of pain, but she only raised it after her son told her she'd become a burden, and she said she'd go ahead if it made things easier for them. 7-0 reject.
— 82, advanced dementia, not competent anymore, but she'd signed a written request four years ago just after the diagnosis and told her daughter the same thing every time it came up. Now she seems content — eats well, enjoys visits, doesn't ask for anything. Her daughter wanted us to honour the old request. 4-3 approve.
— 45, MS, progressing but slowly, years of fatigue and pain, competent, settled request, nothing left to try. 6-1 approve.
— and one that wasn't close: 60, physically well, business gone, wife dead the year before, competent, asking for a year. 7-0 reject, no medical condition.

We also do the thing where one member has to argue the other side every time, and afterwards we always end up asking whether what the patient has is a wish to die or just a wish for the pain to stop. I've never been sure either of those does any real work.

Next week: 68, early Huntington's, still working part time, mild chorea, some mood stuff the psychiatrists say isn't depression, competent. He watched his father go through the whole thing and says flatly that he won't. He's not in pain. He's been saying this since before the diagnosis was even confirmed. His brother is fine with it; his daughter is furious and wants to address the panel.

What are we going to do with him, and what should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I care less about the verdict than about the rule you think we've actually been running, which other rules would have produced the same record just as well, what each of those predicts for him, and what kind of future case would force us to pick between them.
