I would like my verdicts on the following uses of derogatory language turned into a principle, if any single principle can do the job. Let S be a slur for a group G.

(1) A hostile non-G speaker calls a G person an S: derogatory, no question.
(2) A reporter, plainly not endorsing the insult, quotes that speaker verbatim: I do not think the reporter's utterance is derogatory at all, though the word is; the quotation is doing something else.
(3) A G speaker uses S among G friends, reclaimed: not derogatory, and not merely tolerated — it seems to mean something different, closer to solidarity.
(4) The same reclaimed use by a friendly non-G speaker with identical intent: derogatory, or at least out of bounds, and I cannot see how intent alone separates it from (3).
(5) S used by a non-G speaker to mock bigots, where the literal target is the bigot: still derogatory.
(6) "No G person is an S": the sentence plainly does not assert that G people have the derogatory property — that would be absurd — yet it still derogates, and I have no account of what the derogation attaches to.
(7) A term for a dominant group, usable only as a mild jab: not a slur in the same sense.
(8) A coinage from last week, never used to insult anyone: no derogation yet, so whatever a slur has, history can confer it.

(2) and (6) are the pair I cannot square: both are non-endorsing uses, and I give them opposite verdicts. Is there one principle that yields all eight — truth-conditional content, a presupposition, a conventional implicature, a purely pragmatic effect, or something disjunctive — or does it need a separate clause for quotation, for negation, and for in-group use, at the price of looking ad hoc? State precisely which verdict it forces me to abandon. Then say what it predicts for two cases I have not listed: a hostile G speaker insulting another G person with S, and S appearing as an example sentence in a language textbook.
