Design a teaching plan, not the explanation itself, for a ninety-minute session on the thesis that analogy is the core of cognition rather than a peripheral rhetorical or literary device, the thesis developed at length by Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander in Surfaces and Essences. The audience is a mixed group of graduate students in cognitive science, philosophy, and linguistics. Most will arrive equating analogy with metaphor; at least one will arrive suspecting the thesis is either trivial, on the grounds that anything resembles anything else, or unfalsifiable.

Build the plan around a single carrying example, and make it a micro-case rather than any of the puzzles the book itself is known for. Use this one: a chess player glances at an unfamiliar position and immediately registers it as the same as a position from a game she played years ago, before she can articulate what the similarity consists in. Specify what that case must exhibit for it to do the work (the mapping has to live above the surface features; the recognition has to feel immediate and unforced; the earlier case has to be doing genuine work in the present judgment) and what it must lack (any explicit rule, any conscious search, any shared piece placement). Then say how the plan should draw the general thesis out of that case instead of announcing the thesis first and illustrating it afterward.

Give me the order in which the ideas must be introduced, and why that order rather than another: what has to be in place before the next component can be understood, and what breaks if you reorder it. In particular, decide where the notion of a category or concept has to come in relative to the notion of an analogy, and defend that placement.

Then map the misconceptions in the order the audience will actually form them, and say at which point in the sequence each should be headed off: analogy as decoration; analogy as a deliberate, conscious act; analogy as retrieval of a stored match rather than construction of one; the conflation of surface similarity with the deeper same; and the overcorrection in which, because every act of categorization can be redescribed as an analogy, the thesis is taken to assert nothing. Note which of these the carrying example can defuse by itself and which require separate treatment.

End with a check for understanding that would show the thesis has landed rather than merely been nodded at, and with the two or three things I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so. I have in mind the architecture-level machinery Hofstadter built to model analogy-making, the question whether analogy is a distinct faculty or simply what categorization looks like from one angle, and any attempt to say quantitatively how much of thought is analogy.
