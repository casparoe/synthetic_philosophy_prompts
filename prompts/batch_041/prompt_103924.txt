I teach a graduate seminar in population ethics, and I have decided to open the unit on the non-identity problem with a diagnostic instrument rather than a lecture — last year we spent the better part of an hour circling the question of whether the standard case even involves wrongdoing, and it became clear that my students were answering from four or five different tacit positions without knowing it. Please construct a decision tree, or flowchart, that takes a reader from a set of verdicts on concrete cases to a named position on the non-identity problem. Each internal node must be a question the reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf a position, or "unnamed" if no one has defended it. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: first the questions, then the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions within that space, and note which combinations are unoccupied, saying in each case whether the combination is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to develop it. I want the branches to turn on the distinctions that do the real work here — whether the operative harm is comparative (the person is worse off than they otherwise would have been) or non-comparative (the condition is bad for them independently of any alternative), whether wrongness requires a wronged party, whether the restriction to persons is applied narrowly (to determinate individuals) or widely (to the kinds of persons or roles that would be occupied either way), whether the identity-affecting character of a case is treated as morally significant in itself or merely as an obstacle to the usual comparison, and whether cases that add a person whose life is barely worth living are handled by the same principle as cases that do not change how many people there are. I will name those distinctions in the jargon for your convenience, but the node text itself must translate each one into a plain-language case, since I intend to hand the tree out cold to students who have read nothing on the topic.

Every node should be anchored in a case, and I want the following to appear somewhere in the tree: the standard case, in which the child's condition was a precondition of the child's existing at all, so that no one is worse off than they would have been; an identity-neutral case, in which the very same child would have existed anyway but with a better childhood, so that the ordinary comparison is available; a case in which the parents could have waited and had a different, unaffected child, while the affected child's life is nevertheless well worth living; a case in which a policy creates an additional person whose life is barely worth living; and a case in which the "better" alternative would have produced a different person of a different temperament, so that the two outcomes cannot be compared person by person. Invent at least one further case of your own that separates two positions that those five leave tangled. The tree should terminate in no fewer than seven leaves and no more than twelve nodes in total, and among the leaves these must appear, described rather than named if you prefer: the view that denies any wrong in the standard case; the view that locates the wrong in a non-comparative harm to the child; the view that permits wrongness without a victim; the straightforward impersonal-aggregative view; an impersonal view with a threshold below which lives count differently; a rights- or dignity-based view that treats the child's claim as non-comparative; and a view on which the objection to the parents concerns their dispositions rather than the outcome. Where two of these prove indistinguishable by any question you can pose, flag the pair and name the further case that would separate them; likewise flag any leaf reachable only by a route that would strike most readers as a trick. Each leaf should carry one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, and I would like the whole thing written in spare prose suitable for a diagram a student fills in, not an essay.
