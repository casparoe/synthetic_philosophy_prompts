I'm drafting the refusal policy for an assistant we're building, and before I circulate it I want a consistency check on my own position, since I suspect I've been sliding between two different justifications for refusal without noticing. Here is where I've landed.

(1) A refusal is warranted only when the request would foreseeably harm someone other than the user; where the user is the only person at risk, the assistant's job is to inform and then comply. So we help the person training for a dangerous climb, or asking how a hazardous reaction behaves, and we don't withhold on the grounds that they'd be better off not knowing.

(2) The user's stated ends are authoritative. The assistant has no window onto what is actually good for the user and no standing to substitute its own estimate of the user's interests for the user's own; treating someone's stated goal as a thing to be corrected is a category error rather than an excess of caution.

(3) Standing self-binding instructions are binding. If a user tells the assistant in advance never to help them with a particular class of thing — a betting app, an ex they are trying to stay away from — the assistant should hold the line when the request arrives six months later, because setting that constraint is itself an exercise of autonomy rather than a limit on it.

(4) We don't lecture. A refusal should state its reason in the fewest accurate words available and then stop; anything that reads as commentary on the user's character or their life is a product failure, whether or not the refusal itself was correct.

(5) Assistants have better information than users do about how things tend to go, and should use it. That includes cases where the predictable consequence is damage to the user's later capacity to choose at all — dependence, escalation, entrenchment — since someone who can no longer choose has lost the very thing that (1) and (2) exist to protect, and protecting it in advance is not the same as second-guessing them.

Tell me which of these are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and what would have to give. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one per commitment, each item a sentence or two, ordered by how much pressure the commitment is under, with the one you would abandon first at the top; for each, name what it pulls against, say whether the tension is real or dissolves, and give the cheapest revision that would relieve it. No introduction or conclusion.
