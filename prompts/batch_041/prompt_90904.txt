I teach a senior seminar on practical reason and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on the toxin puzzle (Kavka's), but I'm sick of getting the same canned answer every year — the whole "intention isn't just a prediction, and anyway you can't intend what you know you won't do" thing, recited back at me word for word. So I want the question hung on a variant that isn't in the standard secondary lit, so they actually have to think instead of recall.

Here's the variant. Keep everything from the original: the billionaire, the toxin (say roughly 24 hours of genuinely nasty but non-lethal illness, then full recovery, no lasting effects), and the timing — the million is paid to you at midnight if and only if at midnight you intend to drink the toxin at noon the next day; the money's yours no matter what you do afterwards, and you're free to change your mind. The one change: the billionaire adds a sweetener — if you actually do drink the toxin at noon, he'll pay you an extra amount, call it X. That's the only difference.

The question should make them do three things: (a) say what happens to the puzzle as X varies — is there some value of X at which the puzzle just evaporates, and what does that tell you about what the puzzle was ever really about; (b) say what all this shows for the "intention is just a belief about what you'll do" line, given that whether you can form the intention seems to track what you'd actually do; and (c) say whether "rationally intending" and "rationally acting" come apart here, and if so whether that's a genuine defect in our notion of practical rationality or a feature of it.

Please give me the essay question (tight — aim it at 45-60 min of writing), a model answer of about 600-700 words, and a marking scheme. For the scheme: five must-hit points with marks out of 20, a one-liner for each on the specific mistake that should cost the student marks, and a clear statement of what seperates an A from a B on part (a) specifically. One more thing — don't let the model answer pad itself out with causal-vs-evidential decision theory unless it's actually doing work on the question; I don't want them rewarded for importing Newcomb.
