I am planning a ninety-minute session for a reading group of final-year undergraduates in economics and philosophy. They can state Arrow's theorem and they know the difference between a social welfare function and a social choice function, but they have never met Gibbard–Satterthwaite, and I want them to leave not only able to state it but with the sense that it could not have come out otherwise. Do not explain the theorem to me. Give me the teaching plan.

The whole session must hang on a single voting rule, which I shall write on the board in the first five minutes and never take down. Here it is. Five voters choose one of three options, A, B and C. Each voter submits a strict ranking of the three. Any option ranked last by the greatest number of voters is struck out; if two options tie for the greatest number of last-place rankings, strike out the one that loses the head-to-head comparison between them. Of the two survivors, the one that a majority of the voters rank higher wins. If the rule I have just described happens to fail one of the theorem's hypotheses -- if it is not onto, or is secretly dictatorial, or is in fact already strategy-proof -- say so before anything else, because then the session is built on sand.

What I need from you:

- The order in which the ideas must come, and why each is unavailable until the previous one is in place: the voting rule as a function from profiles to a single winner; the requirement that the rule be onto; what dictatorship amounts to here; and the idea of a profitable misreport, which I take to be the pivot of the session and which I want the students to reach only once they can already half-feel its possibility.
- The single profile, drawn from my rule, that should carry the demonstration, and what it must have and must lack for the manipulation to be visible without looking contrived -- in particular, whether the manipulating voter should be lying about their favourite or merely about a lower ranking, and why.
- The misconceptions they will arrive with or form, and the exact point in the sequence at which each must be headed off. I am expecting at least these: that strategy-proofness means truthful voting is a Nash equilibrium; that the theorem says every vote is pointless or every result is rigged; that dictatorship here means a voter with disproportionate power rather than one who always gets their top choice; that it is just Arrow's theorem with the words changed; and that cardinal or randomised rules are an escape. Tell me which of these is a genuine misconception and which is a defensible reading I should not be stamping on.
- What the session must deliberately not attempt at this level, and how I should say so without the omission feeling like a hole.
- One question to put at the end that would show whether they have grasped the theorem rather than merely followed the board.

Please write the plan in British English.
