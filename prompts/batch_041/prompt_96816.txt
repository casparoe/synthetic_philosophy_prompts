For the final meeting of our school's physics-and-philosophy seminar I have been asked to bring in a poem about Maxwell's demon, and I would like the form of the poem to carry the argument rather than merely illustrate the puzzle. What interests me is the accounting rather than the paradox. The demon's sorting looks like a decrease of entropy obtained for nothing, and the standard answer is that the demon's memory is itself a physical register: the record of which molecules it has admitted has to be wiped sooner or later, and wiping a single bit has to be paid for with at least kT ln 2 of heat. I would also like the cost placed correctly: the poem should not blame the demon's measuring, since a measurement can in principle be carried out without dissipating anything, but its forgetting. It should neither announce that the second law has been beaten nor fall back on the claim that the demon is simply impossible; the point is that the books balance once the erasure is entered.

Here is the form I have in mind, and I would like it followed exactly. Write twenty-four lines in the first person, in the voice of the demon keeping its own ledger. Treat the poem as a memory register that can hold four lines at a time: five stanzas of four lines each, in which every stanza after the first repeats the last three lines of the previous stanza verbatim and appends one new line, so that the register visibly slides forward and its oldest line is overwritten at each step. The lines that drop out of the register should not return anywhere else in the poem; they are erasures already paid for. Then close with a coda of exactly four lines, namely the four lines still in the register, scrambled out of the order in which they were recorded, without punctuation, and with the meter broken, as the heat released when the demon finally wipes its memory. Two further constraints. First, the newly appended line of each stanza should be more regular than the one before it, in meter and in rhyme, so that the register's contents grow more orderly as the gas divides, and so that the coda's disorder registers as a price rather than a mood. Second, the hot side and the cold side should be distinguished by the form itself, by line length or by rhyme sound, for instance, rather than by adjectives.

The last line should be the demon's own bill coming due, not a moral about hubris. Someone who knows the physics should find nothing in it wrong, and someone who does not should finish it understanding why information is never free.
