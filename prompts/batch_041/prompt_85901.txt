I'm a third-year philosophy student, and this term I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of philanthropy that spends most of its time on effective altruism and its critics. Last week we read about earning to give — the proposal that someone with a flair for something lucrative should take the lucrative job and donate a large share of the salary rather than work directly on the cause — and I got into an argument afterwards that I still can't settle. My friend's objection is not the familiar complaint that you can't put a number on everything, which I think is cheap. She puts it this way. EA ranks interventions by expected good per dollar, so it can only rank interventions whose good shows up as something the analysis can see: a life saved, an illness averted, an income raised. She calls this the legibility filter. Her second claim is the interesting one: the filter is not merely a limit on what EA can see but a bias in what it recommends, because what survives the filter is what can be produced in units at a roughly stable marginal cost, and that systematically favours bednets, deworming, and cash transfers over goods that aren't unit-producible — a norm changed, a movement built, a procurement policy shifted, or, in the earning-to-give case specifically, the value of a particular person's doing the work herself rather than paying a stranger to do it. On this view EA's rankings are not a discovery about where the good is but a discovery about what is purchasable at the margin. She adds a third twist: the large funders who set the agenda have an institutional incentive to prefer interventions they can defend with a spreadsheet, so the bias is self-reinforcing — the more EA money goes to legible things, the more legible things there are to fund.

I half agree and half think this is a counsel of despair dressed up as insight, so I need to write an essay that decides. Please weigh the case for the critique against the best reply to it — that a method which cannot be checked against anything is not a method, and that the alternative to ranking by measurable expected value is not a richer ranking but no ranking at all — and then reach a verdict on a sharp question: does the legibility filter show that EA's comparative rankings are untrustworthy, so that "fund the bednets rather than the advocacy campaign" is an artefact of the metric, or does it show only that the rankings are incomplete and EA's rhetoric of optimality overblown, while the rankings themselves survive? Take seriously the possibility that the critique bites hardest against earning to give and least against ordinary donation advice, and explain why the difference arises if it does.

Be concrete: include at least one worked comparison between a specific legible intervention and a specific non-legible one, say what would have to be true for the fungibility point to be a genuine objection rather than a restatement of the obvious fact that some goods resist measurement, and consider whether the same argument could be run against every rival methodology — and if it can, say what that shows. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
