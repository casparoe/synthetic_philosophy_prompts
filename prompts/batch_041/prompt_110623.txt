I am assembling a specification before committing to any particular account. The setting is practical as much as metaphysical: a coroner, a historian of a financial collapse, a regulator and a metaphysician all have to say what caused an outcome when what happened is that something did not happen, and I want to know what a theory of causation by omission would have to deliver to be usable in all four places — not merely internally tidy, but good enough to settle cases. Write out the requirements such a theory must satisfy, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Do not survey the literature and do not give me a reading list; the criteria are the deliverable, and I will do the scoring against particular accounts myself.

At a minimum I want the following addressed, and you should add anything else you think genuinely belongs on the list:

- The plain cases. A doctor who does not prescribe the antibiotic; a driver who does not brake; a lookout who is looking the other way. Any theory has to count these as causal, and has to say in virtue of what.
- The case against them. There is a standing temptation to hold that absences are not events, that causation relates events, and therefore that omissions are not causes at all but only things we cite in explaining. Set out what that option is committed to and what it costs.
- The non-agential cases. An antibiotic is absent from a culture and the bacteria grow; a gene is deleted and the phenotype appears; a braking subroutine does not execute and the vehicle collides. Same phenomenon as the doctor's omission, or does the theory need a separate treatment for the artefactual and the merely natural?
- The case with no possible agent. The rain did not come and the harvest failed. Nobody omitted anything. Must the theory call this causal, and if it must, what stops every fact about the world from becoming a cause of every other fact?
- Proliferation. Grant that omissions can be causes and that counterfactual dependence is the test, and everyone in the city who did not call the fire brigade caused the house to burn. What constrains the spread? Separate the accounts that constrain it by worldly facts from those that constrain it by norms, expectations or duties, and say what each constraint buys and what it costs.
- Selection. Even granting that many omissions are causal, we single one out as the cause — the salient one, the blameworthy one, the difference that mattered. Is that singling-out part of causation or part of something else, and can the theory tell the difference?
- Redundancy and preemption. Two people could each have saved the child and neither does; a watchdog sleeps, though the intruder would have got in anyway. Neither omission is a but-for condition, yet at least one looks causal. What must the theory deliver here?
- Nested and compound omissions. A fails to tell B; B fails to warn C. May an omission cause an omission, and does the theory multiply causes or absorb them?
- Omissions as double prevention. The usual move is to treat an omission as causing by preventing a preventer. Say what that buys and where it strains.
- The normative cases. Two structurally identical factories, one failing to fit a safety valve that is standard in its industry, one failing to fit a novel one. If our causal verdict differs although the physical facts do not, is the theory in trouble or is that the correct result?
- Degrees and vocabulary. Causal talk about omissions comes in degrees and in different idioms — the historian's absence of a lender of last resort, the engineer's failure mode, the regulator's non-compliance. State whether the theory owes an account of these, or whether they are decoration on a single underlying relation.

Then, on the structure of the list itself: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp; identify any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied and say which member you would give up, since that is the part I most want to see; and name the single requirement that does the most to eliminate candidate accounts. Close with a brief assessment, no more than a paragraph each, of how regularity accounts, counterfactual and dependence accounts, interventionist accounts, powers-and-mechanism accounts, and the view that omissions are not causes but quasi-causal or merely explanatory fare against the list. Keep that closing section short; the list is where the thinking is. The list itself can be a table.
