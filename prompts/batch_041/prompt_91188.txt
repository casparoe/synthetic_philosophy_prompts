I work on exoplanet detection, where we routinely claim to have found bodies no one has seen, on the strength of wobbles, dips, and perturbations in things we can see; I have lately found that I cannot give a principled account of what licenses that inference, and a colleague pointed me to the Neptune and Vulcan episodes as the historical test case. In 1846 an unexplained residual in the orbit of Uranus was attributed to an unseen planet, and the planet was found close to where it had been predicted; within the same tradition, and by the same perturbation methods, Le Verrier later attributed a residual in the precession of Mercury's perihelion to an unseen planet, Vulcan, which was never found, and the residual was eventually absorbed by a change in the theory of gravity rather than by a new body. Please analyze that pair as a case study in inference to the best explanation: state what the competing explanations were in each case and on what grounds one of them was judged best; say whether the Neptune inference was genuinely abductive or merely hypothetico-deductive reasoning from Newtonian mechanics plus an auxiliary hypothesis, and what turns on which it was; and then give me the honest scorecard — what the abductive framing captures about the pair, what it misdescribes or is silent about, and whether the Vulcan failure counts against inference to the best explanation itself or only against the background physics. Separate throughout what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present any particular position on the truth-conduciveness of abduction as the consensus.
