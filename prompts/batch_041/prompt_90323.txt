In a seminar on artificial intelligence, a participant tried to settle whether a computer could ever do genuine mathematics — or have a mind at all — with a single sentence: 'Gödel's incompleteness theorem proves that the mind is not a machine.' Pressed, he offered this version: take any program that is supposed to capture the whole of human mathematical reasoning; by the theorem there is a truth the program cannot prove but we can plainly see, so no program can capture us. I want to know exactly how much weight that sentence can bear. Treat it as a slogan and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, from the nearly trivial to the very strong, saying for each what it commits one to, whether the incompleteness theorem itself establishes it or merely lends it encouragement, whether it is a platitude, a contentious claim, or simply false, and whether it would actually support the conclusion that no computer could do mathematics or have a mind. Also say which reading is most plausibly doing the work when the sentence is used to close a debate of that kind, and which reading I should press him on. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
