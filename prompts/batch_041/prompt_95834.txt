My friends and I put out a small satirical newsletter (mostly riffs on corporate comms) and I've bagged the centrepiece for the next issue: the Animal Welfare chapter of an aquaculture company's annual sustainability report. Deadpan the whole way, no winking, and the company must never once notice that it's indicting itself. Invent the company — let's say Marlowe Seafarms, salmon in open-net sea pens off a cold coast plus a shrimp hatchery somewhere tropical, so the piece can cover both fish and crustaceans while cheerfully calling all of it our fish. 700-900 words, with subheads, a sidebar case study, and a pull-quote from the Head of Animal Welfare whose bio should make the reader pause for a second (something like a fish physiologist whose main reported metric is survival rate). It should read like a real report someone signed off on, and it should be funny to a person who keeps fish and still readable to someone who's never thought about a shrimp.

The humour has to come from getting the content right, so let me dump what I've got, and fold these in rather than listing them. Fish isn't a natural category at all — a coelacanth is more closely related to me than to a salmon, and shrimp and crabs aren't fish in any sense, which the report should keep quietly ignoring. There's the nociception-versus-pain problem: a nociceptor firing and a reflex withdrawal is not the same as somebody being home to feel it, and the report should treat that contested science as settled in whichever direction happens to be cheaper this quarter. There are the slaughter methods — immersion in ice slurry, asphyxiation on ice, percussive and electrical stunning, crustaceans dropped into boiling water alive (and the odd jurisdiction that has started requiring they be stunned first). There's eyestalk ablation in the hatchery: cutting off one of a female shrimp's eyestalks to make her spawn, described here as a routine maturation protocol and noted as superseded by a proprietary light-management programme whose results remain under review. There's sea lice, and the delousing treatments — thermal and chemical — that are themselves hard on the fish, plus the cleaner fish put in the pens to eat the lice and mostly die there. And there's the metrics gag: the headline welfare KPI is mortality, so a fish that dies slowly is a cost and a fish that dies instantly is a rounding error. If you want one more, there was something in the news a while back about a country extending its animal-sentience law to crabs and octopuses; the report can concede this in a footnote and then say it applies the same high standards across all our animals.

What I'm actually mocking is welfare-washing as a genre — the assumption that a number the company chooses to publish is a fact about an animal, that "humane" names a method rather than a marketing tier, and that certification means much when the industry pays for the certifier (so give the company a proudly independent certification body, funded by a foundation the company also endows). Don't state any of that; the reader should be able to tell what's being sent up without you ever breaking character. No real company names, no real people, no references or footnotes to actual documents, and don't get preachy or slide into an activist's voice — the only voice in the piece is the report's.
