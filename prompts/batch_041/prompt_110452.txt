I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who trades for a living, and we picked it up again at breakfast, which is how I know it got under my skin. His position was the clean one: a bet with positive expected value is a good bet, a repeated bet with positive expected value is a better one, and the only genuine question is how much of your bankroll to stake — everything else is squeamishness wearing the costume of mathematics. Mine was that the expected value is a statement about an ensemble of copies of me, and I don't get to be an ensemble; I get to be one path going forward through time, compounding multiplicatively rather than additively. So what grows along a trajectory is the average of the logarithm, not the logarithm of the average, and the two come apart exactly where it matters. He kept insisting that a favorable game repeated often enough must make you rich. I kept insisting that if you stake a large enough multiple of the fraction that maximizes the expected logarithm of your wealth, you go broke with probability one, however real the edge, and that the ensemble average which keeps climbing is being carried by a vanishing fraction of astronomically lucky trajectories — which is not a description of anybody's life. We ended up conceding that we were probably answering two different questions in one vocabulary, but neither of us could say so in a way that didn't collapse into a slogan.

I want a poem out of this, and I want the form to do the arguing for me. Write a pantoum: five quatrains, twenty lines, with the strict rule that the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and that the final stanza closes the cycle by taking its second and fourth lines from the first and third lines of the opening stanza, so that the ending is made of the same material as the beginning. The voice should be a single bettor who has been staking the same fraction of her bankroll for years. The lines that return should be precisely the ones where the two readings diverge: a sentence that functions as reassurance in the ensemble's mouth should come back word for word identical as a threat on the single path, and the reader should feel the meaning turn over without a syllable changing. I don't want the poem to take my side; if it can hold both, so much the better. Let the hinge be that the log of the average and the average of the log are different animals and that the gap between them is not a rounding error — you can carry that without writing an equation, and you may name the criterion or leave it unnamed, so long as someone who has actually sat down and maximized the expected logarithm would recognize the bettor's rule rather than wince. Somewhere in the poem I want the thing that unsettles me most: that the strategy maximizing what you expect to have is the strategy that takes you to nothing, and that the two sit on opposite sides of a line rather than near each other. If you can manage it without wrecking the repetitions, let the unrepeated lines grow shorter as the poem proceeds, so that the returning lines come to fill the poem as everything else thins out; I suspect that is what the poem is actually about. And no moral at the end — the cycle closing is the moral.
