I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has just published a book defending a Borgesian view of time. Her central thesis is that the argument Borges gives in A New Refutation of Time — where he extends Berkeley's idealist denial of mind-independent matter, and Hume's denial of a persisting self, to the case of time itself — should be taken at face value rather than read as a literary conceit. On her reconstruction there is no genuine succession and no passage; reality consists of momentary, self-contained states, and our impression of duration and becoming is a projection. She adds a second claim that she treats as independent support: she argues the view converges with the Buddhist doctrine of momentariness, on which reality is a stream of momentary dharmas. She is a careful scholar and will not be rattled by a softball.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that push past a restatement of the view. I want at least one question each on the following, and for every question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

- the self-undermining worry: if there is no succession, how can an argument have successive steps, and how can the reader pass from premise to conclusion — and does her own notion of a projection secretly presuppose the very time it denies;
- the idealist scaffolding: does the refutation survive without Berkeley's idealism, or is it hostage to a contested theory of perception;
- what is left once succession goes: whether there is one instant or many, and how a multiplicity of instants can constitute a stream without re-importing an ordering relation that is itself temporal;
- memory and personal identity: what grounds the state that seems to remember a past if no state can be caused by an earlier one, and what is the I that undergoes the illusion;
- the Buddhist convergence: whether momentariness is really an ally, given that it is standardly paired with moment-to-moment causal conditioning, which looks like the succession she denies;
- whether the view is a conceptual claim or an empirical one, and what could count against it.

Order the questions so the interview builds rather than jumping around, and end with one question that gives her the strongest available opening to state the positive case for the view. In a short note to me at the end — not to be read on air — name the single question you think is most likely to expose a weakness in her position, and say why.
