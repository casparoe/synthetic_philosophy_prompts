Question:

A residents' association is told by the council that the village hall will close unless the council receives clear evidence of community support by the end of the month. One resident posts in the association's group chat: 'everyone email the council and say we want to keep the hall.' Over the following week about forty people email the council. Most of them never discuss it with one another; a handful only skimmed the original post; nobody keeps track of who has written; and it is not common knowledge among the forty that they are all doing this. (a) Is what happens here a joint action? (b) Do the residents share an intention to save the hall on Bratman's account? (c) On Gilbert's? (d) Is Searle's notion of collective intentionality a rival to accounts like these, or a description of the same phenomenon at a different level? Justify your answers.

Script:

Joint action is normally contrasted with a mere aggregate of individual actions that happen to point the same way, like everyone in a cinema making for the exit when the alarm goes. In a joint action the participants are doing something together in a stronger sense, and the standard question is what that stronger sense amounts to.

Bratman analyses it in terms of shared intention. For us to J together, each of us must intend that we J, and our intentions have to interlock: each of us must intend to J partly because the other does. He also requires mutual responsiveness and a commitment on each side to the joint activity. Because he uses the word 'commitment' some people hear a moral bond, but his commitment is weaker than a promise — it is a practical commitment to seeing the thing through, not an obligation incurred to the other person.

Gilbert's account is stronger. She thinks a shared intention requires a joint commitment, and that a joint commitment creates a plural subject — a 'we' that is not just a sum of individuals. A joint commitment is created when each party expresses readiness to be jointly committed, and once it exists the parties are obligated to one another and entitled to rebuke one another for defecting. Unlike Bratman, she does not require the parties to want the same thing; the joint commitment itself is what binds them.

Searle accepts that collective intentionality is a genuine and irreducible form of intentionality, but he denies that it requires a plural subject. For him we-intentions are had by individual brains; what he rejects is the attempt to analyse them as I-intentions plus mutual belief. So Searle is really a rival of Bratman rather than a fellow-traveller: Bratman's project is exactly the reductive one Searle says cannot succeed.

Turning to the case: the residents all want the hall saved and each writes to the council in the light of the original post. They have a shared goal, and once the parties know about one another's intentions a shared goal just is a shared intention, so I think Bratman would say there is a shared intention here, though it is borderline because there is so little mutual responsiveness. Gilbert would say there is no joint commitment, because there was no agreement, only a broadcast, so no plural subject. Searle would say there is collective intentionality if each resident was thinking 'we are saving the hall' rather than 'I am sending an email'.
