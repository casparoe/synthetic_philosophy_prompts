I cover science and ideas, and I have been sitting on a panel transcript I cannot make up my mind about. The case was a small coastal town whose museum display follows local memory in recording that a nineteenth-century wreck drowned a great many townspeople and that a named woman saved most of them, whereas the only contemporary documents — a parish register and a harbourmaster's log — record a much smaller loss and never mention her. Two panelists, a museum curator and a local historian, went back and forth, and I would like the exchange adjudicated rather than the history settled.

The curator opened: the museum is not a memory but an institution that makes assertions, and it is now asserting what its own documents contradict; the town may remember what it likes, but a public claim the museum knows to be false is a plain failure of trust, so correct the display. The historian replied that the plaque is not a rival chronicle but a statement of who the town has decided to be answerable to, and that the record is not neutral — a harbourmaster logs what the harbour needs and a parish registers what the church needs, so the drowned poor who left no paper are precisely whom the archive is built to miss; treating two official documents as the final court of appeal is not accuracy but mistaking the state's filing system for the past. The curator granted that the archive is partial but said partial is not false: if the memory is tracking something the documents missed, some reason is owed for thinking so, and otherwise partiality is merely a licence to believe what comforts us — once comfort is the test, no memory can ever be corrected, not the town that remembers its own innocence in a lynching, nor the family that remembers a fortune honestly won, and no one can say where the line falls. The historian answered that the line falls at use: memory is a way of keeping faith with the dead rather than a rival chronicle, and judging it by propositional accuracy is a category mistake, as if one faulted a eulogy for omitting the deceased's debts — but he conceded that some memories are load-bearing, and that once a memory is used to claim land, demand reparation or fix blame, it has stopped being commemoration and started making assertions about what happened, at which point it is answerable to the documents like anything else; the real distinction, he said, is not memory against history but commemorative memory against load-bearing memory. The curator closed by saying that this leaves the distinction to be policed by the very people it tests, since a town will call its memory purely commemorative when challenged and load-bearing when it wants the claim to count, and a test the tested party applies is no test.

Please adjudicate this move by move, rating each of the five from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, then say who holds the stronger overall position and what both of them are missing — in particular, whether the curator's objection to the load-bearing distinction can be met, and whether anything turns on the fact that the documents are themselves the products of institutions with interests. Write in British English.
