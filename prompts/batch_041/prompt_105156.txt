Moral Compromise as Normative Arbitrage

My friend and I disagree about a great many values. She is broadly consequentialist and gives enormous weight to impartial welfare. I am a pluralist and give enormous weight to particular relationships and familial partiality. Last week we argued about a concrete case: a mutual friend received an unexpected ten thousand dollars and asked us whether to spend it on an experimental treatment for her father or donate it to an effective charity. My friend said charity. I said treatment. We argued past each other for an hour. Then I proposed a compromise: give four thousand to the treatment and six thousand to charity, with the family covering the rest of the treatment through a loan. My friend said that was not a compromise but a bribe, and that I was asking her to betray her principles for a smaller betrayal. I said the opposite: that we had both gained.

The usual model of compromise treats values as points on a single scale. If we disagree about how much to spend on X versus Y, we meet in the middle. But that model assumes commensurability. A better model treats each person's value system as a partially ordered set with multiple dimensions, and compromise as a move to a point that is not on either original ranking but is not worse for either party under her own ranking. The gains come from differences in the marginal rates at which each party is willing to trade one value for another. I call this normative arbitrage.

In the case above, my friend cares intensely about impartial welfare and mildly about familial partiality. I care intensely about familial partiality and mildly about impartial welfare. If we each concede on the dimension we care less about, we can both improve our own all-things-considered ranking. She gets a larger charity donation than she would have gotten if I had simply overridden her. I get a larger family transfer than I would have gotten if she had overridden me. Neither of us converts to the other's value system. We are not splitting a fixed pie of moral value; we are exploiting different exchange rates between values.

This is not moral relativism. The trade is possible only if each party treats the other's values as genuine constraints on what can be done, not as mistakes to be corrected. It also requires transparency: if I pretend to care more about impartial welfare than I do, I am not trading, I am manipulating. And it requires that the compromise not violate deontological side-constraints. Some values are not currencies, because treating them as tradeable is itself a violation.

I also want to distinguish two kinds of compromise. A Pareto compromise leaves both parties better off by their own lights. A Kaldor-Hicks compromise leaves the gainers able to compensate the losers in principle but not in fact. Much of what passes for moral compromise is Kaldor-Hicks in disguise: one side gets what it wants, and the other side receives a promise or a gesture rather than an actual improvement. I think this distinction does real work, but I am not sure whether it is stable in the moral case.

The analogy to gains from trade breaks down in at least three places. First, value systems are not fully commensurable even internally; there may be no exchange rate between two of my own values. Second, the parties may disagree about the baseline against which gains are measured, so each may sincerely believe the other is the one being bought off. Third, the trade may be unavailable if the parties' value systems are not merely different but antagonistic, as when one party's core value is that the other party's core value should not be pursued at all. In that case, compromise may be a modus vivendi rather than a mutual improvement.

I would especially like your comments on three things. First, is the central analogy accurate, or does it smuggle in assumptions about commensurability that moral pluralists should reject? Second, is the essay interesting, or is it a restatement of familiar ideas about compromise and bargaining? Third, are the three limits I identify the right ones, or are there more important breakdowns? Please comment on the essay as written, and please respond in American English.
