I run a small philosophy blog and I'm trying to build one of those "which X are you actually?" quizzes, except for moral worth — specifically the cluster of questions about when an action has moral worth and whether it has to be done for the right reasons. My friends and I keep having the same argument in circles: someone says the shopkeeper who's honest only out of self-interest gets no credit, someone else says neither does the person who helps out of love, and then we spend an hour arguing about what "moral worth" was even supposed to mean in the first place. The quizzes I've found online are useless because they just ask things like "do you think only the motive of duty counts?" — everyone knows which answer makes them a Kantian, so people answer by tribal affiliation instead of by what they actually think about cases. I want the opposite: a quiz where you answer ten concrete situations and only find out at the end which position your answers add up to.

So: ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, each phrased so that a reader can answer it without having any idea which position it's a test for. I want the cases to pull apart at least these seams: whether the motive has to be duty/rightness itself or whether love, sympathy, or even a drilled-in habit can carry moral worth; whether the agent has to be motivated by the very feature that makes the action right, or whether it's enough that she's tracking that feature somehow (so someone who does the right thing while believing it's wrong can still get credit); whether acting well out of moral ignorance or a false moral belief is compatible with moral worth; whether worth requires the agent to know the relevant facts, or whether ignorance just excuses without destroying worth; whether the action has to be attributable to the agent at all (does the person whose compassion was engineered in a lab, or who was raised in a cult, get any?); whether worth comes in degrees or is all-or-nothing; and whether it attaches to the action, the motive, or the whole person. Please include at least a handful of the following cases, and feel free to add your own, but keep them this concrete: someone who does the right thing but only because a rival would look better if she didn't; someone raised to think helping outsiders is a sin who helps anyway and feels guilty about it; a nurse who acts well from a habit drilled in so long ago she genuinely can't say why she does it; a person whose compassion was surgically induced and who now volunteers every weekend; someone who does the right thing for the right reason exactly once and then never again; and someone who sincerely tries to do the right thing and fails through bad luck. For each of those, the two or three options should be things a normal person might actually say, not jargon.

The output I want is: the ten questions; a scoring key as a table that maps answer patterns onto positions — and here, describe the positions in plain words rather than by philosopher's name, since I don't want this turning into a reading list and half my readers won't know who's who anyway (if a name is genuinely common knowledge, fine, but don't lean on it). I'd like the key to be able to reach at least these: the strict duty-only view; the view where duty has to be there as a limiting condition but other motives can do the real work; the view where what matters is being moved by the right reasons; the view where what matters is being responsive to the right-making features even if you're not moved by them as such; the view that bolts on a knowledge requirement; the view that moral worth is really just attributability/credit; the view that it's a property of agents rather than of actions; and a deflationary option where "moral worth" isn't a distinct thing at all. Then tell me which of these my ten questions can't actually seperate from each other (I'd rather know than have the key fake it), and whether any combination of answers lands somewhere nobody actually defends a view — and if so, is that because the combination is incoherent, or just unattractive, or just unexplored? Two or three sentences per result as a little blurb, in the voice of a quiz, and please keep the whole thing readable for people who've taken one ethics class. I definately want the wrong-answer options to be tempting too, not strawmen. Thanks!
