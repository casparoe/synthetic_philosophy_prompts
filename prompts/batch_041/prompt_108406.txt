I am presenting a paper at a joint philosophy and physics colloquium in three weeks and I need to prepare for the question period rather than for the talk itself. My paper defends a reformulated kalam argument, and the audience is technically serious but largely unsympathetic. I expect the serious objections to come from the physics side and the logic side, not from people who work on the kalam, and I would like a Q&A prep sheet built specifically for this room.

The argument I will defend

I do not claim that the universe began to exist. That formulation, I argue, equivocates between having a first instant and having a finite past duration, and it leaves the premise hostage to cosmology in ways its defenders have not earned. I run the argument on past-incompleteness instead. Treat a physical history as a maximal, connected spacetime-with-contents of the sort our best theories describe. There is a family of results in classical cosmology, of which the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem of 2003 is the standard one, to the effect that in a spacetime whose expansion has on average been positive, past-directed geodesics cannot be extended indefinitely. So any history continuous with ours is past-incomplete.

The load-bearing principle is what I call the Boundary Dependence Thesis: necessarily, if a physical history is past-incomplete, then either it is a proper part of a larger physical history that is not past-incomplete, or its existence depends on something that is not a state of affairs within it. I argue that the first disjunct cannot be the whole story. If the larger history is also past-incomplete, the principle applies to it again; if the regress of larger histories never terminates, we still owe an account of the totality, and the totality is not itself one more physical history. Either way we arrive at something that is not a physical state and on which the physical order depends. I then argue, in descending order of confidence, that this something is necessary, that it is not physical, and, more tentatively, that it is an agent.

The dependence in the Boundary Dependence Thesis is meant in a thin sense, and I make the thin/thick distinction explicit in the talk. Thin dependence is only the existence of the one necessitating the existence of the other, so that the latter is a condition on the former. Thick dependence is grounding or production, a matter of the one being responsible for the other. I claim the argument needs only the thin relation, and that this is a strength, because it lets me stay neutral on the theory of grounding.

The audience

Two philosophers of physics. One works on the arrow of time and statistical mechanics and is a committed naturalist; she regards talk of the universe having a beginning as a category error inherited from nineteenth-century thermodynamics. The other works on general relativity and the interpretation of singularities; he is fluent in the mathematics of geodesic incompleteness and will not let a loose phrase such as past spacetime boundary pass unexamined.

A cosmologist from the physics department, an inflation theorist, who has been through these arguments before and is tired of them. He will reach the technical objection fastest and care least about the metaphysics.

Two philosophers of religion. One is a theist who works on the divine attributes and will think my conclusion too thin to deserve the name of God. The other is an atheist who has published on the kalam, knows every standard reply, and will recognize the ones I intend to use.

A historian of medieval philosophy who thinks the kalam is a modern construction and that the real work is done by the distinction between what a thing is and that it is.

A logician who works on modality and grounding, and who will be the most dangerous person in the room if the thin/thick distinction is not stated with precision.

Two or three graduate students, one of whom will ask the obvious question I have not prepared for.

What I want from you

1. What to concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks. Which two or three concessions buy me the most credibility with this room, and which apparent concessions would cost me the argument.

2. Eight to ten objections, ranked by expected damage, meaning likelihood multiplied by cost rather than severity alone, each attributed to the person in the room most likely to raise it. For each: the objection as that person would actually put it, in two to four sentences, in their vocabulary and with their presuppositions showing; my reply, short enough to deliver in under two minutes; where the reply is soft; what would count as an adequate answer for that objector; and a verdict of concede, hold, or reframe.

3. Which single objection, if it lands, ends the argument, and which single reply has to land for the session to count as a success.

4. The question I am hoping nobody asks, together with what I should say if it comes.

5. The weakest point in my own position, the one a sharp member of this audience will find however I steer: I would rather prepare to defend it than hope it is missed.

No citations, reading lists, or pointers to literature, since I need to be able to say all of this aloud without notes. Flag any place where two people in this room would give me opposite advice, and any point that is a genuine open problem rather than something I can win on the day. Keep each reply to what I can actually say in two minutes, and cut anything that is there to sound thorough. The talk is forty minutes and the question period is thirty, so assume I will get through at most half of what you give me. Aim for something I can reread in ten minutes. If you think the argument fails in a way I have not anticipated, say so in a short closing paragraph rather than softening the prep sheet to spare me.
