I am a third-year PhD student in metaphysics, midway through a dissertation on de re modality, and I keep running into a wall that I would like your help diagnosing rather than merely summarizing. The standoff I have in mind is the long-running dispute between counterpart-theoretic treatments of de re modal discourse and treatments that allow individuals themselves to be identical across possible worlds. As I understand the two camps, the counterpart theorist holds that no individual exists in more than one world, and that a claim such as "that man could have won the election" is true in virtue of a distinct individual — a counterpart of him — winning in another world, so that de re modality is a matter of counterpart relations rather than of the very same thing being different. The opposing camp holds that this is a mistake about the subject matter: when we ask what could have happened to that man, we are asking about him, and we may stipulate or directly consider possibilities for him without needing any relation or criterion to license the crossing of worlds; on this view identity across worlds is simply identity, and identity is not the sort of relation that stands in need of a criterion. The counterpart theorist replies that the opposing picture either leaves transworld identity mysterious or, when pressed for a criterion, collapses into absurdity (the familiar trouble about what makes the individual in the other world the same individual, and the suspicion that any criterion will either individuate too finely or make too much essential). The opposing camp replies that the counterpart theorist has changed the subject — that counterparts are other things, not him, so counterpart theory is at best a theory of something else wearing the label of de re modality. Neither side seems to move, and both behave as though the other has simply failed to see an obvious point.

My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that the impasse is produced by a conflation the two sides share, between two things that could be meant by the phrase "the same individual across worlds": an identity reading, on which sameness is the logical relation and therefore not the kind of thing for which a criterion could sensibly be demanded, and a substantive "same-thing" reading, on which sameness is a worldly relation between world-bound items and therefore precisely the kind of thing for which a criterion is owed. If that distinction is doing the work, then the counterpart theorist is demanding a criterion for something the opposing side never claimed needed one, and the opposing side is denying a demand that, on its own reading, was never made — and the whole exchange is closer to a verbal dispute than either side admits. But I am not confident this is right, and I suspect at least part of the disagreement is genuinely substantive: about whether worlds are concrete or abstract, about what an analysis of a modal sentence is supposed to deliver, and about whether the project is to give truth-conditions for de re modal sentences in a regimented language or to describe what a speaker is doing when she entertains a possibility for a thing. I have also wondered whether the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, one semantic and one representational or epistemic, and whether that would make the standoff a sign of an ill-posed question rather than merely a hard one.

Please give me a diagnosis, not a survey. I want you to commit to a definite verdict on the structure of the disagreement — whether it is verbal, substantive, a difference in the questions being asked, or some combination, and in what proportions — and to defend that verdict by appeal to what each side actually says and to what would have to be true for the parties to be disagreeing in the way they take themselves to be. Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress and whether any is to be expected: whether there is a distinction, a formal result, or a change of question that could move the parties, or an argument that the dispute is the kind that cannot be settled and why. Along the way, address directly whether my identity-versus-same-thing hypothesis survives your scrutiny, and if it does not, say precisely where it breaks. Where the evidence underdetermines your verdict, tell me what further consideration would settle it rather than hedging. Do not merely lay out the options and leave the choice to me: take a position, give your reasons, and name the point at which your position is weakest.
