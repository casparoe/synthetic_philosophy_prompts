I am a doctoral candidate in political theory with a secondary interest in the governance of emerging technologies, and what I want from you is an argument rather than a survey.

The term "singleton," as introduced by Nick Bostrom, names a world order in which a single decision-making agency operates at the highest level, able to exert effective control across its domain and to prevent both internal and external challenges to that control. The notion is deliberately abstract: a singleton might be a world government, a dominant artificial intelligence, a global legal order with genuine enforcement capacity, or something with no human analogue. I am not asking whether a singleton is likely. I am asking what our existing political arrangements already assume about whether one would be good.

Here is the pattern that puzzles me. Within a state, we treat the concentration of coercive power as an achievement rather than a threat. A government that successfully claims a monopoly on legitimate force is, on the ordinary view, the precondition of peace, contract, and justice; private armies and rival militias are a pathology, and the fragmentation of coercive authority — warlordism — is among the worst political conditions we can imagine. Across states, we aim at the opposite. Great-power diplomacy, alliance formation, nonproliferation policy, and the resistance to any international body with independent enforcement capacity are all oriented toward ensuring that no single agency acquires that same monopoly. A world government with teeth is treated as a nightmare by nearly everyone, and a balance of power is treated as the second-best good. So the same tradition that regards a domestic singleton as the foundation of order organizes itself internationally against a global one.

I would like you to reconstruct the theory of the singleton that this asymmetry presupposes. Concretely: does the pattern commit us to the view that singletons are good when legitimate and bad when not, or to a view about scale, or to a distinction between a singleton over persons and a singleton over states, or to something else? Which rival positions does it quietly rule out — does it exclude the claim that a singleton is intrinsically valuable, the claim that it is intrinsically dangerous, or neither? Is the asymmetry a genuine inconsistency, or does it dissolve once we notice that the domestic case concerns a plurality of persons who retain standing, exit options, and legal recourse, whereas the international case would place a single agency above states that have no appeal? And separate for me what the pattern strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with: it seems to me that several quite different theories could license both halves, and I want to know which of my questions the material actually settles and which it leaves open.

Then tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. If we took the domestic analogy seriously and regarded a global singleton as desirable for the same reasons we regard the domestic one as desirable, what in the current arrangement would have to be abandoned, and what would have to be invented? Conversely, if we took the anti-singleton stance all the way down, would it undercut the legitimacy of the state itself?

Be direct about the limits of the reconstruction. If the puzzle rests on a confusion — if I am trading on two different senses of "control," or leaning on an analogy between persons and states that will not bear the weight, or if the two halves of the pattern are simply products of separate historical contingencies rather than expressions of one theory — say so plainly instead of constructing a reconciliation the material does not support. I would rather be told that my framing is defective than receive a tidy synthesis that papers over the problem.
