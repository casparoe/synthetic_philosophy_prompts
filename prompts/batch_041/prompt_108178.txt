Some of us in the local historical society have spent alot of Tuesday evenings going round and round about why our town hollowed out after the war, and I've ended up applying to the state humanities council for one of their small independent-scholar grants — six months, a few thousand dollars, and a two-page proposal due in March. The trouble is that every account I read and every old-timer I talk to ends up saying the decline was "overdetermined". The mill was going to shut anyway once the tariff went the way it did; the bypass was going to kill Main Street anyway once the highway department made up its mind; the young people were going to drift off anyway once the farms got mechanized; and the flood in '55 just hurried it all along. I think I know what they're getting at — it was going to happen whichever one of those you took away — but the more I sit with it the less it makes sense, because if the mill closing on its own was enough to empty the town, then the bypass didn't cause the town to empty, and if the bypass on its own was enough, then the mill didn't, and then what is it that everyone thinks caused it? A friend of mine who did a philosphy degree says there's a name for that shape of problem — something about two causes each of which would have been enough on its own, and how any theory that says a cause has to make a difference gets stuck on it — and that people mostly treat it as a puzzle about causation in general, not as a thing you'd trip over actually trying to write history. Meanwhile "overdetermined" as the people I've been reading use it seems to mean something much weaker: a lot of things pushing the same way, none of them enough by itself, so taking any one away wouldn't have changed much. Those can't both be what the word means, and I don't think anyone has noticed they're using it both ways, sometimes in the same paragraph.

So what I need from you is the proposal itself, written as me: the question stated so that it comes out one way or the other and not just "it's complicated"; why the usual ways of talking about it (the "it was going to happen anyway" line, and the "lots of factors contributed" line) don't actually settle which of the two I'm entitled to; what I would really do for six months with the sort of material a county society has — the newspaper runs, the mill's own ledgers and letters, the oral history tapes we've already got, and hopefully the highway departments files if they'll let me in; what the finished essay would be claiming if it went well; and what would count as the project having failed, because I'd rather find that out now than in month five. Also tell me honestly whether that's too much for six months and one amateur, and if it is, what the smaller question is that I should actually be asking.

And don't give me a reading list or citations, I'll fill those in myself at the library. What I'd like you to push on is my own hunch, which may well be wrong: that the useful version of "overdetermined" for a case like this is a claim about how the town would have gone under small variations rather than about any single cause being sufficient by itself — the mill very nearly got the defence contract, the state very nearly ran the highway on the other bank, two of the families who left first came back for a year — and that whatever evidence there is for or against it has to come from how close those near-misses really were. If that's a dead end, say so and give me a better angle. The panel is mostly retired teachers and a couple of librarians, so it needs to make sense to people who have never heard the word "counterfactual" and would be put off by a page of jargon.
