I'm a novelist — four books, the last one did okay — and I'm about a third of the way into a new one that keeps breaking on me for a reason I've finally figured out is philosophical and not craft. Setup: two timelines, one woman, Anna, and the split happens before she's born. In one, her mother marries the man she actually married; in the other she marries his brother (long story, it's the seventies, there's a lake). I want the reader to feel it's the same woman in both timelines — same memories of the lake, same fear of boats — and the whole structure depends on that. Then somebody at my writing group said, well, if her mother married the brother, that's a different sperm and a different egg, so it isn't her at all, and I've been stuck for six weeks.

I know there's a real philosophical thing here — the Kripke business about how you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg, and the Lewis counterpart thing where "could have" isn't identity at all. I definately don't need the history of it, and please don't send me to a book. What I need is a procedure I can actually run on a Sunday night in about twenty minutes, on any backstory change I'm considering for any character in the book. A checklist with an order: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the thing just stops and says no, and where it stops and says this isn't a fact question, you're the author, decide it and then be consistent about it. One page, because my editor is the one who'll end up reading it over my shoulder.

Stuff it has to handle, because these are the ones I keep circling:

- Anna's timeline-B origin: same mother, different father, so different sperm and egg.
- The neighbor kid, where I want a switched-at-birth thing: same two gametes, raised by different people. My gut says that one's fine and Anna's isn't, and I couldn't tell you why.
- The one that really scares me: Anna has a twin in one timeline. Same egg, same sperm, the thing split. If origin is what makes you you, the procedure had better not tell me Anna and her twin are the same person.

There's also a version where the embryo gets carried by the mother's sister, and I've read enough to know people now do the three-parent mitochondria thing, and I can't tell if any of that touches identity or just the body. If the procedure has a clause for it, great; if it just has to say outside my jurisdiction, say that.

Tell me where the procedure is doing real work and where it's just me making an aesthetic call with extra steps, and work the three cases — including whichever one I won't like, don't soften it. And tell me what the procedure can't do; I'd rather know the edges than get a false green light. Last line should start with "Verdict:" and be one sentence on whether my central premise — same woman, two timelines, different fathers — survives as written.
