I'm in a decision theory reading group and we keep circling back to the Pasadena game — the one where a coin gets flipped until it first lands heads, and the payoff on the nth toss alternates in sign and grows fast enough that the expected-value series ends up merely conditionally convergent rather than absolutely convergent. So it does "converge" to a finite number, but you can reorder the outcomes and make the sum come out to whatever you want, which is why people describe its expected value as undefined rather than as infinite. What's bugging me is that the standard deflationary move — "expected utility theory just doesn't apply to prospects with undefined expectations, so don't play them, and don't bother having preferences about them" — seems to fall apart once you bring in the Altadena-style companion, which is rigged to come out strictly better than Pasadena in every single state of the world, and yet is just as expectation-less. If expected utility theory can't even say you should prefer the game that dominates the other one, that looks less like a harmless gap in the theory's coverage and more like the theory failing at something it's obviously supposed to do.

Please weigh the main responses people actually give — the "just don't play it" line, the appeal to a dominance principle, attempts to extend the theory with non-standard summation or limiting methods, and the thought that the agent is being asked an ill-posed question in the first place — and then commit to a verdict.
