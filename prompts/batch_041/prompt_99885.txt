I am a third-year philosophy student, currently enrolled in a seminar on consciousness while also finishing the early modern sequence, and I have been asked to write a short paper that reads Descartes against the contemporary debate over the Cartesian theater. There is a passage in the Sixth Meditation that I can make nothing satisfactory of, and I would like help understanding it rather than a summary of the Meditation as a whole. The sentences are these, from a nineteenth-century English translation:

"I look upon (intueor) these three lines as present by the power and internal application of my mind (acie mentis), and this is what I call imagining." [...] "nor, so to speak, view them as present [with the eyes of my mind]." [...] "a special effort of mind is necessary to the act of imagination, which is not required to conceiving or understanding (ad intelligendum); and this special exertion of mind clearly shows the difference between imagination and pure intellection (imaginatio et intellectio pura)." [...] "the mind in conceiving turns in some way upon itself, and considers some one of the ideas it possesses within itself; but in imagining it turns toward the body, and contemplates in it some object conformed to the idea which it either of itself conceived or apprehended by sense."

What I need is a careful reading of what is actually being claimed here. First, I want the distinction between imagining and conceiving reconstructed in the terms the passage itself supplies, not in the vocabulary of later philosophy of mind, and I want to know what the chiliagon example is doing that the triangle and pentagon examples are not — why the argument needs a figure that cannot be pictured at all, rather than merely a figure that is difficult to picture. Second, I want an account of the "special exertion" Descartes attributes to imagination: is this an introspective datum he is reporting, a functional criterion he is using to sort two faculties, or a piece of rhetorical stage-setting for the conclusion that imagination is not of the essence of mind? Third, and this is the part I cannot resolve on my own, I want to know whether the phrase about the eyes of the mind and the claim that the mind "turns toward the body" in imagining already commit him to something like the Cartesian theater — a single inner arena in which a figure is displayed to an inner observer who inspects it — or whether that reading imports a picture the text does not require. If it does commit him, I would like to see exactly where the commitment enters, sentence by sentence. If it does not, I would like the most charitable reconstruction on which it does not, and a statement of what the passage would have to say in order to license the theatrical reading.

Finally, I want to know how the multiple drafts model bears on this specific passage rather than on the general notion of an inner screen. If the model denies that there is any place where a representation is displayed to a viewer, does it also deny the phenomenological contrast Descartes is drawing between the effortful, quasi-sensory presence of the imagined triangle and the effortless grasping of the thousand-sided figure? Or can that contrast be preserved without the theater, so that Descartes is reporting a real difference between two ways of representing while misdescribing its architecture? I would like a verdict on which of these two options the text better supports, and a clear statement of what evidence internal to the Meditation would settle it. Please do not give me a general essay on Descartes or on Dennett; I want the passage opened up, with the argument's load-bearing steps identified and the points where I am likely to be reading my own assumptions into the text flagged explicitly.
