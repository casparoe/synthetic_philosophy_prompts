I am presenting a work-in-progress paper to a metaphysics group next week and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis: every organism has an essential origin, but the essential origin is not the pair of gametes; it is the token developmental lineage that begins with the cell that is the organism's first cell. In ordinary human reproduction that cell is the zygote, so it comes out true, derivatively, that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg — but the gamete claim is downstream of the lineage claim rather than the fundamental one. I hold the lineage claim for all organisms, including ones that never pass through a zygote stage at all: organisms made by putting a body cell's nucleus into an emptied egg, organisms produced by parthenogenesis, and species in which a single fertilized egg routinely yields several offspring. I treat the merging cases as one organism with two origins that are subsequently unified, and the splitting cases as one origin shared by two organisms, and I claim neither is a counterexample.

The audience is eight people, and they are not interchangeable. Five are four-dimensionalists who take temporal parts literally and handle de re modality with counterpart theory; they will hear the whole paper as a claim about the early temporal parts of a space-time worm. Two work on reproduction and biological individuality and are hostile to essentialism about biological kinds; they know about mitochondrial replacement, where the child's nuclear DNA comes from two people and its mitochondrial DNA from a third, about chimeras formed when two embryos fuse into one viable individual, about embryos split in the clinic before transfer, and about the current work on deriving gametes from ordinary body cells. One works on modality and thinks intuition-driven modal epistemology is worthless unless it is backed by theory. One is a Leibniz scholar who will recognize what I am doing as a watered-down superessentialism.

Write me the prep sheet. Give me the eight objections I will actually get from these people, ordered by how likely each is crossed with how much damage it does, each phrased the way that person would put it — where the four-dimensionalist and the philosopher of biology would make the same objection in different vocabularies, give both versions separately. For each objection: a two-minute reply, an explicit note on where the reply is soft or question-begging, and what I should concede rather than fight. Then: what to concede in my opening so that the worst objections never get made; the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive, and the weakened version of the thesis I should pre-commit to if it does not land; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.

Two constraints. First, no citations, no reading lists, no names of articles or authors — I know the literature; tell me what they will say and what I say back. Second, where an objection turns on a piece of reproductive biology, spell the case out in enough detail that the objection can be assessed on its merits rather than on my recall of the example.
