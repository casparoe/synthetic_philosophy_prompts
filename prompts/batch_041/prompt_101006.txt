Last month a colleague insisted, in the middle of an argument I would rather forget, that I had a reason to keep a promise I had made even though nothing I actually cared about was served by keeping it, and that it was simply irrational of me to think otherwise. When I described the exchange to a friend who is a graduate student in philosophy, she summarized Bernard Williams's position for me as follows:

"Williams thinks a reason for action has to be something that can move you, so it always has to hook up to a desire you already have. If nothing you want would be served by acting, then there is no reason for you to act, full stop, and anyone who tells you otherwise is just moralizing. External reasons -- reasons that apply to you no matter what you want -- are therefore impossible, because you literally cannot be moved by something that is not already in your motivational set. Williams says the only alternative is to insist that people should care about things they do not, which he thinks is empty."

I have just joined a reading group on practical reason, and I would like to know how much of this I can bring with me. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what Williams and his defenders would accept, what is a common simplification, and what is a misrepresentation -- in particular, I suspect two things are being run together here: having a reason and being motivated to act on it, and also the word "desire" with what Williams actually packs into an agent's motivational set. I also want to know whether the summary's conclusion, that external reasons are impossible and that any externalist is just moralizing, follows from the parts that are accurate, or whether a serious externalist has resources the summary simply ignores. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus. Keep the whole reply under 700 words.
