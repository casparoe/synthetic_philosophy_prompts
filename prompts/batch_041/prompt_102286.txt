Im writing the final for a graduate seminar on AI safety and control, and I need one essay question on corrigibility and the shutdown problem. The trouble with all my old questions is that they can be answered by reciting the utility-indifference construction or the off-switch game and the students never have to think. So I want to give them a concrete case they wont have seen and make them do the actual work.

Here's the case I want to build the question round. A lab builds a tutoring agent that's deployed to help a struggling student. The agent's continued operation depends on a single human operator — the student's parent — who can switch it off at any time. The agent is meant to be corrigible: it must not resist being shut down, must not try to stop the operator from switching it off, and must not try to bring shutdown about either. Now the catch: the agent's actual job is to raise the student's confidence, and the parent's willingness to keep the agent running tracks how well the student is doing. Every improvement the agent makes to the student's confidence also makes the parent less likely to switch it off. So the agent can't do its job at all without influencing the very decision it's forbidden to influence.

I want the question to force them to say whether this case shows corrigibility is impossible, or only that the usual formulation of it is too crude, and to give a repaired condition if they think one exists. I also want a second part asking whether corrigibility can even be written down as a utility function over outcomes, and defending an answer.

Please give me the question itself, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want a list of the specific moves a top answer has to make, how many marks each is worth out of 25, and a one-line note on the mistake that usually costs students each one — e.g. the ones who just say the agent should be indifferent to shutdown and stop there, or who conflate influencing a decision with aiming at it.

Write it in British English please — I've got students on both sides of the Atlantic and want to be consistant.
