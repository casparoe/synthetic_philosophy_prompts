You are a decision theorist who works on robust cooperation and on the foundations of game theory, and I need you to construct a single worked case for a graduate seminar. Think carefully before you commit to anything, and brainstorm several candidate games internally before you settle; the case you present should be the one that survives every constraint below, not the first one you find.

Open your response with the case itself — the scenario, the payoff matrix, and the transformation, stated compactly in your first few sentences — and then the demonstration that it meets the specifications. No preamble, no restating of this request, no commentary on what you are about to do.

I want to isolate the notion of a safe Pareto improvement from the two ideas it is most often confused with: ordinary Pareto improvement, and improvement in expected value under a shared prior. Take the notion in the following sense. Two principals each delegate play of a one-shot game to someone acting on their behalf. The principals have no usable probability distribution over how the delegates will play, so they cannot rank the game by expected payoff. Instead they consider replacing the game with a different one, and call the replacement safe if, relative to an explicit and modest set of assumptions about how play in the two games corresponds to one another, every contingency those assumptions permit is at least as good for each principal as the corresponding contingency of the original. The improvement is purchased without predicting play and without relying on anyone's rationality.

Here is what your case must satisfy.

1. Two players, one-shot, simultaneous moves, fully specified. Give both a concrete scenario with ordinary human or organisational agents — two firms, two colleagues, two neighbouring households — and the payoff matrix that represents it. Keep it small: two or three actions per player.

2. The original game has a unique Nash equilibrium. This rules out the cheap move of noticing that a game has a bad equilibrium and a good one and recommending the good one; no equilibrium selection is available to you.

3. Your transformation must leave the payoffs at that unique equilibrium exactly as they were. The safety of the transformation cannot be bought by relocating, raising, or reinterpreting the equilibrium payoff.

4. The transformation must be a change to the interaction rather than a move inside it: the actions available, the payoffs attached to them, or standing instructions issued in advance to whoever acts for each side. No side payments, no binding agreement available within the original game, no communication during play, no appeal to the other side's rationality, and no exotic machinery — no perfect prediction, no self-modifying agents, no infinite or hypercomputational reasoning.

5. Non-vacuity: relative to your stated assumptions, the transformation must be weakly better for both players in every permitted contingency and strictly better for both in at least one, and that strict improvement must fall in a contingency the original game also permits. The identity transformation is not a case, and neither is a change that only removes a contingency no one was going to reach anyway — if that is what you have, say so and start over.

Then do the following, in this order.

First, the verification. State your assumption set explicitly, then enumerate the permitted contingencies one by one and show for each why the transformation is at least as good for both players. Do not verify by pointing at an equilibrium of either game; the check has to run contingency by contingency or it is not the check I am asking for.

Second, name the single feature of the case on which the safety depends, and show what happens when that feature is removed. Distinguish carefully between the transformation ceasing to be safe and its ceasing to be an improvement.

Third, contrast the case with what a naive observer would propose — that the two parties should simply both play the cooperative strategy — and say precisely why that proposal is unavailable under the stated assumptions rather than merely unwise.

Fourth, give the strongest objection a critic could press. Two are worth anticipating: that safety here is bought by narrowing the space of possible play so far that the result is a stipulation rather than an improvement; and that your assumption set, not the game, is doing all the work. If your improvement turns out to live entirely in contingencies that no equilibrium analysis would ever predict, say what a party would have to believe about the other side in order to care about it, and whether that belief is reasonable.

Be honest about the limits of the construction throughout. If any of requirements 1 through 5 can only be met approximately, say which, and say what is lost.
