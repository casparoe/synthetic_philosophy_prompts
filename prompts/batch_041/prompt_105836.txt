I spent most of last night arguing with my oldest friend about the problem of dirty hands in politics, and although I still believe I am right, I came away unable to say why his position fails — which is not the same thing as losing, but is close enough to be irritating. What I want from you is not a survey of the subject but a sparring partner: please play a committed defender of his view, sharp and in the first person, willing to concede a point when I have genuinely earned it but never the main claim, and forbidden to step out of the role in order to explain what other writers have said or to ask me what I would like him to say. Keep him in character until I say 'stop'. Have him answer in compact paragraphs of four or five sentences, so that it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and let him put a question back to me whenever he thinks I have quietly changed the subject.

His position, as well as I can reconstruct it, is this. Remorse is cheap, unverifiable, and mostly self-indulgent; the demand that the politician feel it is at bottom a demand that the redress owed to the wronged depend on the sincerity of the man who wronged them; and he suspects the whole tragic register of the debate is a device by which the wrongdoer keeps the last word. The real price of a necessary wrong is therefore paid in capacity rather than in conscience: whoever authorizes it should have accepted in advance a standing handicap on what he may later do — a shortened tenure, a bar on certain offices, a public undertaking never to use the instrument again — and once that handicap is accepted and borne, the debt is discharged, because what we are owed by those who wrong us for our sake is not their suffering but their diminished power to wrong us again. His illustration was a minister who deliberately understates a death toll for two days to prevent a panic that would have killed far more people, and who afterwards accepts that she may never again hold a portfolio in which she could be tempted to repeat it.

My opening move is this. I grant that remorse is unverifiable and often theatrical, but your alternative quietly solves a different problem from the one we have. If what a wrongdoer owes is a reduction of her own future power, then the price of acting rightly in one crisis is a diminished capacity to act rightly in the next, and a system that reliably punishes people for doing what was necessary will select, over time, for ministers who either refuse the necessary act or find a way to pay the handicap out of other people's money — and that cannot be called a price paid, only a price passed on. Worse, your account makes the debt a matter of what she loses rather than of what she has become, so that the minister who accepts the handicap has, on your own terms, settled her account in full; which is just the old claim that a wrong can be paid off, with the ledger now denominated in power instead of in guilt. Answer that, in character, and we can go from there.
