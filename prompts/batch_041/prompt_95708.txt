I've been lurking around the algorithmic fairness literature for a couple years now, mostly the theory side (I don't train models, my math tops out around a first course in probability). The thing that keeps bugging me is what happened after the impossibility results everyone cites — the ones showing that when base rates differ between groups you can't simultaneously have a score that's calibrated within groups and equal error rates across them, or whatever the exact combination is. My impression is that the field absorbed these as "fairness is impossible, pick your poison" and then dispersed into a hundred new definitions, most of which are defended by showing they do something reasonable on a recidivism dataset. That feels like it can't be where the actual value is, but I can't articulate why not.

Here's what I need it for. I'm advising a small foundation that has about $600k and three years to spend on conceptual work in this area, and nobody on the team is an ML engineer, so no "train a better model" projects and nothing that requires a big empirical shop. Which open questions should we fund, in what order, and why? I care much more about the reasoning than the list: I want the dependency structure — which questions are upstream of which, so that we don't blow the budget on something that turns out to be downstream of a question we never answered. Is the live question which criterion is correct, and if so on what grounds would anyone ever settle it? Or is the deeper issue that all these criteria are group-level while the moral complaint people actually make is about individuals, so the whole frame is off? Is there a parallel impossibility lurking for the causal and counterfactual notions of fairness, or do those just relocate the trade-off somewhere less visible? And does the decision threshold turn out to matter more than the score, in which case a lot of the score-level work is beside the point?

One more thread I want you to take seriously rather than swat away. A friend of mine keeps arguing that the real question isn't which criterion is right but who gets to choose it, and with what legitimacy — that this is a procedural and political question wearing a statistical costume, and that the impossibility theorems are interesting mostly as a proof that no technical fix can dissolve the politics. I honestly can't tell if that's a deep reframing or a clever way of dodging the math. Is that line of work upstream of the technical literature, downstream of it, or genuinely orthogonal? And tell me plainly which apparently-open questions we should not fund because the answer wouldn't change what any deployer does — I'd rather hear that three promising-sounding topics are dead ends than get a survey. If it helps, what I'd want from the answer is the criteria you're ranking on, the trade-offs between them, and what we could see in the next year or two that would move something up or down the list.
