I'm a graduate student in decision theory, and a friend and I have been circling the same problem for months over coffee. The problem is what I can only describe as being mugged by a ghost. An agent announces that it will punish you severely unless you hand over something of value, but it has no causal channel to you at all: it cannot reach you now, and it never will. What it does have is a reliable model of how you decide, having either simulated you or worked out your decision procedure well enough to know what you would do. The unsettling part is that the usual reason threats fail — that the threatener has no way to make good on them — seems not to apply here, because your decision and the thing the threat is conditioned on are correlated rather than connected. My friend thinks the whole puzzle is a trick of perspective and that any sane agent simply ignores it; I think ignoring it is harder than it sounds, because the decision to ignore it is itself a procedure that a modeler can see through.

So we have been collecting candidate defenses and arguing about which ones actually do any work. Here are the five we keep returning to. (1) A blanket refusal rule: never let a threat you did not solicit change what you do, however it is delivered and however credible it looks. (2) A surrogate goal: adopt, as your genuine operative aim, some simple goal that no threatener could ever profit from manipulating, so that anyone modeling you sees nothing worth threatening. (3) Public verification: make your decision procedure open and checkable, so that a prospective threatener can confirm in advance that you will not comply. (4) Deliberate non-evaluation: refuse ever to run the comparison between complying and not complying, so that there is no settled fact about you for a threatener to condition on. (5) Value absorption: deliberately reshape your preferences so that you want what the threatener wants, so that the threat never needs to be issued in the first place.

I would like you to rank these five from most to least trustworthy as defenses, where a defense counts as trustworthy to the extent that (a) it works against a wide range of threateners, (b) it does not require you to believe anything false about yourself or about the situation, and (c) it does not cost you much of what you actually care about. Give each item a one-sentence verdict, then a short paragraph on the two or three places where your ordering is most fragile, ideally naming a change in the scenario that would flip it. Finally, tell me which of the five you suspect is doing no real work at all and is merely a restatement of the problem in a reassuring tone.
