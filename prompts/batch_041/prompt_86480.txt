I am a novelist, and I am at the planning stage of a book set in the offices of a humanitarian organisation. Its protagonist is a mid-level administrator who holds, rather more as a temperament than as a doctrine, a broadly Kantian conviction that people are never to be used merely as instruments, whatever the arithmetic of consequences may recommend. For the central scene I need a case that turns on what philosophers call the paradox of deontology, and I should like you to supply it.

What I am after is a situation in which the prohibition on treating a person merely as a means forbids the protagonist to do the one thing that would prevent a larger number of such treatments from occurring, so that her refusal to violate the prohibition leaves her complicit, by omission, in more of the very wrong she declines to commit. Please give me a single case, worked out in concrete institutional detail, rather than a schematic sketch.

Three constraints, if you can meet them. First, avoid the stock illustrations: the transplant surgeon, the runaway trolley, and the familiar wartime variants. I want the instruments to be records, procedures, schedules and other people's labour rather than bodies. Second, the act that the prohibition forbids should leave its victim no worse off — a use of someone's name, labour or private knowledge that they never learn of and that costs them nothing — so that the wrong of using is not hidden behind the wrong of harming. Third, the alternative open to my protagonist should consist in other people being used as means by third parties, rather than merely harmed as a side effect, so that the doctrine of double effect cannot be invoked to dissolve the difficulty.

Finally, tell me briefly why the case is genuinely paradoxical rather than simply a painful trade-off, and what the most promising line of reply would be for someone who holds that the prohibition must be respected in any case. I should add that I want the reader to feel the force of the charge that the protagonist's scruple is a species of self-regard, that she is protecting her own integrity at other people's expense; the example should therefore make that charge hard to brush aside.
