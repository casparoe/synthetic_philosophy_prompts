I'm a third-year philosophy student and the student representative on the research ethics committee at our university-affiliated hospital; I also sit on the ad hoc subcommittee that reviews protocol amendments for the neurostimulation studies, and this term I'm taking a seminar on well-being and its measurement, which is probably why I've been handed this rather than just a vote. On Thursday the full committee takes up a request from the PI of our pilot trial of closed-loop deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression. As amended, the device senses a biomarker and delivers stimulation automatically, but it also includes a patient-facing controller that lets a participant raise or lower the amplitude within bounds the clinician sets. One participant — call her R — has been sitting at the ceiling for eleven weeks. She now scores well inside the protocol's definition of a treatment response on the primary depression scale, and she tells the study psychiatrist that she feels better than she has in twenty years. What has not improved, and by the coordinator's account has gotten worse, is everything the protocol lists as secondary: she has stopped going to work, sees almost no one, and describes her days as riding the sensation. The psychiatrist wants the amplitude reduced; R refuses, says the committee would be substituting its judgment about her life for hers, and has asked that the psychiatrist be taken off her case. The sponsor has written to say that if we restrict the controller it must be restricted for all six participants, and that in its view the primary endpoint has been met — which I take to be at least partly a commercial judgment.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting: a clear recommendation — reduce the amplitude, let her continue, or continue with conditions — with a concrete time frame, reasoning the PI and the sponsor could actually respond to, and an honest account of the strongest argument on the side I'm inclined to lose. Two things I especially want you to get right, because I think they're where the committee will go wrong. First, everyone is treating this as a plain conflict between a competent adult's expressed preference and a clinician's paternalism, and I'm not sure that framing survives the fact that the intervention acts on the very system that produces the preference; say plainly what would have to be true for her report to count as evidence of benefit and what would have to be true for it not to, without pretending the second possibility is obviously true. Second, the protocol's primary endpoint is subjective and its secondary endpoints are functional, and the amendment the sponsor added never says which governs when they diverge; the memo should say how the committee should resolve that, and whether the resolution should be written into the consent document before the remaining participants are enrolled. The memo will be read by a hospital lawyer as well as by clinicians, so keep the empirical claims modest and flag anything that would call for a real capacity assessment rather than a philosopher's opinion.
