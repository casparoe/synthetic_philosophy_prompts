Ok so here's something I keep running into when I teach the ethics of lying and I can't tell if it's a real category or just me being fussy about a case that's already covered.

The pattern is this. Somebody asks me a question, and I happen to know they're walking around with a specific false belief about the situation. I dont say anything false. Everything that comes out of my mouth is true. But I get to pick which true things I say and how I phrase them, and I pick them so that when they slot my answer into the false belief they already had, they end up more sure of it than they were before. The reference-letter version: I write three true and genuinely warm sentences about the parts of the person that are actually strong, I leave out the thing that would sink them, and I know perfectly well that the reader treats the omission as "nothing to worry about." The department-meeting version: a colleague asks if I support their proposal, and I say I have no objections, which is true, I really have no objections, I just also don't think it should pass, and I know that in our dept no-objections reads as a yes. What bugs me is that in all these cases the false belief was already installed before I opened my mouth. I'm not planting it. I'm feeding it. The person walks away wrong, but they'd have been wrong anyway, and every word I said was true.

I know this is near the lying/misleading distinction, and near Grice's point that we hearers routinely infer far more than what's literally said, and maybe near the word paltering, which I've seen used for deceiving people with statements that are technically true. But none of those quite seems to name the feature I actually care about, which is that the deception runs through a false belief the hearer brought with them, rather than one I introduced. Is there an established concept for this narrower thing, and if so what does it include and exclude? If there isnt, coin a term and give me the boundaries. I dont want just the word — I want to know where the edges are. How is this different from ordinary misleading-by-omission, where I'm the one who created the gap? How is it different from just answering the question that was asked, honestly, when the hearer happens to read too much into it? And is it different from paltering, or is it just a special case of it? Then the part I actually lose sleep over: does the fact that I said nothing false carry any moral weight here, or is it a pure technicality I'm hiding behind? My gut says it's somewhere between those two — that there's a real difference but I can't say what grounds it — so I want you to either talk me out of the gut or tell me what makes it true. No reading list, please, I can't look anything up right now, just reason it through with me. And write it as plain continuous prose, no headings or bullets or bold or tables, just the way you'd write it in an email.
