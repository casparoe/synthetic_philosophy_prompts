You are a philosopher specializing in nineteenth-century European thought, with a secondary interest in social epistemology and the philosophy of communication. I want your help diagnosing a disagreement that has stalled, not settling it and not surveying the literature.

Context: I run a faculty seminar on Kierkegaard's social criticism. For four sessions we have been stuck on his critique of the press and of the public — the book-length review of a contemporary novel that English translators render as Two Ages, and that is often excerpted under a title like The Present Age. The impasse is not about the details of the text; everyone in the room knows the text. It is about what kind of claim he is making.

Position A: he is offering a substantive social diagnosis. The press manufactures a public — a phantom, an abstraction that is everywhere and nowhere, that no one embodies and that therefore cannot be held to anything — and this new entity corrodes the conditions under which an individual can act and be answerable for acting. On this reading his claims are empirically assessable: the press really does produce leveling, and one can ask whether he was right about the mechanism and the prognosis.

Position B: he is making an ethico-religious claim, not a social theory. The public is a category of self-deception, a way of being nobody in particular while feeling like part of everything; the critique is addressed to the reader's own evasions, and its truth is not the truth of a prediction about institutions but of a diagnosis of a spiritual condition. On this reading, empirical counterevidence — for instance that print also amplifies genuine individual voices — is beside the point.

Position C: the critique is performatively incoherent. He attacked the press in print, courted publicity, published under pseudonyms, and made his living from the very medium he condemned; the anonymity he denounces is an anonymity he practiced. Whatever the content, the act undercuts it.

Position D, which is mine and which I offer as a hypothesis rather than a settled view: the parties are answering different questions under one heading. A asks what the press does to the conditions of agency; B asks what the public is as a mode of self-relation; C asks whether the author is entitled to say it. Meanwhile the word public is doing at least three jobs — an empirical aggregate, a normative fiction (a tribunal with no members), and a structure of address (a way of speaking that presupposes no determinate addressee) — and the word leveling slides between a sociological process and a moral or spiritual condition.

Your task is to diagnose the disagreement, not to deliver a verdict on who reads Kierkegaard correctly.

1. Brainstorm briefly and visibly first: lay out what each position is committed to, what it would take to refute it, and where the positions may be talking past one another. Then set the brainstorming aside and write the diagnosis proper.
2. Say whether this is best understood as a substantive disagreement about a single question, a verbal disagreement about words like public, press, or leveling, several different questions answered under one heading, or a disagreement about what would even count as evidence for a claim of this kind — and defend your diagnosis against the most plausible alternative to it.
3. If different questions are being answered, say which one the text is actually pressing, and on what basis one can tell, using structural features of the text — who is speaking, to whom, in what genre, under what pseudonymity, and how the medium relates to the message — rather than citation or appeals to scholarly consensus.
4. Consider the test case I find most useful here: anonymous and pseudonymous online speech, in which a speaker addresses an audience that is nobody in particular and can be held to nothing. Does the analysis predict anything about that case that the sociological reading can test and the religious reading cannot, or the reverse? Be honest if the case fails to discriminate between the readings, and say what would discriminate.
5. Tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a reformulation, a piece of evidence, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected here, and explain why.

Two constraints. First, do not give me citations, editions, translators, page numbers, or a reading list; I have the texts and I am not asking for scholarship. Argue from the structure of the positions and the shape of the text. Second, where you are unsure what Kierkegaard actually holds, mark the uncertainty rather than filling it in; a diagnosis that is candid about its interpretive bets is more useful to me than a confident synthesis. Aim for something I could read aloud at the next session to move the discussion.
