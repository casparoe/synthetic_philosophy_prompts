I should like your credence in a claim about aboutness, together with the reasoning that produces it.

The claim sits inside the long-running dispute over original versus derived intentionality. One camp holds that some states are about things in their own right, while the states of books, thermostats, vending machines and (it is said) computers are about things only because we or their makers mean something by them; the other camp holds that this distinction, whatever its rhetorical convenience, does not mark a difference in nature, only in the interpretive stance we happen to adopt. I do not want a rehearsal of the standard examples. Here is the case I should like you to reason about.

Suppose a vending machine, Unit B, was designed by nobody. It is the survivor of a long run of variation and selection over controller circuits, where the only thing selected for was the outcome: dispense a can when a genuine coin is inserted, dispense nothing otherwise. Unit B has one internal state, S, which goes high when its sensor is struck by an object of the coin's conductivity and weight. Throughout the whole selective history, S's going high was perfectly correlated with the presence of a genuine coin. Unit B is then moved to a new environment in which a washer of identical conductivity and weight, but no monetary value, is common; Unit B dispenses cans for washers. Separately, Unit B can be damaged so that S goes high when no object is present at all, and it then dispenses cans into empty air. It has no designer, no author, and no one whose intentions could be said to lend its states their content. (It is, if you like, the two-bitser with its engineer deleted.)

The proposition I want a number for is this: Unit B's state S genuinely represents the presence of a coin. That is to say, there is a fact of the matter about what S is about, such that the washer case is a case of S misrepresenting the situation rather than a mere discrimination the environment has rendered useless, and such that the difference between S and the aboutness of a written sentence is a difference in nature rather than in our descriptive convenience.

Give me your credence in that proposition, decomposed. Include at least the following terms, each with a rough probability and a line of justification:

(a) the probability that intentional content is fixed by a system's causal or selection history, rather than by its present functional role or by the interpreter's stance;
(b) the probability that a designerless process of variation and selection can confer genuine content — the teleosemantic hope — rather than merely producing a state we find it convenient to describe as contentful;
(c) the probability that the original/derived distinction is a difference of degree along a continuum rather than a difference of kind;
(d) the probability that the dispute is at bottom verbal, the two camps answering different questions so that no single proposition is really in dispute.

Then combine them into one number, tell me which term that number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move it most.

I should state where I stand, since I think it locates the disagreement. A colleague who takes her cue from Searle puts the proposition at about 0.9; I put it at about 0.3. Tell me which of the four terms most plausibly accounts for the gap between us, and give your own number for the proposition.
