I've been arguing with a friend about corruption for about a year now and I finally wrote my side of it down as an essay, mostly to stop repeating myself. I have no academic background — I did a bit of economics at night school and that's it — so treat this as an amateur draft and be rough with it. The draft is below, then what I want from you.

THE ESSAY (working title: who owns the point of a thing)

My friend grew up somewhere where the rule is that if you get a government job, you look after your people. Cousins get hired, a brother gets the contract, the family eats. He says the word corruption is a Western export, that the clean impartial state is a story rich countries tell after they already got rich, and that calling his uncle a criminal for feeding his own is just the old move of dressing up somebody else's manners as morality. I used to think this was obviously wrong and now I think both sides of the argument are having the wrong fight.

Both sides argue about the transfer. Was it a gift or a bribe, was it expected, was it proportionate, do the local norms cover it. The relativist says the transfer is covered by the norms, so it isn't corruption. The universalist says a bribe is a bribe whatever you call it. But I don't think the wrongness lives in the transfer at all. I think it lives in whose purposes the institution is serving.

Here's my term: purpose theft. When an official takes money to issue a permit, or puts his nephew on the payroll, the thing that's actually gone wrong isn't that money moved and it isn't that a norm was broken. It's that the institution has been quietly reassigned. The office, the law, the budget, the title, the whole apparatus was assembled for a purpose — and the official has started treating that purpose as raw material for his own. The building faces the public; he turns it around to face his family. Nothing about the money is essential to this. It's theft of a direction, not of a thing.

I like this because it gets some cases right that the usual stories fumble. A wedding present after a fair decision isn't corruption, because it doesn't change what the institution does — the direction is unchanged. Same for hospitality, same for a tip. But a revolving door isn't covered by any bribe law and it is corruption on my account: you wrote the rules and then you went to work for the people the rules were about. And hiring your useless nephew is corruption even where it's legal, because the purpose of the hiring was never to staff the office with the boss's family.

It also explains why "everyone does it" doesn't touch the objection, and why the amount doesn't matter much. What matters is the angle the institution is pointing at.

But then I hit a wall, and this is the part I want you to attack. Purpose theft needs a principal — somebody whose purpose it is. If the village genuinely never thought the clinic was theirs, if it was always understood as the big man's thing that he graciously lets people use, then have you stolen anything? Maybe not. So maybe the wrong isn't the redirection but the secrecy: the institution has to be described one way in public and run another way in practice, and the person running it knows both ledgers. On that version corruption is closer to a lie than to a theft — you're not robbing the public, you're maintaining a false story about what the office is for. Which sounds better to me some days and worse on others, because it seems to make corruption depend on how good the official story is, and a place with no official story of impartiality would then have nothing to betray.

There's also a case that bothers me. Two officials in the same office both want to be seen as the generous one, so they compete to hand out favours and the public actually gets served faster. My theory says that's corrupt and my gut says it's fine, or at least better than a lazy honest office. And the low paid nurse who takes money to jump someone up a queue that's broken anyway — she's not redirecting an institution that was working, she's skimming off one that already failed. I don't know what my account says about her.

So: purpose theft, or the lie of two accounts, or something I haven't got. Help me.

WHAT I WANT

- Where does the argument actually break? Which step is carrying more weight than it can bear? I'd rather hear the one fatal objection than five small ones.
- Does purpose theft do any work that plain old abuse of trust doesn't? If it's the same idea in a new coat, say so, I can take it.
- Is the second half (the two ledgers, corruption as a lie) a real escape from relativism or just a cleverer way of being a relativist? What's the first thing someone who thinks there are universal standards would say back to me, and do I have an answer?
- Are my examples too tidy? Give me cases that break both versions. I already have the competing officials and the nurse.
- Is corruption even the right word for what I'm describing, or am I describing something narrower that happens to overlap with it? If I've just renamed one species of a bigger genus, what's the genus?
- Finally, is any of this interesting, or is it the sort of thing that seems profound at 1am and is obviously confused by breakfast?

Argue with me directly, don't be polite about it, and please don't hand me a reading list — I won't read it and it won't help me. Just tell me where I'm wrong.
