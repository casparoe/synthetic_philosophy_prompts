I was at a reading group on democratic theory last week, and a friend made the case for epistocracy using the pilot analogy that keeps circulating in these debates: nobody would put the controls of an airliner to a vote among the passengers; you defer to the trained pilot; and so, he said, with complex policy, which should be left to those who actually know. When I objected that at least the passengers get to choose where the plane is going, he refined the analogy rather than abandoning it. The passengers set the destination, he said, while the flight crew chooses the route and the altitude — ends are for the many, means are for the few — and he added that the passengers do not even select the pilot, since a certification board does, so my follow-up worry that experts would simply install themselves misses the mark. On the strength of all this he concluded that we should replace an elected legislature with a body selected by competence examinations. I left unconvinced but unable to say precisely where the argument was doing work and where the analogy was carrying it.

So I would like the analogy audited rather than the literature surveyed. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the pilot, the passengers, the destination, the route, the certification board, the control tower, the weather — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I especially want to know which features of the source the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks (a single agreed destination, a fact of the matter about the best route, a crew whose interests align with the passengers', passengers who can in principle leave), which of the disanalogies are load-bearing for his institutional conclusion and which are incidental noise, and whether that conclusion survives once the load-bearing ones are on the table. If a better analogy would do the same argumentative work without the smuggling, say what it is; if none would, say that too.
