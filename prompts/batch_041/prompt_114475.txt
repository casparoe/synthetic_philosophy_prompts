I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of psychiatry, and I have been invited to contribute a piece of short fiction to an interdisciplinary workshop on the mutual shaping of psychiatric categories and the people they classify. What I need from you is the story itself, roughly two thousand five hundred to three thousand five hundred words.

The idea I want the story to carry is the looping effect — the phenomenon Ian Hacking described, in which a classification applied to people rather than to things alters those people, and their alterations in turn feed back into the classification, which is revised, which alters them again. I do not want a story in which this is explained. I want one in which it is enacted, so that a reader who has never encountered the idea nonetheless watches it happen and feels how strange it is.

Please build it around a premise that keeps it clear of the well-worn examples. Instead of a clinician who invents a diagnosis, make the loop run through administrative machinery: a screening instrument, a coding entry, a reimbursement category, a school questionnaire, something of that kind that circulates widely, sorts people, and thereby helps manufacture the population it was designed to detect. Then let the category be withdrawn — removed outright, merged into another, or simply allowed to lapse — and let the story follow what that does to people who had built their self-understanding, their community, and in some cases their livelihood around it. The withdrawal should open a new niche as much as it closes the old one; the people who are un-made by it should be shown doing the un-making themselves, sometimes eagerly, sometimes in grief, sometimes by locating the next available slot.

A few things matter to me here. The classified people must not be passive or merely pitiable; they should be shown pushing back, advocating, gaming the criteria, writing their own materials, and disagreeing with one another about what they are. At least one scene should catch a character reasoning about their own case in the category's own vocabulary, so that the loop is visible from inside a mind and not only from the outside. The story should resist resolving into either of the two easy moralized readings — that the category was a fiction imposed on innocent patients, or that it was a real condition finally recognized — and should instead let the reader sit with the reciprocal causality itself, including the uncomfortable possibility that both readings are partly right at different stages. I would prefer that you not name real diagnostic categories; invent the ones you need, and do not manufacture references.

At the end, in the same continuous prose, add a brief note on where the story takes liberties with the theory or leans on a contested reading of it. I would like you to separate what is settled from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this, and not to present a contested interpretation as the consensus; if the story depends on a reading that some philosophers of psychiatry would reject, say so plainly and describe what the alternative would look like.

Please write the whole response as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Use American English.
