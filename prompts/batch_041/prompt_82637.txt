Since the 1960s the quarrel over Ayn Rand's rejection of altruism — the theme of The Virtue of Selfishness and a staple of the ethics seminars that still rehearse it — has run on without either side conceding anything, and what I want from you is a diagnosis rather than another round of the argument. The Randian side defines altruism as the doctrine that self-sacrifice is a virtue and the service of others the moral ideal, and concludes that altruism demands the surrender of the self; its opponents take altruism to mean nothing more than serious regard for other people's interests, and reply that Rand is demolishing a caricature. My hypothesis is that the impasse is partly verbal — two concepts wearing one word — but that a substantive disagreement hides underneath it: whether there are agent-neutral reasons at all, and whether self-sacrifice is even coherent once the strict self/other dichotomy is questioned. That second point seems to matter, since Rand's own egoism claims to accommodate love, friendship, and mutually beneficial trade, which raises the possibility that the live question is whether non-sacrificial concern for others counts as altruism. Diagnose which kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, metaethical, or two parties answering different questions, one about what motivates people and one about what they have reason to do — and ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims. Then say what would count as progress: a stipulated definition, a distinction, or an argument that the dispute cannot be resolved as posed. Lead with your diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble.
