For the final of an upper-level course on measurement, incentives and institutional design, I need one 60-minute essay question worth 20 marks, framed around a concrete institutional case (a research-funding formula, say, or a hospital waiting-time target) and not answerable by reciting the standard line about measures that become targets. The question should hand students the four variants of the failure that are usually distinguished — regressional (selection on an imperfect proxy selects for the noise as well), extremal (optimization pushes the system into regions where the old proxy-goal relationship no longer holds), causal (the collapse is produced by the regulator's own intervention) and adversarial (the collapse is produced by an agent with goals of its own) — and then ask them, for each variant, whether either of two proposed remedies would work: measuring the goal itself rather than by proxy, and keeping the metric confidential so that it cannot be gamed. It should close by asking them to assess the thesis that any proxy not perfectly aligned with the goal must fail once it is optimized hard enough, and to say which variant, if any, survives both remedies. Give me the model answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two and each stating one move a top answer must make, the marks it carries (four each, twenty in total) and the specific error that should cost those marks; between them the five points should cover all four variants and the closing thesis, and there should be no introduction or conclusion.
