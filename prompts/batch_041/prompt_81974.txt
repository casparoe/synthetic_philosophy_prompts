I'm writing a two-page proposal for a one-semester MA thesis and I've got a half-formed idea I want you to turn into something usable. Here it is: every defence of a Ulysses contract I can find seems to lean on the thought that when the later self tries to wriggle out of the deal, his dissent doesn't really count, because it's produced by the very temptation the contract was meant to guard against — he's under the influence, so to speak. But that's a claim about impaired judgment, and impaired judgment is not the same thing as changed preference. Someone can soberly, wholeheartedly, with no craving clouding anything, prefer to abandon the plan they set up. So the load-bearing premise looks much narrower then people assume. Now the constitutional version: constitutional precommitment is standardly defended by analogy with Ulysses — majorities shouldn't get to repeal entrenched rights on a whim. I want to argue the analogy only goes through if a majority wanting to repeal a right is relevantly like a craving-addled Ulysses, which in ordinary cases it plainly isn't. But I suspect there's a narrow class of cases (panic, wartime, referendum fever) where it maybe is, and I can't tell whether that rescues the analogy or just concedes that constitutionalism's justification is far thinner than advertised.
