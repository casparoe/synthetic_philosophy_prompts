So two friends of mine, Maya and Dev, got into it at a party after I told them about a project a lab I used to work near is doing. The setup: they took a robot with a camera, a gripper, and a couple years of banging around a kitchen, and logged everything it did and said — every 'that's a cup', every failed grasp, all of it. Then they trained a second model on nothing but that transcript. No camera, no body, just the text of what the grounded robot said and did. Call it the twin. The fight was about whether the twin's words mean the same things the robot's did, or whether the twin is just shuffling tokens that happen to line up.

Maya said: empty. A symbol only means something if it eventually hooks onto stuff you can pick out, point at, act on, get wrong. The twin's words are defined only by other words in the transcript — it's a dictionary that never leaves the dictionary. The robot earned 'cup' by having cups resist its grip and spill on it; the twin just knows which strings tend to sit near which other strings. She said if you rerouted the twin's 'cup' output to a random light, nothing in its behavior would break, which shows there was never a cup in there to begin with.

Dev said that's too quick. The twin's symbols aren't free-floating — they're statistically welded to a record of a creature that was grounded, so the grounding gets inherited. His analogy: a blind person learns plenty about colors from sighted people and isn't just making mouth noises. He added that the twin has something the robot didn't: it was tuned on feedback, so it knows which answers get a thumbs-up and which get scolded, and consequences are a kind of contact with the world too.

Maya said the blind-person analogy cheats, because the blind person shares a body, a lifespan, and a world with the sighted — she can bump into the same tables. The twin shares none of that, only the strings. And she said inherited grounding proves way too much: by that logic, if I photocopy a grounded person's diary, the photocopier is now talking about cups. On the feedback point she said thumbs-up isn't the world — learning to dodge disapproval is being grounded in approval, not in cups; you could tune a system to farm thumbs-up while its 'cup' referred to nothing at all.

Dev said fine, drop the inheritance argument, the behavioral one stands by itself: if the twin uses 'red' in every context a sighted person would, corrects you when you call grass red, refuses to say ripe tomatoes are blue — what exactly is missing? He said Maya is positing a little inner ghost that has to be lit up for meaning, and that's the thing to be suspicious of, not the twin. Maya said behavior can't be the whole story, because a big enough lookup table could fake the same behavior, and she doesn't believe the table means red — and she bets Dev doesn't either.
