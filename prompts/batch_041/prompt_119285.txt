Two colleagues of mine fell into an argument last week after our team lead announced that there would be one unannounced spot-check of our work sometime before Friday, and that we would not be able to predict on which day it would come.

(1) Marcus laid out the familiar chain: the spot-check cannot fall on Friday, because if Thursday passed without one we would know it had to be Friday, and then it would not be unannounced; by the same step it cannot fall on Thursday; and so on back to Monday. He concluded that the lead had promised something that cannot happen.

(2) Dana said the chain proves the opposite of what Marcus takes it to prove. The chain is something we do; the moment we accept it we believe no spot-check is coming, and a team that believes no spot-check is coming is surprised whenever one arrives. The promise, she said, is therefore not impossible but close to self-fulfilling.

(3) Marcus answered that this makes the announcement a trick rather than a promise: it can be kept only by getting us to believe something false, and it depends on our reasoning going wrong. If we reason well, we should conclude not that no spot-check is coming but that the announcement is unreliable, and then nothing at all follows about which day it will fall on.

(4) Dana replied that the lead promised only that we would not know the day, and that the announcement's curious self-referential character — it tells us something about our own knowledge — is precisely what makes such knowledge impossible, however well we reason.

Please adjudicate move by move: say who is right about each of the four points and why, identify what each party is missing, and say who has the better of the argument overall.
