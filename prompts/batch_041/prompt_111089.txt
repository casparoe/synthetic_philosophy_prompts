For the past six years I have served as an outside member of the board of a humanitarian organization that responds to food crises, and I have been asked to write the decision memo that settles an argument the board has been having, without resolution, for two years. My career was in agricultural science — thirty-five years on drought, soils, and crop failure — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy seriously but unsystematically. That reading has persuaded me that our argument is not really about facts. It is about concepts we have never made explicit, and the memo should therefore do conceptual work before it recommends anything.

The question before the board is what our organization should say, in its public statements and in the assessment framework we hand to field staff, about the causes of famine. One group holds that famines are natural disasters — drought, flood, crop disease, the failures of weather — and that describing them as anything else is both bad science and bad strategy: it politicizes relief, costs us access, and turns our assessments into weapons. The other group holds that famines are political crimes: that people starve when they are prevented from commanding food rather than because food is absent, and that calling a political killing a natural event launders responsibility. Both sides cite the same three cases — the Bengal famine of the 1940s, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward — and both, so far as I can tell, read their conclusions off those cases rather than out of them.

Draft the memo I will circulate before the next meeting. I am not asking for a history of those famines, and I do not want death tolls: I would not trust my own memory of them, and they are contested in any case. I want the reasoning that would let the board decide. In particular:

1. Say what we commit ourselves to when we call a crisis a famine, and defend a definition. I understand that agencies often work with thresholds — mortality rates, consumption shortfalls, coping indicators — and that such thresholds are partly administrative and partly performative, since whether a famine is officially declared affects whether anyone responds to it. Say which kind of definition we should adopt, and whether a purely descriptive one is even available.

2. Take seriously the objection that "natural" and "political" are not rival causes but descriptions of one event at different levels. My own instinct, after a career spent on drought, is that weather is the trigger and institutions are the setting; I am no longer confident that the distinction survives scrutiny. If a drought is a necessary condition of the deaths and a policy decision is also a necessary condition, on what basis do we single one out as the cause? I have read, though I could not now reconstruct the argument, that we tend to name as "the cause" whichever factor departs from what we take to be the normal course of things — which would make the attribution rest on a baseline we choose rather than find. If that is right, the memo should say so plainly, and say which baseline we are choosing.

3. Address the counterfactual problem honestly. To say that a government caused a famine appears to require a claim about what would otherwise have happened, and in a single case that claim cannot be tested as a scientist would want to test it. State what evidence we would accept for it, how much confidence we should have in it, and whether our public statements should be calibrated to that confidence or permitted to outrun it.

4. Reckon with the institutional consequences of whichever framing you recommend — neutrality and access on one side, accountability and witness-bearing on the other; funding, staff safety, and the risk that our assessments become instruments of the governments they assess. I would rather have a recommendation that says what it is giving up than one that pretends no trade-off exists.

5. End with a bottom line I can put to a vote: what we will say, in what terms, with what reservations, and what would have to change for us to revisit it.

Please state the strongest argument on the losing side in its own terms, and make the conceptual moves explicit rather than gesturing at them. Assume I have no library at hand and will not be looking anything up.
