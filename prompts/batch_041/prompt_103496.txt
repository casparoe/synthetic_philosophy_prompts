I teach an upper-level seminar on wellbeing and public policy, and I want to run a 90-minute session that makes about 20 students actually feel the weirdness of national happiness rankings -- the sense that a country being #1 might not mean what they assume, and that a country deliberately trying to top the list would tie itself in knots. I don't want a lecture where I explain construct validity and Goodhart at them. I want them to run into it with their hands.

Here's the shape I'm imagining, but please build out the real thing: split them into 5 or 6 teams, each team is a country, and over three rounds each team allocates a budget (say 100 points a round) across policy levers. Give them obvious levers -- income and jobs, health, social connection and family time, civil liberties, charity and generosity, anti-corruption enforcement -- and then two or three cheap, slightly sleazy levers that a cynical minister would love: a national pride and optimism ad campaign, and a thing where you nudge the order and framing of the survey before citizens rate their lives (reminding them how much worse off poorer countries are, that kind of priming). Their score each round is a single national number built from a simplified survey-style ladder question -- imagine the one where people place their current life somewhere between the worst and best possible life -- and the team with the highest reported number wins the round. Small stakes: first pick of the snack table, or chocolate, or just a running trophy. No real money.

The bit I care most about: keep a second, hidden track that you compute but don't show them. It's your fake version of how life actually feels day to day, and it responds to health and social connection and maybe sleep, barely at all to the ad campaign, and not at all to survey framing. So teams that pile into the cheap levers can climb the public leaderboard while the hidden track stays flat or drops. Show the leaderboard every round but never the hidden track until the end. And make the reported number a mean, so that two countries can post identical scores while one has everybody moderately content and the other is split between a very happy top and a miserable bottom -- I want a team to discover mid-game that cutting taxes for the already-happy and lifting the very bottom move the same mean in opposite directions morally.

Rules I want in there: rounds are simultaneous, teams see only the public leaderboard between rounds, you reveal that the sleazy levers exist only after round one (or let them find it in the rule sheet, buried, and see who reads), and you forbid them from talking to other teams. Then a debrief that maps each moment onto the idea -- the ad campaign and framing lever onto whether the index tracks happiness or tracks the answer to a survey question; the mean-versus-distribution bit onto whether a ranking can measure a population's wellbeing at all if it ignores spread; the regression-style levers onto the temptation to read the report's explanatory factors as causal policy handles when they're really just a variance decomposition. End by asking them the actual question: should a country try to top these rankings, and if not, what is the ranking even for?

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it -- I'd bet teams spend round one arguing about my model instead of the concept, and someone dumps the whole budget into one lever to see what breaks -- and how to handle that without killing the momentum. And be honest with me about what the activity can't do: I'm handing them a fake true-wellbeing track that I invented, so the game can't tell them what wellbeing really is or settle whether hedonic, desire-satisfaction, or objective-list accounts are right, and it can't reproduce the real measurement headaches like whether the ladder question even means the same thing across cultures. Flag which parts of the philosophical payoff I have to supply myself after the game rather than pretend the game delivered. Keep it runnable by one person with a whiteboard and some slips of paper.
