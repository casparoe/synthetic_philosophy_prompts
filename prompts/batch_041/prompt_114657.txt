I've been following the decision theory threads in the rationalist blogosphere and the philosophy of probability literature for a while, and I keep coming back to the Pasadena game as a kind of stress test for expected utility theory. Here's the setup, in case it helps to have it stated plainly: a fair coin is flipped until the first heads. If heads appears on the nth flip, the payoff is (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n divided by n. So the payoffs are +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on, with the probability of the nth outcome being 1/2^n. If you sum the expected contributions in the natural order, you get the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2. But the series is not absolutely convergent: the sum of the positive expected contributions diverges, and the sum of the negative expected contributions diverges. So the expected value is not well-defined in the usual sense — you can rearrange the terms to get any real number you like. The Altadena game is the same except that 1 is added to every payoff, so its natural sum is ln 2 + 1, but it too has an undefined expected value. And of course Altadena dominates Pasadena outcome-by-outcome, which makes the failure of expected utility theory especially stark: the theory cannot even say that the game that pays more in every possible outcome is better.

What I want from you is not a full theory but the desiderata for one. Write the list of requirements that any adequate theory of rational choice in the presence of undefined expected values must meet. I'm interested in the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the results it must be consistent with, and the puzzles it must dissolve or explain. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. For example, must the theory deliver a verdict on whether the Pasadena game is preferable to a sure $0, or is it allowed to say that rationality is silent here? Must it respect dominance, so that Altadena is ranked above Pasadena? Must it avoid sensitivity to arbitrary reorderings of the outcomes? Must it distinguish undefined expected value from infinite expected value, as in the St. Petersburg game? Must it explain why the natural ordering of the Pasadena series seems privileged, or explain away that appearance? Must it handle the Altadena game's relation to Pasadena — if you add a constant to every payoff, does the value shift by that constant, or does the game remain valueless? Must it say something about repeated play, or about combining these games with ordinary gambles? Must it be consistent with the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, or explain which axiom fails? And so on.

I'd also like you to flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want to see, because I suspect the list itself is where the real result lies. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and explain which ones are in tension and why. Finally, briefly, how do the main existing approaches — plain expected utility theory, limit-based approaches that take the limit of truncated expectations, non-standard analysis, dominance-plus-something, and whatever else you think is a serious contender — do against the list? Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than the scorecard; I'll do the scoring myself.
