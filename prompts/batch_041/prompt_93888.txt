For thirty-five years as a working physicist I took for granted that a theory fitting data already in hand is in better evidential shape than one that does not; only lately, reading philosophy of science, have I learned how hard that platitude is to make precise. The difficulty, I gather, is called the problem of old evidence: on the usual probabilistic account of confirmation, a datum that is already certain cannot raise the probability of a hypothesis, so the fit between an old measurement and a new theory appears to do no evidential work at all. Several repairs have been proposed, but before I choose among them I want the specification.

Write the desiderata: what must any adequate treatment of confirmation deliver if it is to handle old evidence? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of a particular camp. I would like the list to address at least the following, if these belong on it at all — and I would rather be told that one of them is a prejudice than have it quietly accommodated:

- The historical episodes in which agreement with a datum obtained before the theory is treated as part of the case for the theory (the precession of Mercury's perihelion being the stock example). An account that declares old evidence inert loses the very confirmations it was meant to explain.
- The case in which the old datum was not merely known beforehand but was used in constructing the hypothesis, so that the agreement is not independent testimony. The account must not let a hypothesis be confirmed by the considerations that went into inventing it.
- The case in which the observation is old but its bearing on the hypothesis is noticed only later — a measurement that sat unremarked in the literature until a theory made it relevant. I want to know whether the account counts this as old evidence or as new, and on what ground.
- The difference between evidence that is old for a community and evidence that is old only for the individual who has just encountered it. Is the confirmatory relation relative to a person, or to the public record?
- A constraint against solving the problem by imputing mere ignorance to the scientist. If an old datum confirms only because we had not yet seen that it followed from the hypothesis, the account seems to make confirmation turn on our limitations rather than on the world.
- The ordinary asymmetry: a datum arriving after the hypothesis is supposed to count for more than the same datum arriving before. If that intuition must be surrendered, say so plainly.

Close by telling me which single requirement does the most to rule out the accounts currently on offer, and whether any two of the requirements cannot be satisfied together. I would rather learn that the list is inconsistent than receive a tidy one that conceals the conflict.
