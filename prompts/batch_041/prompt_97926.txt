I'm a science journalist and I'm working up a feature on why a small but stubborn corner of mathematics refuses to sign off on a rule the rest of us use without thinking — the law of excluded middle — and I keep tripping over the same knot. A logician I interviewed explained that for an intuitionist a disjunction isn't a bare 'one of these two things is the case'; it's a claim to have a proof of one of the disjuncts and to know which. That much I can hold in my head. But then he added that intuitionistic logic proves not-not-(A or not-A) for any A, and that not-not-not-A is equivalent to not-A. So double negation is strong enough to give you back excluded middle and weak enough that it can't be cancelled, which sounds like a contradiction in a system that's supposed to be a tidy subset of the classical one. And when I asked whether intuitionists think 'A or not-A' might actually be false, he said no, they simply won't assert it — which left me unable to tell whether we're talking about mathematics or about the limits of what anyone has managed to prove so far.

What I'd like from you is a proper untangling of this, pitched at someone who did a bit of logic at university and hasn't entirely forgotten it, but who isn't going to be fobbed off with 'intuitionists reject excluded middle because they're constructivists'. In particular: why does a proof of a disjunction have to tell you which disjunct, and what does that do to the disjunction and existence properties — and are those properties a consequence of the proof-reading of the connectives, or an extra stipulation bolted on? What exactly is a Brouwerian counterexample, and how is it different from a disproof; I suspect the answer is that it shows a statement to be unprovable-in-the-present-state-of-knowledge rather than false, and if so I want to know whether that makes intuitionism a doctrine about our ignorance rather than about mathematical reality. For concreteness, walk me through one of those real numbers defined by a recipe that waits on an unresovled question — I've seen a version that hangs on runs of 7s in the decimal expansion of pi — and show me why 'this number is either zero or not zero' can't be settled. Then tell me whether I've been sloppy in running together Brouwer's intuitionism and Bishop-style constructive analysis, because I gather one is closer to a philosophy of mind and the other to a discipline of writing proofs that carry computational content, and I'd like the difference spelt out. Finally, I've heard it said that intuitionstic logic is the logic of all Kripke frames while classical logic is the logic of frames with a single world — if that's a fair way of putting it, explain why, and tell me whether there's anything to the slogan that classical logic is just intuitionistic logic with every formula wrapped in two negations. Skip the reading list, I want the explanation itself; and don't be shy of the technical detail.
