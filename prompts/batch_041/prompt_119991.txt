I retired from a physiology department a few years back — forty years of sleep work, mostly respiratory, a good chunk of it in the clinic with the apnea patients — and since then I've been reading philosophy in the mornings, which I did not expect to enjoy this much. What I'm stuck on is whether dreams are experiences. I read Norman Malcolm's little book on dreaming and thought it was outragous, then read Dennett pushing back and thought he had him, and now I've gone back to Malcolm and I'm not so sure. What bothers me is that I can't tell how many positions are actually on the table, because half the time two people who sound like they're disagreeing turn out to be answering different questions.

Could you build me a decision tree for this? I want to be able to take a claim someone makes about dreams, feed it through, and land on a position. Start from the one thing I'd have thought everyone agrees on, that the only direct evidence we ever get is what a person says after being woken up, and make the branches questions a person could answer without already knowing which side they'd land on. Branches I can see needing, at minimum: whether the report is a report of anything at all; if it is, whether the experience happened while the sleeper was asleep or got manufactured at the moment of waking or when someone asked about it; whether the experience is perceptual (a hallucination), imaginative (something the dreamer imagines rather than sees or hears), or just thought; and whether the answer has to be the same for every dream or can vary with the kind of dream. On each branch, please mark whether it's a dispute about the facts of the sleeping brain or about what we mean by the word dream — that's what I keep tripping over, and I suspect at least one of these disagreements dissolves once you seperate them.

Cases I want the tree to handle, since they're what convinced me there's a real question: (1) lucid dreamers who, in the lab, make pre-arranged eye movements to signal that they know they're dreaming and to answer yes/no questions put to them, while still asleep; (2) REM sleep behaviour disorder patients who act their dreams out — punching, shouting — with the behaviour matching what they report on waking; (3) dreams that swallow an outside stimulus, where you say someone's name over the intercom and they report a dream in which a voice said their name; (4) reports from awakenings out of non-REM sleep and from the moment of falling asleep, which look a lot like the REM ones; (5) nights where the person reports nothing at all.

Leaves I'd want to see, at minimum: Malcolm's line, if I've got him right, that a dream simply isn't the sort of thing that can be an experience, because the whole notion of a dream is tied to the telling of it; the more empirical version where nothing is experienced during sleep and the story gets assembled on waking; the option that says the evidence can't decide and maybe there's nothing there to decide; the view that dreams are genuine perceptual experiences generated during sleep; the view that they're imaginative rather than perceptual; and the kind-relative view on which only some dreams, lucid ones say, are experiences. If your tree forces a leaf that nobody actually holds, leave it in and say so, and tell me whether it's empty because it's incoherent or just because nobody's bothered. One sentence per leaf on the commitment that would make me wince if I had to sign it.

And flag any two leaves your tree can't really seperate with the cases I've given — I'd rather know where the instrument gives out than have it papered over. Last thing: do those science cases settle anything against Malcolm, or does he get to say that a fellow doing sums with his eyes in REM sleep still isnt dreaming in his sense? Thats the bit I actually want to know.
