Modern psychiatric diagnosis rests on a construction procedure with two features that I want to understand philosophically. First, a proposed category earns its place in the manual only by passing field trials in which independent clinicians, working from the written criteria, assign the diagnosis to the same patients; inter-rater agreement, usually reported as a kappa statistic, functions as the gate a category must clear. Second, most categories include among their criteria a requirement that the symptoms cause significant distress or impairment in the person's functioning — absent that, the symptoms do not amount to a disorder at all. All of this sits inside a manual that presents itself as descriptive and atheoretical: a catalogue of what is there, not a theory about it.

What I want is an account of what this practice presupposes, because I find a tension in it. If the categories simply described real divisions in nature, why would the construction of a category be governed by how readily observers agree about it? Agreement looks like a fact about us, not about the division. And why would the line between disorder and non-disorder run through whether the person is suffering, which sounds like an evaluation, inside a document that claims to be value-neutral and descriptive? Hacking's observation that human kinds are interactive — that classifying people changes them, so the target of the classification shifts as the classification is applied — seems to bear on both features at once.

Here is what I am asking for.

Reconstruct the implicit theory. What view of psychiatric kinds, of the relation between reliability and validity, and of the fact/value boundary does the practice commit to? For each commitment, say whether the practice strictly presupposes it or merely fits comfortably with it, and name the rival view it quietly rules out. Then take the hardest case directly: can the reliability gate distinguish a category that tracks an observer-independent kind from one whose agreement is manufactured by the label's own social uptake? If it cannot, say precisely what that does to the manual's self-description as descriptive — whether the self-description is false, merely misleading, or defensible under a reading you can supply.

Then the inconsistency. Are the reliability requirement, the distress-or-impairment criterion, and the atheoretical self-image mutually consistent, or must one give way? Give a definite verdict, not a survey of positions.

Finally, suppose we replaced the presupposed view with one that took looping effects seriously — treating the categories as interactive kinds and their reliability as a mutable social fact rather than a certificate of naturalness. What concretely would have to change in how a manual is built? Name the changes, and say which current features would survive them.

Be committed throughout: state the single commitment the practice cannot do without, and give a verdict on whether the practice is coherent as it stands, with your reasons.

After the analysis, provide a table with these columns: Commitment | Where in the practice it shows | Status (strictly presupposed / merely fits / left open) | Rival view it rules out | What changes if we drop it. One row per commitment, and a final row giving your overall verdict.
