A friend of mine, Dana, has spent the past few weeks trying to build a self-contained argument showing that the brain-in-a-vat scenario, pressed hard enough, refutes skepticism itself rather than the external world. She asked me to help her find the flaw and I can't, so I'm passing it on. Here it is as she set it out.

(1) From the inside, you cannot tell whether you are an ordinary embodied person or a brain in a vat being fed a seamless simulation. Call any two situations you cannot distinguish from the inside "phenomenally equivalent."

(2) If two hypotheses are phenomenally equivalent for you, then your evidence gives you no more reason to accept one than the other; they stand on a justificatory par.

(3) So you have no more reason to accept "I am an ordinary person who has hands" than "I am a brain in a vat who merely seems to have hands."

(4) If you have no more reason to accept a proposition p than to accept some hypothesis that entails not-p, then you do not know p.

(5) Therefore you do not know that you have hands — or anything else about the external world.

So far, ordinary skepticism. Then Dana continues.

(6) But the vat hypothesis is itself only one hypothesis among many. Consider "I am a brain in a vat that has just now been reset so as to seem to be a person weighing this very argument" against "I am a brain in a vat that has been in the vat for years and is now weighing this argument." These two are also phenomenally equivalent for you.

(7) By (2), you have no more reason to accept the long-tenure vat hypothesis than the just-reset one.

(8) So you cannot be justified in believing that you are a brain in a vat of any particular kind; and since every skeptical hypothesis is phenomenally equivalent to every other, no single skeptical hypothesis can be picked out as the one you ought to believe.

(9) So if (2) is true, the skeptic cannot even present their own position as the justified one. Skepticism, pushed to this point, cancels itself. Therefore (2) is false, and we are entitled to go on trusting our senses.

Dana wants to keep (2). She regards it as the honest engine of the whole skeptical argument, and she is unwilling to give up the conclusion in (5) just to escape (9). I think something slips between the steps, but every time I try to say where, I find myself defending (2) and landing back at skepticism. Which step should she reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost her — in particular, does she have to abandon (2) or (5)? If there is a reading on which the chain is valid end to end, tell me which premise is least costly to give up instead. Take a definite position rather than laying out the options; I want a verdict with reasons, not a menu.
