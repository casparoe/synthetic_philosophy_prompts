Im leading a session next month for our reading group — a dozen people, mostly non-philosophers (three engineers, a lawyer, a couple of biologists), smart but nobody's read any philosophy of mind — and I've somehow ended up with the moral status of digital minds. Don't explain the topic to me, plan how I explain it. I've got 90 minutes and a whiteboard.

What I'm after is the order the pieces have to go in and why each depends on the last. I want them to come out the other side seeing three things: that the question isn't "is it really conscious" in the spooky sense; that having experience and having a stake in your own continuation are two seperate things that can come apart; and that there's a prior question nobody asks, which is which entity we're even talking about — the trained model, this particular run of it, the copy sitting on one server, the whole deployed system — because if you can't say what the candidate is, the status question is malformed. I also want them to feel the pull of what I keep calling the substrate tax: the intuition that silicon owes a debt of justification that carbon gets for free. They should leave able to notice when they're paying it.

So: the one example that should carry the whole session, and what it has to have and lack for that to work (I'd rather not build it on the Chinese room or the Turing test, but tell me if that's a mistake). The misconceptions they'll bring or form — my guesses are "this is about whether chatbots secretly suffer", "moral status is all or nothing", and "if we can't tell, we either have to treat them as full persons or ignore the whole thing" — and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off rather than let it sit. What I should leave out at this level and say out loud that I'm leaving out. And the one question to put to them at the end that would show whether they've got the shape of the problem rather than just an opinion about it.
