I'm a novelist, and the book I'm finishing has a character who's more or less a Peirce stand-in — fallibilism, the insistence that you can't just pretend to doubt what you don't actually doubt — and I've dropped him into a scene with a puzzle I can't get him out of. It goes roughly like this: if you really know that p, then you also know that any evidence that ever shows up against p would have to be misleading, since otherwise you never knew p in the first place; so knowing something seems to license you to wave off the counterevidence before you've even seen it, which is just dogmatism in a nicer suit. I think it's called the dogmatism paradox. What would Peirce actually do with that? Which premise would he call rigged, is there a version of it he'd accept as showing something true about belief, and would he end up saying knowledge is impossible or that we've simply got the wrong picture of it? Take the concrete case too — a scientist who knows her result is solid and so skips reading the failed replication of it — and tell me what he'd say she should do. Dont tell me he died before anyone posed the puzzle; I know, thats the point. He should be reasoning it out on the page from what he thought about doubt and inquiry, not citing papers.
