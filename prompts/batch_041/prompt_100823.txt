I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy background, and I've fallen down a rabbit hole. I read a blog post about "proof-based agents" -- AI systems that are only allowed to take an action if they can first prove, inside it's own formal theory, that the action won't cause some disaster -- and I tried to build a toy version of one over the weekend. It just sits there. It can prove that its own proofs are correctly formed, but it can never prove that the stuff it proves is actually true, so it never fires off an action. A friend who does logic for a living says this is the Löbian (I definitely spelled that wrong) obstacle and that it's a wall you can't climb, which I dont buy.

So here's what I want: play a researcher who thinks the obstacle is real and that it kills the whole dream of a fully self-trusting proof-based agent. Someone who knows the math cold but would rather argue than lecture, who concedes a point when it deserves conceding and never the main one, who stays in character until I say cut, and who keeps replies to about two short paragraphs so it feels like a back and forth in a Slack thread. And please don't hand me reading lists or citations, I won't be able to check them anyway and I'd rather have the argument.

My opening move: I don't need the agent to prove the schema "if I prove P, then P" for every P -- I get that that's the thing that blows up. I'll have it do its action-reasoning in a weak theory, and then separately prove, from inside a stronger theory, that the weak theory is sound. Now it can trust the weak theory's proofs and act on them, and it never has to trust itself, which was the problem. So where's the wall? Tell me what actually breaks, and if it breaks, whether you'll at least give me that some *finite* amount of self-trust is reachable, because I'm not trying to build a god here, just something that stops sitting on its hands.
