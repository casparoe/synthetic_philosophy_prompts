I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics, and I have grown tired of essays in which students paraphrase a dilemma back to me and then declare it intractable. I would like an exercise that forces them to inhabit a position instead. Please write, at roughly 700 to 800 words, an in-universe document: an internal memorandum circulated within a fictional state agency — call it the Bureau of Formal Knowledge, or invent a better name — whose charter makes it the authority on what the state will recognize as knowledge. The world of the memorandum is one in which two official doctrines have been in force for decades and have never been squared with one another. The first is that statements of arithmetic and geometry are about objects that really exist, though those objects occupy no place and no time. The second is that for a person to know something there must be an appropriate causal connection between the person and the thing known; the Bureau's inspectors are trained on the formulation that knowledge is registered only where contact can be traced. The memorandum is the report of a mid-level official asked to explain why, under both doctrines at once, the state's mathematicians appear to know things they could not possibly have touched, and to propose a remedy.

The memorandum should be written in the flat, unbothered register of someone who has no idea he is standing in the middle of a famous problem: numbered sections, a short annex, the occasional procedural aside, and an initialed note in the margin from a superior who raises the deepest objection without recognising it as one. It should canvass at least three remedies, each of which must correspond to a real position in the philosophy of mathematics and each of which should be presented with its genuine costs rather than as a gag. Candidates include: reclassifying every theorem as a conditional, so that nothing need be known about any object; relocating the objects into the relations among them, so that there is nothing left to inspect; declaring the marks in the textbooks to be the numbers themselves, and then coping with the auditors' complaint that on this policy arithmetic becomes false whenever the ink smears; and issuing licences to a small number of mathematicians on the grounds that they possess a special faculty, together with an account of the licensing criteria and of what happens to unlicensed mathematicians who keep getting the right answers. I would like the inspectors' field work reported at least once in deadpan detail — a visit to a classroom, a request for travel expenses, an interview with a mathematician about how the number seven got into her head — and the memorandum should end without resolving anything, though not without a recommendation.

Two constraints. First, the document must not cite or mention any actual philosopher, book, or paper, and must not use any of the technical labels that would give the game away; the agency has never heard of any of this. Second, nothing may be winked at: no narrator, no irony markers, no nudges to the reader. The comedy should come entirely from the collision between ordinary bureaucratic procedure and the thing that procedure cannot handle. What is being satirised is the assumption that knowledge counts as knowledge only where contact can be traced, and, behind it, the assumption that a single theory must deliver both a uniform account of what mathematical statements mean and an account of how we come to know them. It should be legible to a specialist, who ought to be able to name the real options the memorandum is groping toward and should find the treatment fair rather than cute, and to a general reader, who should be able to see by the end what the difficulty actually is.
