Alright, I need help. I teach the upper-level ethics seminar and the final has to go to the printer Friday and I've been putting off the last essay question, which is the only one I actually care about. Its on supererogation. Problem is every year I get forty essays that say the same thing: some acts are good but not required, saints and heroes do them, here's a drowning child, the end. I want a question where that gets you a C.

So here's the setup I've been workshopping with a colleague, and I want the question built around it.

Start from the usual picture: acts come in forbidden, obligatory, and optional, and the optional ones that are still morally good are supererogatory. Fine. Now take what we've been calling a ratchet case. My example: Nadia can sign up as the on-call responder for a clinic in a remote town. Signing up is optional, nobody's making her, it costs her money and every other weekend, so it looks like a textbook supererogatory act. But once she signs up she's the only one with the key to the drug cabinet and forty patients now plan their lives around her being reachable. Quitting after six months isn't a return to her old life, it's a wrong, arguably a serious one. So the act was optional to begin with but it converts a big chunk of her future into obligation. Same shape with becoming someone's emergency contact, or being the person who starts feeding the feral cats. Call an act self-escalating if doing it once makes it the case that continuing or repeating is thereafter required of the same agent, and one-shot if it doesn't.

I want a distinction in the question: narrowly optional means you may do it or not on this occasion, with no change to the rest of your duty profile either way; widely optional means optional, and your overall obligations look the same whichever way you go. A self-escalating act can be narrowly optional without being widely optional.

The claim I want them to attack is one my colleague Devlin keeps asserting: supererogation requires wide optionality. If Devlin's right then self-escalating acts are never supererogatory, the saint-and-hero examples are all one-shot acts and secretly load-bearing, and most of what we actually praise people for (taking on roles, stepping up) falls outside the category. If Devlin's wrong, I want to know exactly what narrow optionality buys us and why the definition of supererogation shouldn't care what an act does to the agent's future duty profile.

So write me the exam question. Hand them the ratchet case and the two senses of optionality, don't assume they know any of it, then make them do three things: (1) say whether a self-escalating act can be supererogatory, and defend it; (2) say what that implies for the standard definition, does it need patching and with what; (3) a short closing part where they have to say whether we should praise Nadia more or less than a one-shot hero who does the same amount of good and walks away clean.

Then give me a model answer of about 650 words and a marking scheme out of 20. For the marking scheme I want the specific moves a top answer has to make, things like the point that the standard definition ranges only over the act and not over the agent's post-act duty profile, and the point about whether required in the definition means required at the time of choice or required all things considered, and I want a one-line note for each move on how students typically blow it. Make it clear what seperates an A from a B. Keep the question itself answerable in 50 minutes by someone who's done the reading but has never seen this case before.
