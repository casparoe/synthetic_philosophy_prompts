So I got into a thing at the local forecasting meetup the other night that I still cant stop chewing on. The guy I was talking to is one of those hardcore subjectivists — de Finetti is basically his patron saint — and his whole line is that the reference class problem just evaporates once you stop pretending there's an objective probability out there to get right. You don't need to pick the correct reference class, he says, you just need to be coherent; whatever class you're effectively conditioning on is already baked into your prior, and that's the end of it. Which sounds slick until you notice the prior has to be a distribution over something — over some space of possibilities — and deciding what that space is, and what's exchangeable with what, looks exactly like picking a reference class. So I pushed back that he hasn't dissolved the problem, he's just relabeled it, and he said that's the price of admission for any epistemology and it isn't special to probability, and I said yeah but for probability the reference class seems to be the entire content, so 'relabeled' is carrying a awful lot of weight there.

Anyway I want to see this played out properly, so: write me a dialogue between these two, one arguing the subjectivity-dissolves-it line and one arguing the no-it's-your-problem-too line. I want both of them at full strength and actually engaging each other, not trading monologues. Let the subjectivist give the strongest version of his reply — that the objector is secretly demanding an impossible God's-eye reference class, and that any prior is legitimate so long as it's coherent and updated properly — and let the objector press hard on the most-specific-class move, where you either end up with a class so broad it's useless or a class of one (this very case) that by definition gives you no frequency information at all, and on whether exchangeability judgments aren't themselves just reference class choices in disguise. Then have them actually try to settle one concrete case instead of floating in the abstract — like a doctor telling a patient his chance of some outcome is 30%, where the reference class could be all patients with the diagnosis, or patients his age, or patients treated at this hospital, and the numbers come out different — and see whether either of them can say which class the 30% is really about without cheating. No citations or 'so-and-so argues this in chapter 4' stuff, I don't care who said it, I just want the argument itself.
