Ok so I want the naturalism vs normativism fight about disease, but done on a case that isn't the DSM-and-homosexuality example everybody reaches for. Use blood pressure.

Here's the episode. In 2017 the big American cardiology guidlines moved the cutoff for hypertension down from 140/90 to 130/80, so overnight tens of millions of adults became hypertensive without anyone's arteries changing. The Europeans didn't follow — they kept 140/90 — so the same person with the same reading is sick in Ohio and healthy in Berlin, which is a nice accidental natural experiment. There's also the older category, prehypertension, for the in-between range, since relabelled as just elevated blood pressure, so we've got a band of people who are officially neither sick nor normal. Two more facts you should have: the evidence for treating that newly-sick band in otherwise low-risk people is genuinely contested, and the trial that did most to push the threshold down measured blood pressure in an unusual way (unattended automated readings with no staff in the room) that tends to read lower than a standard office cuff, so it's arguable the new numbers aren't even commensurable with the old ones.

What I want is the episode run through the naturalist/normativist debate. What does a Boorse-style biostatistical view say about a person sitting at 132/84 — did we discover a disease there, or just move a line? Does his account even let something asymptomatic that hasn't impaired any function yet count as a disease, or does that fall on the illness side of his disease/illness distinction? Then the normativist reading: is the fact that a committee voted the tell, and does a threshold that tracks some judgment about how much stroke risk is tolerable prove their point, or does it only show something about treatment recommendations rather than about disease?

Two things I especially want pushed on. First, the reference class problem: Boorse needs non-arbitrary comparison classes, but age-matched classes already look like a choice — why is a 65-year-old with the blood pressure of an average 65-year-old normal rather than abnormal relative to the species? If that choice smuggles in a norm, does naturalism survive it or just relocate the problem? Second, the disease vs risk-factor line: prehypertension, prediabetes, elevated-this-and-that. Once we call asymptomatic people sick on the strength of a number, is that a problem for both accounts or only one of them?

Then be honest about where the case is uninformative — if the frame is too coarse for this episode, say so instead of forcing it. And at the end: after all this, which side should I actually beleive, and how much did the case move you? No reading lists, just the argument.
