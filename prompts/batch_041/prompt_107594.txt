I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I'm working on a feature about how random bits of behaviour harden into rules that everyone follows. I've been interviewing a philosopher who works on convention in the Lewis tradition — roughly, the thought that a convention is an arbitrary regularity that sticks because it's a solution to a coordination problem, and everyone expects everyone else to keep doing their bit. I've been lobbing examples at her and getting verdicts back on whether each one is a convention, or a norm, or just a coincidence. So far:

- driving on the left: convention
- using pounds and pence to buy things: convention
- calling a table a 'table' and not something else: convention
- everyone at one particular firm using the same project-management software purely because everyone else there does: convention
- two strangers on a narrow footpath both stepping to the same side: not a convention — it's a one-off, not a standing regularity
- basically everyone sleeping at night rather than in the day: not a convention
- a fashion that sweeps through a school and then vanishes: not a convention

Now here's the case the piece actually turns on. In a small town there's an unwritten rule that if you're invited round for dinner you bring a bottle of wine; turn up empty-handed and nobody says anything, but you quietly stop getting invited. It's definately arbitrary — they could just as easily have settled on flowers or a pudding — everyone expects everyone else to do it, and everyone does it. But nobody's doing it because it sorts out a coordination problem; they're doing it because not doing it gets you frozen out.

So: will she call that a convention? Tell me the rule she seems to be running across the examples above, and what other rules would fit those same examples just as well. Then give me your best guess at her verdict and the reasons for it, plus which of the earlier examples — or some new case I could put to her — would best tell those rules apart. If the examples genuinely don't settle it, say so, and spell out what principle she'd be signing up to with each answer; I suspect 'convention' ends up meaning something quite different depending on which way she goes, and I want to be able to say that fairly in the piece. One more thing — does the whole 'norms evolve' angle change any of this? Could something that starts out as a sanction-backed norm turn into a convention over time, or the other way round?

British English please, and no need for citations or reading lists — just reason it out.
