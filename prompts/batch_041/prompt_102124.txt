In a graduate seminar I am taking on skill and expertise, a fellow student brought a discussion of whether a system that has read every repair manual ever written can be said to know how to rebuild an engine to a halt with the line "you can't learn to ride a bicycle from a book," and the room treated the matter as settled. I doubt the sentence can bear that weight without further specification, and I want to see precisely what work it could be doing. Set out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct claims that one-liner might be making, ranging from the nearly trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false. Separate along the way the claims that concern how a skill is acquired from the claims that concern what a skill is, and distinguish the modest thesis that propositional knowledge is not sufficient for skilled performance from the stronger thesis that knowing how is not a species of knowing that at all, saying what turns on the difference. Where they fit, treat the intellectualist position associated with Stanley and Williamson, the anti-intellectualist position Ryle argued for, and the tacit-knowledge tradition associated with Polanyi as candidate readings, and state which reading each of them would have to reject. I especially want to know which of the readings would actually license my classmate's dismissal of the manual-reading system's claim to know how, and which would leave that question open or tell against the dismissal. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
