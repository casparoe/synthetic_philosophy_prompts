I am preparing a unit for a graduate seminar on aggregation, and I need a purpose-built case rather than the well-worn one that weighs a single enormous harm against an astronomical number of trivial ones. That case does its work with quantities no one can picture; I want one that does its work with structure. Please construct a case to the following specifications.

Specifications. (1) Setting and scale: the decision must be one an ordinary institution could actually face — a hospital, a school, an employer, a municipal authority. Two options only, labelled A and B, set out in a single paragraph. No death, no torture, no irreversible harm, and no quantities an administrator could not count: the larger group must number fewer than ten thousand, with no exponentiation, no 3^^^3, and nothing science-fictional. (2) Option A concentrates: it imposes a single noticeable, memorable harm on one person, or on a very small number of people. State the harm, its intensity, and its duration. (3) Option B disperses: it imposes a far smaller harm on each of many people. State the number and the harm. The individual harm in B must fall at or below the threshold of noticeability — someone who received it, asked a moment later, could not report it and would not change their behaviour on account of it. (4) Common scale: before giving any verdict, say how you are measuring the two harms, why that measure is defensible, and how the total in A compares with the total in B. Roughly equal totals are ideal; if equality cannot be had without absurdity, say so and explain why.

Then, in order: (a) Give the verdict and the reasoning — not merely the label — of (i) a totalist who sums welfare across persons; (ii) a threshold or lexical theorist for whom any harm above the noticeability threshold outranks any number of harms below it; (iii) a theorist who holds that a sub-threshold exposure is not a harm at all, since no one is worse off for having received it. (b) At least two of the three must disagree. Identify the one structural feature that generates the disagreement and show that it is load-bearing: change only that feature and report exactly how each verdict moves. If some alteration makes the disagreement vanish, name it; if none can, say what that reveals. (c) Confront the objection that you have stipulated your conclusion — that by describing B's harm as unnoticeable you have defined it out of the moral ledger rather than argued it out. Answer the objection, or, if it cannot be answered, concede it and state precisely what the case can still establish. (d) Close by saying what the case is for: what claim about aggregation the reader should take away, and what it would take to defeat that claim.
