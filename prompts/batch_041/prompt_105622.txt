Ok so I've been argueing with a friend who's been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the old days and thinks Aumann's agreement theorem is basically the final word on rational disagreement. We're stuck on the common prior assumption. She has alot of verdicts on toy cases and I want to know what she'll say about a new one. Here's her track record:

- Two Bayesians with the same prior over some state space, both updating by conditioning on their private information. Their posterior probabilities for event A are common knowledge between them (not just each knows the other's posterior, but they know that they know, etc.). Each also knows the other's information partition and that the other is rational. She says they have to agree. No rational agreeing to disagree.
- Two Bayesians with different priors -- one thinks a coin is fair, the other thinks it's biased toward heads -- but their posteriors for A are common knowledge. She says they can rationally agree to disagree. No common prior, no theorem.
- Two agents with the same prior, their posteriors for A common knowledge, and their information partitions common knowledge, but one of them isn't actually a Bayesian: she uses some non-probabilistic heuristic, ignores base rates, whatever. Friend says no, they can disagree. The theorem needs both to be actual Bayesians.
- Two agents with the same prior, both actual Bayesians, their posteriors for A common knowledge, but neither one knows the other's prior -- they happen to have the same prior, but it's not common knowledge that they do. She says they can disagree. The common prior has to be common knowledge in the relevant sense.

Next week's case: two agents with the same prior, and it is common knowledge that they have the same prior. Both are actual Bayesians. Their posterior probabilities for A are common knowledge. But neither knows the other's information partition -- they don't know what evidence the other saw, though they do know the posterior. And it's not common knowledge that both are rational; each thinks the other might be a non-Bayesian. What will she say? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, and which further case would best tell them apart.
