I am setting the final examination for a third-year undergraduate module on the ethics of moral and political compromise, and I need you to draft one essay question together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must be answerable in ninety minutes and must not be answerable by reproducing the familiar thought that compromise is a species of trade — that two parties who weight the relevant considerations differently can each do better by conceding something to the other, as two traders with different relative valuations both gain by exchange. My students can reproduce that thought; I want a question that makes them say what the analogy requires and where it fails.

The students have no access to the internet, and I do not want the answer to turn on recalling who said what, so please build the question out of the material below and have the question itself state that material. The question, the model answer and the marking scheme must not ask for or reward names, works, dates or citations.

The material to be stated in the question:

First, the trade analogy set out properly. For the analogy to deliver a gain, each party must prefer the package to the alternative to the package. The alternative is not the other party's preferred outcome — each ranks her own preferred outcome above the package — but the disagreement outcome: deadlock, the status quo, a coin toss, or one side imposing its will. The gain is therefore always a gain relative to a baseline, and the baseline is a threat point, which may be morally objectionable in itself or may have been manufactured by one of the parties.

Second, a distinction between positional and non-positional goods. Where what a party values is its relative standing — not being overruled, not having to defer, not being seen to have given way — the good cannot be divided between the parties, and there is nothing to trade.

Third, a distinction between what can and cannot be conceded. A party can concede conduct, advocacy, resources, or the priority it gives in practice; it cannot concede a conviction. What is exchanged is a budget of compliance and advocacy, not a value.

Fourth, a short case. Two trustees of a hospital trust must allocate a fixed sum. One holds that the trust's overriding duty is to relieve suffering; the other that it is to enable as many people as possible to live independently. Deadlock means the money is returned and neither receives anything. They agree a package: less palliative care than the first wanted, less support for independent living than the second wanted, and the remainder spent on something neither of them values at all, which is the price of agreement. Each ranks the package below her own preferred allocation and above the deadlock.

Please give me, in order: the essay question; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words, written as a strong candidate would write it under time; and a marking scheme of five required points worth four marks each, twenty in total, each point accompanied by a single line on how candidates typically lose it. The question should pose the problem rather than list the points for the candidates, and a question that can be answered by observing that the parties' values may be incommensurable, and stopping there, is too easy: the crux should be the baseline against which the gain is measured and the question of what is actually being exchanged. Make clear in the marking scheme which moves separate an upper second from a first.
