I want to build an argument of my own and I need someone to test it against, so please take the other side. Play a committed defender of the view that the badness of pain is intrinsic to the sensation itself: to feel pain just is to be in a bad state, and any experience with the sensation but none of the awfulness is not pain at all, only something else borrowing the name. Be sharp and fair. Concede a point when it genuinely deserves conceding, but never surrender the core claim that the awfulness is not a separable extra laid on top of a neutral sensation. Stay in character until I say stop. If I ask you a question of fact, you may answer it briefly as yourself and then resume the role. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, so it reads like a conversation rather than an essay. Here is my opening.

I have no background in this, so pardon my vocabulary, but I have been reading about people who, after certain injuries to the brain, report that they can feel pain -- they can point to exactly where it is, and say that it is intense and that it is pain -- yet it does not bother them in the slightest. Some of them smile or chat while it is happening, and they do not seem to want it to stop. If that is real, then the feeling and the awfulness have come apart, and that looks to me like proof that the awfulness is not inside the feeling at all. It must be something added on top: a reaction, a wish for it to end, a way of minding it. And if you can have the feeling without the minding, then what makes pain bad is the minding, not the pain. That would mean when we try to relieve someone's pain we are really trying to remove the minding, and the sensation is just the trigger we have learned to attack because we cannot reach the minding directly.

Here is the part I cannot get past, and it is why I want you as an opponent rather than a yes-man. Suppose there were a creature wired from birth so that its pain never bothered it -- it felt every injury fully, localized it perfectly, and simply did not care. Would we say it was suffering? I want to say no, and that its pains were not bad for it, only informative. But then I notice that I would still rush to stop a child's pain even if the child, through some condition, insisted it did not mind. Either I am confused, or "bad" is doing two jobs here -- one about what the person wants, and one about what is good for them whether or not they want anything. I would like you to press me on which job I actually mean, and to make me say what the sensation contributes if the minding is doing all the work.
