Had a proper shouting match with my friend Priya on Friday night. She works on machine learning, I did my PhD in philosophy of mind, and we ended up on whether the stuff she builds all day can mean anything at all. She reckons of course it can, the words do work, people use them, done. I said that's the kind of answer that sounds great until you ask what the words are doing the work on, and then we were three hours in and neither of us had a decent account of what would even count as settling it. Went to bed annoyed, mostly at myself, because I realised I don't know what the live questions in this area are any more. I know the famous ones — tracking-style accounts, selection-based accounts, the no-history cases where you imagine a duplicate with no evolutionary past, the misrepresentation problem — but I've got no sense of which of them are actually worth a person's next three years.

So here's what I want. Suppose I've got a small pot of money: enough for me plus one postdoc, three years, full time, philosophy only, no lab work and no empirical studies of my own. What are the open questions in naturalising mental content that actually matter, and which two or three would you put the money on? I want the reasoning more than the list. Which questions are upstream of which, which ones a philosopher can make real progress on without retraining as a neuroscientist, and which of the famous ones are basically settled or were never that important in the first place.

Two things to fold in. First, the AI case is what started this, so I want to know which questions, if they got answered, would actually move the verdict on whether a trained model has states with content — as opposed to just being an excuse to rehash old positions. Second, I keep bumping into a cluster of ideas I can't place properly: that purposes might come in different flavours, so what a state was selected for, what it was designed for, and what its consumer does with it might come apart; that content might come in degrees rather than being all or nothing; and that maybe the whole project should be reframed as being about what work content-talk does for us rather than about what makes a state about something. Is any of that a real research programme, or is it just a way of changing the subject?

Also tell me what would change your ordering — what result or argument in the next couple of years would make you shuffle it. Don't send me a reading list, I've read enough to be dangerous.
