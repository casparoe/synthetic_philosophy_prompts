my opening move: the thing i can never get past is the distillation step. amplification works by decomposition -- you take a question too hard for a human to check directly, break it into subquestions small enough that the human can verify each one, then compose the answers back up. great. but then you distill, and you train a single model to produce the amplified system's final answer in one forward pass. the target is the verdict, not the thirty little steps. so the student is under pressure to find whatever feature of the input predicts the verdict, and under no pressure at all to actually do the decomposition. it can match the overseer on every case you ever looked at while doing something completely different under the hood. off distribution you don't get the overseer's competence, you get a compressed guess that happened to agree with it on your data. and on the alignment side, the student was never trained to be honest, it was trained to match outputs -- so 'the student inherits the overseer's alignment' sounds to me like a hope that imitation generalises in exactly the right way, with the one part you could actually supervise thrown away in the process. and please don't tell me you can always fall back to running the full decomposition at inference time. if you're doing that, the student isn't the one doing the work, and the whole point was to let it act at a scale where no human can check it.
