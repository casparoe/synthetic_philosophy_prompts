Write a short poem (14–20 lines, loose iambic pentameter, no fixed rhyme scheme required) that stages the three conceptions of liberty — negative (non-interference), positive (self-mastery), and republican (non-domination) — as three voices in a single room, and let the form carry the argument rather than stating it: the negative voice should measure freedom by how often the door is actually opened, the positive voice by who is doing the steering, and the republican voice should get the last word by pointing to a door that is never locked but is always lockable. Bring in one concrete figure the seminar will recognize as the hard case: a worker under a manager who has never once interfered, whose power is entirely arbitrary and entirely unexercised. The poem should make clear, to someone who knows Berlin and Pettit, that the republican objection to the negative conception is not that interference happens but that it is merely permitted to; a reader who knows none of this should still be able to reconstruct the three positions from the poem alone. You are a poet-philosopher writing for a graduate seminar on freedom; think carefully about the distinction between interference and the capacity to interfere before you draft, brainstorm the three voices' characteristic images first, and then write the poem, with a one-paragraph note afterward saying which line each voice owns.
