I am a high school junior and the student council president. In two weeks, I have to give the opening address at a school-wide assembly where the administration will announce a pilot program to replace our current final-exam policy with a portfolio-based assessment. The audience will include students, teachers, administrators, and one school board member who teaches economics at the local university. I want the speech to argue for a specific version of the pilot, not to survey the pros and cons.

The dispute has become an empirical stalemate. Some teachers insist that final exams are the only way to ensure retention; others argue that exams mainly measure test-taking ability and that portfolios better capture learning; the school board member has a model in which exams sort students by conscientiousness and thereby improve later outcomes; student surveys show that exam stress is correlated with anxiety and that many students would do better on portfolios, but no one knows whether that would translate into learning gains. I do not want to pretend to resolve this. Instead, I have been reading about safe Pareto improvements—roughly, changes that make at least one person better off and no one worse off relative to the status quo, not just under the best estimate of the relevant facts, but under every plausible model of those facts. I want the speech to commit to a concrete policy (for example, an opt-in two-year pilot with a guaranteed right to switch back after one term) and to defend it as a safe Pareto improvement: under every plausible model of how assessment affects learning, no group is worse off than under the current exam policy, and at least one group is better off. The concept should do real work: it should constrain what I can claim and how I can frame the proposal, so that I am not smuggling in an empirical prediction I cannot defend.

Please write the speech. It should be formal and polished, suitable for a mixed audience: the economics teacher should recognize the structure of the argument, but my classmates and their parents should be able to follow it. It should take a definite position and give reasons, rather than laying out options and leaving the decision to me. After the speech, add a brief note on where the speech takes liberties with the idea of a safe Pareto improvement or leans on a contested reading of it.
