I'm putting together a handout for a graduate seminar in normative ethics, and for one section I want the answer in the form of a tier list rather than an essay.

The levelling-down objection is standardly pressed against egalitarian views: they appear to imply that one outcome can be better than another even though no one is better off in the first and some are worse off. Prioritarianism is usually said to escape the objection because it is not concerned with inequality as such, only with the weighted sum of benefits, where benefits to the worse off receive greater weight. I want the response to probe how robust that escape is by varying how the priority weighting is specified. Treat each of the following as a distinct view:

1. Absolute-level priority: the value of an outcome is the sum, over persons, of a strictly increasing and strictly concave function of each person's absolute well-being; the function is the same for everyone and depends on no one else's level.
2. Comparative priority: the weight given to a benefit to a person increases as that person's level falls relative to others — for instance, priority tracks how far below the mean a person's level is.
3. Threshold priority: below a fixed absolute threshold, benefits carry a large extra weight; at or above the threshold, benefits are simply summed with no extra weight.
4. Gap-reduction priority: outcome value is total well-being minus a penalty proportional to the gap between the best-off and the worst-off person.
5. Deontic priority: the priority-weighted sum is the axiological core, but it is constrained by a side-constraint forbidding any act that makes someone worse off without making anyone better off.
6. Fixed person-indexed weights: each person is assigned, once and for all, a weight inversely related to their initial level, and outcome value is the weighted sum of well-being computed with those fixed weights.

Sort these six into tiers — A, B, C, D — according to how well each both (i) blocks the levelling-down implication and (ii) remains a distinctively prioritarian view rather than collapsing into egalitarianism, into ordinary aggregation, or into a purely deontic constraint. Within each tier, order the items from strongest to weakest. For each item, give one or two sentences naming the specific feature that earns it its place and, where relevant, the specific feature that holds it back.

Then add a short closing section. For the item you rank highest and for the one you rank lowest, identify the single consideration that, if it turned out to be correct, would most change its placement — for instance a reading of the objection under which axiological ranking and permissibility come apart, or a reading under which "prioritarian" is defined so narrowly that a view fails by definition. End by naming the one or two placements you are least confident about and why.
