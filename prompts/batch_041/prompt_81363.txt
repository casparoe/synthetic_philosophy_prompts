I am writing a seminar paper on early modern theories of political obligation, and I keep returning to Pascal's fragments on justice and force. The famous line — La justice sans la force est impuissante ; la force sans la justice est tyrannique — is usually read either as Augustinian pessimism about fallen human nature or as a proto-Hobbesian claim that authority rather than truth is the source of law. I want to defend a third reading, and I would like you to argue for it as forcefully and as carefully as you can, rather than merely cataloguing the alternatives or hedging between them.

My thesis is that Pascal is not making a claim about the origins of law at all. He is making a claim about the conditions under which a population can sustain a legal order, and the crucial move is to treat belief in the justice of the laws not as a justified inference but as a functional requirement — something the order needs from its subjects, not something the subjects are owed on the merits. On this reading, when Pascal warns that it is dangerous to tell the people that the laws are not just, he is neither being hypocritical nor merely elitist; he is identifying a structural feature of legal order, namely that its stability depends on a form of uptake that cannot survive full transparency about its origins. The same cluster of fragments makes the point from the other direction, treating the plurality's force rather than its wisdom as what makes its judgments binding, and treating the sword as conferring a genuine right rather than a mere capacity.

Please argue that this reading, extended consistently, yields a serious and uncomfortable challenge to democratic legitimacy. The challenge is that a democracy cannot simultaneously (a) treat every citizen as a co-author of the law, entitled to full knowledge of how the law was made, and (b) sustain the kind of unreflective attachment to law that Pascal treats as necessary for order — and that the standard liberal responses (public reason, constitutional patriotism, deliberative legitimation) either presuppose the very belief Pascal is skeptical about or covertly reintroduce the distinction between the knowing few and the believing many. Anticipate the strongest objections, especially the empirical one — that modern democracies survive high levels of cynicism about their own founding and their own procedures — and explain why the Pascal of these fragments would not be moved by it. Keep the argument internal to Pascal's own commitments where you can; I am less interested in whether he was right about seventeenth-century France than in whether the position is coherent and whether it really has the consequence for democracy that I think it does.
