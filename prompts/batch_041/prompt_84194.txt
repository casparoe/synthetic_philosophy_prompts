I'm a statistics PhD student who helps run the foundations reading group in my department, and I keep running into the same problem: my classmates — mostly applied people working on clinical trials, epidemiology, and econometrics — have strong commitments about probability that they can't name and that don't line up with the standard menu of positions. One of them will insist that a 95% confidence interval has a 95% probability of containing the true value and then, two sentences later, deny that it makes any sense to put a probability on a fixed unknown parameter. Another says the decay of a single radium atom over the next minute is a genuine single-case chance, but that the coin he's about to flip has a probability only by courtesy of the ensemble of similar flips he can imagine. I want to build a sorting instrument for the group: a decision tree that starts from the one thing we all share and works downward through questions about our commitments, so that the disagreements turn on substance rather than on labels.

Take the starting commitment to be this: probability claims are meaningful and obey the usual calculus (values between 0 and 1, a sure event gets 1, disjoint alternatives add). Everything downstream is fair game. Each internal node should be a question a person can answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to — never anything like "are you a frequentist?" — and each leaf should carry the name of a position, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. The tree should branch, at minimum, on: whether a probability claim is fundamentally about the world or about an agent's state; whether probabilities attach to token events or only to types and ensembles; what fixes a numerical value (actual frequencies, hypothetical long-run frequencies, symmetries, coherence constraints on a rational agent, or laws of nature); whether objective chance is even possible in a deterministic physics; whether two agents with the same evidence must agree on their probabilities; whether learning that the objective chance of an outcome is p obliges a rational agent to set her credence in that outcome to p; how to handle the fact that any single event belongs to many groups with different frequencies, so that there seems to be no unique frequency to call its probability; and what a probability claim about a single unrepeatable event amounts to. I need at least these positions to appear as leaves: finite frequentism; limiting or hypothetical frequentism; a single-case propensity view; a long-run or setup-based propensity view; a symmetry-based logical view; objective Bayesianism; subjective Bayesianism; a view on which objective chances are fixed by whatever systematization of the world's overall pattern of events does best as a whole; and a deflationary view on which talk of objective chance is at best a useful fiction. Drive the branching with the cases my classmates actually fight over: one atom of a radioactive isotope over the next minute; a coin that will be flipped exactly once and then destroyed; a coin drawn from a bag of coins of unknown biases; a unique historical event, such as whether it rained in a particular city on a particular day a thousand years ago; a shuffled deck or a well-mixed urn; a patient facing a one-off surgery; and that 95% confidence interval for a fixed but unknown parameter.

Three further things I want. First, organize the leaves by where they locate the probability — on a token event, on a type or ensemble, on an agent's state, or on a physical disposition of a setup — and by what they take to fix the number; build the tree out of those questions first and hang the labels on the resulting combinations, so that if two of the labels I listed turn out to be the same position under different names, you collapse them and tell me. Second, mark any combination of answers that nobody occupies and say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to explore it. Third, tell me honestly which leaves your tree cannot separate — I am particularly worried about long-run propensity versus limiting frequentism, and about objective Bayesianism versus the symmetry-based logical view — rather than papering the gap over. Finally, for each leaf add one line predicting how someone who lands there would answer the confidence-interval question, since that is the diagnostic that starts all our fights. Aim for nine to twelve internal nodes and no more than about twelve leaves.
