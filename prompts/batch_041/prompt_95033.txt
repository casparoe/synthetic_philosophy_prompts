I was recently appointed to a citizen advisory panel that my city council convened to design a pilot in what its sponsors call futarchy: for a contested proposal, two conditional markets would open before the council's vote, one resolving on the value of a pre-agreed outcome measure in the event the proposal is adopted and one resolving on the value of the same measure in the event it is not, with the panel recommending the branch whose market price is higher whenever the gap exceeds a stated threshold. The design is meant to split the decision in two, with the panel voting on what we value and the markets betting on what is true. The panel has eleven members and meets on Thursday evenings in a branch library. The first proposal in the queue is a pedestrianization scheme for six blocks of our main commercial street; the outcome measure the council has tentatively settled on is the number of continuously tenanted storefronts on those blocks twelve months after implementation, counting a unit only if the same tenant has occupied it for at least six months; the panel chose that measure by a 6–5 vote; and the council has not yet said how the market on the branch that does not occur is to be settled.

Two of my fellow panelists, Ravi and June, have now argued this out over three consecutive meetings, and since I have deliberately stayed out of it, the chair has asked me to write the exchange up for the record before we vote on whether to recommend the pilot at all. (1) Ravi said the council currently decides contested factual questions by whoever speaks loudest, whereas a price can be traded against by anyone, and a trader who turns out to be right gets paid, so the price drifts toward the best estimate available; the value judgment stays with us, because we chose the measure. (2) June replied that the measure is not a neutral input to a value-neutral calculation: counting a luxury boutique and a discount grocer each as one tenanted storefront assumes the two are interchangeable, and pedestrianization is precisely the kind of policy that raises commercial rents and churns out the businesses serving long-standing residents, so the market could be perfectly accurate and the recommendation still wrong. The real decision, she said, was made at the measure vote, which we won 6–5 and which almost nobody outside this room will ever read. (3) Ravi answered that this is an argument for a better measure, or for several measures, not an argument against markets; that every procedure has an agenda-setter, and ours at least has to write its agenda down in public; and that the familiar defense applies, namely that manipulation is self-punishing, because anyone who pushes a price away from what he believes hands money to the traders on the other side. (4) June said that holds only if every participant is in it for the money: a landlord who expects to profit from pedestrianization can buy the adopt side at a loss and book the loss as marketing, a bloc of residents can do the same on the other side, and on six blocks with a handful of participants the sum required to move the price is small enough to be raised over a weekend. What the price then measures, she said, is willingness to burn money, and the panel would be running a dollar-weighted vote in a body whose entire mandate is to be representative of the people who live here. (5) Ravi responded that the panel can subsidize liquidity and cap individual positions, which makes distortion expensive; that willingness to spend is itself evidence of how much people care, evidence that one-person-one-vote throws away; and that if a determined actor losing money to move a price were disqualifying, we would have to distrust every market we rely on, including the ones that price insurance and elections. (6) June closed by saying that the second half of the design is then gone: if the price encodes willingness to pay rather than belief, we have two preference-aggregation mechanisms and one of them is plutocratic, and describing the second as factual is a way of laundering the first.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each of the six numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then say who has the better of the overall dispute and why. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing — in particular, whether June's objection is fatal to the design as the council has specified it or whether some repaired version survives it, and whether the still-unsettled question of how the non-occurring branch is to be priced bears on either position.
