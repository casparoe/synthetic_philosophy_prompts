I am a high school student writing an essay for a philosophy of technology unit, and I would like you to read the film Source Code through the debate over whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real. The film's premise is that a soldier, Colter Stevens, is repeatedly sent into the last eight minutes of a commuter train that has already been destroyed, in order to identify the bomber; the train world is a simulation built from the memory of one of the victims. At the end, Stevens remains inside the simulation, which continues beyond its original eight minutes and even sends a message back to the real world. Most discussions of the film treat the ending as a paradox or a happy twist. I want to use it to test two ideas. The first is what I call the ontological continuity criterion: a simulated world and its objects are real, and its harms are real, when the simulation continues to exist and have effects independently of the purpose for which it was created. The second is what I call the harm-transfer thesis: a virtual harm is real only if it can be transferred to the inhabitant's real-world standing, projects, or body. Does the film's ending vindicate either of these views, or does it suggest a different account of virtual realism—one based, perhaps, on the fact that the simulation is constituted by real computational processes, or on the fact that the simulated people are experienced as real by those inside? Please give me a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical debate seriously: identify the scenes or structural features that illuminate the issue, explain where the film complicates or resists the philosophical positions rather than merely illustrating them, and say what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I cannot look anything up. End with a one-sentence verdict in the form \answer{...}, where the brackets contain a short label such as continuity criterion vindicated, harm-transfer thesis vindicated, neither, or a better formulation of your own.
