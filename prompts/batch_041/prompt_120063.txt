ok, dissertation framing chapter, and I've been circling the national character thing for weeks. Not the "is it real or is it just essentialism" debate, I know that one. My problem is I can't even say what a theory of national character would have to *do* before I can tell which of the existing accounts is doing any work. So: before I read one more paper, write me the desiderata.

Two things I want the list to force, since they're exactly where I keep getting stuck.

First, the unit. Make the theory declare whether it's a theory of (a) the joint distribution of individual traits in some population, (b) a property of the collective that isn't just a sum or pattern of the members' traits, or (c) a self-image the population holds about itself, which does its causal work by being believed. If (b) is even intelligible, the list should contain whatever it takes to keep (b) from being spooky, and whatever it takes to keep (a) from being trivially true.

Second, the time constant. "Character" promises some stability, so an adequate theory owes me: how long the thing lasts, what evidence would show it changing, and how that differs from a mere snapshot of one cohort. If nothing could count as the character changing, I don't think the theory is making a claim at all.

Cases the list has to handle, with the reason behind each: within-group vs. between-group variance (heritability is a within-population statistic, so it can't settle a difference between two populations no matter how high it is in each); the ecological fallacy; the places we already use group statistics on purpose and mostly think it's fine (insurance pricing, ancestry-based screening in medicine) versus the places we dont (hiring); the self-fulfilling / stereotype-threat loop, where the belief about the group helps produce the trait; the reference-class problem, i.e. who counts as a member (passport, ancestry, language, self-identification?), including second-generation immigrants and diasporas; and the replication trouble in the cross-cultural survey dimensions, since if the measurements don't hold up the theory can't lean on them.

Also a case I made up to keep myself honest: two countries identical in the mean and variance of every trait you care about, except that in one the traits cluster (the gregarious ones are also the punctual ones) and in the other they're uncorrelated. Different national characters or not? The list should force a theory to answer, and if a candidate has to say "no difference," that's a cost it should have to pay out loud.

Then, at the end: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's prejudices; say which pair you think can't be jointly satisfied (I suspect some version of "must be reducible to facts about individuals" and "must be a genuinely collective explanatory property," but tell me if I'm wrong, thats the part I actually want); and name the single requirement that knocks out the most contenders. One more thing, I care about the "is it ever OK to generalize about peoples" side too, so somewhere in the list there needs to be a slot for conditions of *use*, not just conditions of truth: what separates a generalization that's true from one it's permissible to act on, and why the same true statistic is fine in one context and not another.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, which requirement does the most excluding and whether any pair is jointly unsatisfiable, then the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate the question.
