For the last several months a reading group I sit in has been circling a single question — whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer — and has settled, largely without argument, on one analogy to carry the discussion: the scalar reward an agent maximizes is to that agent what pleasure and pain are to us. Both factions in the group lean on it. One concludes that we already possess, in the reward signal, a working model of a valence signal, and that whatever moral caution we extend to creatures with valenced experience should extend, at least tentatively, to systems carrying one. The other concludes that a number in an objective function is precisely what pain is not, and that the analogy is a category error wearing functionalist clothes. I would like it audited rather than endorsed or dismissed.

First, state the analogy as an explicit mapping — a table of correspondences is welcome — specifying what plays the role of the valenced experience itself, of its sign and intensity, of the common currency that arbitrates between competing motivations, of the motivational pull toward or away, of the subject that undergoes it, and of whatever fixes which outcomes count as good or bad in the first place.

Second, mark where the mapping breaks. I expect the interesting fractures to concern the provenance of the signal (specified from outside by a designer versus generated within the system), whether the system bears any attitude toward the signal or merely moves under it, whether the signal is intrinsically or only instrumentally motivating, and whether anything in the architecture could count as the signal mattering to the system rather than merely to its trainer. Say which of these breaks are load-bearing for the moral conclusion and which are decorative.

Third, a rival analogy has been floated in the group: reward is to the policy as fitness is to the organism — a selection gradient rather than an experience. Evaluate it on the same terms and say whether it fares better than the first.

Fourth, adjudicate. Does reward-as-valence support the cautious conclusion, the dismissive one, or neither? Does the rival analogy push in the opposite direction, or is it equally idle? If neither can carry the weight, sketch what a better analogy would preserve and what it would have to abandon.

Two standing requests. State the functionalist case and the anti-functionalist case each at its strongest, since I argue regularly with partisans of both. And address the fact that in the relevant neuroscience the signals are often parsed as prediction error, or as wanting rather than liking, rather than as pleasure: I want to know whether that parsing counts against the analogy or is merely a refinement of it.
