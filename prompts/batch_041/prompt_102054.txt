I run the ethics desk at a mid-sized humanitarian NGO, and field teams keep sending up the same sort of request: may we do a thing we've formally forbidden — pay a bribe at a checkpoint, hand a beneficiary or staff list to a local commander, take an armed escort through a contested district — on the grounds that refusing means a much bigger version of the same harm lands on the people we're here to help? My own instinct is deontological: some things we just don't do, full stop, whatever it costs elsewhere. But a board member keeps pressing the obvious objection, that if the thing is bad because of what it does to people, then surely we should be minimising how much of it happens, and refusing here makes more of it happen overall. I can't articulate why I'm not just being precious about keeping my own hands clean, and I'd rather not discover the answer mid-crisis.

So please write me a decision procedure I can actually run on one of these requests in an afternoon: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a firm no, and where it has to go to the board because it's a values call rather than a factual one. It has to be usable by a field officer who has read no philosophy at all, it should say what to do when we simply can't get a number for the "bigger harm" on the other side, and it should be honest about where it will misfire or just launder my gut feeling into a flowchart. Then run it on two cases: paying a bribe at a checkpoint to get a medical convoy through, and handing over a list of staff names to a militia that has threatened them. I'd like one of those to come out in a way I won't like, so I can see the procedure biting.

Max 900 words, and lead with the bottom line — the rule in one sentence — before you give me the steps.
