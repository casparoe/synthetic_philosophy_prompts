I've been arguing about weakness of will with a reading group for most of a term, and I've ended up with a cluster of commitments that I suspect don't all fit together, though I can't see where the fault line runs. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) Akrasia is real: there are cases in which someone acts against his own all-things-considered judgment, and these are not cases of changing one's mind or of discovering a preference one didn't know one had — something has gone wrong. (2) What is going on in those cases is a reversal of preference across time, and the right way to formalize it is a discount function that isn't exponential (the sort of thing usually written with a beta and a delta) — the rate at which the future is discounted declines with distance, which is exactly what lets the ranking of two options flip as the tempting one approaches. I am content to call that the explanation and not to look for anything behind it. (3) Reasons do not decay with temporal distance: that a benefit or a burden lies in the future is not by itself a reason to weigh it less, and an agent who discounts purely on that ground is making a mistake rather than expressing a distinctive taste. (4) An intention is not a strong present preference. Its office is to settle a question and to stop one from reopening it at every opportunity, and the capacity to form intentions and hold to them is constitutive of agency rather than a workaround for a psychological quirk. (5) There is one agent here and not a relay team of momentary selves; my future self is at most a convenient façon de parler, and I don't think anything normative follows from treating my later stages as separate parties with whom I am bargaining. (6) Two verdicts I would defend: binding oneself in advance — deleting the app, handing a friend the password — is sensible, and one's earlier self is right to do it; but the man who prefers the cigarette now, accepts the cost, and feels no struggle and no regret is not weak-willed, even though he ends up exactly where the regretful smoker does. Running under all of it is a methodological conviction: an account of akrasia has to be recognizable from the inside, and a theory that cannot make sense of what the struggle is like has not explained the thing I want explained.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which only appear to, and for each real conflict what exactly forces the choice — I don't want to be told that two of my views are in tension without the argument that connects them, and I would rather hear that a conflict I take seriously is harmless than have every pair flagged as a problem. Order them by how much damage they do. Be willing to say that two commitments I think are independent are quietly doing the same work, or that one of them is carrying more weight than it can bear. Then take a definite position: name the single revision that repairs the most at the least cost, say what that revision costs me, and say which commitments survive untouched. Do not hand me a menu of options and leave the choice to me; I want your verdict, with reasons, and I want you to flag separately anything you think no amount of analysis settles and that I will simply have to decide.

Give it to me as a JSON object with the following fields and nothing else. 'bottom_line': a string of one or two sentences giving your verdict on whether the set hangs together and which commitment is the weakest link, with no hedging. 'tensions': an array with one object per pair of commitments, sorted from most to least severe, each having 'commitments' (an array of the numerals as strings, e.g. ['2','4']), 'real' (a boolean), 'severity' (one of 'fatal', 'serious', 'mild', 'apparent only'), 'why' (a short paragraph giving the argument that they conflict, or the argument for why the appearance dissolves), 'who_moves' (which commitment has to give and the smallest concession that would do it), and 'cost' (what is lost by making that concession). 'cheapest_repair': an object with 'revision' (the single change you would make), 'cost' (what it costs me), and 'survives' (an array of the numerals of the commitments left intact). 'undecided': an array of strings, each a question the analysis leaves open and that I must settle by choosing a side. 'confidence': an object with 'level' (a number between 0 and 1) and 'notes' (one sentence on what would move it). Keep the prose inside the fields tight, and let 'bottom_line' commit.
