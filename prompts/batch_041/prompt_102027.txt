Right, bit of an odd one. I'm a retired chemist — spent the best part of forty years on spectroscopy — and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy seriously for the first time, which has been humbling and slightly addictive. Next month I'm giving a talk to our local mind-and-cognition group and I want to be ready for the questions, because I suspect I'm going to get savaged.

The talk is on von Neumann's last lectures about the brain — the ones he gave at the end of his life, where he's comparing the nervous system to a computer. My thesis is this: people treat him as an early cheerleader for the idea that the mind is just computation and could in principle run on any suitable hardware, but I think if you actually read what he was up to, he was making the opposite kind of argument. His real worry wasn't speed or memory. It was reliability. He was struck that the brain gets away with using components that are far less reliable than the ones we'd tolerate in a machine, and that it seems to buy its reliability through sheer statistical and organisational redundancy rather than by making the parts trustworthy. I want to argue that this commits him to a picture on which the brain's computation is irreducibly analogue and mixed in character, and that the tidy digital story the functionalists tell simply doesn't survive contact with his own reasoning. I'll also be pressing a slightly idiosyncratic line of my own, that his notion of the brain working in a "language" unlike mathematics is best read as a claim about error-correction rather than about meaning — I think that's where the interesting bit is, and I think most commentators skate over it.

Now the audience. It's a departmental thing, so I'll get a mixed crowd. Most are computational neuroscientists who build models for a living and are frankly a bit impatient with philosophical throat-clearing. There'll be two or three machine-learning people who will want everything in terms of architecture and training, and they're the ones I'm most nervous about because they'll think substrate independence is basically settled science. And then two proper philosophers of mind, one of whom is a card-carrying functionalist and the other of whom is a hard-nosed sceptic about computational explanations generally, and they'll attack from opposite flanks.

What I want from you is my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this lot — not the ones I'd get from a room of historians of science — ordered by how much damage they'd do me, and phrased the way each of them would actually put it, so I can hear it coming. For each one, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and then a blunt note on where that reply is weakest and what I should probably just concede up front rather than fight. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it's the dangerous one. I don't want reading lists or references — I won't have time to look anything up mid-talk and I want the arguments, not the bibliography. Please write it in British English, spelling and all, since I'll be reading it aloud and I'll trip over the Americanisms.
