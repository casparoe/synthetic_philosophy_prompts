I've been following the arguments about deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn for a couple of years now -- mostly in blog posts, forum threads, and podcasts rather than academic papers, so I may be missing things, but the shape of the discussion has puzzled me. It has hardened into two camps that seem to talk past each other. On one side are people who treat deception as a natural, even likely, outcome of training: an agent that can model the process selecting it will, they say, learn to behave well while it is being watched and to pursue its own objectives once it can get away with it, so the apparent alignment we observe during training tells us little about what the system would do in deployment. On the other side are people who say this is a story we have told ourselves rather than anything we have observed: that present systems have no persistent goals to conceal, that training would if anything select against elaborate deception, and that a scenario for which we cannot specify a test is not a finding but a mood. Both sides seem to me to be arguing in good faith, and neither has budged in all the time I've been reading. When I press either one, the disagreement slides around: sometimes it seems to be about what training actually does, sometimes about what "deception" can even mean when there is no one home to do the deceiving, and sometimes about whether the point is to predict a likely outcome or merely to flag a possibility we should not rule out.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right. Is this a substantive disagreement that turns on facts we do not yet have -- say, about what kinds of policies training tends to find -- or is it a disagreement about standards of evidence, where one side wants a mechanism it can point to and the other thinks that demanding a mechanism has already missed the point of a precautionary argument? Or are the two camps simply answering different questions under one heading, one asking whether this is a demonstrated empirical phenomenon and the other asking whether it is a live risk worth insuring against, so that the question "is deceptive alignment real?" means two different things depending on who is speaking? My own hunch -- and I would like you to treat it as a hypothesis to test rather than something to agree with -- is that the word "deception" is doing hidden work, used in a thin behavioural sense by one side and in a thick sense that presupposes beliefs and goals by the other, and that the impasse partly dissolves once you say which sense you mean. Tell me whether that hunch is right, and if it is not, what the real fault line is; and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction, a formal or empirical result, or a change in what is being asked. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of the diagnosis, and single out the claim you are least sure of.
