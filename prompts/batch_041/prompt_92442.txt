I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about species, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. I will set out the cases as I find them, with the verdict I give in each, and I would ask you to treat these as data rather than as positions to be corrected.

- Two human populations on opposite sides of the world: the same species, without hesitation, though they have been separated for tens of thousands of years.
- Dogs and wolves: different species, though I understand they interbreed freely and their offspring are fertile. Yet the great variety of dog breeds, which differ from one another in size, coat, and build far more than any dog differs from a wolf, I call a single species. Appearance, then, is plainly not doing the work.
- Two populations on opposite banks of a river that never meet, but which would interbreed if the river dried up: one species. They have not exchanged a gene in a hundred thousand years, and I am not tempted to split them.
- A ring of populations around a barrier, each interbreeding with its neighbours, where the two ends of the ring overlap without interbreeding: I call the two ends different species, even though each is joined to the other by an unbroken chain of interbreeding. Neighbourly gene flow matters to me; the transitive chain does not.
- Two populations that look identical, live side by side, and never interbreed because they breed in different seasons: different species, and I feel no pull to merge them merely because I cannot tell them apart.
- A plant whose chromosome number doubles, so that its offspring breed with one another but not with the parent stock: a new species, and I am willing to date the split to a single generation. No gradual divergence seems required.
- Two lineages that are fully interfertile in the laboratory but have been separate in the wild for several million years: different species. The bare possibility of gene flow does not settle it for me here.
- A sterile hybrid such as a mule is not a species at all; I take the horse and the donkey to be different species, and the hybrid to be evidence of nothing in particular.
- A hybrid lineage that arises, stabilises, and thereafter breeds only within itself: a species, even if it is only a few decades old.

These verdicts cannot all survive if a species is simply the largest group of populations that can interbreed, since that reading would merge dogs and wolves and would split the river populations. The principle I am tempted by, and would like you to test, runs roughly as follows: a species is the largest set of populations that are not reproductively isolated from one another in practice, and the smallest set that is on its own evolutionary trajectory and would not merge back into a neighbouring set were the two to meet. I suspect this principle is doing two jobs at once, and that its two halves will pull apart on the cases above — in particular that the phrase "in practice" is hiding a choice between isolation that is geographical and isolation that is biological, and that the cases of the ring and of the long-separated but interfertile lineages fall on opposite sides of that choice.

What I would like from you is this. State the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best fits the pattern; for each clause, name the case that forces it and say whether that clause could be dropped at the cost of a single verdict; then apply the principle to cases I have not raised — asexual organisms, the fossil record, two populations that interbreed only once in a hundred matings, and any other case you think divides the candidates — and say what it rules. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot keep, so that I know what I would have to give up. If no single principle survives, I would rather have two candidates and a clear statement of what divides them than one compromise contrived to save every verdict.
