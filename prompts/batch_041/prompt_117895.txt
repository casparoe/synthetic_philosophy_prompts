I am trying to reconstruct, for a seminar handout, the argument usually called the Frege–Geach objection to expressivism, and my reconstruction appears to prove considerably more than the objection is supposed to prove. Set-up, so that nothing depends on your recalling a particular text: expressivism is the thesis that a moral sentence such as "Lying is wrong" does not state a fact but expresses a non-cognitive state — disapproval, a plan, a norm — and that this expressive role, rather than a truth condition, is what the sentence means. The objection notes that the very same sentence occurs both asserted and unasserted, and that validity appears to require that the occurrence carry the same content in both places. My reconstruction runs as follows.

(1) The meaning of a sentence is what it contributes to the larger sentences in which it occurs, and it contributes the same thing in every occurrence.
(2) What a sentence contributes is the state of mind it expresses.
(3) Asserted on its own, "Lying is wrong" expresses disapproval of lying.
(4) Occurring unasserted in the antecedent of a conditional, "Lying is wrong" expresses no such disapproval; the speaker commits herself to nothing about lying.
(5) By (2), (3) and (4), "Lying is wrong" contributes different things in the two occurrences.
(6) An inference by modus ponens is valid only if the antecedent of the conditional and the corresponding premise have the same content.
(7) Hence the inference "If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore getting your brother to lie is wrong" is invalid, and an expressivist can make it valid only by giving up (2).
(8) But (2) and (4) are perfectly general: the antecedent of any conditional is unasserted, so the same reasoning shows that no conditional's antecedent contributes the same content as any asserted premise.
(9) So no instance of modus ponens is valid.
(10) Modus ponens is valid.
(11) Therefore (2) is false: a sentence's meaning is not the state of mind it expresses, and expressivism is refuted.

I want to keep (10) and (1), and I am prepared to lose (2) if I must; what I want to know is what else goes with it. Where would you break the chain, and what does breaking it there cost? If the reconstruction is sound and expressivism really is refuted, say so, and describe the nearest position that survives. If it is not sound, I want the precise defect — not the name of a familiar strategy, but the premise or inference that strategy requires me to give up, and what giving it up costs elsewhere in the theory of meaning.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. The first should say where you break the chain; the second why that link looked compelling; the third what exactly the defect is, if there is one; the fourth and fifth each price one of the two most defensible repairs, saying in each case what the position that needs the repair can no longer claim.
