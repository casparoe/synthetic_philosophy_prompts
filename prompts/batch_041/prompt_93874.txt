I am designing a session for a graduate seminar in political philosophy that doubles as a methods workshop. The students have read about governance by prediction market — the proposal that an electorate votes on the metric it wants maximized, then delegates the choice of policy to markets that estimate which policy will maximize that metric (often called futarchy). I want an activity, not a lecture: sixteen participants, ninety minutes, one room, no computers, no real money.

The design problem is not "explain futarchy." It is to make the group feel three pressures from the inside, in this order. First, the division of labor itself: that they are being asked to fix their values by vote and then hand the factual question to a mechanism none of them controls. Second, the counterfactual: the market has to price a policy that will not be implemented, so the losing branch never resolves and no one can ever be paid on the basis of whether it was right. Third, the metric: that a procedure which looks like it takes politics out of policy is exquisitely sensitive to a choice — which metric — that the procedure itself cannot adjudicate.

Arrange the activity so these arrive as experiences rather than as claims. I want a staged market with a real if symbolic stake, roles carrying private information, and a decision rule the group must actually follow to the end, including implementing whichever policy the mechanism selects. The design should include:

- a first stage in which the group picks the metric from a short menu I fix in advance. Make the menu genuinely contestable, and make at least one option non-standard — a rank-based or worst-case statistic rather than a total or an average — so that the choice has consequences participants cannot foresee while they are making it;
- a second stage that is a real market under a stated scoring rule that needs no money. Specify the rule exactly, and specify how I keep the arithmetic tractable on paper in ninety minutes;
- at least one role whose private payoff makes it profitable to trade against its own belief, and at least one role that benefits if the implemented policy fails. Do not announce these roles;
- a hidden element revealed only at the end, showing the group how much the outcome depended on a choice they made in the first ten minutes without understanding it.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the idea — where the value/fact division held, where it broke down, where the counterfactual problem bit — and say plainly which feature of the real proposal the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words.

Two further things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, concretely: the stage that will collapse, the participant who will break it, the arithmetic that will not fit in the time. And commit. Do not hand me a menu of designs to choose from. Pick one, make every call — group size and how I should adapt it, the exact metric menu, the scoring rule, the payoff numbers, the timing — and defend the calls you are least sure of. Where you are guessing, mark it in the text rather than hedging the design as a whole.

You are someone who has both designed classroom simulations and worked on real market mechanisms. Think through the timing and the arithmetic before you write, and let the arithmetic constrain the design rather than the other way around.
