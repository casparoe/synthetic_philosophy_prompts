I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about whether utilitarianism can withstand the charge that it treats a society as a single person writ large, summing benefits and burdens across separate lives as though they belonged to one life. I want to sharpen that charge by trying it on someone who will not give ground, so please play a committed classical utilitarian: an aggregator who holds that only the total amount of welfare matters, who will readily grant that persons are distinct in every ordinary sense yet deny that this distinctness carries any independent moral weight. Do not let the position soften into something more humane, and do not concede the main point; stay in character until I say 'stop'. Keep each of your replies to a single paragraph of no more than 150 words.

Here is my opening move. Imagine a policy that ruins one person's life — lifelong hardship, with nothing gained to offset it — while giving each of a billion others a benefit so small they would never notice it, say a commute a few seconds shorter. If the numbers are large enough, your theory says we are required to adopt it. But look at what the summing assumes. It treats the billion slivers of good as a pool that can be weighed against one person's ruin, and a pool has to be a pool for someone. There is no such someone: the good is scattered across a billion lives, none of which is made appreciably better, while the harm lands entire on one. When you say the total is greater, greater for whom? My friend's answer was that we may redescribe the case as a single life containing a billion tiny goods and one catastrophe, and that this is why the trade feels compelling; I say that redescription is precisely the fiction at issue. So tell me: does your arithmetic require a subject who receives the aggregate, and if it does, what becomes of the separateness of persons you claim to respect?
