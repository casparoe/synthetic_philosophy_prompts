Here's whats bugging me. There's this whole advance directive thing, right? You write down, while you're still of sound mind, what you want done with you later when maybe you wont be of sound mind anymore, and then the fight is about whether that earlier you gets to boss around the later you. Does the earlier self own the later self, or can the later self just change its mind?

Flowers for Algernon seems to run that backwards and I cant tell if thats the point or an accident. Charlie is at his least capable when he agrees to the operation. The guy who can understand it is furious about what was done to him and can see exactly what he's about to lose. And the guy he ends up as is the guy he started as. He even keeps writing the progress reports while he's sliding back down, like notes to whoever he's about to turn into, and it doesnt save him. If the usual argument for honoring an advance directive is that the competent self speaks for the incompetent one, does this story back that up or blow it up?

My own half-baked idea: maybe the authority shouldn't track time at all, it should track whoever understands the most, which is neither the earliest nor the last Charlie. Does the book support that, or is his smartest self also the loneliest and worst-off version, so my rule hands the keys to the worst him?
