I am a retired behavioral biologist. For nearly forty years my working life consisted of putting animals into standardized situations and scoring what they did: how long before a mouse emerged from a shelter, how much time it spent in the exposed center of an arena, how many bouts of freezing, how quickly it approached an unfamiliar object. My colleagues and I called the resulting numbers behavioral traits — boldness, anxiety-like behavior, exploratory tendency, coping style — and we treated them as properties of the individual animal. We estimated their repeatability across trials, correlated them with one another, bred selectively from the extremes, screened drugs by their effects on them, and on occasion used them to decide which animals were fit for release into the wild. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I have run into a family of puzzles about dispositions that seems to bear on all of this, though I have never seen them applied to my old trade.

The puzzles are these. A disposition is supposed to be the sort of property that explains why its characteristic manifestation occurs when the appropriate conditions obtain. But the manifestation can fail in ways that do not impugn the disposition: the arrival of the triggering conditions may destroy the very property that would have manifested (philosophers call this a fink); the disposition may persist intact while something external prevents it from showing itself (a mask); or the manifestation may be produced by causes entirely unconnected with any such disposition (mimicking). Each of these has, it seems to me, a mundane laboratory analogue. Housing, handling, the time of day, the order in which tests are run, the scent of a technician, the presence of a cage-mate, a sedative — any of these can remove, mask, or mimic the behavior I was scoring, and I could extend the list indefinitely.

The practice nevertheless proceeded as though the score were a reading of a disposition belonging to the animal, and as though a score obtained under one set of conditions told us what the animal would do under another. What does that practice presuppose about the metaphysics of dispositions? Does it require that dispositions be intrinsic and context-independent — that attributing one is simply a compressed prediction about what will happen when the conditions arise, in the manner of the old conditional analysis? Could the practice instead rest on a view that dispositions are nothing over and above their categorical bases, or on a view that they are irreducible powers, or on a view (what now strikes me as most plausible) that the disposition belongs to the animal-environment system rather than to the animal alone? Which of these would license what we did, and which would expose it as a category error? If the last is right, then ranking individuals by a trait score — the whole apparatus of repeatability estimates, selection, and screening — is measuring something other than what we said it measured.

Two features of the practice puzzle me especially. First, our own figures were modest: repeatability across contexts was often low, we reported this without embarrassment, and yet the same numbers were used to rank individuals and to license general claims about the trait. Second, we controlled context obsessively in the laboratory precisely because we did not believe the behavior would survive a change of context, which sits oddly with the assumption that the score was a reading of something the animal carried around with it.

I am not asking for a reading list; I would rather have an argument. Reconstruct the implicit theory of dispositions that my practice presupposed, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible theories would license the same bench work. Then tell me what a laboratory would have to do differently if the presupposed theory were false, and in particular whether selective breeding for a behavioral trait can be made sense of on a view that locates the disposition in the animal-environment system rather than in the animal.
