Alright, I need help with something I did not see coming. My grandad passed away in the spring and somehow I've ended up as chair of the little family foundation he started in the 80s. Its about $3.5m a year and every penny of it goes to one region: a landlocked, malaria-heavy stretch of the Sahel where he ran a clinic in the 60s and never stopped caring about. My first board meeting is in three weeks and I'm supposed to bring a recommendation for the next five-year funding cycle.

Our program officer put three options on the table:

1) Governance. About $1.2m a year for five years to a national NGO to digitize the regional land registry and get the local courts actually enforcing title. My aunt (econ PhD) is all in on this one.

2) A geography and health bundle. About $1.5m a year: bed nets, rehabbing an irrigation scheme, and upgrading the road to the market town. My cousin (MPH) is all in on this one.

3) Trust and culture. About $0.4m a year for a network of savings groups plus a radio drama series about trusting people outside your own family. My uncle is all in on this one.

Everyone has a pet theory about why the region is poor and they all sound plausible once you're sitting in the room. My aunt says look at the two Koreas, look at East and West Germany, and she keeps citing some paper about how many colonial settlers died in different places. My cousin says look at the map — no coast, bad rain, sleeping sickness, that's the whole story. My uncle says the problem is nobody will cooperate with anyone they aren't related to.

Here's the thing that's actually nagging me. The region straddles a border a colonial boundary commission drew in the 1890s, and it cut right through the middle of one ethnic group's territory. So there are villages 20km apart speaking the same language with the same customs, except one side ended up under French civil law and the other under British common law, and the land law and the way the local courts actually function still differ a lot today. My aunt says this is our natural experiment, hand delivered. Our program officer says no — that border roughly follows the tsetse line and the rainfall gradient, colonial investment on the two sides was never random, and anyway whatever it tells us about national income over 60 years doesn't tell us what our checkbook should do over the next five. She also points out there's a decentralization bill in parliament that could change who controls land records by 2027, which she says cuts both ways.

That last point is what I keep circling back to. Act as a development economist who also genuinely knows the philosophy of social science and causal inference — someone who has thought hard about what can and cannot be learned from history. Start with a short scratch section where you think out loud about which parts of this are actually decidable and which aren't, then write the memo I'll circulate to the board. The memo should pick a concrete option (or a modified version of one) with numbers and a time frame, say what we'd measure and what would make us change course, and be straight with me about the epistemic limits — we can't randomize a region's history, so what are we actually entitled to conclude from five years of grants? Give the strongest version of the case for the option you're rejecting, and keep the whole thing under about 1,500 words. Please don't just tell me that institutions matter — I know they matter, the question is what our money should do. And no reading lists, I need arguments I can definately defend in front of my aunt.
