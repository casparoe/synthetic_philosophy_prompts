I'm a third-year student in a seminar on statutory interpetation (14 people, 80-minute sessions, normal classroom, tables we can shove around). We did the Hart/Fuller stuff and the vehicles-in-the-park hypothetical in week one, and last week we read about the interpretation/construction distinction and I still don't really see what turns on it. I have to run a session for the class in two weeks and my professor is fine with me doing something hands-on.

Act as a legal philosopher who designs simulation exercises for law schools, and design me a classroom activity that makes people actually feel the force of the textualism vs purposivism dispute instead of just arguing about it. Start by brainstorming for yourself about what each view is really committed to and which of those commitments a live exercise can dramatize and which it can't, then give me the activity. Please think carefully about the mechanics -- I care more about whether it works with real people than about whether it's elegant.

What I mean by "feel the force": I want them to have been on both sides. To have written a rule they were sure was clear and then watched it get read in a way they didn't intend, and to have had to apply someone else's words with no way to ask what they meant.

So I want something like two rounds with a handoff between them. Rough idea, change it if you have something better: round 1, small groups draft a short statutory provision for a fictional jurisdiction, and I give them a purpose brief, so the purpose is genuinely in their heads. Round 2, their text goes to a different group, who get only the text plus a fact pattern it doesn't quite fit, and have to produce a holding. Then the reveal: the drafters find out what happened to their statute and get to defend it. I predict the drafters will instantly turn into purposivists ("thats not what we meant") and the appliers into textualists ("but look at what it says"), and I want the activity built so that moment lands. Please don't let the facilitator say the words textualism or purposivism until the debrief.

Some things I want in there:

- Invent the statute and the facts from scratch. No real statute, no real case, no vehicles in the park -- we've done it, and I don't want anyone able to win by remembering how a real court decided.
- Make the invented material feel like an actual regulatory statute rather than a logic puzzle: a definitions section, an operative provision or two, and one term doing all the work. That term should be one where the ordinary reading is genuinely contested and not settled by looking it up -- something like review, person, reasonable, substantially similar.
- At least one fact pattern where the textually better reading gives a harsh or silly result, so they have to decide whether to reach for the absurdity escape, and where they can't tell whether the absurdity is in the statute or in their own policy views.
- Something that makes the recover-the-intent move look less innocent. A staged reveal, a provenance problem in my purpose brief, a note from one drafter that contradicts the brief, whatever you think is cleanest.
- A run sheet with times for 80 minutes, a real 20-ish minute debrief, materials I can print, how to split 14 people (including the awkward odd number), and a small stake of some kind so that signing on to a holding costs them something.

Then the parts I actually need most: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the failure modes where the appliers just refuse to decide because the text is ambiguous, or the drafters write something so vague that round 2 has nothing to bite on, and what I should do in the moment. And be honest about what this activity can't reproduce -- I assume it can't reproduce precedent, or the sheer length and mess of real statutes, or what it costs real people when a court gets it wrong. Tell me which feature of the dispute I'll have to supply verbally in the debrief because the game can't carry it.

One last thing: in the debrief, say something about whether the appliers were disagreeing about what the text means or about how to apply a meaning they actually agreed on, because that's the bit I definately don't get yet.
