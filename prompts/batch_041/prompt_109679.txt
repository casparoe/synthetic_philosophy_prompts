I am trying to understand an eighteenth-century argument against capital punishment. The passage, as I have paraphrased it, runs roughly like this:

Punishment is just only insofar as it is necessary to protect the common good. The death penalty is not necessary for that purpose, because the fear of long imprisonment and hard labor can deter potential criminals as effectively as the fear of death. Moreover, executions are public spectacles that accustom people to cruelty and may even inspire imitation. If the state kills, it teaches that killing is permissible when it serves an end. And because no human tribunal is infallible, an irreversible death sentence risks destroying an innocent person, which is the worst injustice. The death penalty may be permissible only in the rarest case of extreme disorder, when the security of the nation cannot be preserved by any other means.

Help me understand how this argument is supposed to work. Is it best read as an absolute prohibition, or as a presumptive prohibition with an exception for extreme emergencies? How does the deterrence claim relate to the retributive intuition that some crimes deserve death? And how does the concern about executing the innocent function within the argument: is it an independent objection, or does it depend on the claim that punishment must be proportionate and certain? I am not looking for citations; I want a reconstruction of the argument's logic and an assessment of where it is strongest and weakest.
