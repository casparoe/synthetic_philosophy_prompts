I'm building a unit for an applied-ethics course on the moral status of everyday conventions, and I keep hitting the same gap: everyone has confident intuitions about tipping, queueing, and jaywalking, but no one has written down what a theory of these practices would actually have to accomplish before we start arguing over whether any particular norm binds us. So please act as a philosopher working across moral philosophy, social ontology, and the theory of convention, think carefully and brainstorm the full space of cases before you commit to anything, and produce a specification rather than a verdict -- the desiderata that any adequate theory of the normative force of local conventions must satisfy.

To keep us off the examples everyone rehearses, treat the following invented practices as part of the test set, alongside the familiar ones (tipping at a restaurant, first-come-first-served queues, crossing mid-block when no traffic is coming). In the fictional town of Marrow Bay, drivers at a four-way stop wave through whoever arrived second rather than first, on the reasoning that the first arriver has already been waiting and is therefore the patient one; locals regard a driver who insists on the ordinary order as rude. In the same town, mourners leave a smooth stone on a bereaved neighbour's windowsill, and omitting the stone is taken as evidence that you did not care. At a Marrow Bay café the bill already includes service, yet the staff expect a written note of thanks in a book by the door, and leaving nothing reads as a rebuke. On the local bus, boarding passengers thank the driver aloud, and visitors from a city where no one does this are thought cold. Assume further that some local conventions are morally odious -- for instance a custom requiring the youngest person present to eat last, which in practice falls hardest on members of one sex -- and that conventions change, so that what was obligatory a generation ago now marks you as a crank.

Now give me the requirements list. For each desideratum, state the requirement in a sentence, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp -- and be willing to tell me that some of the things people demand of such a theory (that it derive obligation from consent, that it make every convention morally binding, that it be silent on manners, that it treat visitors exactly like locals) are prejudices rather than genuine requirements. I especially want the part where you identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied -- say, a requirement that obligations be knowable or voluntarily assumed running against a requirement that outsiders be bound by conventions they have never heard of, or a requirement that only just conventions bind running against the thought that the moral force of a convention is independent of its content -- and explain why the tension is not merely apparent. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to eliminate otherwise popular theories, and, if you can do it briefly, a few lines on how a Humean coordination account, a Kantian universalizability account, and a virtue- or civility-centred account fare against the list. A table is welcome for the requirements themselves. Do not give me a reading list or citations; the cases I have supplied are the material, and I want your reasoning about the criteria, not a survey of who said what.
