I'm a philosophy of science professor, and my colleague Ruth — our department's resident scientific realist, the one who insists "closer to the truth" is a real, rankable relation even between two theories that are both false — has been running a little game in our Thursday reading group for two years now: you name two false theories of the same subject matter, she says which one is closer to the truth. I've been keeping a tally, partly out of genuine interest and partly because theres a running office bet about whether she's actually applying a rule or just going on vibes. Her verdicts so far: Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than Aristotelian physics; Copernican astronomy is closer than Ptolemaic, even though she cheerfully agrees both are false and both get the orbits wrong, so she's plainly willing to rank two false theories against each other; the kinetic theory of heat is closer than caloric; given a toy case where a hidden constant's true value is 10 and the candidates are "somewhere between 0 and 100" and "exactly 10.5," she said 10.5 is closer, which annoyed two people in the room who thought "between 0 and 100" had the edge by being true; given the same constant with candidates "roughly 10, give or take 5" and "exactly 40," she picked the vague one; and she once ranked two versions of the same theory that differ only in the value of a parameter nobody can measure yet, picking the one nearer the true value — when I asked how that wasn't just wishful thinking, she said the parameter is a real feature of the world whether or not anyone has measured it.

Now, next week I'm presenting a case and I want to call her verdict in advance, because I am definately winning this bet. Two theories of a hidden mechanism, and (ex hypothesi, we know this) the true mechanism is a field. Theory P gets every observable prediction right to the fifth decimal place, but it manages that by positing that the mechanism is a plenum of invisibly small gears — there's no independent evidence for the gears and they're simply not there. Theory Q posits something very close to the actual field structure, but one of its constants is slightly off, so its predictions miss the data by a few percent.

So: predict Ruth's verdict on P versus Q, and then tell me what rule she's actually running. Candidates I can think of: the old Popper-style definition in terms of true and false consequences (the one Tichý and Miller killed off in the mid-70s by showing it makes any two false theories incomparable), a Niiniluoto/Oddie-style account on which truthlikeness is literally a weighted distance from the truth, or a plain empirical-adequacy line on which only what we can in principle observe counts toward closeness. Say which of those her six verdicts above actually leave standing — I suspect at least two of them fit her track record equally well — give me your best guess with your reasons, and tell me what further case I should put to her to tell the survivors apart.
