I've been persuaded by a reading of Principia Ethica that I now suspect may be a trap, and I'd like help seeing where it goes wrong, or whether it is right after all. On this reading, Moore's two highest intrinsic goods, the enjoyment of beauty and the pleasures of friendship and personal affection, are both ultimately pleasures; so his view is really a pluralistic hedonism, and his long attack on hedonism in Chapter III comes down to a quarrel about which pleasures count and how they compare. It also follows, on this reading, that friendship outranks beauty because it supplies more, or more dependable, pleasure, which is why Moore can treat the two as sitting on a single scale. Two things bother me. First, if the pleasure is the good, then the beautiful object and the friend seem to matter only as causes of the pleasure, which looks like exactly the external, means-to-end account of value that Moore says he rejects. Second, his claim that the value of a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts seems to make the pleasure and its object inseparable, so that neither the pleasure alone nor the object alone is the good. So which is it: is the hedonistic reading simply mistaken, and if so, what is the good supposed to be, the whole state of affairs, the appropriate emotional response, the object, or something else? And does Moore actually rank friendship above beauty, or is that part of the mistake? Take a friendship that has turned painful and yields little pleasure: does Moore have to call it nearly worthless, or can he still call it good? Please answer in American English, and focus on diagnosing the misreading rather than summarizing the book.
