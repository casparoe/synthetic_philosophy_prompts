Two friends of mine, both well-read in philosophy though neither is a professional, fell into an argument about disagreement and asked me to referee. Here is the exchange, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

A: When I learn that someone who knows as much as I do and reasons as carefully as I do has reached the opposite conclusion on some question, I have to move toward their answer. Holding my ground at full confidence just because the reasoning is mine is dogmatism.

B: That cannot be right. When I learn that you disagree, all I learn is that one of us has gone wrong somewhere. It does not tell me that the one who went wrong is me. My own reasoning is still the best evidence I have about the question itself, so I should keep it.

A: But your confidence in your own reasoning is precisely what is now in question. You have just discovered that a process you trust delivered a different verdict for someone whose process you trust equally. If you keep your confidence unchanged, you are assuming your process is reliable in a way you now have no independent basis for.

B: I do have a basis — I can rehearse the argument and see that it is good. And notice that your view is in trouble by its own standard: philosophers disagree about whether disagreement requires conciliation, so by your own lights you should already be only about half-confident in conciliationism. You cannot tell me to conciliate while being unsure whether conciliating is the thing to do.

A: Those are not the same case. Conciliationism is a norm for disputes over first-order questions — whether the defendant did it, whether the trial replicated. It says nothing about how confident to be in itself, and a rule can be correct even if people disagree about it. I can be modest about the philosophy and still conciliate where it matters.

B: That looks like exempting your own theory from the humility you demand of everyone else. You say peer disagreement is a reason to lower confidence — except when the peer disagrees about that very claim. And your first-order line is doing all the work: our disagreement about conciliationism is as much a disagreement between equals as any other.

A: Fine, but here is something you are ignoring. Perhaps the evidence does not pin down a single rational credence, so that two people with the same evidence can both be perfectly reasonable in disagreeing. Then your disagreement with me does not show that either of us is wrong — but it can still give me a reason to move, because what I have learned is something about my own fallibility in applying the evidence, and that bears on the question too.

B: That is incoherent. If the evidence permits both answers, then learning that you took the other permitted one gives you no reason to move; moving would just carry you from one reasonable state to another for no reason at all. Conciliationism and the view that the evidence permits both answers cannot both be true.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each turn, say how good the move is, from clearly mistaken through clearly right, and why. Then say who is right about what, what each of them is missing, and who has the better of the argument overall.
