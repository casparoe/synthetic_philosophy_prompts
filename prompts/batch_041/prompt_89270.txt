A dispute keeps resurfacing in the foundations-of-mathematics reading group I run, and I have stopped believing that it is a single dispute. On one side sits the working classical mathematician: the completed infinite is a legitimate object of study; Hilbert's hotel and Cantor's diagonal argument are perfectly intelligible; an existence proof need not exhibit its witness; the theorems of classical analysis are simply true of the real numbers. On the other side sit two figures who are usually run together — the strict finitist, for whom the only genuinely meaningful mathematics is the mathematics of finite configurations and the rest is at best an instrument for deriving finitary results, and the intuitionist, for whom existence means constructibility and the law of excluded middle fails wherever the question is genuinely undecided (a real number defined digit by digit, to be 0 unless and until an open problem of arithmetic is settled, is neither provably zero nor provably nonzero, so the classical trichotomy is unavailable). Note that the intuitionist does not deny that the classical theorems follow from the classical axioms; he denies the axioms.

What puzzles me is how little mathematics actually separates the parties. Classical and intuitionistic arithmetic are equiconsistent — each is consistent exactly if the other is — so no consistency result can adjudicate between them. Every claim of the form a given computation never halts, or no counterexample exists (i.e. every Pi-0-1 sentence), that classical arithmetic proves is already a theorem of intuitionistic arithmetic. So the two logics provably agree on all concrete numerical facts and diverge only over the infinite and the undecided. Meanwhile, the constructive content of classical proofs can be extracted case by case, and the constructive side keeps conceding that it cannot refute the classical theorems, only decline to accept them.

My hypothesis is that at least three disagreements are tangled together here: a formal one about relative strength, which looks largely settled and settled in neither side's favour; a semantic one about what the quantifiers and the word existence mean, where the parties may simply be talking past each other; and a residual one about what mathematics is for. Diagnose it: which of these is doing the real work, is the semantic strand a genuine disagreement or a verbal one, and what, if anything, would count as progress — a conservativity or extraction theorem, a sharpened distinction, or a reframing of what is at stake? If the answer is that nothing would count as progress because the disagreement is bedrock all the way down, say so and explain why.
