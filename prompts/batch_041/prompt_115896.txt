Ok I've got a thing I keep going back and forth on and I want someone to just tell me what principle I actually believe. It's about when you're allowed to take other people's word for it that something is good or beautiful.

Here's where I land on a bunch of cases:

- I have basically no sense of smell. A wine friend tastes a bottle and says it's corked. I have no way to check. I'm happy to say I know the wine is corked.
- Same friend, same bottle, different claim: this is a gorgeous Burgundy. I nod along and I'll happily repeat it to other people, but if you ask me whether I know it's gorgeous I have to say no. I'm a parrot.
- A friend who knows my taste in music says I'll love the new record. I believe them, and I do think I know I'll love it. Somehow this one doesn't bother me at all.
- A mathematician friend tells me a proof is elegant and I haven't worked through it. I'll say I know it's elegant. If I later read it and it turns out to be a pile of lemmas I'll take it back, but until then I think I've got genuine knowledge. And this is basically the same shape as the Burgundy case, which is what's bugging me.
- A critic I trust says a film is a masterpiece and I haven't seen it. I won't say I know it's a masterpiece, only that it's supposed to be one.
- Same critic says the film is structurally a mess and is basically a rewrite of an earlier film. I'll say I know both of those things. So I'll take testimony about the structure and the provenance but not the verdict.
- A painting I've loved for years, an expert tells me it's actually by a different, lesser hand. I look at it again and it moves me less. I think that's rational, and I changed my aesthetic judgment on the strength of testimony. But I didn't have to see anything new, I'd already looked at it a hundred times. So why does that work when the masterpiece case doesn't?
- A friend describes a concert I missed: the second half was transcendent. I don't think I can know that. But she also tells me the drummer broke a stick and the PA cut out, and I know those fine.
- Someone tells me the joke they heard last night was hilarious. I... think I can know that?? Which is wierd given the concert.
- My partner says I only still like a certain band out of nostalgia. I think she's probably right, and I think I now know something about my own taste that I couldn't have gotten alone. Though that one's testimony about me rather than about the band.

So: is there a principle, or a couple of competing ones? My half-formed guess is 'you can take someone's word for facts about a thing but not for how it strikes you.' But the proof case and the nostalgia case both look like how-it-strikes-someone, and the joke case might be too, and the forgery case is about how it strikes *me* and I still changed my mind. Also I genuinely dont know whether the corked wine fits my guess or not, because corked feels like a fact and gorgeous feels like a striking, but they came from the same nose on the same night.

Give me the principle or the closest thing, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and say what it predicts for a few I haven't decided: a friend describing a perfume I can't smell, a friend telling me a translation of a poem is more faithful than another one, and a friend telling me my own poem is good.
