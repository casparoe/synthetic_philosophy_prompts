Right, so — I've just retired after thirty-odd years in bacterial physiology (signalling, mostly) and I've been filling the evenings with philosophy, which is turning out to be a dangerous habit. I've got myself stuck on teleosemantics, the view that a state means whatever its supposed to do, where "supposed to" gets cashed out by natural selection. I'd rather have it out with a real defender than a strawman, so here's what I'd like.

Play a committed teleosemanticist — the Millikan-ish line is fine, or your own variant, but you have to actually hold it: content is fixed by the selection history of the mechanism, not by what the organism knows and not by what a scientist intends. Don't fold at the first awkward case; concede only what you genuinely have to. Write in the first person, keep each reply to a paragraph or so, and stay in character till I say stop. If you need a fact about the biology, just ask me and I'll supply it.

My opening move is a case from my own bench, because I reckon it's nastier than the usual frog-snapping-at-flies stuff. We built a gut bacterium with a synthetic two-component sensor: it fires when it detects a sugar the poor thing has never met in its entire evolutionary history, and that signal switches on a fluorescent protein. No selection, no history — we assembled the whole circuit last Tuesday. The bug now lights up whenever the sugar's about. So does the sensor's on-state mean "sugar present"? If yes, where on earth did that content come from, given there's no selection history to fix it — did we inject meaning with a pipette? If no, then meaning looks a lot rarer than anyone claims, and I can build you an evolved bug that behaves indistinguishably. Which horn do you take? And if your answer is that the designer's intention fixes it, be ready for my next question, which is where the designer's own states get their content — because that looks suspiciously like shoving the problem back one step.
