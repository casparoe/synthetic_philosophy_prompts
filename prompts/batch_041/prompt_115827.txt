I'm running next week's session of our cross-department reading group — me, two biologists, a couple of CS people, and one historian of science. Nobody's done any metaphysics and I don't want it to turn into alot of terminology. The topic is dispositions and the old conditional analysis: fragile just means would break if struck, soluble just means would dissolve if dropped in water, and so on — the move that lets you trade talk of powers for talk of plain counterfactual facts. I want to spend most of the ninety minutes showing why that trade fails.

The cases I have in hand: (1) the fink — a live wire wired to a cutout that kills the current the instant anything touches it, so the wire is live but would never shock you, because the mechanism removes the disposition's basis at the very moment the trigger arrives. (2) The mask — a genuinely elastic ball sealed inside a rigid shell, so it wouldn't bounce if you dropped it, basis perfectly intact, manifestation blocked. (3) A shatter-proof pane rigged to shatter on impact, so it behaves exactly like fragile glass while not being fragile at all. And two of my own that I'm not sure about: (4) a wire where the cutout fires only if the wire is being tested, so it's live but no possible test could ever reveal it, and (5) a person disposed to stay calm in a crisis, except that the calm is what lets them see how bad things are, and seeing it is what wrecks the calm — the disposition eats itself the moment it's exercised.

Don't explain any of this to me. Plan how I should teach it. What order do the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what — in particular, do I need the conditional analysis to look genuinely attractive before I break it, and what makes it attractive to this crowd rather than just silly? Which single case should carry the whole session, and why that one rather than the wire or the ball — what does the carrying case need to have, and need to lack? Which of my five should I drop, if any? What misconceptions will these people form, and where exactly in the sequence do I head each one off; I'm already expecting two — that a fink is just an interfering cause like the shell, and that once the conditional is dead, dispositions have been shown not to exist or to be up to the observer. I also want the last twenty minutes to land a payoff they'll care about: whether you can say a system was disposed to deceive when all you know is that a monitor would have shut it off, and whether the disposition or it's categorical basis is the more fundamental thing — and whether saying the latter just starts a regress. Tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so out loud, and give me one question to ask at the end that tells me whether they got it or just nodded along. American English, please.
