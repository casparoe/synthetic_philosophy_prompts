I am a novelist, and for the past two years I have served as a lay member of the asylum appeals panel of our national tribunal. We must decide, by the end of next week, an appeal from a woman whose application was rejected on credibility grounds. The immigration judge found that her account of a night raid contained a perceptual impossibility and therefore could not be true. She says she was in the courtyard when the soldiers came; she says she saw the red of the blood on her brother's shirt before she saw the bayonet enter his back. The judge reasoned that no one can see an effect before its cause, and that a witness who reports such a thing is either lying or confabulating. The government's representative urges us to uphold the finding. The appellant's advocate argues that trauma distorts time, and that the detail is exactly what one would expect from a mind under extreme stress.

I have been reading about the color phi phenomenon, and I want the panel's decision to be informed by it rather than by folk psychology. When two differently colored dots are flashed in quick succession at different positions, observers report seeing a single dot move from the first position to the second and change color, and they report the second color as occurring before the second dot was actually shown. Philosophers distinguish two explanations. On the Orwellian view, the observer genuinely experiences the first color and the movement, and only later does memory revise the record to include the second color at the earlier time. On the Stalinesque view, the brain holds the first stimulus, waits for the second, and constructs a single edited experience that is then presented to consciousness, so the observer never experiences the unedited sequence at all.

Please draft the panel's decision memo. It should recommend one of three courses—uphold the credibility finding, remand for a new hearing, or grant the appeal—and give a concrete timetable for whatever we decide. It should explain, in terms the other panel members can follow, how the color phi debate bears on what we can infer from an impossible perceptual detail, and it should give the government's strongest argument its due rather than dismissing it. It should also address a question I keep returning to: if the Stalinesque view is right, does the appellant's testimony become more credible, less credible, or simply differently credible than if the Orwellian view is right? And what should we do about the fact that the tribunal's own guidelines treat any physically impossible detail as a per se ground for adverse credibility?

One further wrinkle. The appellant's account includes a detail that is not just a color-before-cause but a color she could not have named—she says the blood was a shade she had never seen before, like wet copper under a green sky. If the Stalinesque view requires the brain to construct the specific phenomenal character of an as-yet-unseen stimulus, is that any less mysterious than the Orwellian view requiring memory to do the same? And does that make the detail more or less probative of fabrication? I need a decision we can defend in writing, not a seminar.
