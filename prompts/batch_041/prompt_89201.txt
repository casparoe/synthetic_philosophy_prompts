Ok so I have this idea for a story I want you to write, and I'll explain the whole setup myself so you dont need any background reading. It's a giant mechanical computer. Take a huge flat chamber, miles across, sealed, no friction, filled with hundreds of billions of tiny identical steel balls bouncing around under plain old Newtonian mechanics. Nothing special inside the balls, no hidden microchips, no quantum tricks, just balls and collisions. The trick is how theyre arranged: they're packed into tight clusters, and each cluster is built so that as a whole it behaves like one solid puck that slides and collides as a single unit. Let's call the clusters blobs. When two blobs collide, the balls inside them reshuffle in a way that also shifts which neighboring blobs are free to move, and it turns out that this implements logic gates. So the whole thing is a working computer, and if you stand back and watch you can literally see a game of chess being played out by the sliding around of these clusters. I think some philosopher came up with a version of this, I forget who, so just use my version and ignore whatever the original said.

Here's the thing I want the story to chew on. When one blob bumps another and that changes the course of the computation, did the blob push it? Or did only the individual balls push anything, and "blob" is just a word we made up because counting balls is hopeless? Same question one level up: did the chess move cause the next chess move, or is the only real pushing happening at the ball level, with the chess game being a story we tell ourselves about the balls? I dont want the story to resolve this, I want it to make the reader feel the pull of both answers.

So: make it about a person who actually has to work on this machine. An engineer doing maintenance, or an apprentice learning the trade, or someone running it for a paying client. The plot should force them to answer the question in practice rather than talk about it — maybe they can only diagnose the fault by thinking in terms of blobs, or someone insists on tracking individual balls one at a time and it goes badly, or there's a dispute over who's responsible when the machine does something it shouldn't have. No character should stand around lecturing the reader about causation, and please dont end on a tidy verdict. Like 1200 to 1800 words, casual prose, it doesnt have to sound like a physics paper.
