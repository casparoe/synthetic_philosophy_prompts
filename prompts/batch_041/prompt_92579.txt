I'm a high school student helping run a session for our school's ethics club on hard choices — the cases where two options each seem to have a real claim on you and no amount of thinking settles which one wins. I want to open with a sorting quiz that tells people which position they actually hold, rather than the one they would claim to hold if you asked them straight out, and I need you to write it.

Eight to ten questions. Every question should be a concrete case, or a concrete thing a person might say or do — a scholarship that can only go to one of two equally deserving students; a friend's recital on the same evening as a family obligation; a choice you make by coin-flip and then feel uneasy about; a choice you are sure of but cannot justify to anyone else; a decision to arrange your life so that these situations never arise. Three answer options each, written as ordinary answers and never as named positions: the quiz has to sort people by what they say about cases, not by the labels they like.

Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions, distinguishing at least these:

1. hardness is ignorance — the options are comparable in one common currency, and we simply lack the information or the algorithm;
2. hardness is a missing fact — there is no truth about which option is better, so no choice can be correct or incorrect;
3. hardness is real but not a missing fact — one option really is better, yet the comparison cannot be expressed in any shared unit (comparable without a metric);
4. the correct choice leaves a remainder — you were right to choose it and still owe something to the option you passed over;
5. the choice settles the answer instead of finding it — what makes an option right is your commitment to it;
6. "which is better?" is the wrong question — what matters is consistency, precedent, or the kind of person the deciding makes you.

The part I care about most is pulling apart 1, 2 and 3, which everyone I have talked to runs together; make sure at least two questions separate them, and do not let the wording of any question give away which answer leads where.

Tell me if some combinations of answers do not correspond to any position, and why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unoccupied — and flag any two positions your quiz cannot separate rather than pretending it can. Describe the positions in plain language; please do not name philosophers or books, since nobody in the club has read them and I do not want the handout to turn into a reading list.

Last line: a single sentence beginning "Summary:" naming the distinction my quiz handles worst.
