I have an argument about causation by omission that I cannot shake, and I want to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs. (1) An event causes an outcome only if it belongs to the outcome's causal history. (2) The gardener's failure to water the plant belongs to the causal history of the plant's death: had he watered it, it would have survived. (3) So the gardener's omission caused the plant's death. (4) If an omission can cause an outcome, then an absence can cause an outcome, since an omission is just an absence of action. (5) If an absence can cause an outcome, then any absence whose presence was necessary for the outcome is a cause of it. (6) The plant's death required the absence of rain, the absence of a neighbor's watering, and the absence of a soil fungus cure. (7) So each of those absences also caused the plant's death. (8) But if countless absences are causes, then singling out the gardener's omission as the cause is arbitrary. (9) Therefore either the gardener's omission is not a cause, or causation by omission cannot support the ordinary judgments we make about it. I would most like to keep (3) and (8). Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it force me to say about the other cases? Answer in no more than 500 words.
