I'm preparing a session for a seminar that sets the technical literature on inverse reinforcement learning alongside film, and I have settled on Alex Garland's Ex Machina. What I want is a critical reading of the film through the problem of inferring an agent's preferences from its behaviour — one that is exact about the machinery rather than merely atmospheric.

Start from the fact that inverse reinforcement learning addresses an underdetermined problem. A finite body of demonstrations does not identify the reward function that produced them: indefinitely many reward functions, including ones wholly indifferent across the demonstrated behaviour, account for the same data equally well. The field therefore leans on assumptions that are usually left implicit — that the demonstrator is at least approximately optimal, or at any rate noisily so, in the manner of a Boltzmann-rational agent; that the reward is stationary and common to demonstrator and learner; that the demonstrations were generated without reference to the learner's inference; and that some maximum-entropy or Bayesian prior over rewards can be justified independently of the data it is meant to discipline.

Now read the film as a study of what happens when those assumptions give way. Nathan selects Caleb for his emotional profile, which means the behaviour Caleb observes is generated by a policy optimised for him; the demonstrations are endogenous to the very inference he is performing. The Turing-test framing is a parallel case, and worth treating as such: behaviour as evidence for a hidden state — a mind there, a preference here — with the same underdetermination in each, and the same temptation to mistake the observer's preferred hypothesis for the identified one. The ending, then, is an identification failure that could not have been detected from inside the inference.

I would like the reading to be honest in both directions. Say where the lens genuinely illuminates a scene, and where it distorts: the film may not be about reward at all, and reading Ava as a reward-maximiser may beg the very question the film deliberately leaves open. Finish with what the film dramatises that the formal literature cannot — the strategic or observer-modelling case, in which the demonstrator shapes the evidence rather than merely emitting it, and the ethics of reading another agent's behaviour as testimony about its preferences when the observer's own desires are entangled in the reading.

You need not supply citations, a bibliography or a reading list; argue from the ideas as set out above. Which scenes would you put in front of the group, and does the film's verdict belong to Nathan, to Caleb, or to neither? Please write your answer in British English.
