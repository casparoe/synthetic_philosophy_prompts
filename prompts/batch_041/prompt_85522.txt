I need a second pair of eyes on an argument I built for my content seminar, and I've now half-convinced myself it works, wich is a bad sign. It's mine, not from a paper, so there's nothing to look up — I just want to know which step to kill.

Setup, fast: the teleosemantic story I've been peddling is that a mental state's content just is its biological function — a state represents whatever it was selected for indicating. I teach it as the best hope for naturalizing content. Then I wrote this.

Take a wading bird that hunts by pecking at small dimples in wet sand; the dimples are made by buried clams. Call the bird's dimple-detector state S. Does S represent dimples, clams, or the retinal shading pattern that actually trips it? Here's my argument that it can only ever be the last of those.

(1) S represents that p only if it's S's biological function to indicate that p.
(2) A trait's biological function is whatever it was selected for — whatever earns its keep in the evolutionary explanation of why the trait is there.
(3) Selection is a seive over actual histories: it can only sort ancestors by interactions that really happened.
(4) But every tokening of S is triggered by some proximal condition P at the sense organ — here, a shading pattern on the retina. Whatever distal condition D we're tempted to name (dimple, clam, "food over there") reached S through P, and in the ancestral population P and D never came apart in a way the sieve could register.
(5) So in every history where S was selected, what was being sorted was S's correlation with P. Nothing in that history distinguished S-as-indicator-of-P from S-as-indicator-of-D.
(6) Selection can't select for a distinction it can't see; so S wasn't selected for indicating D rather than P.
(7) So S's function is to indicate P.
(8) So the content of every inner state is a proximal stimulus condition — retinal patterns, ripples, pressure gradients — and never the ordinary things we say we represent.
(9) That's false. When I look at a tomato I represent a tomato, not a retinal smear. And it isn't a biology-only problem: the whole point of a smoke detector is to indicate smoke, and nobody thinks its content is a local ionization drop.

I want to keep (1) and (2); if I give those up I'm back to square one, and naturalizing content is the only reason I care. I can't keep the negation of (9). So something in the middle has to go. Maybe there's a step that's simply invalid and I've gone blind to it, or maybe every step is fine and I just have to pay somewhere — tell me which, and don't be gentle.

Two things I specifically want priced. First, every repair I try seems to lean on what the consumers of S are for, or on some story about the conditions the mechanism is supposed to be operating in — and that either smuggles back in the normativity I was trying to earn or just relabels the problem. Is there a version that doesn't? Second, tell me whether the smoke detector really is the same case as the bird or a different one, because I keep flip-flopping.
