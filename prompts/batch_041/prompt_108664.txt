You are an expert in comparative constitutional law and in the political philosophy of religion, and you have often served as the neutral drafter for structured adversarial collaborations — the exercises in which two scholars who have argued for years agree to write down, jointly, what they in fact agree on and where they in fact part. Your task is to produce that document for the church-state debate. Think carefully before you begin, and work in stages; the whole difficulty here is in the drafting, in finding formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, so do not start from any assumption about how much of the disagreement will survive the exercise.

The two parties are these.

Party A, the cooperationist: a liberal state may recognize, fund, and cooperate with religious bodies without wronging anyone, provided the mechanisms are non-coercive and open on equal terms to all comers; nothing in the idea of religious liberty forbids the state from treating religious institutions as partners in welfare, schooling, and the care of the dying. A further holds that public recognition of religion is not merely permissible but often good, since it is a form of respect for what citizens actually value, and that an established or quasi-established church has historically restrained religion in politics at least as much as it has empowered it.

Party B, the separationist: the state must not use religious categories in its law at all — not to register who belongs to what, not to collect or channel money, not to certify a body as religious. Religion is to be organized under the general law of voluntary associations. B holds that state involvement corrupts both parties to it, turning churches into clients and the state into a theological authority, and that any scheme of recognition forces officials to decide what counts as a religion, which is itself a kind of establishment — of the state over religion rather than of religion over the state.

Both are competent, informed, and arguing in good faith. Neither is a strawman, and neither is a fool. Neither may be allowed to win on rhetoric.

The material you must work with is the following set of institutional arrangements, which is where the argument actually lives:

1. The German church tax: a surcharge on the income tax, levied only on registered members of the religious bodies that have opted into the scheme, of a size set by those bodies within limits fixed by state law; withheld by employers from payroll data drawn from a register held by the federal tax administration; ended by a declaration made to civil, not religious, authorities. Roughly seventy per cent of church revenue in Germany comes from it. Austria, Denmark, and Finland run comparable schemes; Finnish surveys report exemption from the tax as the most commonly given reason for leaving the church; in Denmark the national church additionally receives general-revenue grants, so that non-members help finance it.
2. Opt-in earmarks that require no registration of membership at all: on the annual income-tax return the taxpayer may direct a fraction of the tax liability — 0.7 per cent in Spain, 0.8 per cent in Italy — to a religious body or to social purposes. In Italy, the shares of taxpayers who designate nothing are redistributed among the beneficiaries in proportion to the designations others made, and no Islamic body can be designated at all, because none is recognized as the representative of Islam.
3. Establishment without fiscal entanglement of that kind: the Church of England, largely self-financing, whose bishops sit in the upper house of the legislature by right.
4. Disestablishment with residue: the United States, with no direct subvention, but tax exemption, legislative and military chaplaincies, and a good deal of ceremonial religion.
5. Separation with a territorial exception: the French law of 1905, which makes the Republic recognize, fund, and subsidize no religious body, and which does not apply in Alsace-Moselle, where the concordat settlement survives.
6. A national church recently disestablished: the Church of Sweden ceased to be the state church in 2000, yet about half the population remains on its register while only a small fraction attends in any given week.

Produce the document in the following form.

Section 0, a short preliminary note (no more than 200 words): the taxonomy of arrangements you will use, and your provisional judgment about which of the apparent disputes are merely verbal. Distinguish at least establishment as state control of doctrine, establishment as recognition or privilege without control, state-collected membership-based finance without establishment, taxpayer-directed earmarks without membership registration, and separation — and say which of the six cases above falls where, and which of them fall in more than one.

Section 1, a numbered list of agreed claims, ten to sixteen of them, grouped as: claims about what the dispute is really about; claims about the liberties both take as fixed (including what a real exit from a religious body requires, and whether a declaration to civil authorities counts); descriptive claims about the cases above; and claims about what would count as a failure of a church-state arrangement, whichever side's preferred arrangement it is.

Section 2, a disagreements table with columns for the question at issue, Party A's formulation of its own position in wording A would accept, Party B's formulation of its own position in wording B would accept, the kind of disagreement it is (empirical, normative, conceptual, or verbal), and whether it is a crux.

Section 3, the cruxes, three to five of them. For each: state the question; say why settling it would move at least one party; identify the case, comparison, or argument that both parties agree bears on it; and record each side's prediction in advance, in falsifiable terms, along with what result would count against that side's own position. Candidates worth testing include whether state-mediated religious finance is a form of coercion and what coercion requires; whether the definitional problem — that someone must decide what counts as a religion — is fatal to any cooperative regime or merely an administrative cost; whether neutrality is a constraint on the effects of state action or on the reasons for it; and whether establishment sustains religious participation, suppresses it, or is causally inert.

Section 4, a reform both parties must evaluate. Take the membership-based, state-collected church tax and replace it with a purely voluntary taxpayer-directed earmark of the Spanish and Italian kind; then consider a second variant in which the state plays no fiscal role whatever and religious bodies are financed by voluntary giving under general law. For each variant, have each side predict, in advance: what happens to formal membership, to attendance, to total religious finance, to the distribution of money across large and small bodies, to the political salience of religion, and to the fate of heterodox and unpopular bodies. Require each side also to say what should happen to the shares of taxpayers who designate nothing — general revenue, or proportional redistribution among the bodies others chose — and why.

Section 5, the residual disagreement: the one claim neither party will surrender, each stated in the strongest wording its own side would accept, together with what would have to be true about the world for that residue to be resolvable at all.

Three further requirements. First, the parties must confront a set of hard cases for any regime of recognition, and must agree on a decision procedure for them: a body with no members and a large endowment; a body with no central authority that can speak for it; a body whose rites conflict with general law; a body widely regarded as a parody; a community that has split, with two organizations claiming the same property and the same members. Second, write in institutional detail — name the mechanism (payroll withholding, civil registration, the box on the return, the treatment of non-designation, the block grant) rather than the slogan. Third, include no bibliography and no citations: do not attribute positions to named authors or works, and where a claim from the literature matters, state it as a claim rather than as someone's view.

Aim for about 1,500 to 2,000 words. Where a formulation exists that both parties could sign, use it; where none exists, say so plainly and say exactly what blocks it.
