I'm a second-year PhD student and a friend in my cohort keeps pushing a trade book on behavioural econ at everyone, so I want to know how much of it's Allais section I can repeat in a seminar without getting corrected. Here's the gist as she relays it:

Allais's 1953 experiment asks you to choose in two problems. First: 1A pays $1 million for sure; 1B pays $1 million with probability .89, $5 million with probability .10, and nothing with probability .01. Second: 2A pays $1 million with probability .11 and nothing otherwise; 2B pays $5 million with probability .10 and nothing otherwise. Most people take 1A and then 2B. But the .89 chance of a million in the first problem and the .89 chance of nothing in the second are just common background, identical across the two options in each problem, so by the independence axiom (a.k.a. the sure-thing principle) they cancel out, and your preference in the first problem should carry over to the second. Taking 1A and 2B therefore contradicts expected utility theory, which says you should take either 1A and 2A or 1B and 2B. What the paradox really shows is that people are risk-averse, they'd rather have a sure million than a gamble with a higher expected dollar payoff, and since expected utility theory assumes we maximise expected value, it's simply false as a description of how people choose. Allais himself took it to be a fact about human weakness: the axioms were fine, people were just being irrational. Hence the paradox refutes expected utility, which is why Kahneman and Tversky replaced it with prospect theory.

Go claim by claim: what's right, what's the usual harmless simplification, what's flat wrong, and in particular what would someone who still defends expected utility, or Allais himself, object to in how she's telling it? Then tell me whether the punchline, the theory is refuted and prospect theory is what took its place, survives once the mistakes are fixed, or whether it was riding on them.
