I've been reading an encyclopedia-style overview of two-dimensional semantics — the author is a philosopher of mind best known for work on consciousness, writing for a general audience — and I want to know how much of its characterization I should trust before I rely on it. Here is the gist of its main claims, paraphrased by me:

"Two-dimensional semantics is the view that every expression has two meanings. The first dimension is what the expression means in the actual world — what it picks out here and now. The second is what it means at other possible worlds, as we vary the world of evaluation. The framework's payoff is an account of the necessary a posteriori. Terms like 'water' are introduced by a description of the local substance; that description supplies the expression's first-dimension meaning, and once we discover that the local substance is H2O, the identity 'water is H2O' becomes a necessary truth. So the framework grants Kripke his necessity but takes back the a posteriority: what looked like a posteriori knowledge is really the a priori first-dimension meaning in disguise. Chalmers then turns the apparatus on consciousness. Since the facts about experience are not a priori entailed by the physical facts, a zombie world is conceivable in the first dimension, and because first-dimension conceivability entails possibility, physicalism is false."

My instinct is that something has gone wrong somewhere. I know the framework is usually presented as *explaining* the necessary a posteriori rather than dissolving it, and that Chalmers is standardly described as defending a link between a specially qualified kind of conceivability and possibility, not conceivability as such. But I'm a reader, not a specialist, and I can't tell which sentences are doing the damage.

Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and give me an accuracy audit: what a philosopher working in this area would accept as fair, what they would wince at as a simplification or a conflation of two things the view keeps apart, and what is simply wrong. I would especially like you to address (a) whether the two "dimensions" are being characterized correctly, and whether it is fair to say that *every* expression has two meanings; (b) whether the claim that the framework makes the necessary a posteriori "really a priori" is a fair statement of some version of the view or an overstatement of a narrower thesis; (c) whether it is fair to speak of "the" framework and "its" payoff when there are several two-dimensional programs with different aims — for instance the tradition running through Stalnaker's work, whose diagonal is put to uses quite different from Chalmers's; and (d) whether the summary's use of the apparatus in the zombie argument is accurate or a simplification. Where the summary misrepresents a position, tell me what a defender would say in its place, and distinguish the points on which the defenders themselves disagree from the points on which the summary is simply at odds with the view.
