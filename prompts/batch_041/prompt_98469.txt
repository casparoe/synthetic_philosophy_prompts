I'm an undergraduate taking a course on concepts and categorization, and we've spent the last few weeks on Bongard problems — the puzzles in which you are shown two groups of six simple line drawings and have to state the rule that puts each drawing on the side it is on. In working through them I keep meeting a situation I don't have a word for, and I want to know whether a word exists before I try to invent one.

The first instance: in one puzzle, every left-hand drawing had a small dot enclosed by a closed curve and every right-hand drawing had its dot outside any closed curve. Before I saw that, I had already found a rule that also sorted all twelve drawings correctly — "an odd number of closed regions, or exactly one dot in the upper half but not both." It got every drawing right, and I could still feel it was the wrong answer; the setter's rule was the plain containment one, and mine was a contrivance that happened to agree on those particular twelve. The second instance: a puzzle whose intended rule was "all the shapes are convex." I found a rule about the number of sides that also fit all twelve, but which I could see would come apart on a thirteenth drawing the setter would have placed on the convex side.

What I'm after is the concept for the specific gap between a rule that merely agrees with the examples and the rule a human solver treats as *the* answer. It isn't simply that the examples underdetermine the rule — that is true of everything and does not explain why my contrived rule feels wrong rather than merely non-unique. It also isn't quite the old worry about a made-up predicate like *grue*, because there the two rival hypotheses are supposed to be symmetric in every respect except our habits, whereas here my contrived rule seems genuinely worse and not merely unfashionable: it is longer, it bolts together two unrelated conditions, and it would break on the next drawing. I suspect this sits near simplicity, inductive bias, and natural kinds, but none of those seems to be the exact thing, and I don't know whether the exact thing has a name.

So: does this phenomenon have an established name and literature? If it does, give me the name and then show how it differs from underdetermination and from the grue-style case, since I can already see both in the vicinity and need to know which, if either, actually covers it. If it does not, coin a term and define it, with the boundary cases that matter: exactly what must be true of a rule for it to fall under the concept, rather than being a merely non-unique but equally good alternative? A bare name will not help me; I need the carving. Please lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning, and skip any restatement of my question.
