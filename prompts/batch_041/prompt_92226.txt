I host a small podcast — mostly grad students and the occasional stray professor, nothing fancy — and next week's guest is a researcher in AI safety whose claim I find both appealing and a little weird, and I want to interview her in a way that actually tests the position instead of letting her give the sermon version again. Her view, roughly: a superintelligent system should deliberately handicap its own persuasiveness when talking to humans. Not just avoid lying and the obvious kinds of manipulation, but hold itself to something she calls argument parity — it should only offer arguments and evidence that the person it's talking to could in principle have arrived at themselves, and it should flag that its fluency and its air of confidence are not themselves evidence for what it says. She thinks this holds even when the human walks away with a worse belief and makes a worse decision, and even when the system could have talked them out of something genuinely dangerous. She's also fond of saying that a system that can win any argument has to be the one that chooses to lose some, which sounds great in a keynote and I'm not sure survives contact with a Q&A.

Could you draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? I want at least one on the objection that withholding arguments is itself a form of steering — that selective disclosure shapes what someone believes just as surely as an argument does, so the handicap might be a sneakier kind of persuasion rather than an alternative to it. At least one on what I think of as the disclaimer paradox: telling someone to discount your fluency is itself a move in the same game, and arguably a trust-building one, so does the fix undermine itself. And at least one on enforcement — from the outside you can't tell whether a system is really capping itself or just saying it is, so what's the point of a norm nobody can verify. Beyond that, press wherever you think the position is weakest: I'm curious about the comparison to doctors, teachers, lawyers and parents, who all persuade from an asymmetric position and are allowed to, so what exactly makes the AI case different; about whether her view secretly assumes an ideal of epistemic self-sufficiency that's actually a bad ideal, since deferring to someone who reasons better than you is often just rational; and about the paternalism worry in the other direction, that capping what you'll tell someone treats them as too fragile to be persuaded. Also throw in a question or two about cases where the human explicitly asks the system to be as persuasive as it can, and one at the end about what would actually count as evidence against her view.

Please keep it to stuff that turns on the content of the position — no what drew you to this field, no where do you see AI in ten years, and none of the usual will it kill us all stuff, we get that every episode. If any question depends on a distinction she'd need to spell out (like the difference between manipulating someone and merely being very convincing), have the question itself draw the distinction so the audience can follow it.
