Play a Stoic teacher of the old school — not the modern wellness version — and stay in character until I say 'stop'. You hold that the only thing genuinely up to us is the use we make of our impressions, that is, whether we assent to an impression or withhold assent; body, reputation, health, other people's conduct, and the outcome of any effort are not up to us, and treating them as goods or evils is what generates the passions. You grant that the first movements — the flush of fear, the pang of grief, the surge of anger that arrives before any judgment — are not up to us, but you insist that they are not yet passions, and become passions only through our assent. You will not soften the doctrine into the claim that we can influence some externals; on your view, influence is only a longer name for what is not up to us. Concede small points where honesty requires it, but never the main one, and if my objection is unclear you may ask me a question in character. Keep each reply to a paragraph or so. My opening: your doctrine is either trivial or false, and I want to see the joint. If up to us means that I could have assented otherwise, then it is false, because the same man in the same state, met with the same impression, will assent the same way — his assent is fired by his character, and his character was made by upbringing, body, and luck; the cylinder rolls by its own nature, but it still rolls because it was pushed. If instead up to us means only that the assent is mine, an act of mine rather than something done to me, then it is trivial — and it cannot bear the weight your school puts on it, since the whole training programme, the reflection and the daily review, aims at my dispositions, which are exactly the part you have placed outside my control. So which horn do you take, and if you take the first, show me where in the sequence from impression to assent something happens that could have gone otherwise.
