Hey, I'm building a unit on cosmological arguments and I want a real episode as the anchor instead of the usual Aquinas-by-numbers stuff. So take the steady-state vs. Big Bang fight in mid-century cosmology and read it through both the kalam argument and the contingency version. Write as a philosopher of religion who actually knows the physics — don't hand-wave the cosmology to make the argument come out clean.

Quick orientation on the episode, in case it's useful: Bondi, Gold and Hoyle put forward the steady state in the late 40s, an eternal universe with continuous matter creation and no beginning — and for Hoyle especially, dodging the first-moment picture that Lemaître's expanding universe suggested was part of the appeal. Then in the mid-60s Penzias and Wilson pick up the cosmic microwave background, and reading it as relic radiation basically kills the steady state. The name Big Bang was Hoyle's own, and he meant it as a put-down.

Here's what I want, and I want the mapping run in both directions. For each arguement, say what the episode looks like through it. Kalam needs a temporal beginning, so the steady-state universe is exactly the sort of thing its second premise denies; the contingency argument (whatever is contingent needs a sufficient reason) doesn't care about a beginning at all, so even an eternal steady-state cosmos stays on the hook. Which features of the episode does each argument actually explain, and where is it silent or overreaching? Then flip it: what does the episode show about each argument — confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated?

One thing to fold in, because it's the interesting bit and I don't want a stock answer: the Grim Reaper paradox. Roughly — infinitely many reapers, one for each moment of an infinite sequence that has no first moment, each set to kill you at its moment if you're still alive then; then you can't survive, yet no particular reaper can be the one that killed you, because each only acts on the condition that you were still alive. Causal finitists use it to argue an infinite past is impossible. If that argument works, does it hurt the steady-state picture specifically, or does it land on the kalam's second premise in a way the contingency version escapes? And please be careful with the difference between a temporal beginning and mere contingency — I expect that's where people trip.

And separate what's genuinely settled from what's actually contested among people who know the material — whether the causal premise holds, whether beginning-to-exist is even well-defined at the Big Bang, whether the PSR is defensible — and don't sell me a contested line as the consensus.
