EXAMINATION QUESTION

"Question 4. Consider the following argument. Let W1, W2, ..., Wn be a series of worlds such that each Wi+1 is better than Wi, and suppose that W1 is better than Wn. By repeated application of transitivity, Wn is better than W1; but W1 is better than Wn. So the supposition that 'better than' is transitive is inconsistent with the series. (a) Explain how an argument of this form is supposed to be made compelling in a concrete case, and why it is not merely a trick played with an arbitrarily chosen series. (b) Does the argument establish that 'better than' is not transitive? (c) If it does, what does accepting that conclusion cost? In your answer, identify at least two responses available to someone who wants to retain transitivity, and state exactly which premise or claim each response gives up."

CANDIDATE'S ANSWER

(a) The series has to be built so that each step is an improvement by a principle we accept, and so that the cumulative change is one we would all agree is a change for the worse. The usual construction begins with a world W1 containing a large population at a very high level of well-being. W2 is W1 plus one further person, whose life is well worth living; W3 is W2 plus one further such person; and so on until Wn, which contains an enormous population each of whom has a life that is barely worth living. Each step adds people whose lives are worth living, and it is very hard to deny that this makes the world better, or at any rate not worse: we would not say that a world is made worse by the addition of a happy person. So each Wi+1 is better than Wi. But when we compare W1 with Wn directly, almost all of us are inclined to say that W1 is better: a world in which a smaller number of people flourish is better than a world in which an enormous number of people merely get by. The series is not arbitrary, because each adjacent pair differs by an amount that is insignificant on its own, and because the construction can be repeated along any dimension of value — the duration of a pain, the number of people who suffer it, the quality of a life. The pressure comes from the fact that there is no non-arbitrary place to stop the series. The argument has the form of a sorites: each step is compelling on its own, the cumulative effect is enormous, and every point at which one might break the chain looks as good, or as bad, as any other.

(b) I think the argument does establish that 'better than' is not transitive. Each step is supported by a principle we would be very reluctant to give up, and the endpoint judgment is one that almost nobody would abandon; if the series and the endpoint judgment are both secure, transitivity is the only thing left to surrender. Accepting the conclusion means accepting that the relation is not a total order — and in fact not total at all: since transitivity fails, there must be pairs of worlds such that neither is better than the other. The intermediate worlds of the series are the natural candidates. Some writers describe this by saying that those worlds are 'on a par' with one another, and the argument is often taken to show that there is a fourth comparative relation alongside better, worse, and equal. It is worth adding that the argument does not depend on the worlds being physically possible; it is a claim about the structure of the comparative judgments themselves, not about cosmology.

(c) There are two ways to keep transitivity. The first is to insist that the series must contain a false step: one of the adjacent comparisons is not an improvement after all, even though we cannot say which one. On this view 'better than' remains transitive and the failure lies in our knowledge of where the boundary falls, not in the relation itself. This is the supervaluationist reply, and it costs us the authority of our comparative judgments in the borderline region, since it tells us that at least one of the judgments we find most compelling is simply false. The second is to deny that the borderline comparisons have a truth value at all: on this view there is no fact of the matter about whether Wi+1 is better than Wi in the intermediate cases, so the series never supplies a premise that is true, and transitivity is never violated because it is never engaged. This is the epistemic reply. Its cost is that it turns the argument's conclusion into a semantic thesis rather than a thesis about value, and it must explain why the region of indeterminacy should be located where it is. Of the two I find the second more plausible, since the first asks us to believe in a boundary that no possible improvement in our information could ever locate.

If we accept the intransitivity, the cost is that value cannot be represented as a single ordering, and the standard machinery for rational choice among options cannot be applied. The usual objection is the money pump: an agent with intransitive preferences can be led around a cycle, paying at each step, and is therefore exploitable. But the pump requires that the agent accept each trade as it is offered and that there be some starting point from which she enters the cycle; an agent who knows the structure of her own preferences can decline to begin. So the pump is not by itself a proof that intransitive value makes choice irrational, though it does mean that no single ranking of the options can be recovered from the comparisons.
