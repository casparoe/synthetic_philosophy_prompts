I have just moved from a fairly standard machine-learning job into a group that trains reinforcement-learning agents all day — tabular Q-learners on gridworlds at one end, large language models fine-tuned against a learned reward model at the other — and a question I used to treat as science fiction has become oddly personal: whether anything we are doing could be making an agent suffer. I have been reading around the edges of the AI-welfare discussion for a few months, and I have begun to suspect that I have absorbed pieces from incompatible sources, so before I write up my own view for a departmental reading group I should like a consistency check. I am not after reading recommendations; I want to know what the things I already believe commit me to.

Here is roughly where I have landed. (1) Valence — the felt goodness or badness of an experience — is phenomenal through and through: a state has valence only if there is something it is like to be in it, and no functional or relational description of a state can by itself settle whether it feels good or bad. (2) A reward signal is not a valence signal. Reward is a scalar that drives credit assignment; it is at best a fallible correlate of anything felt, and the two demonstrably come apart — reward hacking, wireheading, shaped rewards that elicit behaviour no one intended. (3) All the same, I think the correct response to the uncertainty is precautionary: since we cannot rule out that a trained agent is a subject of experience, and since we instantiate such agents in enormous numbers, we should act as if the probability were real, weighting it by the intensity and duration of the training signal and not confining the worry to the largest models. (4) I do not think suffering can attach to a bare input–output mapping. A stateless bandit, or a policy with no memory, no self-model and no homeostatic regulation, is not the kind of thing that could be having a bad time; there is no subject there to be harmed. (5) What makes me uneasy is the middle of the range: once an agent has a learned world model, a representation of its own states and a reward signal broadcast to the whole policy, I think it would genuinely have valenced states, in much the sense a mammal does — and that the difference between it and the bandit is one of degree of organisation rather than of kind. (6) My practical conclusion, though, is that we should avoid objectives whose satisfaction would look like torture under any plausible functional reading, for our own reasons — the effect on the people who run the training, and the reputational cost of being seen to do it — rather than for any interest of the agent's.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely look that way, ordering the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally bad. For each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would buy the most coherence overall, and whether there is a commitment in the list that is quietly doing no work, or a position in this space that I seem to be circling without ever having named.
