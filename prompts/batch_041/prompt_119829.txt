Two years ago I came across the phrase free-floating rationales in a review I was reading for reasons that had nothing to do with the review, and it has been lodged in my head ever since. I have never read the book it came from and I have no training in any of this — I read in the evenings, on my own, and I argue with myself in a notebook. Last month I finally wrote up the argument I have been circling, and I would like you to read it as a piece of reasoning and tell me where it fails. It is not a summary of anyone's views; it is my own attempt, and I would rather be told that it is confused than that it is unoriginal.

The essay follows.

**Reasons That Nobody Has**

*A case where the reason is held.* A woman takes an umbrella because she thinks it will rain. The consideration — rain coming — is represented somewhere in her, and it is represented in roughly the form in which it explains her action. Whatever else is true, this is the easy case.

*A case where the reason is plainly unheld.* A vine's tendril coils around a rough stake and not around a smooth post. Why does the coiling exist at all? Because coiling around something it can grip is what has kept vines of this kind alive and reproducing. Nothing in the vine represents gripping, or survival, or anything of the sort. The consideration explains the pattern without being anywhere in the system. This, I take it, is what the phrase is for.

*The case I cannot get past.* A bird nesting in reeds will push an egg out of its nest if the egg looks wrong to it. The consideration that explains why egg-rejection behaviour exists in this species is something like: an egg that is not mine will be raised at my chicks' expense. That is the reason, in the free-floating sense — nobody has it, the bird least of all. But the consideration the bird's perceptual system is actually keyed to is much cruder: it is keyed to speckling and to size. And here is what I keep stumbling over. The speckling is not a rough proxy for the relatedness-and-cost consideration. It is a different consideration. Where the parasite's eggs happen to match, the bird is fooled: the free-floating reason is unmet and the behaviour fires anyway. Where a nest-mate's egg differs, the free-floating reason is met and the bird destroys it. So the case has two candidate reasons in it, and only one of them is held, and the unheld one is not the one the bird is tracking.

My thesis, such as it is: the phrase is doing two jobs. In the vine case, a reason floats entirely free — nothing carries it. In the bird case, something is carried, but it is not the thing that does the explaining. Only the first of these deserves the name, and the second is better described as a competition between two explanations of one behaviour, which I should stop dressing up as a free-floating rationale. I would like to know whether that split is right or whether I have invented a distinction where there is only a difference of degree.

*The criterion I would use.* A consideration is a free-floating rationale for a pattern of behaviour when (i) it figures in the correct explanation of why the pattern exists at all, not merely why this instance occurred; (ii) had the world been relevantly otherwise, the pattern would have been otherwise in the way the consideration says it should be, over generations, or over a market, or over the life of an institution; and (iii) nothing in the system represents it.

*Three things that worry me about the criterion.* First, clause (iii) looks idle: anything satisfying (i) and (ii) that is not represented satisfies (iii) automatically, so the weight falls entirely on (i) and (ii), and those are claims about how patterns persist rather than about reasons. If that is so, I may be renaming function and adding nothing.

Second, the cases where I most want the notion are not biological. A by-law may exist because it holds some nuisance at bay, though no living official could say which nuisance, and the by-law might survive long after the nuisance is gone. A price rises because supply fell, but no trader represents the whole of that story. If the notion covers these, it seems to cover almost everything and becomes cheap; if it does not, I want to know what is special about the bird.

Third, and worst: when I state the free-floating reason in the bird case I state it in my vocabulary, after the fact, from outside. Nothing in the world fixes that description except my confidence that the counterfactuals would have gone the way I say. How do I tell a free-floating rationale from a just-so story that I have fitted to whatever the bird happens to do?

What I would like from you:

- Whether the split I propose in the bird case holds up, or whether the second kind collapses into the first.
- Whether the criterion survives being carried outside biology to artifacts and institutions, or whether I am generalising a notion that only works for organisms.
- Where I have confused a reason with a cause — I suspect this happens at least twice.
- Which clause is the dead one, if you think the whole thing reduces to function or to an equilibrium explanation. Say so plainly rather than gently.
- One case I have not considered that puts pressure on the criterion, and what it costs me to accommodate it.
- Any sentence that overclaims.

I am not asking for a reading list or for names to look up; I have no library and no way to check anything. If the idea has a standard name, give me the name in a sentence and move on. What I want is the argument: what is wrong with mine, and what would have to be true for it to be right.
