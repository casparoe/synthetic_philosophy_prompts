I sit on the state advisory panel that votes on whether new agricultural chemicals get registered, and Thursday we take up a soil fumigant for strawberry growers. The applicant's assessment puts lifetime cancer risk for the most-exposed farmworkers at 3 in 100,000; our department's own re-analysis, using a low-dose extrapolation that our consulting toxicologist says is just as defensible on the data we have, comes out at 2 in 1,000. Everyone on the panel now agrees the honest answer is a range and that we dont know where in it the truth sits — the fight is over what you do with that. Half the panel wants to approve with buffer zones and PPE, reading the range as mostly fine; the other half says that if we can't say which probability is right we have no business licensing the stuff, and that the farmworkers who'd be breathing it aren't the ones collecting the strawberry profits. Our one decision theorist keeps saying this is a solved problem — license only if the use clears the bar on every probability function we're willing to entertain, or, if that's too crude, at least never pick an option that some other option beats under every probability function we're willing to entertain — and I don't think either move is costless or obviously right. Draft the opinion I'll read at the top of the meeting, in my voice, no hedging, and no citing anything I can't check. Sections, in this order: Recommendation (one paragraph, up front — the vote, plus any conditions attached to it); The choice in front of us (half a page at most); The strongest case for approving on the midpoint (one paragraph); The strongest case for refusing to register (one paragraph); Where I come out and why (about a page — this one has to survive the charge that I'm just picking whichever number lets us say yes); What I want minuted (a few lines).
