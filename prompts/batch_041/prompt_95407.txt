I'm a third-year student in a seminar on twentieth-century political thought, and we've spent the past few weeks on Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial and the storm of criticism it provoked. What frustrates me is that the seminar discussion keeps going in circles: roughly half the room treats the banality of evil as a deep and largely correct insight about a distinctively modern form of wrongdoing — atrocity committed by thoughtless role-followers and careerists rather than by monsters — while the other half treats it as either a misdescription of perpetrators, who on this view are typically driven by conviction and ideology rather than by inner vacuity, or as a moral evasion that dilutes responsibility by relocating it into thoughtlessness, procedure, and mere rule-following. For my final paper I'd rather produce something more useful than a thirteenth opinion, so I want you to draft the joint statement that these two camps could actually both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Neither camp should have to cite any literature — I want every claim formulated so that it stands on its own — and you don't need to settle the contested historical questions about what the actual Eichmann believed or intended; where a claim turns on those, mark it as conditional and say what evidence would settle it.

Please organize it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what the banality thesis does and does not assert; whether it is best read as a claim about a class of perpetrators, about the relation between thought and moral judgment, or about the structure of modern administrative wrongdoing; what both sides can grant about the trial's evidentiary situation and about Arendt's own rhetorical choices; and what, if anything, both sides concede the thesis gets wrong or overstates. Second, a disagreements table: for each remaining live disagreement, the precise formulation of each position in wording that its own side would accept, the crux on which it turns, and whether you judge any of them to be merely verbal — if part of the quarrel is really about what the word "banality" is being used to name, say which part and why. Third, for each crux, a test, argument, or case that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Finally, I'd like the statement to push the concept past its original setting. Take one contemporary case of administrative or technical wrongdoing — say a mid-level engineer or compliance officer who keeps a system running that harms people at scale, and who is neither a fanatic nor obviously coerced — and say where each camp predicts the thesis will and will not apply, what each side would expect to find in such a person's self-understanding, and whether the residual disagreement looks the same there as it does in the Eichmann case or takes a different shape.
