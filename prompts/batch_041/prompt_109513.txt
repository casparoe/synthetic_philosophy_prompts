In several jurisdictions a fine is not a fixed sum but a day fine: the court sets a number of day units reflecting the gravity of the offence, then multiplies by the offender's daily income, so that two people convicted of the same offence owe very different amounts. Other jurisdictions charge a flat sum for the same offence, so the amount is equal and the burden is not. What does the day-fine practice presuppose about what a punishment is and what makes one punishment more severe than another — is severity being measured by the burden the offender feels rather than by the deprivation imposed? If so, what becomes of the retributivist idea that the penalty should fit the crime rather than the criminal's means, and could a retributivist keep the day fine by redescribing the deserved penalty as a burden rather than a sum? I am also puzzled that the very systems which scale fines to income impose prison terms of the same length on rich and poor alike; is that combination coherent, or does it presuppose that money and liberty are not commensurable forms of deprivation? Reconstruct the theory of punishment this practice commits itself to, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if severity were measured objectively instead, in units of value taken away.
