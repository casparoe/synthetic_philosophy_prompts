Our team runs blameless postmortems after every production incident, and after a rough stretch of outages I want to try an experiment: write the next one straight, in the usual format, from an occasionalist point of view. Occasionalism, as I understand it, is the view — I associate it with al-Ghazālī and the Ashʿarite theologians, and I gather some early modern European philosophers held something like it too — that no created thing has any causal power of its own; that God alone brings about every event; and that the things we call causes are only the occasions on which the effect is created. On that view the database does not drop the row, the deploy does not break the config, and the retry storm does not take down the auth service; each of these is merely what regularly precedes the event that is created. I take it the stock illustration is that contact with fire is followed by burning, but the fire is not what burns the cotton, and that this is supposed to leave prediction and engineering entirely intact, because the regularity is real and dependable even if it is a habit rather than a necessity. I am not a philosopher, so treat that as my best reconstruction and correct it wherever it is wrong.

Write me the postmortem: roughly 800–900 words, in labelled sections of the kind a real one uses (Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause, Contributing Factors, What Went Well, Action Items, Lessons Learned — adjust as you see fit), written for the team wiki as though the whole reliability group had actually adopted the position and nobody is winking. It should be deadpan from the first line to the last; no character may step outside the doctrine to tell the reader it is absurd, and no one should deliver a lecture on the metaphysics — the doctrine should be baked into the document's ordinary voice. Invent the incident; something mundane like a retry storm or a cache stampede will do, so the philosophy does not have to compete with exotic technical detail.

The humour has to come from the doctrine's actual content, so please hit these points, and get them right:

- The Timeline should read like any other timeline and then be quietly reinterpreted in the Root Cause section, which should say plainly that nothing and no one caused the outage, and mean it as a finding rather than a cop-out.
- Regularity without necessity: the document must still be able to talk about SLOs, error budgets, and on-call rotations without collapsing into "so nothing can be predicted." Let someone observe that the habit is dependable enough to page on, which is all monitoring ever needed.
- Action Items are the hard part and the funniest part. In a blameless culture we still assign work to people; if no finite thing causes anything, why does adding a mutex help? Handle it the way the tradition handles human responsibility — the act is created by God and only acquired by the agent — and let at least one person admit that this feels like a dodge without anyone managing to repair it.
- Since there is no contradiction in the cotton failing to burn, the runbook should treat the exception as possible in principle while planning as if it will not happen.
- Give me one dissenting voice, invented, with no real person attached to it: a colleague who insists that a correlation this stable must be backed by a mechanism, or who complains that if nothing finite causes anything then every outage was willed by the first cause and someone owes us an explanation. Nobody should resolve this by the end.
- Do not let the piece conclude. The action items should still be standing, the doctrine should still be intact, and the tension between them should be the last thing on the page.

The target of the satire is the position itself and the practice of the blameless postmortem that is trying to house it — not anyone's religion and not any living person — so keep it fair: someone who knows the kalām debates should recognise the moves and find the jokes accurate rather than cheap, and someone who does not should still find it funny as office comedy. Do not put quotation marks around anything supposed to come from a real text; if a character wants a slogan, invent one.
