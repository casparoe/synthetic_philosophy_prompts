I am not an academic, but I have been in a Shakespeare reading group for a few years, and we have arrived at The Merchant of Venice. What keeps tripping me up is a set of distinctions I met in some social commentary a while back and have not been able to put down: the idea that societies can be sorted by how they handle insults and injuries. In an honor culture, standing is something you maintain yourself, with your own hand, in front of an audience, and an unanswered insult is a real loss. In a dignity culture, everyone is assumed to have a worth that does not depend on anyone's recognition, and disputes are handed to impartial institutions that settle them by rules. In a victimhood culture, status flows from having been wronged, so the position to occupy is that of the injured party, and the community's recognition of your suffering becomes the currency. My own suspicion, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that the third is not a genuine break from the first but the same machine running in reverse: standing still comes from public recognition, only now you rise by being wronged rather than by being strong, and the person who is merely accused has no way to answer, because in that economy the accusation is already the injury.

What I want is a reading of the play through these three lenses, written for someone who knows the play well but not the theory. Where does each culture actually show up on stage? My first guesses are these. The bond, and the insistence on satisfaction down to the letter, look like honor logic. The court that convenes before the Duke, with its statute about what an alien may and may not do to a citizen, and its appeal to mercy, looks like the dignity culture arriving. And Shylock's long account of being spat on and called names looks like a claim staked in the currency of victimhood, which the court grants him just enough of to destroy him. I am especially curious about the caskets, because a test that punishes the man who trusts outward show and rewards the one who hazards everything for a plain metal box seems to be the honor and dignity distinction in miniature, and I would like to know whether that is a real parallel or something I have inflated.

Tell me honestly where the framework breaks down. Does the play take a side among the three, or does it show each of them eating the others? And the question I actually care about, which is why I am asking at such length: is any one of these three ways of handling injury morally better than the others, or does the play suggest that the difference between them comes down to who is permitted to do the injuring? If the play has something to say here that the abstract argument cannot reach on its own, say what that is.
