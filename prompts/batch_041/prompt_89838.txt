A friend in my reading group reconstructed the argument of al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers against necessary connection and handed me this chain, and I can neither accept it nor find the break. (1) If A genuinely causes B, then given A and the absence of interference, B must follow. (2) When fire meets cotton, we observe contact and then burning — a succession, never a must. (3) Nothing that cannot in principle be observed may be posited as part of our account of nature. (4) So the necessity we attribute to fire is a projection of our expectation; what is real is the regular sequence, and that regularity is owed to God's habit of producing the burning whenever the contact occurs. (5) But God's habit is no more observable than fire's power: on either account what we see is the same sequence. (6) So the principle that did the work in (3) and (4) also cancels the conclusion drawn in (4). (7) And if we relax (3) enough to let an unobservable producer stand behind the regularities, we have relaxed it enough to let the powers of things stand there too, and nothing in the relaxation itself prefers one unobservable producer to many. (8) Either way the argument either leaves nothing a cause — not even God — or leaves causes exactly where the philosophers had them, and the occasionalist conclusion is an unstable middle.

I want to keep (2); I am confident we never observe a must. Which step should I give up, why did it look compelling, and what does giving it up cost the occasionalist — in particular, what does each of the two obvious repairs buy and lose, the one that keeps God as the sole producer and the one that hands the powers back to the things? And if the same reasoning reaches my own acts, what does that do to the account of responsibility the occasionalist wants to keep?
