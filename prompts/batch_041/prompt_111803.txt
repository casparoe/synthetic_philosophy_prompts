I teach a third-year philosophy of science seminar — 14 students, one 90-minute session — and I want them to feel underdetermination of theory by evidence in their hands rather than hear me assert it. My plan: two teams each get the same log of about forty readings from a sealed device, and I'll fix the log so that two seperate and incompatible mechanisms fit it exactly — one positing a hidden internal state that can never be opened up, the other a purely input–output rule with no hidden state at all. Each team writes its mechanism on a card, then has to predict held-out readings; the catch is that every reading they're permitted to test is one both theories predict, so the scoreboard stays tied and no experiment breaks it, however many they run. One participant plays referee, secretly knowing which mechanism is real but not allowed to say — so the truth is fixed and just out of reach, which I suspect is the bit that'll sting. Give each team a couple of 'patch' cards that let them adjust an auxiliary assumption when a result surprises them, and make them argue afterwards about when patching is legitimate and when it's just saving the phenomenon. Design it properly — rules, materials, scoring, timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto weak versus strong underdetermination, the role of auxiliary hypotheses, and the non-empirical virtues that do the real work in theory choice. What I want them to come away with is that the evidence genuinely can't settle it, and that this is a constraint rather than a licence, so they shouldn't leave thinking science is made up. Tell me what properties the log needs, what'll predictably go wrong the first time, and which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce, becuase I'd rather know now.
