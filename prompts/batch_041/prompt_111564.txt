For about fifteen years I've read rationalist blogs and forums more or less daily, and one habit I've picked up is asking whether a choice is being made on the merits or on the order in which things happened to arrive. I'm stuck on a live version of that. I help run a small research group; we're choosing the format for data we expect to still be using in thirty years, and we already have eight years of work in the format we'd be leaving. A colleague wants whatever is most convenient today. He says the fear of being trapped by an early choice is mostly folklore — good standards win, bad ones get replaced — that if every lab wiped its slate tomorrow they'd all converge on something similar anyway, and that the same inventions keep getting made independently by different people at around the same time, which he reads as proof that the road you take hardly affects where you land. He thinks my worry is the sunk-cost fallacy in costume.

I don't buy it, but I can't say why cleanly. My best attempt at a test: a choice is genuinely hostage to its own history only if (a) the losing option was actually available at the time, (b) the people choosing were deciding on the information then available rather than on what we now know, and (c) the difference still costs us today. He says that's unfalsifiable — we only ever get one run, there's no control group, and any replay I imagine is rigged by the way I set it up.

Can this be turned into a question with a determinate answer rather than a just-so story on either side, and does the answer change what we should actually pick for the archive?
