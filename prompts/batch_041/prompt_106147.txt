You are a philosopher of the social sciences with expertise in the metaphysics of kinds and in cohort analysis, and you have been brought in as rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration. Two camps have argued for years about whether generations — the sort of grouping named by labels like Boomer, Millennial, and Gen Z — are real kinds. Call them the generation realists, who hold that these groupings pick out genuine features of social reality, and the cohort nominalists, who hold that they are at best convenient bins imposed on a continuous stream of birth years. The two camps have agreed to stop arguing past each other and to produce a single joint statement that both can sign.

Before drafting, spend some time working out where the two positions actually touch and where they merely appear to, and decide which apparent disagreements are genuine and which are verbal; only then write the document. Do not attribute claims to named authors, do not cite or gesture at a literature or a reading list, and do not appeal to what any particular scholar is supposed to have said; work only from the positions described here. Define in plain terms any technical vocabulary you introduce.

Both camps have agreed in advance to the following shared framework, and your document must operate within it.

- Real kind. Treat a classificatory kind as real, for the purposes of this document, only if membership in it supports projectible generalizations that are not artifacts of the classification procedure itself and that survive controlling for the separate effects of age and of historical period. State plainly what "projectible" and "surviving controls for age and period" are being used to mean.
- Three grades. Both sides accept that a grouping could be (i) a label of convenience, (ii) a predictively useful grouping, or (iii) a causally efficacious social kind — one that is real in part because people take it to be real and act accordingly. The dispute is over which grade, if any, generation labels occupy.
- Two scenarios. Both sides accept the following pair of hypotheticals as legitimate test cases and have agreed to state their predictions about them in advance. (a) The legislated cohort: a state assigns everyone born in a given decade to a named generation and builds schools, pensions, and marketing around the label, so that members grow up knowing their generation name. (b) The silent cohort: a cohort shares a formative event but is never named, never discussed as a unit, and never addressed by institutions as a group.

Produce the joint statement, with these sections.

1. Agreed claims — a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, about what generations are claimed to be, what would count as evidence for or against their reality, and what the dispute is and is not about.
2. Disagreements — the precise points of remaining disagreement, each phrased in wording that the side it describes would accept as its own position, without slanting.
3. Cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; for each, name the crux, say which side it threatens, and explain why.
4. For each crux, the argument, case, or body of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance — including, where relevant, their differing predictions about the legislated and silent cohorts.
5. Verbal disputes — any part of the apparent disagreement that you judge to be merely about words, with a statement of what the parties were actually agreeing about, and any part you judge to be genuinely substantive.

Begin directly with the document itself: no preface, no restating of this request, and no commentary before or after it. Make the agreed claims neither vacuous nor slanted, and make the residual disagreement exact enough that a reader could tell what evidence would move each side.
