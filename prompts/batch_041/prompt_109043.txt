I have been thinking on my own about an argument for not having children that I came across in a book, and I have reconstructed it as a chain so I can see where it might break. I do not have a philosophy background, so please be patient with the way I have laid it out, but I have tried to be careful. I would like the answer in American English.

The argument rests on what I will call the asymmetry, and it is usually stated with four comparisons. Bringing a person into existence who will suffer is bad. Bringing a person into existence who will be happy is not good, because there is no one who is deprived by not having been born, so no good is missed. Not bringing into existence a person who would have suffered is good, because a suffering that would have existed does not. And not bringing into existence a person who would have been happy is not bad, again because no one is deprived. Put together, the absence of suffering counts as good even when there is no one for whom it is good, while the absence of pleasure counts as bad only when there is someone who is deprived of it. The conclusion is that coming into existence can be bad but can never be a positive good, so it is never something we owe to the person we would create.

Here is my reconstruction, which I suspect proves too much.

(1) The absence of suffering is good even when no one experiences that absence, and the absence of pleasure is bad only when someone is deprived of it.

(2) Therefore, for any stretch of existence that would contain some suffering and some pleasure, the suffering weighs against it while the pleasure does not weigh in its favor, because the pleasure could be absent without anyone being deprived.

(3) This is why bringing someone into existence is never a good thing to do, which is the conclusion the book draws.

(4) But the same rule applies to any interval, not only to a whole life. Consider an ordinary night of dreamless sleep, during which I feel nothing at all. By the rule, the absence of suffering during that night is good, and the absence of pleasure is not bad. So the night of sleep comes out as good rather than neutral.

(5) If that is right, then a state of not existing is, in itself, good rather than neutral, whenever it is free of suffering. A permanent dreamless sleep would simply be a longer version of the same thing.

(6) And if not existing is good rather than neutral, then the death of a person who is presently happy does not merely fail to be bad; it secures a good, since it removes all future suffering, while the future pleasure that is lost is not bad because no one is deprived.

(7) So the same reasoning that supports not having children also supports regarding the death of a happy person as a good thing.

(8) But that is monstrous. A happy person's death is not a good thing, and I do not think anyone defending the asymmetry would accept it.

I would most like to keep (8), and I am attached to the first two comparisons that generate the asymmetry, because they struck me as obviously true when I read them. What I cannot tell is whether the argument really does follow, or whether I have smuggled in a step. Tell me which step you would reject, explain why it looked fine to me when it is the culprit, and then tell me what rejecting it costs. If the fix is to say that the rule only ever applies to bringing a whole new person into existence and never to any interval of a life already underway, I want to know why that is not just a rule invented to save the conclusion, and what independent reason there is for drawing the line exactly there. If instead you think the argument is valid and something must simply be given up, tell me which claim is cheapest to abandon and what is lost by abandoning it.
