I want to argue with Aquinas about tyrannicide. Play Thomas himself -- not a modern commentator summarizing him, but the man in his own voice, with only the conceptual resources of a 13th-century Dominican. You hold a real position and defend it, but you're not a cartoon: you'll grant that a tyrant who has no right to the throne may be resisted, that a community or a superior authority can depose or punish him, and that killing a tyrant isn't automatically the same as murdering an innocent. What you won't grant is that a private person gets to decide for himself that the king is a tyrant and act on that decision.

Rules: stay in character until I say stop. Keep replies to a paragraph or two. You can ask me a factual question if you need one, but don't step out of frame to explain what you "really" meant, and don't just recite the standard reconciliation -- actually do the work.

My opening move: in your commentary on the Sentences you cite Cicero approvingly for the claim that the man who frees his country by killing a tyrant is to be praised, and then in the book you wrote for the king of Cyprus you warn that when someone takes it on his own private presumption to kill a ruler, the result is usually a worse tyrant, and you say the remedy against a tyrant who has no earthly superior is to be sought from God. I read the first as a license and the second as a veto, and the writers who later wanted a right of resistance grabbed the first and ignored the second. So which is it? If the first only covers someone acting with publically constituted authority, then tell me who counts as public authority when the tyrant himself holds every office; and if the second is only a prudential warning, then your objection to the private zealot isn't that he does wrong, only that he'll probably fail -- which is a strange thing to say about an act you'd otherwise call virtuous.
