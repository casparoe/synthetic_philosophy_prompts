The question (20 marks: 10 for reconstructing the distinctions, 5 for the account of when abandonment is licensed, 5 for the case):

Set out Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes. On his account, what licenses a scientist to give up on a research programme? Illustrate with a historical episode of your choosing.

The student's answer:

A research programme, for Lakatos, is a series of theories rather than a single theory. Its hard core is made up of the central assumptions plus the adjustable auxiliary hypotheses needed to connect the core to observation; the negative heuristic forbids working on the core, while the positive heuristic tells you which auxiliaries to develop in the protective belt around it. Lakatos's key point is that we appraise these series rather than individual theories, and that a theory is only really falsified once a better theory is available to take its place.

A programme is progressive when it keeps predicting novel facts, some of which are then corroborated — Lakatos calls this a theoretically and empirically progressive problem-shift. It is degenerating when the belt is patched up ad hoc, so that the changes only accommodate anomalies that were already on the books. When that happens the accumulating anomalies have effectively refuted the hard core, and the rational move is to drop the programme.

Take phlogiston chemistry. Once Lavoisier's oxygen theory was on the table, the phlogiston camp kept adjusting the belt — phlogiston as a principle with negative weight, and so on — and the programme went degenerating. On Lakatos's account that is enough to say the phlogiston programme was given up for good rational reasons. And the upshot is that his methodology gives us a rule we can apply as the anomalies come in: once the belt starts needing ad hoc patches, we know the programme is finished.

So — a mark out of 20, the margin comments, and a line on whether the case is used well or just bolted on.
