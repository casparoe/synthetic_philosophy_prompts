I am a novelist, at work on a book set in one of those small, prosperous, socially liberal countries that perpetually tops the international rankings for women's participation in politics and paid work. One of my characters is a demographer, and I want her to be able to say something both true and genuinely surprising about a finding I keep encountering — the one the newspapers call the gender-equality paradox: that in the countries scoring highest on measures of gender equality, men and women sort into more sharply different fields of study and work rather than more similar ones. Since I am not a social scientist, I have been leaning on a popular book about the subject, and I have drafted a speech for her that follows its argument fairly closely. I would like you to audit that argument before I commit it to a character.

The passage I have drafted runs like this:

For a generation everyone assumed that the more equal a society became, the more men's and women's choices would converge — that the difference between who becomes an engineer and who becomes a nurse is the residue of patriarchy and would wash out once patriarchy did. The data have now refuted that assumption. Across dozens of countries, the richer and more gender-equal the society, the wider the gap between men's and women's choices; in Scandinavia women are less likely to take up science and engineering than they are in Algeria or the Emirates. And since these countries have done everything a state can do to lift social pressure off the choice — equal pay, long parental leave, board quotas, cheap childcare — whatever remains must be nature. The paradox is the cleanest natural experiment we have: take culture away and the underlying difference in interests stands revealed. It follows that the shortage of women in technical fields is not an injustice to be repaired but the free expression of a real difference, and that any policy pushing women toward work they would not otherwise choose is at best futile and at worst paternalistic.

I do not need a reading list; I need to know how much of this a careful researcher would sign her name to. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me what is accurate, what is a simplification I could get away with in dialogue, what a specialist would wince at, and what is simply false. I am especially unsure about the hinge of the argument — the move from the gap being widest where equality is highest to the conclusion that the difference is natural — and about the further move from natural to not an injustice. A colleague in my writing group waved the whole thing off as a statistical artifact of how the equality index is constructed; I would like to know whether that dismissal is any more defensible than the speech itself, and whether the finding, correctly stated, bears on questions of nature, culture, or justice at all.

Finally, tell me what the strongest honest version of each side would sound like, so that I can give the careful lines to my demographer and reserve the overreaching ones for a character who deserves them.
