I've been arranging music for years -- I play keys in a couple of bands, I write horn charts, I've done an a cappella version of a Beyoncé song and a string quartet arrangment of a Radiohead tune for a friend's wedding. So I think in lead sheets, changes, voicings, keys, ranges, grooves, and in intros that have to get cut because the singer needs room for the last chorus.

Lately I've been reading alot about poetry translation -- the kind where the translator has to keep the meter and the rhyme and the syllable count and maybe an acrostic -- and I keep noticing that the whole discussion sounds like arranging. Hofstadter's book Le Ton beau de Marot is basically a guy translating one short Renaissance French poem by Clément Marot into English a few dozen times, each version obeying a different set of constraints, and using that to argue about what a poem even is. Here's the pile of claims I keep running into:

1. Sense and form aren't separable. Render the meaning in plain prose, drop the rhyme and meter, and you haven't translated the poem, you've summarized it. The poem isn't a box with a message inside.
2. Constraints are generative, not just limiting. The rhyme forces an image you'd never have found otherwise. Remove the constraint and you get something blander, not something freer.
3. The reader-effect view: the translation is right if it makes the English reader feel what the French reader felt. Objection: the English reader isn't the French reader, and nobody can check the counterfactual.
4. The translator is an author. The translation is a new work with its own integrity -- but it's also answerable to something outside itself. Double loyalty, no way to fully satisfy both.
5. There's no fact of the matter about the single best translation, yet some are obviously better than others and a few are embarrassing. So relativism is false, but there's also no decision procedure.
6. Every choice costs you something else. Rhyme or literal sense. Word order or idiom. Period flavor or clarity. Preserved ambiguity or a smooth read. You can't bank the wins.

What I want: take those one at a time and restate each in my vocabulary -- charts, covers, arrangements, standards, keys, transposition, the tune versus the arrangement, the fake book version, what actually survives a change of instrumentation, what the audience in the room hears. Be honest about where you're fudging. Don't just find a musical word for every poetic word and call it a translation; if a claim comes out as something no working arranger would ever say, tell me that instead of forcing it.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. Which of the six come through clean, which come through only with a distortion you had to paper over, and which have no counterpart at all in how I think about arranging. For the ones that don't survive, tell me which way the failure runs -- is my vocabulary missing something real about meaning and form, or was the original claim vaguer than it looked and my vocabulary is just refusing to launder it? And do it in reverse too: is there anything I know as an arranger that the poetry-translation crowd has no way to talk about -- the body in the room, dancing, the fact that a performance is gone the second it ends, the fact that half the audience has never heard the original and doesn't care -- and does that gap mean their whole framing is missing something, or just that they're after a different thing?

Concrete examples would help, actual covers or arrangements where the tune-versus-arrangement distinction really bites.

End with one final line that begins Verdict: and then picks exactly one of these three labels, followed by one sentence saying why.
