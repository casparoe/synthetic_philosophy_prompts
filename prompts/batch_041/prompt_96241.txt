After thirty-five years in experimental physics I have taken up philosophy, and I keep returning to Moore's paradox: the oddity of asserting a sentence of the form "p, but I do not believe that p," and of its cousin "p, but I believe that not-p." What I cannot decide is whether the oddity is a single phenomenon or a family of them. Please give me your credence that one unified explanation covers the whole family — by unified I mean an account locating a single source of the absurdity, not a list of separate mechanisms — and decompose it: (a) the probability that the defect is pragmatic, a violated norm of assertion, rather than semantic; (b) the probability that the omissive and the commissive variants share a source; (c) the probability that the restriction to the first-person present tense is essential rather than an artifact of how we report beliefs; and (d) the probability that whatever explains the classic cases extends to the three constructions below. Give a number and a line of justification for each, combine them, and then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most.

The three constructions: "The reaction is exothermic, but I have no evidence that it is"; "There will be a detectable signal, but the chance of one is negligible"; and the same content in the past tense or in the third person, which sounds perfectly unobjectionable to me. If a unified account cannot be stretched to cover all three, say so plainly and tell me which fall outside. For what it is worth, I put myself at about 0.6, largely out of a physicist's habit of expecting unification, and I would like to know whether that habit is doing the work.
