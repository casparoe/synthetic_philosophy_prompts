Write a short story built around sphexishness: the tendency, named after the digger wasp that will re-run its entire burrow-inspection routine from the beginning whenever its paralysed cricket is nudged a few inches out of place, to behave in ways that look deliberate from the outside but turn out to be fixed sequences that cannot be interrupted or revised. Take the story beyond the familiar moral that such behaviour is really mindless, because the example itself is contested: some ethologists and philosophers of biology argue that the wasp's repeated inspections are adaptive rather than mechanical, and that the image survives because it is rhetorically useful rather than because it is accurate. Let that doubt shape the fiction. Give me a human character — a surgeon, a carer, a middle manager, a spouse — who either notices a sphexish routine in herself or has it diagnosed in her by someone else, and make it impossible for the reader to decide whether the routine is a compulsion she is trapped in or a sensible response to a world that keeps moving the cricket. Please resist the tidy reveal in which she either breaks free or is exposed as a machine; the ambiguity is the point, and it should be the same ambiguity that hangs over the wasp.
