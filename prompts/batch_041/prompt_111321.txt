Bit of an odd request, and I'll explain why it's coming from me of all people. I'm a journalist — science and ideas, mostly features, a column now and then — and a freind of mine teaches a second-year unit on probability and the philosophy of science. He had to fly out for a family thing, asked me to cover one session on credence and the principle of indifference, and then, since I was in the room and had done the reading, asked me to mark the short-answer question he'd already set. I can hold my own in a conversation about this stuff, but I am definately not the person who should be telling a student they're wrong about something technical, and there's one script I can't call either way. So: could you grade it?

A grade is fine — letter or percentage, whatever's conventional — but what I actually need is the comments, point by point, the way a marker writes them in the margin: what's right and why, what's wrong and why, and what the student should have said instead. I have to be able to defend this if they appeal, so a general impression ("good, but a bit loose") is no use to me.

The question:

3. (Short answer, one page max.) Bertrand's paradox is often said to show that the principle of indifference is untenable.
(a) Set out the paradox: give the three standard ways of picking a 'random chord' of a circle, and the probability each assigns to the chord being longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle.
(b) What exactly does the paradox show about the principle of indifference? Does it show that there is no fact of the matter about the probability, that the principle is unusable, or only that it needs to be restricted or supplemented? (A colleague in the department insists it should be read as a consistency constraint on credences rather than a machine for generating them — feel free to take that up or set it aside.) Defend your answer.
(c) A classmate proposes the following shortcut: skip the geometry, apply indifference directly to the two outcomes — the chord is longer than the side, or it isn't — and read off 1/2. Assess this proposal.

The answer I'm stuck on:

Take a circle with an inscribed equilateral triangle, and ask for the probability that a chord drawn 'at random' is longer than a side of the triangle. 'At random' can be cashed out in more than one innocent-looking way, and the answers come out different.

(i) Endpoints. Choose the two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference. Given the first endpoint, the chord beats the side exactly when the second endpoint falls in the third of the circle opposite it, so p = 1/3.

(ii) Radius. Fix a diameter, choose a point on it uniformly, and take the perpendicular chord through that point. The chord beats the side exactly when its midpoint lies within half the radius of the centre, so p = 1/2.

(iii) Midpoint. Choose a point uniformly in the disk and take the chord for which that point is the midpoint. The chord beats the side exactly when the midpoint falls inside the concentric circle of half the radius, so p = 1/4.

Three procedures, three answers, and nothing in the statement of the problem picks between them.

The lesson is that 'equally possible' is doing no work on its own. Each procedure induces a different measure on the same set of chords, and 'a chord chosen at random' doesn't determine a measure, so indifference can't be a self-sufficient rule for continuous quantities: it has to be fed a parameterisation before it says anything. Fix the physical procedure — think of actual straws falling on the circle — and the probability is perfectly determinate, and reproducible in the lab. The three answers are answers to three different questions rather than rivals. To that extent the paradox is a warning about how indifference gets applied rather than a refutation of it.

Bertrand seems to want more: he wants to show that the classical definition of probability, favourable cases over equally possible cases, collapses in the face of the continuum. But if the moral is that there's no fact of the matter here, it proves too much, since once the mechanism is fixed the number is stable and checkable. What I'd say is that the question as stated has no answer, and that this shows the probability isn't a feature of the situation at all but of the description we choose to give it — there's nothing there for indifference to get right or wrong, only a convention to adopt.

On (c): I think the shortcut is basically right. If you have no information distinguishing 'longer' from 'shorter', you may as well give each 1/2, and it's reassuring that this lands on one of the three answers above, the one from the perpendicular-bisector method, which suggests 1/2 is the real answer and the other two are artefacts of a badly chosen parameter. It also saves doing any integrals.

I'd add that indifference is really only in trouble with continuous magnitudes; with finitely many mutually exclusive alternatives it is unproblematic.

If it turns out the answer is basically fine and I'm just being paranoid, say so — I'd rather know than mark someone down out of nerves.
