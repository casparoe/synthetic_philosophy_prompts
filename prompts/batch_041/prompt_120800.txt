I teach a 75-minute graduate seminar on existential risk and AI governance, about 16 students. Design a runnable game that makes them feel the force of the unilateralist's curse in a precise form: many agents each receive a private, noisy but fairly reliable signal about whether a one-shot intervention is net-positive; each can unilaterally trigger the intervention without communicating; only the first trigger matters; and the intervention is beneficial if the underlying state is positive and catastrophic if it is negative. Calibrate the signal reliability and the no-money payoffs so that acting on a positive signal is individually justified on expected-value grounds, yet the group-level probability that at least one agent acts on a false positive rises sharply with the number of agents. Run a single-agent baseline and then at least two rounds with larger groups, recording outcomes on a board; keep the true state hidden until after each round. The game should let them discover through play that individual reliability does not aggregate into collective safety. Include setup, materials, timing, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the curse and distinguishes it from the bystander effect and the volunteer's dilemma. The idea should emerge from the rules and incentives, not from a lecture. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle that, and which feature of the curse the activity cannot reproduce, so I know what I must supply in the debrief.
