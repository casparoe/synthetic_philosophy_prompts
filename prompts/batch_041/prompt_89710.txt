I write about the behavioural sciences and philosophy for a general-reader magazine, and I am assembling a feature on the way inconsistency has become the cardinal sin in both fields — the one failing that neither a utility function nor a person is allowed. For a sidebar I want an imagined dialogue, clearly presented as a construction of mine rather than a transcript, between two people who disagree about the argument that has done more than any other to make inconsistency look fatal: the money pump. Picture them at lunch after a conference session on the topic; one is a philosopher of decision theory who defends the standard argument, the other an economist who has spent a career insisting that money-pump arguments prove much less than their admirers claim.

I want both of them to stay with one concrete case rather than drifting into generalities. Here it is. A woman I will call Nadia is choosing among three flats: one by the park, one near her office, one near her sister. Her rankings are stable and sincere all week. She prefers the park flat to the office flat, the office flat to her sister's flat, and her sister's flat to the park flat. A trader who has worked this out offers her, on Monday, to swap her sister's flat for the office flat if she pays him fifty dollars; she accepts, because she prefers the office flat. On Tuesday he offers to swap the office flat for the park flat for another fifty; she accepts. On Wednesday he offers to swap the park flat back for her sister's flat for a third fifty; she accepts. She ends the week in the flat she began in, having paid a hundred and fifty dollars, and at no point did she choose anything she did not, at that moment, prefer. Please also build in a further wrinkle: on the Sunday before any of this, the trader had offered her a sixty-dollar contract promising never to make these offers at all. The two speakers should disagree about whether taking that contract would have been the rational move, an unnecessary capitulation, or itself a second mistake on top of the first.

One speaker should defend the traditional conclusion — that Nadia's preferences are defective, that a cycle like hers is irrational, and that the case reveals a diachronic dimension to rationality, namely that a chooser's successive decisions must hang together as the decisions of a single agent with a single ranking rather than merely being defensible one at a time. The other should concede the arithmetic and deny the moral: that the pump establishes only that cycles carry costs, not that they are irrational; that being exploitable by a clever trader is a curious criterion of rationality; that the demand for one unified ranking across time is a substantive ideal of personhood smuggled in as a theorem; and that once her preferences genuinely cycle it is not even clear that Nadia ends the week worse off than she began, since the hundred and fifty dollars is the trader's gain, and calling it her loss presupposes the very consistent measure of value the case is supposed to show she lacks.

Neither should be a strawman, and I want genuine exchange rather than two monologues, with each speaker taking up the other's strongest point in their own words before answering it. The defender of the traditional view must not be allowed to win by definition: if the other says the pump begs the question by assuming Nadia will keep trading, that objection has to be met on the merits. The sceptic must not be allowed to win by shrugging: he owes an account of what he would call irrational if not this, and of how his position avoids the consequence that no one is ever irrational merely for failing to fit their choices into any plan.

Write the dialogue as continuous prose, each speech introduced in plain text by the speaker's name and nothing else: no headings, no numbered points, no bold, no tables, no stage directions, just the two voices, the way one would render a remembered argument in a letter.
