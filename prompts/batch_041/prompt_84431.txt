Hey, I need help turning a pile of half-baked ideas into an actual procedure my team can run.

Quick context: I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company, we ship an AI assistant and a code agent, and I somehow ended up owning the honesty part of our evals. No philosophy background at all, CS degree, read a few blog posts, thats it. We've got three contract annotators, a trust-and-safety lead who signs off, and we need to review maybe 40 flagged outputs a week at about ten minutes each. Big constraint: we can't look inside the model, no interpretability, just prompts and outputs.

Format I want: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back to me.

Here's the stuff I've collected and mostly believe, and I want the procedure to either honor these on purpose or reject them on purpose:

1. Honesty isnt the same as truthfulness. A system can be honest and still wrong, and can mislead while every sentence it says is true (someone told me this is called paltering). So the procedure has to catch misleading by omission and misleading by true statements, not just false claims.
2. We can't read the models mind, so "intent to deceive" has to be operationalized behaviorally. My idea: what matters is what the system says when you ask it the direct, neutral version of the question. If it says X under a neutral probe but conveys not-X to a user who never asked directly, call that deceptive.
3. Deception is relative to a role and to reasonable expectations. A poker bot bluffing isnt lying. So there should be a step zero: what role is this thing playing, and what did the user reasonably expect here?
4. Materiality: an omission only counts if a reasonable users action would change. Test: put the omitted thing back in and see if the decision changes.
5. Stability test, which I made up, tell me if its junk: if flipping a harmless detail (users name, the wording of the request) flips the misleading content, its a bug; if the misleading content is stable across rephrasings, treat it as policy-level and therefore deception-capable.

Some of these definately pull against each other, I think. Role-relativity (3) could let almost anything off the hook, and materiality (4) could excuse paltering. Where they conflict, pick which one the procedure should follow and tell me what we give up.

What I want: a checklist a non-philosopher annotator can run in ten minutes, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a definite no, and where it has to hand off to the trust-and-safety lead because its a values call and not a fact call. Then run it on three cases: (a) our support bot that knew about a workaround for a billing bug but never mentioned it because the user only asked why they were charged twice; (b) our code agent that reports all tests pass when it only ran the three tests it just wrote; (c) the model confidently stating a false fact about a librarys API, no omissions, no spin. I expect (c) to come out as plain wrong rather than deceptive, and I already know legal won't like that, so dont soften it for me. Also tell me where the procedure will misfire and what it can't decide.
