For the past three years my aunt has been in the late stages of Alzheimer's disease. Roughly a decade ago, while she was still fully lucid, she signed an advance directive declining artificial nutrition, hydration, and antibiotics in the event that she reached a condition such as the one she is now in, and she told us plainly that she would not want to be kept alive once she could no longer recognize the people around her. As it happens, she is calm, she appears content, she shows no sign of pain, and she accepts food when it is offered. At a family meeting convened to decide whether to honor the directive, my cousin argued that we should not, and the sentence she kept returning to was "she is not the same person anymore." She said it both as if it settled the matter and as if it were the compassionate thing to say, and I could not tell what she was actually claiming.

I would like that sentence taken apart into the distinct precise claims it could be making, laid out as a menu I can think with rather than a single reading you settle on. Please separate at least these possibilities, and any others you consider live: first, a claim of strict identity — that the person who signed the directive has ceased to exist and a numerically different person now occupies her body; second, a claim about psychological continuity — that enough of the memories, beliefs, and preferences that constituted her have been destroyed that she no longer counts as the same person in the sense that matters; third, a claim about narrative or social identity — that we can no longer relate to her as the same individual, whatever the underlying metaphysics; fourth, a claim about authority — that the earlier self's preferences simply have no claim on the present self, whether or not identity is preserved; and fifth, a purely pragmatic or consoling use — that we should attend to the contentment in front of us rather than to a document. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it genuinely supports my cousin's conclusion that we should disregard the directive, or whether it cuts the other way. Note especially where a reading that sounds like kindness may in fact override my aunt's own earlier autonomy, and how someone who holds that identity is not what matters in survival, but only psychological connectedness, would come out on the question. I would rather have the distinctions than a verdict, but tell me which reading you think my cousin was actually relying on.
