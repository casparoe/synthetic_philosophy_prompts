Last night a friend and I spent two hours arguing about Bertrand's paradox — the chord version — and I would like an audit of the analogy he kept retreating to. His claim was that a chord is just a point in some space, and that choosing one at random is like throwing a dart at a board: the geometry of the board supplies the uniform measure, and that is why the three familiar procedures for drawing a chord (random endpoints, random midpoint, random distance from the center) yield three different answers — a third, a half, and a quarter — to the question of how likely a random chord is to be longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. On his account, those are simply answers about three different boards, so the episode teaches only that one must specify the setup; it is not a counterexample to the principle of indifference. My objection was that the problem is precisely that no setup is given, and he answered that a question with no setup has no answer, which is not the same thing as a paradox.

How far does the analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondences first — board, dart, thrower's aim, uniform measure — then say where the mapping breaks, which features of the dartboard case it imports that the chord case lacks, and whether his conclusion survives or is instead carried by the analogy's ambiguity; if the analogy does support a conclusion, tell me whose it is. If a better analogy captures what really goes wrong here, give me that too. Lead with your bottom line in one sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
