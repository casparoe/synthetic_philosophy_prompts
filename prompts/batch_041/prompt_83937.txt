I'm a third-year phil student and, through a chain of events I won't bore you with, I'm the undergrad rep on my university's dining and procurement committee. We meet Thursday and I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand, so I need you to draft it. Background: catering is about to sign a two-year contract with a company that makes patties and protein bars out of farmed black soldier fly larvae, and our events catering buys a ridiculous amount of shrimp. A student campaign has been pushing us to adopt a real welfare policy for university food purchasing, and they want it written in terms of sentience rather than species -- protect whatever can suffer, don't draw the line at 'vertebrates' or 'animals'. The chair thinks that's a trap and wants us to just say we'll 'take animal welfare into account' and move on. And there's a bloc that says fine, but why start with bugs and shrimp when the chickens and eggs we buy are definately from horrible conditions -- why spend our credibility on maybe-suffering instead of certain suffering?

Two things I want in the memo, because I think they actually change the answer and nobody on the committee has heard of them. In my animal minds course we went through the checklist people use to guess whether a taxon is even a candidate for sentience -- nociceptors, some kind of centralized nervous system, integration of that info, motivational trade-offs (accepting a worse option to avoid something noxious), self-administering painkillers -- and octopuses and crabs apparently pass a good few of those, which is not what most people expect. Second, my flatmate in the EA soc keeps going on about 'welfare ranges' -- the idea that even among sentient beings, the spread of possible good and bad experience might differ a lot by species, so insect suffering could be real but small on a common scale, which is an argument for weighting it rather than ignoring it. I half buy both of these, but I can't tell whether either one actually supports the campaign's sentience-only framing or undercuts it.

So: draft the opinion I'll circulate. It needs a concrete recommendation I can put to a vote, with a time frame and who does what, and it has to take the 'fix the certain stuff first' objection seriously rather than batting it away -- if you think that's not really the strongest consideration on the other side, tell me what is. And if you think a sentience-based framing is just the wrong instrument for a procurement policy, say so and say what we should use instead.
