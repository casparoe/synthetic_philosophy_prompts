I'm a PhD student in political theory working on a paper about Hegel's treatment of war, and I'd like your comments on the argument sketch below — specifically on its accuracy as a reading of Hegel, on whether the framing is interesting, and on where it overreaches.

The sketch: Hegel's account of war in the Philosophy of Right is usually read either as a proto-militarist glorification of conflict or as a regrettable concession to the realpolitik of his time. I want to argue that both readings miss the structural point. On my reading, Hegel's claim that war is the moment in which the state's ideality over against its members becomes actual is continuous with the Logic's account of the individual's relation to the genus: just as the individual living thing dies and in doing so is sublated into the genus's process, so the individual citizen's sacrifice in war is the medium through which the state's sovereignty — its individuality, its being-for-itself — is realized. War is not a means to an end but the negative moment through which the ethical whole is actualized.

Two further claims. First, the passage where Hegel says war preserves the ethical health of a people is best understood not as a homeopathic claim that war is good for a people but as a claim about the corruption of civil society: long peace allows particular interests to harden into the appearance of self-sufficiency, and war is the moment in which their dependence on the whole is revealed. Second, Hegel's rejection of Kant's perpetual peace is not a rejection of peace as such but a claim about the logic of the state's relation to other states: a league of states would be a contradiction in terms because the state's sovereignty cannot be limited without ceasing to be sovereignty.

What I want from you: is the parallel with the Logic's doctrine of the Concept defensible, or am I overreading the systematic connection? Is the corruption reading of the ethical health passage textually supportable, or does it downplay the more troubling surface claim? And is the argument interesting as a piece of political theory, or does it end up being a clever reconciliation that doesn't change how we read the text?
