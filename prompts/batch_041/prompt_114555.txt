I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in democratic theory — eighteen students, one 85-minute session, flat seminar room, no screens, chairs I can rearrange. I want a runnable activity that makes the group feel, rather than merely hear about, two things about the phrase "the will of the people": first, that a body of individually consistent people can, under perfectly ordinary majority voting, arrive at a collectively incoherent position on issues that are logically connected; and second, that the body supposed to *have* the will — the people — cannot be constituted by a vote of the people without circularity. The activity has to stage both, in that order, without naming either, and without any front-loaded lecture. Participants should discover the first problem at the tally and the second problem at the moment they try to fix the first.

Give me a complete design: setting and staging, materials, the rules and any roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact wording of the ballots and any cards, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the concepts, plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. Also be frank about which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in speech.

Constraints and content to build in:

- Keep it low-tech: index cards, printed ballots, a whiteboard, no devices. Nothing that requires real money.
- Do not use the words "discursive dilemma," "boundary problem," "general will," or "social choice" anywhere participants can see or hear them until the debrief.
- The logical linkage among the substantive issues must be real but not advertised. Space the votes out, present the items as an ordinary policy package in shuffled order, and do not print the items on a single sheet in an order that reveals the structure.
- I want the first problem to be robust rather than lucky. So do not rely on the group happening to vote into a paradox: hand each participant a short constituency brief that fixes a coherent set of commitments on the linked items, and require them to argue publicly for their brief before each vote. The briefs should be written so that every individual is internally consistent while the majority on each item, tallied separately, is not. Work out the numbers for eighteen participants yourself and give me the briefs, the expected tallies, and a fallback brief or two in case I have an odd number present.
- The second problem should arise from the first. After the incoherent tally is on the board, introduce a "unity chair" whose job is to deliver a single coherent platform that can be called the will of the room. Force the choice between a coherent minority platform and an incoherent majority one, and make both options cost something visible.
- The membership problem must be enacted, not described. Before any substantive vote, a contested group claims the right to vote — pick one that fits a campus setting, such as long-term residents of the town who are not students or staff, or a neighboring district that the decision will affect. The room votes on whether to seat them, and that vote governs who votes thereafter. Make sure the design exposes the circularity: the seating vote is decided by a body whose own composition is exactly what is in question. Do not let the activity resolve this; let it end unresolved, with the two costs of resolving it stated plainly in the debrief.
- In the debrief, do the following. Tie the shuffled-item tally to the general point that majority rule over connected propositions has no guarantee of coherence, and explain why the problem is not stupidity or bad faith on anyone's part. Tie the seating vote to the point that the demos is logically prior to its own will, so "the will of the people" cannot be the ground of who the people are. Name the two ideas only now, give the standard one-line statements of each, and say which of the two a real populist would resist and why. Then state honestly what the activity cannot reproduce — I have in mind at least that it cannot generate the felt authority of a leader or movement that claims to *embody* the will rather than tally it, and that it cannot model the temporal claim that the will persists and is betrayed across time; add anything else you judge it cannot reach. For each thing it cannot reproduce, say what I must do in speech instead.
- Predict the first-run failures concretely and give me the fix for each. At minimum: someone spots the logical linkage before the tally and announces it; participants treat their briefs as a game and vote their own opinions instead; the unity chair dominates or collapses; the seating vote feels rigged because I chose the disputed group; the group resolves the incoherence too quickly by dropping an item and misses the point. Add any others you expect.
- Assume I have run seminars before but never this activity, and write the design so I can hand it to a co-teacher who has not read this brief.

Write it as a usable plan, not an essay about the topic.
