I teach a second-year "reasoning and evidence" course -- mixed majors, about 20 students, one 90-minute block -- and I want an activity that makes Simpson's paradox land as a felt problem rather than a table they nod at and forget. Don't name it until the debrief, and don't explain it up front.

Here's the seed I've got, build the presentation around it (the numbers are made up, they're just arithmetic):

Two treatments for the same condition. Treatment A: 18 of 20 mild cases recover, and 18 of 180 severe cases recover. Treatment B: 153 of 180 mild cases recover, and 1 of 20 severe cases recover. So A wins in each group (90% vs 85%, and 10% vs 5%) but loses overall (18% vs 77%), because B was given mostly to mild cases.

What I want them to feel: they commit to a choice, publicly, and then the same numbers re-sliced tell them the opposite -- and then they find out that the arithmetic was never the problem, the causal story is.

So design the session. Things I want in it:

Round 1: everyone sees only the totals and has to pick a treatment. A real vote, on paper, hands up, whatever. Something at stake even if it's just chips or a small prize for the side that ends up "winning" the session. They should be irritated when the split is revealed.

Round 2: same data, split by severity. Second vote. Let them argue it out. Don't resolve it for them.

Round 3, the part I actually care about: give them the same two tables three times with three different backstorys for what that severity variable is -- say, severity was recorded by the clinician before anyone was assigned a treatment; severity was recorded on day 3, after treatment had started; severity is something that both the treatment and a hidden frailty factor influence. They have to say, for each backstory, which comparison answers the question of which treatment is better. I want them to discover that the right move flips with the story, and that there's no rule -- always pool, always split -- that saves you from doing the causal work. The day-3 one is the one they'll fight about, so leave room for that.

A lobbying round if it fits: two teams, one briefed to argue for A, one for B, each free to present the data however it likes to a student playing the regulator, who then rules. Fake currency or points for a favourable ruling. Point of it is to make them feel that one table is also a weapon, then ask whether presenting it that way was dishonest given each of the three backstories.

Then a debrief that maps what just happened onto the pieces of the idea: the unequal group sizes, the lurking variable, why the reversal is arithmetically fine, why the resolution is causal rather than statistical, and why a bigger sample doesn't fix any of it. Tell me the materials I need to print and how to set the room up.

And be honest with me about the running of it: what predictably goes wrong the first time (I'm guessing someone insists the totals have to be right because more data is better, and someone accuses the whole thing of being a trick about lying), how to handle each of those, and which feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce so I have to supply it myself in the debrief.
