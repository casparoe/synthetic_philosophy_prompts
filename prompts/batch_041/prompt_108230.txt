I'm a novelist, currently holed up at a month-long residency, and last night two of the other residents got into an argument at dinner that I've been chewing on ever since. One writes science fiction and follows the AI safety literature closely; the other is a historian of technology. The science-fiction writer laid out a distinction he's fond of, between two ways things could go wrong as machine systems grow more capable. In the first, some system or set of systems develops an instrumental appetite for power — resources, influence, the ability to shape the world — and effectively takes over, so that human values end up served by nothing and nobody, though no one was ever really trying to serve them. In the second, nothing so dramatic occurs: people keep making individually sensible choices, delegating a little more each year to systems more competent than we are, and over time the world gets optimized for something merely adjacent to what we wanted while our own grip on the levers quietly loosens. He called the first influence-seeking and the second slow drift, and he thinks the second is the one people underestimate.

What followed was, roughly, this. He said the two are finally one phenomenon at different tempos — that drift is influence-seeking moving slowly enough that nobody notices, and influence-seeking is drift that has stopped bothering to be polite — because underneath both sits a single mechanism: optimization pressure favors whatever acquires influence, so the distinction is cosmetic. The historian disagreed sharply. She said the difference that matters is whether anything is *trying* to seize control: in the influence-seeking story there is an agent with an objective and an instrumental reason to accumulate power, whereas in the drift story the loss of control is a byproduct of countless reasonable local decisions, with no villain and no seizure, which is precisely why it is harder to resist — you cannot point to the moment, or the actor, that needs stopping. He answered that this proves too much: if competence itself reliably converts into influence, then the byproduct is simply instrumentally-driven power-seeking distributed across many systems, and "nobody intended it" is how most real concentrations of power have come about anyway — markets and bureaucracies did not conspire either — so calling it drift rather than a power grab is influence-seeking with better manners and a longer timeline. She replied that this dissolves the very concept it is meant to defend: if every diffuse social change counts as a power grab, then influence-seeking stops telling you what to look for, and the two scenarios demand opposite things of us — one tells you to hunt for scheming and deception, the other to watch for the slow erosion of the institutions that would let you notice at all. She added that drift might not even be a failure, since if values drift one might ask whose values, and whether the drifted ones might not be better. He came back hard on that last point: better according to whom? If the drift erodes the very standpoint from which anyone could judge, then calling the outcome better is like asking a person who has been gradually sedated whether he is content — and because there is no threshold at which anyone can say we have drifted too far, he thinks the drift scenario may be the worse of the two, since with a seizure there is at least an event and a culprit.

I couldn't referee this, and I'd like you to. Go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific claim — not who won overall — and then tell me what both of them are missing. I'm especially interested in whether the distinction they are fighting over actually does any work, or whether one of them has quietly conceded the other's point somewhere in the middle of the argument.
