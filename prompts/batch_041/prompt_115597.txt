I'm part of a small reading group that meets every couple of weeks on institutional design, and we've been working through proposals for supplementing or replacing ordinary electoral democracy with market mechanisms. Next month's session is on futarchy, the proposal on which a polity votes on the welfare metric it wants to pursue — GDP per capita, life expectancy, some composite index — and then conditional prediction markets decide the specific policies, with a policy being adopted if and only if the market's estimate of the metric in the world where the policy passes exceeds its estimate of the metric in the world where it fails. My job is to bring the closing joke. The group's taste runs against the generic "betting on politics is absurd" satire and toward jokes that only land if you have actually thought about the mechanism, so I'd like your help writing one.

Please write a single joke, tellable aloud in roughly six sentences, whose punchline turns on something specific and real about how the proposal is supposed to work rather than on the general idea that markets are crass or that politicians are venal. Targets I'd find especially promising, if you want to pick one: the fact that the conditional markets must be settled against a metric that has to be fixed in advance, so the proposal needs a well-defined measure of welfare before any market can price anything; the worry that an agent who stands to gain more from a policy's passage than he loses on the market can profitably bet to move the price rather than to be right; or the reflexivity of using a market to decide whether to keep using markets. You need not use any of these — if some other feature of the mechanism yields a better joke, use it — and you may invent a small concrete scenario (a country, a policy, a trader) to hang it on, provided the mechanism stays faithful. What I want to avoid is a joke that would work equally well for any prediction market or any libertarian scheme; the punchline should be unavailable to someone who has not grasped the conditional-market structure.

After the joke, add a short note of a few sentences flagging where it takes liberties with the proposal or leans on a contested reading of it. Be explicit about which parts of the mechanism the joke relies on are uncontroversial among people who have studied these proposals and which are live disputes — for instance, whether manipulation is genuinely profitable or self-limiting, whether the metric can be specified without circularity, whether such markets would be thick enough to be informative. Do not present a contested criticism as the settled verdict on futarchy; where the joke depends on a criticism that is genuinely disputed, say so plainly.
