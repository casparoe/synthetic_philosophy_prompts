I'm a backend dev with no philosophy training, so sorry in advance if I'm mangling any of these terms. I keep noticing a move in arguments about how much of a mind is built in versus learned, and I want to know whether it has a name.

Here's the pattern. Someone claims a system couldn't possibly get some ability from the data it sees, so the ability must be there from the start. Then somebody builds something that does get the ability from data, and the response isn't "fair enough" -- it's "well, it only managed that because of the way it's wired; the wiring is doing the work, and the wiring is innate too." Then whatever the wiring is -- a prior over hypotheses, an architecture, a bias -- gets promoted to being the real innate thing, and we're back where we started, except the thing that's supposedly innate has just moved one level down. And this can keep happening.

Two concrete ones I've run into. (1) The claim that no learner could pull grammar out of the messy language kids actually hear, so there must be a language-specific organ. Then people show that generic statistical learning picks up alot of structure, and the reply is "but the learner still needs a prior that ranks some grammars above others, and that prior is innate." (2) In ML, people say a net can't do X without hand-coded knowledge; a bigger net does X; and then it's either "that's just the architecture acting as a prior, which is innate" or the opposite crowd going "hand-built knowledge always loses to scale anyway."

So really two questions. First, is there an established name for this specific move -- where a demonstration that something is learnable gets re-read as a demonstration that something *else* is innate, so the innateness claim never actually takes a hit? I know "poverty of the stimulus" is a thing, but my sense is that's a much more precise technical claim about the data underdetermining the answer given a fixed space of hypotheses, whereas what I'm describing feels more like a way of arguing that can't lose. If it isn't just poverty of the stimulus, what's the nearest thing, and where's the line between a legitimate version of the argument and the version that can never be beaten? And does the "scale always wins" crowd have a name for their side of it, or is that just the same pattern facing the other way?

Don't just hand me a label -- I care about where the edges are. What makes something a genuine instance of the pattern, versus me just watching two people talk past each other? If I've smushed together two genuinely different things here (say, a real technical claim and a bit of bad rhetoric), please say so plainly rather than being polite about it. And can you write the answer in British English please.
