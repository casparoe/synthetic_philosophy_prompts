I never studied philosophy at a university, but I have read it on my own for years, and for the last three I have run a small discussion group that meets twice a month. Most of the people in it are like me: bright, well read, no formal training. We have just finished a term on art and criticism, and I want to close with a written exercise in the style of a university exam, since several of them are thinking about applying to graduate programs and want to see what that feels like. So I need one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme I can grade their attempts against.

Aim it at people who can follow an argument and have done the reading, but who have never written a philosophy paper. It should be hard enough that reciting the familiar positions will not carry them, and it should not turn on knowing the literature, because none of us do.

Here is the topic I want it to turn on. There is a rule of thumb that nearly everyone in the group accepts and none of us can state properly: that you cannot honestly judge a thing beautiful, or good as art, unless you have experienced it yourself. You can take someone's word that a bridge is safe or that a town is crowded, but not, people say, that a film is wonderful. I do not know whether philosophers have a name for this rule and I do not want the exercise to depend on the name; I want the idea itself put under pressure.

To give it something to bite on, use the following case, which is mine and comes from no book. Imagine a woman, call her the stand-in, who has never seen a particular film. She has listened for years to a friend who loves it, and the friend has described it so well that she can now do two things. She can predict, reliably and in specifics, what a first-time viewer will notice, which moments will move them, and roughly how they will rate it afterward. And if you ask her own opinion, she says, sincerely and without hesitation, that it is a great film, and she is right: her predictions have never failed, including about herself, and when she finally does see it her reaction is exactly what she expected. Did she know it was great before she saw it? Was she entitled to say so? And when she said so, was she making the same move an experienced viewer makes, or only a convincing imitation of one?

I want the question to force them to separate two things that testimony might carry: a verdict, and a way of looking. My own hunch, which they should feel free to reject, is that the rule is really about the second and only pretends to be about the first. More importantly, I want them to say which of three things the rule is a claim about — what can justify a judgment, what it takes to be in the state of finding something beautiful, or what it takes for the words to count as a judgment of taste at all — and to decide, on the strength of the case, which of those readings the rule can survive and which it cannot. The question should require a definite verdict on the stand-in, not a survey of possibilities.

Please give me the response in four sections, in this order.

1. The question. About 150 words, written to the students, with the case and any instructions they need.

2. Model answer. About 700 words, written as the answer itself. It must argue rather than survey. It should not name any philosopher or cite any work; where it needs a position, it should describe the position in plain words. I would rather it spend its words on the argument.

3. Marking scheme. Out of 20 marks. List the specific moves a top answer must make and what each is worth, and add a line on what separates a full-marks answer from a solid passing one.

4. Common mistakes. Five entries, one line each, naming the mistake and the marks it should cost.
