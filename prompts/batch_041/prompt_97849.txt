For most of the past decade I have chaired the informal dispute-resolution stage that sits in front of the formal grievance machinery in my university's interdisciplinary humanities program, and only recently have I come to think that much of my caseload is the wrong kind of problem. The change came when a disagreement I was myself party to — over whether a colleague's book was really work in our field or in an adjacent one — came within an inch of ending a collaboration of fifteen years, and I realized afterward that we had never once disagreed about any fact. We had been using the same words for different things, and each of us had read the other's persistence as bad faith. I have since read enough of the philosophy of language, and of the recent writing on verbal disputes, on the ontology of the disputes themselves, and on the deliberate revision of concepts, to suspect that a large share of what reaches my committee is either a dispute about words or a dispute about which words we ought to adopt. Our reflex of treating every complaint as a substantive disagreement wastes time and does real harm: it forces people to litigate the meaning of a term under the description of a fight about the world, and it rewards whoever is more fluent at that misdescription.

I would like you to turn that material into a decision procedure I can actually run, case by case, in about an hour, with no philosophical training beyond what I have just set out, and with the understanding that my recommendations go to the dean, who has the final say. I do not want a literature review, a reading list, or citations; I want the procedure, and I want it to carry whatever from the theory genuinely survives being made usable by someone who is not a philosopher.

Please give me, in order:

1. The questions to ask of a complaint, in the sequence I should ask them, with a short statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each. At a minimum the procedure must find a way to apply the basic test — hold every non-linguistic fact fixed and agreed, and ask whether anything remains for the parties to disagree about — and a way to apply the substitution test, in which I take each party's own account of what a contested term means, insert it into the other party's claim, and see whether the disagreement survives the substitution.

2. An explicit treatment of the distinction between a dispute that is verbal and one that is merely verbal. A complaint can turn on a difference in usage while still containing something real, namely a disagreement about which usage or concept we ought to adopt. Some of these are best understood as parties pressing for a word to mean one thing rather than reporting what it already means — a negotiation about language conducted in the grammar of a claim about the world — and those are not empty; they are policy questions wearing the costume of fact. The procedure must separate these from cases where nothing survives once the words are fixed, and it must say what to do with each: dissolve the first kind with a written stipulation, route the second kind to the dean as a question of what our program should mean by a term, and reserve the formal process for what is genuinely substantive.

3. A place in the procedure where it concludes that the parties' usages are each internally coherent and each anchored in a legitimate tradition, so that there is no fact of the matter about which is correct, and where the honest output is coordination rather than adjudication — agree on a term, disambiguate, or agree to stop using the word at each other. Include, too, the option of proposing a new or sharpened term when neither existing usage will do.

4. The points at which the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person, because what remains is a question of values rather than of fact, or because the case is one where I cannot tell whether the dispute is verbal and where the conservative default is therefore to treat it as substantive.

5. A guard against the obvious abuse: the diagnosis of a dispute as merely verbal can be used to silence a party who has a real grievance, by relabeling their complaint as a confusion about words. The procedure should make that move difficult, and should say what evidence would show that a party is misusing the diagnosis.

Then run the procedure on two cases and report the output for each, including the answer I will not like.

- Case one: two faculty disagree over whether a particular course satisfies our requirement that students take a theory course. One holds that theory means conceptual and foundational work; the other that it means formal or mathematical modeling. They agree completely about what the course contains and about what students would learn in it.

- Case two: a faculty member complains that a colleague's failure to cite a body of prior work in a published article amounts to academic misconduct. The complainant means by misconduct a serious breach warranting institutional sanction; the respondent means by it merely a lapse of scholarly care. They agree about what was and was not cited.

Finish by telling me plainly what this procedure cannot do, and which of the two cases above it handles badly.
