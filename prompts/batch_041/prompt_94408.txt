Construct a thought experiment for a graduate seminar on Moore's paradox: a case engineered to drive a wedge between the two leading families of explanation of why a sentence of the omissive Moorean form — one that conjoins a proposition with the speaker's disavowal of belief in that proposition, as in a sentence to the effect that it is raining but the speaker does not believe that it is raining — is absurd.

For the purposes of the case, take the two families to be the following.

The act account. The absurdity is a defect of assertion. To assert that p is, among other things, to represent oneself as believing that p, or to be bound by the norm that one ought to assert only what one believes. A speaker who asserts the omissive sentence flouts that norm, and the absurdity is generated at the level of the speech act rather than at the level of content. The account therefore predicts that the absurdity is absent whenever nothing is asserted — when the sentence is merely entertained, supposed, quoted, or read.

The transparency account. A first-person belief ascription is not made by introspection but by looking outward: one answers whether one believes that p by considering whether p, and answering yes just when one would answer yes to whether p is true. Because the first-person ascription and the assertion of p are issued from the same world-directed stance, the omissive sentence forces the thinker to occupy and abandon that stance at once. The absurdity is thus located in the first-personal state rather than in any norm of assertion, and is predicted to be present wherever the proposition is first-personally judged, whether or not anything is asserted.

Your task is to construct a case in which the two accounts give opposite verdicts, in the direction that is hard for the act account: the omissive proposition must be absurd in a purely non-assertoric, first-personal episode, with no assertion, no audience, and no communication of any kind. The case must satisfy all of the following constraints.

- The subject is a competent, sincere, rational adult language-user who understands the sentence perfectly; there is no self-deception, no confusion about their own mind, and no independent irrationality.
- The subject never asserts, utters, writes, or communicates anything, and is not engaged in inner speech that functions as assertion or self-persuasion; the episode is to be a bare first-personal occurrence of the thought or judgement.
- The second conjunct of the proposition is true of the subject: they really do not believe that p.
- The setting is ordinary and human-scale; no brain in a vat, no exotic technology, no science fiction.
- The absurdity must manifest in something the subject is required to do with the proposition — for instance, in an inability to take it up as the content of a sincere bet or as something to be endorsed in deliberation — rather than being merely reported as a feeling.

Then, in order:

1. Present the scenario in no more than one paragraph.
2. State the verdict each account is forced to give about whether the episode is absurd, and the reasoning that produces that verdict.
3. Identify the single feature of the case that makes the verdicts diverge, and explain why that feature, rather than some other, is doing the work.
4. Run the removal test: say what happens to each verdict once that feature is removed.
5. Say how a defender of the act account would attack the case, and whether the attack succeeds.
6. Close with an honest assessment of whether the case is a genuine instance of Moorean absurdity or only an instance of something weaker, such as mere falsity or practical impossibility, and what turns on the difference.
