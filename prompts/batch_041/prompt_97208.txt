I'm putting together a one-page handout for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mind, and I want to force a comparison that the standard textbook treatments of pain tend to skip. The phenomenon I want the handout organized around is pain asymbolia: patients who, typically after damage to the insula and nearby cortex, report that a stimulus hurts, can localize it and even rate it as intense, and yet are not distressed by it — they do not withdraw, do not describe it as unpleasant, and some say the pain is present but that they do not mind it. What interests me is what this does to accounts of why pain is bad in the first place. Please rank the following six accounts of pain's badness from most to least able to accommodate asymbolia, and give each a one-sentence justification: (a) pain is bad because of its intrinsic sensory quality, for instance its intensity; (b) pain is bad because of its unpleasantness or affective charge; (c) pain is bad because it thwarts a desire to avoid or end it; (d) pain is bad because it represents the body as harmed or in danger; (e) pain is bad because it issues an imperative to stop or escape; (f) pain is bad in a way that is independent of any of these responses, an objective badness attaching to the state itself.

I would also like a second and independent ranking of the same six accounts by how plausible you find them as an account of ordinary pain, so that the seminar can see whether the best account of asymbolia and the best account of ordinary pain turn out to be the same one. Then, as a third and final item, tell me which single account, if any, comes out at the top of both rankings, and whether on that account the asymbolic patient's pain still counts as bad for them — that is, whether it damages their welfare even though they are untroubled by it. Please keep the entire response under 600 words, and give each ranking as an ordered list with a one-sentence rationale per entry rather than as continuous prose.
