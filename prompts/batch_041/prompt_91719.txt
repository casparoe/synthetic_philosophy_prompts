I'm a staff writer on a science-and-ideas desk, and I'm working up a review-essay on the recent crop of trade books that promise to teach general readers how to think like a decision theorist. One of them opens with a chapter-length summary of the argument that rationality requires maximizing expected utility, and the more I stare at it, the more I suspect it is doing something slippery — the kind of passage that reads as authoritative to anyone who hasn't done the technical work. I'd like a careful audit before I commit to a line in print. Here is the passage, near-verbatim from the introduction: "Decision theory has an actual proof that you ought to maximize expected utility. It starts from a handful of innocuous consistency requirements: your preferences must be complete and transitive, you must treat equivalent descriptions of the same gamble alike, and the order in which options are presented must not change your mind. Savage showed that anyone who obeys these rules can be represented as holding a utility function and a set of subjective probabilities, and that her choices will then look exactly like those of someone maximizing expected utility. The proof cuts both ways: violate any one of the axioms and you are 'incoherent' in the technical sense, and incoherence is expensive. An incoherent agent can be made to accept a sequence of trades that leaves her with less money no matter how the world turns out — which is why the theory is normative, and not merely a description of how people happen to behave. The same machinery tells you how to learn: a coherent agent's beliefs must be her prior probabilities conditionalized on the evidence, so Bayesian updating comes free with the package. The practical moral is that if you are not maximizing expected utility, someone — a bookie, a market, a rival — can take your money; and the theorem guarantees that each person has a single best utility function, fixed entirely by her preferences."

What I need is an accuracy audit I can actually use. Take the summary clause by clause and sort it into three piles: what a decision theorist working on these foundations would sign off on, what is a defensible popularization that a specialist would still want flagged, and what is simply wrong or unsupported. I am particularly unsure about the claim that the money-pump argument covers every violation of the axioms — I have seen it deployed against intransitive preferences and find that use convincing, but I don't know whether it stretches to the completeness axiom, or to the independence-style axioms, and if it doesn't, I want to know exactly where the gap opens. Likewise, I would like to know whether the learning claim is part of the same result or a separate theorem with its own premises, and whether "incoherent" is being used here in its technical sense or in a looser everyday one.

Finally — and this is the sentence my editor is most likely to keep, so I want it right — tell me whether the closing conclusion, that rationality requires expected utility maximization and that this licenses talk of a single best utility function for each person, actually follows from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, or whether it depends on premises the author has quietly left out. I am happy to be told the passage is fairer than I think; I would just rather know which sentences are load-bearing and which are decoration.
