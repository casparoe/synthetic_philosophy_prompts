I serve as rapporteur to the Designation Committee of the Vellmark Memorial Foundation, the body that decides which historical atrocities are formally characterized as genocide in our permanent exhibition and in the public register we maintain. Our charter defines the crime in the terms the Genocide Convention uses: certain listed acts, committed with the intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part, and only a group of that kind. The preamble credits Lemkin, and my understanding is that the definition he originally proposed also reached political and social groups and treated the deliberate destruction of a group's culture as part of the crime, and that both of those elements were dropped while the convention was being drafted. The committee must now rule on the Nathari question, and I am to draft the finding.

What the record establishes is roughly this. The Nathari are about 1.4 million people, concentrated in one upland province, distinct from the surrounding majority in religion, language, and self-description. Over roughly three years a militia backed by the central government destroyed almost every Nathari shrine, seminary, and manuscript library in the province; forcibly converted or expelled some 700,000 people; and killed about 4,000, mostly men, in scattered massacres. Militia leaders repeatedly described the Nathari as a foreign sect that had to be removed from the province and their places of worship erased, while denying any intention of killing them and stressing that those who left were free to settle elsewhere. The government adds that a faction of the Nathari maintains an armed wing that has fought the militia, and that the campaign has always been directed at that insurgency rather than at the community.

Our precedents, which I am bound by:

- The campaign against the Aldari: some 120,000 killed out of 900,000, with recorded statements by commanders that the Aldari could not remain in the region. Designated.
- The purge of the Builders' Union: some 60,000 killed, with clear evidence that the aim was to destroy the movement as a political force, families included. Not designated; the reasoning was that the charter's list does not reach groups defined by political affiliation, however monstrous the killing, and that the events fall under other headings.
- The killings in the Marrow district: about 7,000 men of military age, confined to a single district, with intent to destroy the group there. Designated; the committee held that a group may be destroyed in part, and that the part may be defined by geography.
- The erasure of the Sennic: shrines, schools, and manuscripts destroyed, forced conversion, large-scale displacement, but relatively few killings. Not designated; destruction of a group's culture, absent intent to destroy the group physically or biologically, was held to be persecution rather than genocide.
- The Ostval campaign: no statements of intent available at all; intent was inferred from the sustained and systematic pattern of the acts. Designated.
- The Tarrow war: some 200,000 killed, but the committee found the aim was to seize territory and drive the population out, with the group's identity incidental to who was in the way. Not designated; characterized as ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity.

I would like the finding to do several things at once.

First, tell me which rules the record above is jointly consistent with — the committee's past decisions should not settle on a single rule, and I want to know the candidates, including the ones I have probably not noticed.

Second, say what each candidate rule predicts for the Nathari, give me your best guess at the verdict and the reasons for it, and identify which precedent is the closest analogue.

Third, name one further case, real or hypothetical, that would do the most to tell the leading rules apart, and explain why it discriminates.

Fourth, state plainly what principle the committee adopts if it designates the Nathari events as genocide, and what it adopts if it declines; I want the choice described in terms of the commitments it carries, not just the outcome.

Fifth, and separately from all of that: how much should the consequences figure in the committee's deliberation? The government has said it will leave the negotiations if the label is applied; survivors and their organizations have said that anything short of the label is a second abandonment; and our register is cited in later legal and political proceedings. If the facts do not meet the definition, is there a moral difference between saying so and finding language that names the harm without the word, and is a refusal to apply the word ever wrong in itself, independently of whether the definition is satisfied?

Finally, tell me whether the disagreement here is really conceptual, about what the definition means, or empirical, about what happened, and whether that distinction should change how I write the finding.
