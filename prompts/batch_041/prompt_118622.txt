For years now, whenever the argument turns to how fast takeoff would go, the same comparison does the heavy lifting: an intelligence explosion is supposed to be like a nuclear chain reaction. You cross some threshold, and past that point the process sustains and accelerates itself, running to completion before anyone can react. The comparison is used to license a specific conclusion: that the transition is essentially a single irreversible event, that there is no real window in which outsiders could intervene, and that anyone reasoning in terms of gradual policy responses has misunderstood the physics of the situation. I am not asking you to settle whether superintelligence arrives. I want the analogy audited.

Start with the correspondence table, as literal as you can make it: what plays the role of the fissile fuel, the neutron, the multiplication factor, the moderator, the control rods, the reflector, and the critical mass, and, most importantly, what distinguishes a reactor from a bomb. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I especially want to know whether anything in the target plays the role of delayed neutrons. In a reactor, the fact that a small fraction of the neutrons arrive seconds late, from decaying fission products, is what makes the reaction controllable at all on human timescales; strip that out and you get prompt criticality, a runaway far too fast for anyone to respond to. Is there a structural lag in AI development that keeps the process in the controllable regime, or does the analogy, read strictly, imply that the interesting cases are prompt-critical by construction and therefore unsteerable? Second, a chain reaction is a stock-limited process: the fuel is finite, the reaction ends when it is spent, and the energy released per event is fixed by physics. Improvement in capability does not obviously consume a conserved quantity or saturate at a constant, and that looks like a disanalogy that bears directly on the no-second-chances conclusion, though I want you to tell me whether it actually does or whether I am overreading it.

Finally, sort the disanalogies into the ones that carry argumentative weight and the ones that are merely decorative, and say what the comparison can actually support. Which claims about takeoff speed and the size of the intervention window survive the audit, which were being carried by the imagery rather than by the structure, and is there a better comparison for the same target? If the honest result is that the analogy supports a much weaker conclusion than the people wielding it want, say that plainly rather than splitting the difference.
