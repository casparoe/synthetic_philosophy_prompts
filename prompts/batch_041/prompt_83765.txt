I have been in a free-speech reading group for two years, and the same argument keeps recurring without either side moving. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of it. Four positions are actually on the table. The first holds that the canonical liberal standard — coercion, whether by law or by social pressure, is legitimate only in order to prevent harm to others — is correct, and that the real work lies in deciding which things count as harms. The second holds that this standard is empty: read one way, 'harm' covers any setback to anyone's interests, in which case nearly every act, and every act of suppression, qualifies and the principle issues no verdicts; read the other way, 'harm' means wrongful setback, in which case the principle presupposes the moral theory it was supposed to constrain. The third position is practical rather than meta: some hold that certain speech does ordinary causal damage and that the principle therefore licenses restriction, while others hold that suppression does damage of the same order and that the principle must therefore be a presumption to be weighed rather than a threshold to be crossed. The fourth holds that all of this is displaced empirical disagreement about what speech actually does to people, and that the rest is anecdote.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that most of the apparent conflict is generated by two independent ambiguities — moralized versus non-moralized 'harm', and threshold versus weighing readings of the principle — compounded by a third axis the group never separates: whether the principle governs what the state may do, what individuals may do to one another, or what public opinion may exact. On this hypothesis the two main camps are frequently defending different readings rather than different moral views, and what remains once the readings are fixed is a genuinely empirical question about effects.

Please test that. Locate each position described above in the space defined by those three axes, say which combinations are actually occupied, and diagnose any empty combination — is that view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Then tell me what kind of disagreement survives disambiguation: verbal, two different questions under one name, a difference about which cases count as data, a difference about what counts as a reason, or a disguised empirical dispute — and which of these dominates. Use three cases to force the issue: one candidate for harm without wronging, one for wronging without harm, and one candidate for a purely self-regarding act, saying what each camp must say about each and whether the group's inability to agree on the cases is itself diagnostic. Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — and should the persistence of this debate be read as a sign that the question is ill-posed? If my hypothesis fails, say where it fails and what should replace it.
