I've been working through the first two books of Plato's Laws — the stretch in which the Athenian Stranger, over the objections of his Spartan and Cretan companions, argues that a well-ordered city should license drinking parties and treat them as an instrument of civic education — and I've become convinced that the proposal has never received the administrative afterlife it deserves. So I'd like you to write the founding document of that institution as it would exist now. Take the role of a philosopher who knows the text closely and also has an ear for bureaucratic deadpan; think carefully about which provision follows from which argument before you begin, and sketch a short outline for yourself before drafting, because I want the clauses to map onto the reasoning rather than onto a general impression of Greek drinking. The document must never cite Plato, the Laws, or any ancient author, and must not name a real modern state or agency: it should read as contemporary administrative prose issued by a placeless municipal authority — something like an Office of the Sober President, or whatever name better fits the register you settle on.

Here is the raw material to work from, so that nothing has to be guessed at. Wine is a drug for boldness: it manufactures confidence and fearlessness, and no drug does the opposite work for fear. It magnifies pleasure, pain, anger and desire while dismantling perception, memory, judgement and understanding, so that the drinker's soul reverts to the condition it had when he was a small child — which is why the drinker is least able to command himself. The drinker's soul is therefore like iron in a fire: softer than usual, more plastic, and so ready to be moulded; the moulder is the legislator. Character can be tested in other ways, in contracts and partnerships or in war, but those tests stake money and life; the test in wine, and in play, is cheap and harmless, and does double duty, giving both experience of the passions and practice in resisting them. A drinking party therefore needs a sober and wise presiding officer set over the drunk — a drunken pilot at the helm is a wreck in waiting — whose task is to keep the peace and send the drinkers away better friends than they arrived. The proceedings should keep an order of silence, speech, drinking and song, with music and chorus. Discipline is by shame rather than force: the base boldness that wine produces is to be met with the finest fear, which the argument calls modesty, a divine fear; and disobeying the sober leaders of the party should carry shame equal to or greater than disobeying the officers of Ares. There are age rules: no wine at all before eighteen, since one does not pour fire onto fire; wine in moderation between eighteen and thirty; no drunkenness whatever for the young; and at forty, at the common meals, one may call on Dionysus for the rite and play of the elders. Wine, finally, was given as a remedy for the harshness of old age, so that we grow young again and forget our ill humour — and those over sixty are the natural leaders of the Dionysian party.

Write roughly 750 to 900 words in the form of an official handbook or operating code: numbered sections, headings, an eligibility schedule, a section of contraindications, and a compliance or penalty provision. The target of the satire is the practice of state-engineered character formation — the idea that a government may require its citizens to be tested for virtue under supervision and graded on their conduct while under the influence. All of the humour should come from the provisions themselves; never wink, never editorialise, never announce that anything is troubling. Let the objections surface by themselves, so that a reader feels at once what is attractive in the scheme — wine as medicine for age, a cheap and safe way of learning one's own limits, a community that trains self-command instead of merely preaching it — and what is alarming — tiered eligibility, shame as the enforcement mechanism, an officer empowered to decide whether the test has been passed. Include at least one provision whose logic visibly strains, such as the liability of the Sober President himself, the retake and expiry rules for certification, or what the test is for once it has been passed, and do not flag it as a joke. A reader who knows the Laws should find that every provision is a fair reading of the argument; a reader who has never heard of it should still be able to tell what is being ironised. No citations, no bibliographies, no names of real scholars. After the handbook ends, on a separate final line, give a one-sentence summary beginning "Verdict:" that names what is being satirised and the Platonic idea the piece turns on.
