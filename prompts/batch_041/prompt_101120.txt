I have recently become involved with a small community group that is drafting a proposal for a local mutual-credit currency, and in preparation one of the more bookish members circulated a short précis of Aristotle's views on money and exchange. The précis has since been quoted in our discussions as though it settled the matter, and I would like to know how much of it I should trust before I let it shape my own position. Here it is as it was circulated:

"Aristotle held that money is a purely artificial convention with no value of its own, and that because money is barren — it cannot breed — any attempt to make it grow is contrary to nature. Lending at interest is therefore the most unnatural of all economic activities, and since trade is simply a way of making money grow, Aristotle condemned commerce and moneylending alike as corruptions of the natural household economy. In his account of exchange he holds that the goods exchanged must be equal in value, and that money is what measures that value and thereby makes exchange possible. His authority is the reason the Church condemned usury for a thousand years. The lesson for us is that Aristotle's position is untenable, since trade plainly creates real value and any modern economy would collapse without interest; his economic views are simply the prejudices of a pre-market society."

My instinct is that this is not entirely fair to Aristotle, but I cannot tell where the line falls between what he actually held, what is a defensible reading of him, and what is a distortion. Please go through the précis and tell me, claim by claim, what a specialist in ancient philosophy would accept, what they would regard as a common simplification, and what Aristotle explicitly denied or would have rejected. Then tell me whether the précis's concluding verdict — that Aristotle's economic views can be dismissed — follows from anything he actually held, or whether it is a standard objection dressed up as a refutation.

Because I intend to use your answer to argue a point in front of people who will push back, I would ask you to be careful about the status of each claim: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
