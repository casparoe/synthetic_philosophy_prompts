I am trying to get my own position on satisficing and bounded rationality into presentable shape before a seminar, and what I want from you is an audit of the package rather than an exposition of the literature. Here is roughly where I have landed. First, satisficing is a structurally different kind of decision procedure from optimizing, and not merely optimizing with search costs folded into the objective function: what distinguishes it is that search is terminated by a stopping rule that consults an aspiration level, and that the aspiration level is a substantive part of the theory rather than a technical convenience. Second, the standard by which a heuristic should be judged is ecological, in the sense that a heuristic is rational when it fits the structure of the environment it is used in, and not because it satisfies internal axioms of consistency or completeness. Third, I think it is legitimate, and frequently the best available explanation of behaviour, to describe an agent as maximizing something, as long as that description is understood as as-if and carries no claim about the computations the agent actually performs. Fourth, I think aspiration levels are endogenous: they drift upward when outcomes exceed them and downward when outcomes fall short, so the threshold an agent uses at a given moment is the residue of its history rather than a free parameter the theorist sets. Fifth, I take bounded rationality to be a normative programme as well as a descriptive one, in the sense that it ought to be able to tell an agent with limited time and computational power what it would be rational for it to do, not only to predict what it will do. Sixth, a verdict I would like to keep: in a one-shot, high-stakes choice where the option set is small, enumerable and cheap to survey, optimizing is what one should do, and satisficing in that setting is a failure of nerve rather than a strategy.

I have begun to suspect a pattern that I have been calling the threshold treadmill. Because the aspiration level is updated on realized outcomes, and because clearing a threshold tends to raise it, a satisficer in a stable environment may find its stopping rule retreating ahead of it, so that the procedure ends up recommending more search than an optimizer would have undertaken, which is the opposite of the economy that satisficing was supposed to buy. I do not know whether that is a real consequence of the commitments above or an artifact of the way I have stated them, and I would rather be told than guess.

So please go through the six and tell me which are genuinely in tension with which, and how badly. I expect some pairs to look inconsistent and turn out not to be; say so explicitly when that happens, and say what is doing the work in the dissolution. For each genuine tension, identify what would have to give, or what a reformulation would cost, and rank the tensions by severity instead of treating them as equally bad. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what that revision costs me elsewhere, in particular whether it leaves the normative half of the position intact or collapses it into description. Finally, tell me whether the threshold treadmill is entailed by the package or is a mistake in it.

Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, boldface or tables, the way you would write a letter to a colleague; where I have asked for a ranking, do it in the flow of the sentences.
