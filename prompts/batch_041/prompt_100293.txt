Once a month I run a kinda low-key "weird ideas lab" for about a dozen adults -- mostly rationalist-adjacent folks, a few with CS or math backgrounds, most without -- 90 minutes, one room, two whiteboards, a big pile of index cards. No laptops or phones allowed, so whatever I run has to work with paper and hand-simulation.

I want to do a session on typogenetics, the toy genetics from Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. The setup: strands over the four bases; the strand gets read in non-overlapping pairs and each pair maps through a fixed lookup table to an amino acid; each amino acid is a command (stuff like cut the strand here, delete a base, switch into copy mode, insert a particular base); a gene is the stretch coding for one enzyme, ended by a punctuation pair; an enzyme is just the sequence of amino acids, it binds somewhere on a strand and runs its commands. The one wrinkle that does all the work: copy mode never gives you a literal copy, it gives you the complementary strand, so getting back to the original takes two trips around. Hofstadter's puzzle is to find a strand that, when its own enzymes are let loose on it, eventually yields a copy of itself.

I dont want to photocopy the chapter and I definately dont want to open with a 20-minute lecture. Design me the whole activity. Specifically: the rule card you'd hand out (keep the amino acid table small enough that people can hold it in their heads), the materials, how you'd split the room into teams, what they're physically doing each minute, and a scoring scheme with small stakes and no real money that makes landing a self-rep feel like a win without rewarding the cheap fake solution. I'd love a twist where, after somebody succeeds, what they thought they'd built turns out to be partly borrowed from the room. Then the debrief: strands as data and program at once, the self-rep as a quine and as the fixed-point move behind Gödel, and why the round trip through the complement is the same trick as a system managing to talk about its own code.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this -- teams brute-forcing, someone finishing in ten minutes while everyone else is stuck, a team that "wins" by fudging where enzymes bind -- and how to handle each. And be frank about what the activity can't reproduce. I know the ribosome that reads strands into enzymes is not itself an enzyme inside the system, so the group is standing in for a machine that real DNA has to build for itself; tell me what that costs the lesson, and what else the game can't show. Skip the reading list.
