I'm a second-year PhD student working on collective action problems — climate stuff mostly, but also diet and voting — and there's a shape of argument I keep running into that I can't put a name to. Lately I've started to think it might actually be two shapes rather than one, and I can't tell if I'm onto something or just making a distinction without a difference.

Here's the first shape. Someone says: look, one flight, one burger, one vote — each of these is a drop in the bucket. The harm (warming past some line, the animals, the election outcome) only happens if *enough* people do it. And whether my drop is the one that tips it over is completly arbitrary — it's not like I can point at some particular bit of damage and say 'that's mine.' So the conclusion is: I can't be blamed, because my act doesn't stand in the right causal relation to any determinate harm.

Here's the second, which is the one actually bugging me. Someone says: fine, but the harm is going to happen anyway — enough other people are already doing it that my doing it or not doing it changes nothing at all. So there's no point in me stopping, and no blame either. The causal story here isn't 'my drop might be the tipping one'; it's 'the bucket is already overflowing regardless of me.'

Both get called the same thing in conversation ('my contribution doesn't matter'), and both usually get filed under the inefficacy objection. But they look structurally diffrent to me. In the first, the individual act *does* matter causally and the problem is that we can't trace it to a determinate harm; in the second, the act genuinely makes no difference because it's redundant. And the moral upshots seem to diverge too — in the first there's a live question of how to distribute responsibility across the group, whereas in the second the group's harm is doing all the work and the individual is just sort of along for the ride.

Is there an established name (or two names) for this? I know the inefficacy objection, and I know people say 'many hands,' and I've seen talk of threshold effects, imperceptible contributions, overdetermination — but I can't tell which of those is the one I want, or whether the thing I'm circling is just the inefficacy objection under a different description. What I really need is the carving, not a label: what exactly is the first pattern, what exactly is the second, are they one thing or two, and where does each one stop? E.g. does the first survive if the threshold is sharp and known, so there really is a determinate tipping act? And does the second just collapse into ordinary overdetermination, or into something else entirely — or into nothing, i.e. is it actually a confused version of the first? If nothing established fits either one, I'd rather you coin terms with real definitions and tell me the boundary cases than hand me a near-miss.
