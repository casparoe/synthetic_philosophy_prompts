Ok, I'm not an academic at all, I just read a lot of blogs and somehow I've ended up writing one question for the problem set of a course my housemate teaches on how groups of people make decisions. The topic is information cascades -- you know, the ones where everyone behaves perfectly rationally and yet they all end up copying whoever went before them. Every textbook uses the same jar of marbles thing where each person is right about the jar 2/3 of the time, and the punchline is always that once two people in a row say the same thing, the third person just goes along with them even if their own marble points the other way. My housemate says the students memorise that punchline and can't actually redo the reasoning or see what it quietly depends on. So I want a question built on the same jar setup but with a twist that stops them reciting it.

The twist I want: keep the jar and the 2/3 accuracy, but make the first two people get there evidence from the same source -- say they both saw the same viral post, or the same mate told them both -- so their two observations aren't really two independent bits of evidence, they're closer to one. I want the students to work out what happens to the cascade once you break that independence assumption, and I also want a part where they have to explain why a cascade is fragile and can lock in on the wrong answer even though nobody did anything irrational.

Can you write the actual exam question with a few parts, then a model answer, and a marking scheme that spells out which specific moves separate a strong answer from one that's just remembered the marbles? Say 20 marks, aimed at final-year undergrads. No references needed, just the question, the answer and the marks -- and keep it self-contained enough that a clever student who never took the course could still do it.
