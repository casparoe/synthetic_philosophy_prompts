In 1980 the biologist Paul Ehrlich and the economist Julian Simon formalized a ten-year wager. Ehrlich and two colleagues named five industrial metals — chromium, copper, nickel, tin, and tungsten — and bought, on paper, $200 worth of each at September 1980 prices, for a $1,000 stake. Simon took the other side, betting that the inflation-adjusted price of that basket would be lower in September 1990. All five metals fell in real terms, and Ehrlich paid up. The episode is usually filed away as a refutation of neo-Malthusian alarm, of the sort found in Ehrlich's own work of the late 1960s, but I want it treated as a test of Malthusian prediction, and as an occasion to interrogate the idea that a theory can be right but early. Argue the case rather than surveying the literature; I have no use for a reading list.

First, decompose the thing being tested. Malthusianism is not one claim but at least four: a mechanism (population expands toward the margin of subsistence, and returns to a fixed factor diminish); a timing claim about when the mechanism bites; a taxonomy of the checks that hold population down; and a policy prescription. Say which of these the wager actually tests, which it leaves untouched, and whether the price of five specific metals is even a proxy for the mechanism. Attend to the mismatch of granularity: an aggregate, long-horizon, distributional claim about tail risk was settled by a single binary comparison of mean prices on two dates.

Second, severity. Ask what makes a test severe, and whether this one qualifies. Simon let Ehrlich pick both the commodities and the date, the wager specified no model linking scarcity to prices, and the outcome is notoriously sensitive to the choice of base year — the same basket compared across other windows in the twentieth century would often have gone the other way. Does a design like that license the inference that the mechanism was refuted, or only that two men disagreed about the near-term price of tin? Be explicit about what a Malthusian would and would not be rationally required to give up on losing.

Third, catalogue the rescue strategies available to a defender, and classify them. At least these: horizon extension (the test was too short); variable substitution (prices track extraction technology, substitution, and market structure rather than depletion, so the test measured the wrong thing); conditionality (the mechanism only bites absent exogenous technological change, so the decade tested the antecedent, not the consequent); and re-specification (the theory was never a claim about a fixed basket). Then sort them using the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem-shifts: which of these moves generate new, independently checkable predictions, and which merely absorb an anomaly while leaving the core intact?

Fourth, the harder case. Malthus's mechanism predicts that rising incomes yield rising population; the demographic transition reverses the sign of that relationship. Work out whether right but early can survive this. Is the theory merely early, or is the mechanism conditionally sign-reversed, in which case it is a conditional claim about pre-transitional regimes and the label right but early is a category error? Distinguish right but early from its neighbours — right but ignored, right for the wrong reason, and wrong but prudentially useful (the expected-value argument that even a small chance of catastrophe justifies action) — and say which, if any, the Malthusian case actually instantiates. Where it helps, compare with the standard cases of premature theories, such as heliocentrism or continental drift, and say whether they are structurally analogous or whether they were rejected for lacking a mechanism rather than for being ahead of their evidence.

Finally, give a verdict. What would it take for a long-horizon predictive theory to be legitimately retained after a failed dated test, and is there a principled line between patience and unfalsifiability, given that any sufficiently long horizon can be used to immunize any theory? If the honest form of a Malthusian claim is a probability over scenarios rather than a dated prediction, say what the appropriate test of such a claim would be — the calibration of the probabilities rather than the outcome of any single wager — and what that implies about how the episode should be read.
