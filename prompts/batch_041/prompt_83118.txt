I'm a software engineer by training and the only non-academic on the five-member review panel of a small foundation that funds independent research, and I've been asked to do two things at once: write up our decision on a proposal we reviewed on Tuesday, and circulate a short memo before the next meeting setting out how the panel should handle a pattern we have now hit twice. The charter says a proposal is funded only if it satisfies all three review criteria — that the question matters, that the work is feasible within eighteen months, and that the design is sound — that members vote on each criterion separately, and that the decision letter state our findings and the reasons for them. Here is the tally I ran afterwards, because something looked wrong. On "the question matters" we were 4–1 in favour; on "feasible within eighteen months" we were 4–1 in favour; on "the design is sound" we were 4–1 in favour. Three members each voted against exactly one criterion and for the other two, and the remaining two members voted for all three. So every criterion commands a majority, while the proposition our funding rule actually turns on — that the proposal satisfies all three — commands two votes out of five. Read one way the proposal must be funded, because the rule funds what meets the criteria and we have just found, criterion by criterion, that it meets them; read the other way it must be refused, because a majority of the panel rejects the claim that it meets all three. My draft letter currently manages to say both, and the applicant, who is a lawyer and has already written to the compliance office, says the letter is arbitrary on its face — which is roughly what our grant terms say about decisions whose stated reasons don't support the outcome.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the other four members and the compliance officer. It should recommend what we record as the decision in this particular case, with a date by which the letter goes out (we owe the applicant one within thirty days, and the appeal window opens when it does), and, more importantly, what standing procedure the panel should adopt so that this stops recurring. Work through at least three options and commit to one: (a) treat the criterion-by-criterion votes as the panel's findings and let the funding rule do the rest, so the proposal is funded; (b) treat the vote on whether all three criteria are met as the panel's judgment, with the criterion votes advisory, so it is not funded; (c) stop recording findings at all and issue outcome-only letters. Option (c) is what a colleague who did graduate work in social choice floated to me as the standard escape, but it collides with the charter and would hollow out the appeal rights, so I want to know what we would be buying. Be genuinely fair to whichever option you reject — the two members who voted against the package are not fools and will read this memo closely; they voted sincerely, criterion by criterion, and they have a real view about what the panel is for. And please don't just tell us to have members vote as though they were voting on the package; I want to know whether that is a fix or the same problem wearing a hat.

Two further things, since I don't have the vocabulary for any of this. First, my colleague called the pattern a discursive dilemma and said there is a theorem to the effect that no rule for combining individual judgments can guarantee a consistent collective result without giving up something we would rather keep; if that is right, say so and then tell me which inconsistency we should learn to live with, and if it is overstated, say what exactly is overstated. Second, I am tempted to describe our situation as a consistency problem of the kind I'd handle in a distributed data store — replicas that agree locally and disagree once you merge them — and I suspect the analogy is doing more harm than good, so correct me if it is. End the memo with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names your recommended option in a short phrase.
