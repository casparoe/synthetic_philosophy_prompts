I host a small long-form interview podcast that sits between economics and philosophy — one guest per episode, usually someone whose work is too technical for the standard venues — and next month's episode is with a philosopher-economist who has spent about a decade arguing that interpersonal comparisons of utility are not a pseudo-problem. Her position, as best I can reconstruct it from her papers, is that if you take cardinal utility derived from choices under risk seriously, and then add the device of asking how a single well-informed observer would rank being each of the people in question, comparisons across persons become as respectable as comparisons within one person, and the traditional ordinalist objection — that any such comparison is merely a smuggled ethical judgment — loses most of its force. She is very polished, and when she is pressed on the residual difficulties she tends to say that they are "a measurement problem, not a conceptual one," and moves on. I am not a specialist; I did a master's in economics and now work in a policy shop, so I need questions that are sharp enough to make that line work harder than it usually has to, but that can still be asked and answered out loud in a conversation rather than in a seminar.

Please draft eight to ten questions for me, in the order I should ask them, with each question followed by a single follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question each on the following, and I would rather you spend the questions here than on setup: first, the zero-point problem — since a cardinal scale is only fixed up to a positive affine transformation, what is supposed to fix the origin across persons, and how do we get genuine comparisons of levels rather than only of differences; second, the single-observer objection — whether asking one observer to rank being person A against being person B delivers a comparison between two people or merely a comparison within one evaluator, and whether preferences over being someone else are even coherent; third, the dependence on expected-utility theory — what happens to the whole apparatus if the representation fails in the ways it is usually alleged to fail, and whether substituting some other cardinal proxy such as hedonic or life-satisfaction measures repairs the view or merely relocates the problem; and fourth, whether the comparisons that come out are determinate enough to carry the distributive weight she wants them to carry, given that some aggregation rules need only level comparability while others need comparability of units, and given that any comparison of two lives seems to presuppose a prior decision about which features of those lives to weigh. I would also like one question that forces her onto a concrete case — a policy that helps many people slightly versus one that helps a few people enormously — because that is where the view gets uncomfortable, and a closing question about what she would regard as evidence or argument against her position.

A few constraints. Assume the guest is a composite rather than a specific living person, so write for the position, not the person, and do not attribute any particular sentence to a named author. Do not ask her to define her terms, do not ask for reading recommendations, and do not ask anything that a summary of the standard welfare-economics curriculum would answer; the point of the episode is the part of her view that the curriculum leaves out. Where a question presupposes a technical distinction, phrase it so that a listener who does not know the distinction can still follow what is at stake in the answer. If any of the follow-ups depend on her giving a particular reply, say in a clause which reply you have in mind, so that I can adapt on the fly if she says something else.
