I'm a high school student in our philosophy club, and I'm trying to understand the dispute between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness before I write an essay on it. Our advisor explained that an epistemicist holds that every vague predicate, such as "heap," has a sharp boundary, but we cannot know where it is; a supervaluationist holds that "This is a heap" is true only if it is true on every admissible precisification, false only if false on every one, and otherwise neither true nor false. To make this concrete, she asked us to imagine a dial that moves the boundary for "heap" one grain at a time, with every setting that respects the clear cases counting as admissible, and she called the whole range of settings the precision space. She also stressed that each individual setting is perfectly sharp. That makes me wonder whether the two views really differ in the way they are usually presented. If every admissible setting draws a sharp line for "heap," and the epistemicist simply singles out one setting as the real one while calling it unknowable, then aren't both views agreed that there is a sharp line in every admissible way of making the predicate precise? The only difference seems to be that the epistemicist says we cannot know which line is correct, whereas the supervaluationist says there is no single correct line to know. But if neither view gives me a knowable answer about the 1,000th grain in a sorites series, isn't the practical upshot the same? When I have to decide whether to call a pile a heap, can I just choose whichever setting in the precision space makes my argument come out valid, since there is no knowable fact to get wrong? Or am I conflating not knowing which sharp line is correct with there being no sharp line that is correct?
