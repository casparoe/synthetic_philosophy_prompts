I've been reading Hume's essay on suicide, the one his friends talked him out of publishing while he was alive, where he argues the act could only be a crime if it violated a duty to God, to our neighbour, or to ourselves, and then takes each of the three apart. Now take Canada's medical assistance in dying regime: first legalized for people near death, then widened to people whose death isn't reasonably foreseeable, with the further widening to people whose sole underlying condition is a mental illness postponed again and again because nobody can agree on what safeguards would make it safe. Run that whole episode through Hume's three-prong structure. Which prongs map cleanly? My sense is the God prong has basically evaporated and the self prong is now doing all the work. But where does the structure break down? Hume waves off the neighbour objection by saying we only owe society the benefits we actually deliver, but a public healthcare system with MAiD built into it doesn't look like a voluntary association of independent contractors, and the mental-illness exclusion seems to rest on doubting the person's own verdict about whether their life is worth living, which Hume's framework has alot of trouble accommodating. Then go the other direction: what does this case show about Hume's argument? Does it vindicate him, or does it reveal that he dismissed the neighbour prong too fast?
