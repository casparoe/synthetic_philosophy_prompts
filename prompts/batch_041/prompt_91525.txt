Last quarter we shipped a probabilistic forecasting component and got burned by something I still can't explain properly. Two engineers specified priors over what I would have called the same unknown quantity: one put a flat prior on the spread of a noise term, the other put a flat prior on the logarithm of that spread. After the data came in they got materially different intervals and, in a few cases, different decisions downstream. When I went digging for the root cause I landed on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference — the circle-and-chord setup where choosing random endpoints, a random radius point, or a random midpoint gives you three different answers to the same question — and I now suspect our disagreement was a small instance of something much larger. I also suspect the standard advice I keep running into, that the paradox is just a matter of specifying your sampling procedure more carefully, is doing less work than people think, because in our case both engineers could state their procedure perfectly and the procedures were both defensible.
