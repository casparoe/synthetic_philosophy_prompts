I sit on a small ethics committee that our city council has asked to draft a short model code for local election conduct, and I keep running up against a line that everyone around the table treats as obvious and that I cannot reconstruct. On one side of the line the prohibitions are severe and uncontroversial: it is a crime to pay someone to cast their ballot a particular way, or to hand a voter money, groceries, a job, or a ride to the polls in exchange for voting as you wish; and paying a legislator to vote your way is bribery. On the other side almost anything goes: it is perfectly lawful to pay canvassers to knock on doors and make the case, to hire consultants to produce advertisements designed to change how people vote, to pay pollsters to learn what would move them, and to spend enormous sums persuading a legislator who cannot legally be paid a dollar for the same vote. So the law is plainly not protecting election outcomes from the influence of money, since money is permitted to do exactly that. It looks as though it protects the vote itself: paying for the vote as such is forbidden, while paying for the production and transmission of reasons for voting is not. What does that distinction presuppose? I am not a philosopher -- my training is in public administration -- but a colleague in the law school tells me this is a version of what philosophers call the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I would like to know whether that is the right diagnosis or whether the practice is doing something else entirely.

I want the implicit theory behind the line reconstructed, and I want the reconstruction to answer several questions. Does the prohibition presuppose that there are considerations that genuinely count in favor of voting one way, alongside other considerations that may move a voter but are not reasons for the vote at all -- or that they are reasons, but of a kind that cannot make a vote correct, fitting, or well-founded? Does it require that a vote be treated as a judgment answerable to how things are, rather than as the expression of a preference or a bare decision, given that on a preference or decision picture there is arguably no such thing as the wrong kind of reason? Or is the distinction doing work that has nothing to do with reasons: protecting the equal standing of citizens, preventing a fiduciary breach by elected officials, securing the epistemic benefits of independent judgment, preserving the expressive meaning of the franchise, or merely following a convention that has outlived whatever rationale it once had?

Please test the reconstruction against the cases our code will actually have to decide: a civic lottery that pays citizens a small sum for turning out but says nothing about how they vote; paying a voter to read a voter guide and attend a candidate forum; paying someone to abstain or to spoil their ballot; an employer who ties a benefit to turning out; and the secret ballot itself, which conceals a voter's reasons from everyone and might therefore presuppose that those reasons do not matter, or might presuppose the opposite, that they matter so much that the voter must be shielded from pressure. Say which parts of the practice strictly require which presupposition and which are merely well explained by it, identify the points at which the practice is underdetermined because several rival theories would license exactly the same line, and tell me what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforwardly aggregative one under which only the effects of money on outcomes matter. Finish by saying what my committee should write if we take the reconstruction seriously, and what we should write if we conclude the line is a historical accident we no longer believe in.
