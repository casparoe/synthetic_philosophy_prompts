So I've been reading H.G. Wells's The Time Machine out loud to my kid at bedtime — she's nine and mostly in it for the Morlocks, which, fair — but I keep tripping over the first chapter, where the Time Traveller lays out his whole thing about time being a fourth dimension. The part I'm stuck on goes:

"There is no difference between Time and any of the three dimensions of Space except that our consciousness moves along it." And then a bit later: "All these are evidently sections, as it were, Three-Dimensional representations of his Four-Dimensioned being, which is a fixed and unalterable thing." And then the bit with the weather chart: "Scientific people," proceeded the Time Traveller, after the pause required for the proper assimilation of this, "know very well that Time is only a kind of Space. Here is a popular scientific diagram, a weather record. This line I trace with my finger shows the movement of the barometer. Yesterday it was so high, yesterday night it fell, then this morning it rose again, and so gently upward to here. Surely the mercury did not trace this line in any of the dimensions of Space generally recognised? But certainly it traced such a line, and that line, therefore, we must conclude, was along the Time-Dimension."

I can sort of follow the shape of what he's doing — he wants time to be just another direction, like length or breadth, so that a person's whole life is a kind of long object with all its stages already in it — but I dont actually see how the barometer thing is supposed to work as an argument. Like, sure, the mercury went up and down over time, so its path traces a curve; how does that show the curve is "along" time the same way a road runs along a north-south line? It feels like he's smuggling something in, and I can't tell if I'm just not getting it or if Wells is being loose on purpose because it's a novel and not a physics lecture.

The reason I care is that I've also been poking at this thing called the block universe, and an arguement for it that I think is due to Rietdijk and Putnam, and I want to know whether Wells's guy is saying the same thing or something else entirely. The way I understand the argument (probably badly): special relativity says whether two distant events count as happening at the same time depends on how you're moving; so for you, some event way off somewhere might be "happening right now," while for me, moving differently, that same event is already over or hasn't happened yet. If you insist that only your own present moment is real, you seem to get a contradiction, since you and I each have a decent claim to a different set of "now" events — and the way out is to say past, present, and future are all equally real, nothing ever really becomes, time just is. So: can you walk me through what the Wells passage is actually claiming, step by step, and then tell me whether it's the same claim as the block universe one or a different animal? Is the barometer argument a real argument at all, or is it doing something sneakier than it looks? And does the block universe actually depend on Einstein, or could you get there from Wells's four-dimensional thinking on its own? One more thing while I've got you: what's supposed to be wrong with the picture where there's one real "now" that slides forward through time — I keep feeling like that's obviously right and everyone else is overthinking it, and I'd like to know why I'm wrong about that.
