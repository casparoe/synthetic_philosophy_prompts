I'm producing a long-form interview episode for a series on big-picture philosophy, and my next guest is a philosopher who has spent the last several years arguing that we are living through the hinge of history — not as a mood or a rhetorical flourish, but as a claim with determinate content and direct ethical upshot. His version rests on three planks: he defines the hinge as the interval during which the expected causal influence of human action on the long-run trajectory of civilization is maximal; he argues that the present interval qualifies because of a convergence of lever-like technologies, instruments whose effects are not bounded by the next century; and he insists the view is no mere generational vanity, since it concerns variance in influence rather than the importance of the people who happen to be alive.

I don't want him to spend the hour restating any of that. Please draft eight to ten questions that make him work, with a follow-up attached to each question, keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. At least one question must press each of the following, and I'd like the pressure to come from the actual joints of his position rather than from generic skepticism. On the definition: how expected influence is supposed to be made determinate when the comparison class — the other intervals of history, most of them still in the future — is unavailable, and whether the definition quietly assumes the conclusion. He will probably say we can estimate variance from what we know about the technologies; press him with what I think of as the backward hinge test, by which I mean asking him to run the same metric over earlier candidate hinges — the shift to agriculture, the industrial revolution, the arrival of nuclear weapons — and to say what the present has that they lacked, without leaning on hindsight about how those episodes turned out. On the self-serving structure: whether a belief that we sit at the hinge is systematically insulated from disconfirmation, both because it licenses extraordinary measures that ordinary eras would not authorize and because every era that has held it could tell the same story; his reply will likely be the variance point again, so the follow-up should force him to distinguish the claim that influence is unusually high from the claim that our uncertainty about the future is unusually high, since those come apart and only one of them flatters us. On the practical upshot: whether the hypothesis does any independent work beyond the general case for longtermism — whether it reorders our options or merely raises the stakes on options we already had.

I'd also like you to build two of the questions around a pair of ideas I want to put to him, because I think they will stretch him. The first is a thought experiment: a civilization that genuinely sits at a hinge but has no epistemic access to that fact, no way of knowing its own pivotal position — does the hypothesis have any first-person grip on how such a civilization should reason, or is it a claim that only becomes action-guiding for those who already believe it, which would make it oddly self-referential? The second is a distinction I want him either to accept or to reject: between the hinge as a fact about the world and the hinge as a fact about our evidence, on the thought that these can diverge, and that much of the force of the idea seems to trade on running them together. Finally, close with a question about what evidence could count against his view, and make the follow-up press on whether any imaginable observation could, given how he has set the view up.

Write the questions out in continuous prose — no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold text, no tables — the way one would lay them out in a long email to a producer, so that each question and its follow-up flow into the next. Where a question depends on a technical distinction, state the distinction plainly enough that the guest and the audience both know what is being asked.
