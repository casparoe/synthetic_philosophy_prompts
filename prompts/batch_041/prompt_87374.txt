Write a satirical piece in the form of an official public notice from a fictional regulator — an Office of Nomological Affairs — announcing that the jurisdiction has adopted a Humean, best-system account of laws of nature as its official metaphysics, and answering citizens' questions about what follows. The register is deadpan administrative prose: a brief preamble, numbered sections, cross-references to a schedule, and a closing appendix that reproduces two or three comments received during consultation alongside the Office's replies. Around 700–850 words. Invent the agency and the objectors; name no real people, institutions, or texts, since the target is a position and a bureaucratic habit of mind, not an individual.

The comedy must be load-bearing, so it should come out of the position rather than out of the furniture. Two constraints govern it.

First, the Office is Humean on every line. It may not say or imply that a regularity produces, forces, compels, or is responsible for the events that fall under it; it may not treat a law as something that can be obeyed, broken, waived, or enforced; and it may not quietly restore what the Humean picture gives up. Where the governing picture would say that a law explains its instances, supports counterfactuals, or makes them necessary, the Office has to say what it thinks is actually happening instead — and it must not pretend the difference is merely verbal.

Second, the objectors must be granted the strongest version of the anti-Humean case, and it must be a case an anti-Humean could really make: laws necessitate their instances, they explain rather than summarize, they are discovered rather than chosen, and the best-system account leaves the mosaic without genuine modality while making the laws hostage to our standards of simplicity. The Office should respond to this the way a regulator responds to anything unwelcome — reclassification, deferral, a working group — and that evasion, not a punchline, is where the piece should become uncomfortable.

At least three of the answers should engage directly with technical pressure points, and should get them right:

- a regularity with exactly one exception: is it still a law, and who decides?
- regularities stated in predicates that cut the world oddly (a generalization true only of objects examined before some arbitrary date, say): ruled out by principle, or by taste?
- if nothing necessitates anything, what grounds the counterfactual that a struck match would have lit?
- whether anything changes at all for the working scientist, and if the answer is nothing, what the disagreement was ever about.

Handle the modality with care: the Office's own prose will keep reaching for the thought that the laws could have been otherwise, that the mosaic might have differed, and any such slip should either be paraphrased away or flagged. Aim the irony from both directions at once — at the picture of laws as a summary the Office is free to redraft when the fit displeases it, and at the picture of laws as small sovereigns issuing commands to the world — and keep it deadpan, without signalling to the reader that a joke has been made. A reader who knows the debate should find the Humean half accurate and the anti-Humean half fair; a reader who does not should still be able to tell which side is which and what is at stake. By the end, the reader should feel the real question — what, if anything, is lost when laws stop governing — rather than merely having watched a bureaucracy squirm.
