You are a poet and a philosopher of decision theory with a specialist's grasp of expected-value fanaticism. I am assembling a teaching anthology on the topic, and I want a poem that does conceptual work rather than merely illustrating a position. Write a sestina in the voice of a decision-maker who has internalized the expected-value framework and is now being asked to act on it. The case: a hospital ethicist must choose between spending a fixed budget on a certain, modest improvement in the lives of present patients and spending it on a speculative program that offers a one-in-a-googol chance of an infinite good. The expected-value calculation, if the payoff is infinite, favors the program; the poem should feel the full force of that conclusion without turning it into a caricature or a punchline. Use the six end-words probability, value, infinity, ward, ticket, and hand. Let the repetition do the structural work: the poem should begin in the finite, clinical world of the ward, and the same words should be progressively pulled toward the infinite, so that by the envoi the reader sees how the framework can make the present disappear into the expected. The sestina's form, with the same six words returning in a fixed pattern, should itself enact the fanaticism it describes. Do not resolve the tension with comfortable irony or a moralizing final line; let the form carry the argument to its uncomfortable conclusion, or let the speaker resist it from inside the form, but make a definite choice about which and commit to it. After the poem, add a short note of 150 to 250 words in which you state the stance the poem takes and identify where it takes liberties with the decision-theoretic idea or leans on a contested reading, for instance about infinite utilities, the measurability of tiny probabilities, or the aggregation of persons across time. Before drafting, brainstorm briefly about how the sestina's repetition can track the structure of the argument; then write the poem. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the fanaticism literature recognizes the moves, and clear enough that someone who does not can follow the stakes. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts.
