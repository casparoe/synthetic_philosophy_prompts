I am a software engineer who works on model evaluation and infrastructure, and I have no formal training in philosophy, so I would like this treated as a conceptual stress test rather than a literature review. I have come to believe that the debate approach to scalable oversight is basically sound: if two capable models are made to argue opposite sides of a question before a human judge who is less expert than either of them, and if the judge can ask for evidence, code, derivations, or concrete predictions, then the truthful side should have an advantage, because any deceptive argument must survive an adversary who is motivated and able to expose it. The position I want to defend is that this kind of adversarial exchange can extend meaningful human oversight to questions whose answers no human can directly verify, and that it is more promising than relying on a single model's confidence or chain of thought.

Please tell me what would count against that position, sorted by how decisive the defeaters would be rather than as a flat list of concerns. Which assumptions about the judge, the debaters, or the training objective would have to fail, and what would the world look like if each did? Which failures are merely awkward and which would be fatal? What does the position predict that rival oversight schemes do not, and what evidence from actual model behavior, from what can be proved about debate protocols, or from simple game-theoretic reasoning should move me? I am also considering a more specific variant that I have not seen discussed much: the judge is not a single human but a panel of narrow automated checkers, each with a publicly known bias or blind spot, and the debaters must choose which checker to route each claim to; the verdict is a weighted vote, with the weights set in advance by the human overseer. Does this variant inherit the case for debate, or does it introduce new defeaters, for example around checker gaming, collusion between debaters and checkers, or the judge's inability to verify that the right checker was chosen? Finally, I would like an honest assessment of whether the original position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every failure can be redescribed as a failure of the judge, the debaters, or the training setup rather than of the debate idea itself, is that a problem for the view, and what would a genuinely falsifying observation look like? I would personally find a concrete experiment or a theorem most persuasive, but I also want to know what the position's defenders would themselves concede as counting against them, not only what its critics say.
