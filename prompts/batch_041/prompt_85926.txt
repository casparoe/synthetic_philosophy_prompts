I am not an academic—I studied accounting and now work in an office—but I have been reading and listening to a lot of arguments about whether advanced artificial intelligence could pose an existential risk, and I keep running into a slogan that people use as if it settled the matter: smart doesn't mean good. I first noticed it in a long podcast debate, where one speaker used it to knock down the cheerful thought that a sufficiently intelligent machine would automatically be wise or benevolent, and then a friend of mine who works in software engineering used the same line to mean something stronger—that a machine could be superintelligent and still be perfectly indifferent to human welfare, or even hostile, because nothing about being good at solving problems tells you what problems to solve. The version of the existential-risk argument I have in mind is the one that says a very capable system could pursue goals that are not ours, and because it is very capable it could be very effective at pursuing them, with catastrophic results for humans who happen to be in the way. Someone else told me that the technical name for the key premise is the orthogonality thesis, and that it is often run together with instrumental convergence, which I understand to be the idea that many different final goals lead to the same intermediate moves, such as staying alive, acquiring resources, and resisting being shut off. I cannot tell whether these are one claim or several, or which of them the argument for existential risk actually needs.

What I want from you is not a defense of one side but a disambiguation. Lay out the precise claims that the slogan could be making—claims about what smart means (raw IQ, general problem-solving, optimization power, prediction, planning), about what good means (benevolence, caring about humans, having values we would endorse, being aligned with human intentions), and about what relation between them is being asserted. Some readings I can imagine: there is no conceptual entailment from intelligence to benevolence; every possible final goal is compatible with every level of intelligence; in actual agents, intelligence and moral motivation are statistically independent; instrumental rationality is goal-neutral, so a smarter agent is better at whatever it wants, including ends we oppose; a system can be internally coherent and effective at pursuing its own goals while being utterly unaligned with human values; and capability and alignment are separate engineering problems that do not solve each other. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and explain why. Then say which readings are actually doing the work in the case for existential risk and which are rhetorical flourishes, and separate the orthogonality claim from instrumental convergence: which is which, and does the x-risk case need both? I do not need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list—I will not be able to check them, and I would rather have the arguments laid out than a list of sources.

Please write the response in American English and organize it as follows: (1) "The slogan and the work it was doing" (100–150 words); (2) "The menu of readings" (the main section, roughly 800–1000 words, with each reading numbered and labeled, each stating the precise claim and giving a verdict of platitude, substantive, or false, plus a sentence or two of justification); (3) "What the x-risk argument actually needs" (250–350 words); (4) "What an x-risk skeptic can grant" (200–250 words); and (5) "Bottom line" (about 100 words, naming the reading I should assume when I hear the slogan and the reading I should challenge).
