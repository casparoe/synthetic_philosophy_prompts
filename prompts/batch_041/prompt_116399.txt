Right, I need a hand with something. I run a fortnightly reading group that's turned into a bit of a zoo — a couple of physicists, a biologist, two philosophers and a historian of science who's convinced the whole topic is a category error. Next term's theme is thought experiments in science and whether they can actually give us knowlege, as opposed to being entertaining ways of dressing up an argument we were having anyway.

What I'd like from you is a decision tree I can hand out as a one-pager that takes someone from a few starting commitments to a named position on how thought experiments work, epistemically speaking. Build it as a flowchart in text: each branch node is a question with two or three answers, phrased so a person can answer it without already knowing where it leads, and each leaf is a position with a name (or 'unnamed', if nobody actually holds it) plus one sentence on the commitment that position has the hardest time defending. Please build it from the questions upwards rather than listing the positions you've heard of and bolting questions onto the back of them — I want to see which combinations of answers are even available before you tell me who's sitting in them.

A few distinctions I'd like to actually do work on the tree, not just get a nod:

- does the thought experiment deliver knowledge from the armchair, or is it really an argument with some premises left tactfully out of sight?
- is the epistemic work done by the content of the scenario, or by the fact that you had to imagine it yourself? I half-believe the second, and I'd like the tree to be able to tell me where that lands me.
- does it tell you what's possible, or what's actual or necessary? I've started thinking of these as unlocking and locking experiments — unlocking ones make you see that something you'd assumed was impossible isn't, locking ones make you see that something you'd assumed was on the cards isn't. Not attached to the labels.
- does the authority come from the scenario being reconstructible as a formal argument a computer could check, or from it being a device that shifts what you're willing to say?
- whether the scenario even has to be physically possible in the first place.

Run it against Maxwell's demon, Newton's bucket, the Heisenberg microscope, Schrödinger's cat and Leibniz's mill. If any of those five won't sit anywhere sensible on the tree, say so instead of shoving it into the nearest leaf.

Two more things. If there's a combination of answers that nobody seems to hold, tell me — and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And if two leaves end up doing the same work, flag them as inseparable rather than pretending the tree tells them apart.

No reading list, ta, I've got more than enough of those. Aim for eight to ten leaves, and British English please, since the room is mostly Brits.
