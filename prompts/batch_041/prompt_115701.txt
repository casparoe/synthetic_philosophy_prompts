I run a third-year seminar on philosophy of psychiatry, 16 students, one 90-minute slot a week, and the week on classification is the one I dread. I stand at the front, I explain the looping effect, they nod, someone says "so it's a self-fulfilling prophecy then", I say no, not quite, and we all shuffle out. Same thing every year. What I want is an activity where they walk into the loop themselves and only find out afterwards that it had a name. So: design it for me.

Constraints and setting. One 90-minute session, 16 students, a normal seminar room with a whiteboard and a printer. No lecture up front — I don't want the words "looping effect" or the name Hacking to pass my lips until the debrief, and ideally the penny drops a beat before I say anything. The students are bright but they've done maybe one week of philosophy of science, so keep the apparatus light.

The mechanism I have in mind, which you can rip up if you've a better one: the students get sorted into two newly invented categories by an instrument I'll call the Ferris–Ackroyd Style Indicator (it's fictional, I'll write the blurb myself, you don't need to know it). The sorting rule is openly arbitrary — I'll use seat order, alternating down the rows — but the students don't know that yet. Each of them then gets a one-page profile of their type. Crucially, both profiles are flattering, vague and Barnum-ish, and each one prescribes a role in the task to come: one type is told it's the "calibrator" who adjusts toward other people's judgements, the other is told it's the "anchor" who holds its position. Then they do a short paired estimation-and-negotiation task in which those two styles actually make a difference to what happens. Then they re-rate themselves. Then — this is the bit I care about — a couple of students acting as the institute's research assistants hand out a *revised* version of the manual, whose criteria list now includes the very behaviours we've just watched them perform. Then we reveal the seat-order sorting and pick the whole thing apart.

What I need from you: the full runnable thing. Rules, materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, the wording or at least the design principles for the two fake profiles, the scoring/observation sheet the research assistants fill in, and the task itself (I want the role to bite, not just be decoration — if the calibrators don't visibly calibrate, the whole session dies). Then the debrief, staged so it goes in the right order: first the eerie moment of recognition, then the behavioural drift, then the revised manual, then the reveal. And map each of those moments onto the piece of Hacking's picture it's standing in for.

Two more things, and I'd rather have honesty than polish on both.

First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it? I'm thinking: someone announces the test is obviously nonsense in the first five minutes and poisons the room; the drift doesn't show up because my task is too anaemic; the reveal lands as "so psychiatry is just made up then" and I spend twenty minutes doing damage control. Tell me which of those you'd bet on, plus whatever I've missed, and what to do in the moment.

Second, be straight about what the activity can't do. I assume it can't reproduce the thing that actually matters about real looping — that it plays out over decades, through institutions, insurers, advocacy groups and people's lives, and that the kinds in question are real and often painful, not seminar toys. Tell me what I have to supply myself, and where I should say so out loud rather than let them over-generalise. A note on the indifferent-kind contrast would help too — I want them to see why quarks don't read their own entry in the manual.
