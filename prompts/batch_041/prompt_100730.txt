I spent 35 years as a taxonomist before I got interested in philosophy, so I keep running into the same old argument wearing new clothes. Heres the one thats been bugging me lately.

Take the Letter Spirit business, the project where a program is meant to design fonts and has to know when a shape is still an 'a' and when it's just a squiggle. Seems to me there are two stories you could tell about what makes an 'a' an 'a'. One is that there's an abstract skeleton underneath it — the bowl, the spine, the tail, and how they hang together — and the letter just *is* that relational structure, with the actual curves and thick and thins being decoration on top. The other is more like what I'm used to from biology: no essence at all, just a central prototype with neighbouring shapes falling away from it by degrees, family resemblance all the way down, the way I used to argue about species.

Compare these two. Which one really handles the two things that need handling — that all a's are recognizably the same letter, and that no two of them are ever the same shape? Then the style part: when a designer makes a whole alphabet in one voice, the a-ness and the b-ness have to move together, and I want to know which account makes that coordination come out naturally and which one just has to assume it. And finally, tell me honestly whether this is my species problem in a new suit or whether a letter is a different kind of beast altogether. I'd rather have a verdict than a both-sides-are-right.
