I am a policy analyst at a state transportation department and also serve on an internal review panel that vets the benefit-cost analyses behind our major projects, and I have been asked to explain to skeptical colleagues why our methodology treats people's actual choices as the measure of what is good for them. The practice is roughly this: we infer the value of travel-time savings from the routes commuters actually take when one route is faster but tolled, we infer the value of safety from the wage premiums workers actually accept for riskier jobs, we convert those inferred values into dollars, and we aggregate them across everyone affected to decide whether a project is worth building. Two newer features complicate the picture. First, we apply equity weights, so a dollar of estimated benefit to a low-income commuter counts for more than a dollar to a high-income commuter. Second, we have a pilot preference-purification protocol under which analysts may set aside a person's revealed preference and substitute the preference the person would have had with better information, more time, or fairer options, on the grounds that some choices are adaptive, manipulated, or made under unjust constraints. My colleagues insist this is all one coherent framework, but I suspect the framework is committed to a particular and perhaps unstable view of what utility is.

Please act as an expert in welfare economics and the philosophy of utility, think carefully, and brainstorm the possible readings before you settle on your answer. I want you to reconstruct the theory of utility and welfare that this practice presupposes. What must be true about preference, choice, well-being, and interpersonal comparison for the core practice to make sense, and which rival accounts of utility, such as hedonic, objective-list, informed-desire, or preference-satisfaction accounts, does it quietly rule out or accommodate? Then tell me whether the equity weights and the purification protocol are consistent with the core commitment or whether they smuggle in a different theory, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it, and finally explain what would have to change in the practice itself if we replaced the presupposed view with a hedonic or objective-list account. Give me your response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
