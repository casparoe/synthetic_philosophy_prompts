I run a monthly reading group — mixed bag, about 14 people, mostly grad students plus a couple of sharp undergrads — and next months session is on the butterfly passage in the Zhuangzi. I've done it twice now as proto-Cartesian skepticism and it's dead on arrival: everyone agrees it could be a dream, nobody feels anything, we're out in twenty minutes. So I want to try something different. Design me a 75-minute activity that actually gets them stuck inside the passage rather than talking about it from the outside. No lecture up front, and I dont want them to hear the words "skepticism" or "the transformation reading" or anything like that until the debrief, because I want them to walk into the wall themselves.

Here's the thing I keep circling. The passage doesn't really ask "how do I know I'm awake?" so much as "which one is dreaming the other?" — and those aren't the same question, and I want them to feel that gap in their bodies, not just nod at it. What I want them to hit, and to hit it by trying and failing to solve it, is that every test they can invent from where they're standing fails to break the symmetry: any check that "proves" you're awake is exactly the kind of thing you'd also do and pass inside a dream, so the test can never privilege one direction over the other. So build the activity around a real, small decision they have to make, one that only feels resolvable if one of the two states is secretly the privileged one. I had a vague idea of handing people paired roles — one person is told they're awake and the other one's the dream, and the other person is told the exact reverse — and then making the pair settle it between them, but you do whatever makes the mechanic work; I'm not attached to that version.

Then the debrief needs to do two things I can't fake with a handout. First, pry apart the reading they'll have built for themselves (doubt, uncertainty, "maybe none of it's real") from the one the passage actually ends on — the transformation one, where the point isn't that you can't know which side is real but that the two are just phases of one another and there's no privileged side to stand on. Second, hit them with a line I can never phrase well, the Wittgenstein-flavoured one: that "I might be dreaming right now" isn't even a coherent thing to be unsure about, because dreaming isn't a state you're in and can inspect while it's happening, it's something you only ever report on the far side of waking. I want them to see that this cuts against the game they just played — the game quietly assumes you can be inside the dream and check — and then to argue about whether that's a cheap trick or a real problem. So give me the rules, roles, and a timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea; an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that; and — this matters — tell me which piece of this an activity simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply as the facilitator.
