For thirty-five years I did experimental work in the physical sciences, and since retiring I have spent an embarrassing amount of time reading philosophy of mathematics with a small reading group. I keep returning to Eugene Wigner's complaint that mathematics proves unreasonably effective in the natural sciences, and to my sense that almost everyone who discusses it — scientists and philosophers alike — runs together several distinct claims. Before I put my question, let me set out the distinctions I have arrived at, since the answer I want depends on them.

First, direction. Sometimes a piece of mathematics developed with no empirical motive whatever later turns out to fit nature; call this import. Sometimes mathematics developed under pressure from physics later becomes a central object of pure mathematics in its own right; call this export. Second, kind of fit. Some formalisms fit because they were built, step by step, to accommodate data we already had — a retrospective fit that verges on tautology — while others dictate structures, relations, or symmetries that no one had gone looking for and which are then found in nature; call the second anticipatory fit. Third, base rates. We remember the hits, and I suspect we also quietly rewrite the history of a successful formalism until its applicability looks as though it had been evident all along.

What I would like from you is the most important implications of the puzzle, understood under those distinctions, for the practice of a working scientist — in particular, for how much weight one should give elegance, unification, and formal beauty when choosing between theories that are otherwise empirically comparable, and for how one should divide one's own effort between chasing formal structure and chasing phenomena. I would also like to know, more briefly, what the puzzle does and does not imply for the institutional case for supporting mathematics that has no application in view. And please tell me plainly which implications people commonly draw that do not follow at all. I am writing a short piece on this for the alumni magazine of my old department, and I want it to be about consequences rather than exegesis.

I am not after a survey of the literature or a reading list — I have no patience for citations I cannot check — and I would rather have an argued answer that states the strongest case against your own conclusions fairly.
