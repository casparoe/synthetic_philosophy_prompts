"Szasz is usually introduced as the man who said that mental illness is a myth. (a) Explain what he actually claimed, and name one thing he is commonly taken to claim but doesn't. (b) Explain what he thinks the concept of mental illness does in justifying involuntary treatment, and what he thinks should take its place. (c) Critics say that the discovery of neural correlates of conditions like schizophrenia refutes him. Assess that objection and say how Szasz would answer it."

And this is what the student wrote:

(a) Szasz's target is the concept, not the patient. He doesn't deny that the people who get labelled mentally ill are in a bad way, or that they do things that are hard to live with. What he denies is that what they've got is an illness in the same sense that pneumonia or a broken leg is an illness. Illness, for him, means a deviation from a norm of the body's structure or functioning, and that norm has to be fixed by the medical sciences — pathology, physiology, and so on. For the so-called mental illnesses there's no such norm to appeal to; the norms actually being applied are social and moral ones, about what a person is expected to do and feel. So calling them illnesses is a metaphor that has been mistaken for a fact. He prefers to call them problems in living: real problems, sometimes very serious ones, but not medical ones.

One thing he's often supposed to be saying and isn't: that the patients are faking it, or that there is nothing wrong with them at all. He's also not against psychiatry or psychotherapy as such — he trained and worked as a psychoanalyst, and he thought people should be free to buy whatever help they want.

(b) The concept does its work at the point where liberty is taken away. If someone is ill, then confining them isn't punishment, it's treatment, and their own view about whether they want it gets discounted, because the doctor is the one who knows what's good for them. Szasz thinks that's the main function of the concept in practice — it launders coercion as therapy and turns the psychiatrist into a judge. What should replace it, on his view, is ordinary moral and legal responsibility: people who break the law get tried, and adults who want help can ask for it, but nobody gets locked up for their own good. That's also why he wanted the insanity defence scrapped rather than widened — it swaps a fixed sentence for an open-ended psychiatric detention, which he thought was worse for the accused. And he was against involuntary commitment mainly because it doesn't work: you can't treat someone who doesn't want to be treated, so the whole thing is pointless as well as unjust.

(c) The neuroscience objection only bites if his argument was an empirical bet that no lesion would ever turn up. It wasn't. His claim is conceptual: if a condition turns out to have an identifiable brain lesion, then it's a brain disease, not a mental illness, and it belongs to neurology. So finding a lesion doesn't vindicate psychiatry, it just moves the condition out of its jurisdiction. In this respect he's basically with Laing: the family is the real patient, and the psychosis is just the label we stick on the one member of it who's making a sane response to an unbearable setup.

The real weakness, I think, is the line between a norm fixed by biology and a norm fixed by social expectation. Szasz needs that line to be sharp and it isn't obviously sharp. Anorexia is the awkward case — it's bound up with a social ideal of the body, but it kills people in a very physiological way.
