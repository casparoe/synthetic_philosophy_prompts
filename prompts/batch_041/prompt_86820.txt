I'm writing a chapter on the imperial dimension of Mill's liberalism, and I keep running into a difficulty I'd like you to work through with me. The episode I want to use is the East India Company's annexation of Awadh in 1856, together with the run of annexations carried out under the so-called doctrine of lapse in the same period — Satara in 1848 and Jhansi in 1853 are the ones I mean to keep in view. What makes the case interesting is the double standard embedded in the Company's own justifications: under the doctrine of lapse a princely state could be taken over when its ruler had died without a male heir or was judged unfit to govern, whereas Awadh was annexed on the separate ground of misgovernment — the ruler was said to be governing so badly that the Company's paramountcy obliged it to intervene. Mill was not a bystander to any of this. He joined the Examiner's Office at India House in 1823, was its head by 1856, and retired in 1858 when India passed from the Company to the Crown; his official career consisted largely of drafting and defending the despatches that made such annexations administratively real, and after the transfer he wrote in defense of the Company's record in India against its critics.

What I want from you is a two-directional analysis, not a verdict on Mill's character. Take his political theory as the lens: the harm principle and the liberty of the individual as set out in On Liberty, the utilitarian standard that runs through everything he wrote, and — this is the part I care about — his concession that a despotic government may be legitimate for peoples he classed as barbarous, so long as it aims at their improvement and actually effects it. Map that onto the annexations. Who is the despot, and who is the intended beneficiary? Does the improvement proviso function as a genuine test that a given annexation could have passed or failed, or is it unfalsifiable in practice, so that any seizure of a state can be redescribed after the fact as tutelage? Does the harm principle have anything to say here at all, or does Mill's own theory supply him with a principled exemption for the colonial case — an exemption that, if taken seriously, threatens to hollow out the liberalism?

Then run the analysis the other way, and tell me what the episode shows about the theory. I'm especially interested in whether the heirlessness criterion and the misgovernment criterion are doing the same work or different work under a utilitarian reading: a ruler who dies without an heir is not thereby a bad ruler, and a bad ruler need not be heirless, so if the real justification is the happiness of the governed, neither criterion tracks what is supposed to matter. Say where Mill's theory is simply silent — about who counts in the utilitarian calculation, about what improvement could even be measured against, about whether the testimony of the governed counts as evidence — and where it forces an answer Mill would have found uncomfortable. I would rather have a disciplined mapping that ends by conceding the theory's limits than a synthesis that pretends the pieces fit.
