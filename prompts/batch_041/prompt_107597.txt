I'm running the last session of a grad seminar on collective intentionality and joint action -- the week we do group agency and the discursive dilemma, i.e. the thing where you can get a majority for each of two premises and a majority against the conclusion that follows from them, so the group ends up on record with a verdict that belongs to nobody in it. I want to open the session with a poem, and I want the poem to be the phenomenon rather than describe it.

So put on two hats for me: poet, and someone who's read Searle on we-intentions being primitive, Bratman on shared intentions and meshing subplans, Gilbert on joint commitment, and who can tell me where I'm cheating philosophically. Take a minute to brainstorm how the form could carry the idea before you start writing -- I don't need to see the brainstorm, just do it.

Here's the ask. A poem in four stanzas. Stanzas 1-3 are each in the voice of one member of a three-person group deciding something small and concrete (a flatshare, a band kicking out a drummer, a committee picking a venue -- your call, keep it human). Each of those stanzas has to be internally consistent: the speaker says where they stand on two seperate questions and then draws the conclusion that follows, so nobody in the poem is being sloppy or irrational. Stanza 4 is the group speaking as 'we', assembled out of whatever position the majority took at each point -- so it ends up asserting something that no majority of its members believed. It should read like a totally normal thing for a group to say, smooth and confident, so that someone who isn't keeping the tally as they read won't notice anything is wrong; but the tally should be recoverable from the poem without you ever spelling it out. No P's and Q's, no tables, no labels -- the logic has to live in the drama.

One more thing the poem has to do. In stanza 4 there should be one line where the 'we' is doing something that can't be unpacked into what each of us did (something only the group can be said to do), and one line where it can be unpacked that way. If a reader who knows the literature can't point at both lines and say which is which, the poem hasn't earned its place on the handout.

Form: your call, but give it a spine -- a rhyme scheme, a meter, a repeated line, something that makes the turn into stanza 4 feel like a turn. I know thats alot to ask of one poem.

Then a short prose note after the poem, 150-250 words, written for a seminar handout and not a journal: tell me where the poem takes liberties, which reading of collective intentionality it's quietly leaning on and which it's ignoring, and where the philosophical bet it makes is most likely to be wrong -- what would have to be true about group agency for the poem to be a distortion rather than an illustration, and which consideration would move you off that reading. If some version of the dilemma can't be staged this way without cheating (bad arithmetic with three people, or a majority verdict that isn't actually forced), tell me that instead of fudging it -- I'd rather know.
