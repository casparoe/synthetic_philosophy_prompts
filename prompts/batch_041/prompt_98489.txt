I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of mind and I keep getting into the same argument with a friend in my department about split-brain cases — she thinks they show consciousness can literally divide, I think the cases prove a lot less than people claim. We've both noticed that everyone reaches for the same handful of moves when this comes up, so I want to try something different, and I need you to build the case for me, to spec.

Here's what I'm after. There are two things people usually run together in these debates, and I want a thought experiment where they come apart — and specifically in the direction nobody bothers constructing. Call the first the one-field condition: there is a single subject of experience, one phenomenal field, not two streams running side by side. Call the second the one-controller condition: a single system that has access to all the relevant information, can compare it and act on it — one agent, one decision-maker, one voice. In every standard split-brain story the interesting verdict is (arguably) two fields but one controller: two streams of experience, but one body, one person who reports and takes responsibility. I want the reverse. I want a case where the one-field condition is clearly satisfied — one experience, no two-streams business — but the one-controller condition clearly fails, so there are genuinely two centers of control, each with access to different information, each able to act, and yet only one stream of experience behind them. I'm trying to argue that these two conditions are logically independent, so the case has to earn its keep: if you can only get the split by secretly assuming the two controllers share nothing, that's begging the question and it's no use to me.

Constraints: realistic — something a neurologist could plausibly see, no commisurotomy, no sci-fi, no telepathy, no zombies, nothing that divides the brain by magic. The disunity has to live in control and access, not merely in memory or in the subject's own report, so confabulation can't be doing the work, and the two controllers need to be able to disagree about facts, not just about which muscle to move. And don't stipulate the verdicts — they have to fall out of how you wire the case. Once you've built it: walk me through what each of the two conditions says about it and why, tell me which single feature is carrying the whole thing and what happens if I delete that feature, and then give me the strongest line a defender of the one-controller condition would take against the case, because that's the side I'm least sure about. If the thing can't actually be built without cheating, say so plainly rather than handing me something that only looks like it works — I'd rather hear that now than in front of my advisor. I don't need a reading list, just the argument. Finish with a final line of the form \answer{...} holding a short phrase saying whether the construction succeeds and which feature does the work.
