Ok so heres a thing that bugs me about how courts actually behave, and I want you to tell me what theory of law it assumes.

Take a legal system that's pretty clear about one thing: judges apply the law, they dont get to refuse just because they think a statute is stupid or cruel. The legislature passed it, it's on the books, you enforce it. A judge who says "I won't apply this because it's unjust" is, on that view, just breaking the rules -- substituting their own morality for the law.

But then the same systems do stuff that looks like the exact opposite. Courts refuse to enforce a contract because its terms are unconscionable, or because enforcing it would offend public policy. Courts won't send someone back to a country where they'd be tortured. Courts have refused to give effect to amnesty laws shielding people who committed mass atrocities, or to let a limitations period run on crimes against humanity. And after some regimes collapse, courts have basically said: yeah, that statute was passed properly and everyone followed it, but it was so monstrous that it never counted as law, so the person who informed on their neighbor under it can still be punished. That last move is the spicy one, since it says a formally valid law can fail to be law at all.

So: what does that whole package presuppose about the nature of law? Does the practice commit you to thinking there's a moral test for legal validity -- that unjust-enough rules just aren't law? Or can you explain everything that happens with a view where law's validity is one thing (source, pedigree, whatever the system's own criteria say) and the moral limits live entirely in what judges are permitted to do -- in adjudication, interpretation, discretion -- rather than in validity itself? I want you to actually distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit well with. Maybe the practice is compatible with several theories and doesn't settle the question at all; say so if that's where you land, and show why.

Some things I'd like you to sort out:

- The apparent inconsistancy between "we don't judge the justice of statutes" and "this contract/statute/amnesty is too unjust to enforce." Is it a real inconsistency, or do the two halves operate on different objects (legislation vs. private agreements vs. foreign acts) in a principled way?
- What work does the word "law" do in the sentence "that was so unjust it wasn't law"? Is it a claim about validity, about what courts should do, or just rhetoric?
- If you think the practice presupposes something like a moral criterion of validity, tell me what would have to change if we dropped it -- e.g., what a court would have to do differently in the informer cases, in the amnesty cases, in the torture-extradition cases.
- If you think the practice is compatible with a purely source-based view of validity, explain how, and be honest about which cases strain it.
- Is there anything the practice quietly rules out? For instance, does it rule out the idea that legal validity is just whatever the officials happen to treat as valid?

Don't give me a history of the debate or cite anybody -- I don't need a reading list, I want the reconstruction. Assume I know roughly that there's a long-running argument about this and that I don't care who said what.

Also one more wrinkle: in some systems courts will void a law for a procedural defect -- passed by the wrong body, wrong majority, whatever -- and nobody blinks. But they get very nervous about voiding one for being morally awful. Is that asymmetry itself doing philosophical work, or is it just institutional caution?

And keep the answer honest about underdetermination -- if two very different theories both predict this pattern of behavior, say which observations would actually separate them, if any.
