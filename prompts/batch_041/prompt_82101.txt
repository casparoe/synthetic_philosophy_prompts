I recently spent an evening arguing about Newcomb's problem with a friend, and I came away unable to decide whether his framing of the debate is fair. He is a well-read amateur rather than a specialist—the sort of person who has absorbed a good deal of popular writing on decision theory—and this is how he laid out the terrain for me.

"Newcomb's problem is really a test of whether you believe in backward causation. A Predictor has already put either a million dollars or nothing in the opaque box; you may take the opaque box alone, or take both boxes. One-boxers observe that taking only the opaque box is correlated with finding the million, so they take one box. Two-boxers observe that the money is already there or not, so taking the extra box can't cost you anything; two-boxing strictly dominates, since whatever the Predictor did you finish a thousand dollars ahead. The only way one-boxing could come out ahead is if your choice somehow reached back and altered what the Predictor had already done. That is why the puzzle boils down to whether causation can run backwards in time—and why evidential decision theory, which tells you to one-box, is committed to backward causation, while causal decision theory is simply the view that respects the arrow of time."

I suspect there is something wrong here—I have a vague sense that he is running together two things that decision theorists keep apart—but I could not put my finger on it, and he was far more confident than I was. What I want is a careful audit rather than a verdict. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a philosopher working on decision theory would accept it, would wince at it as a misleading simplification, or would reject it outright. In particular, I want to know whether the closing claim—that the problem reduces to a question about backward causation, and that evidential decision theory is thereby committed to it—actually follows from anything true in the rest of the summary, and whether the dominance argument he gives for two-boxing is as decisive as he makes it sound. Please be specific about which premises the one-boxer and the two-boxer would each dispute, and about what a defender of evidential decision theory would say in response.
