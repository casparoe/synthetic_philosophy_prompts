I'm writing a chapter on the limits of Aumann's agreement theorem and I want to use the Challenger launch as the test case -- everyone reaches for card games and dinner parties, nobody uses an engineering disaster. Setup, in case it's useful: the night before the launch there's the Thiokol-NASA teleconference, both sides are staring at the same O-ring data (the erosion, the cold, the flight history), Thiokol's engineers say dont launch below 53F, there's an offline caucus, senior management overrules them, and it flies the next morning in weather colder than that floor. Though the commission's own finding is that the decision rested on incomplete and sometimes misleading information, so part of the question is whether the shared-evidence premise even holds. Aumann, as I've got it: same prior, common knowledge of each other's posteriors, common knowledge of rationality, and the posteriors have to coincide -- the proof basically runs off the cells that are common knowledge between the two information partitions. So do the mapping honestly. Who are the agents here, what's the state space, is there really a common prior, and which assumption actually breaks -- the prior, the common knowledge of rationality, or something the theorem never modelled at all, like the fact that it takes the posteriors as sincerely announced, which is a bad assumption when one side can end your career and the launch is already on the schedule? Then the other direction, what the case does to the theorem. My own take is that it shows Aumann is a consistency constraint with no diagnostic power -- it tells you something in the setup must be broken, never whose prior is the bad one, and here the majority was the wrong one. Tell me if that's too cheap a shot. 700 words max, and lead with your verdict on whether the theorem even bites on this case.
