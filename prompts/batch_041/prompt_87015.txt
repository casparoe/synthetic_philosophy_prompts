I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a book set in a fictional rust-belt city, and the plot turns on infrastructure: a highway spur that cut a Black neighborhood in half, a bus route that keeps getting moved, an app that decides where the patrol cars sit. My characters keep having the same fight — is the thing itself political, or is it only the people who built it and the people who run it? I've been going back and forth on it alot, and before I write another 40k words of people shouting at each other I want the spec rather than the argument.

So: what would a theory that artifacts have politics actually have to deliver to be any good — good enough that I can put defensible positions in my characters' mouths? Give me the requirements list: the cases it has to get right (the famous low bridges on Long Island that kept the buses out, though I gather the history there is messier than the story; a bench with armrests; a plaza built for crowd control that turns out to be the best protest site in town), the distinctions it can't collapse (design vs. use, intended vs. unintended, what an artifact does vs. what it means, and the gap between having political effects and having a politics), and the things it has to stay consistent with. Say which requirements can't all be met at once, and lead with the one that does the most work — the one that knocks out the most candidate views — then the rest, each with the case behind it. No preamble, no restating the question, and please don't send me off to read anybody; I want your list.
