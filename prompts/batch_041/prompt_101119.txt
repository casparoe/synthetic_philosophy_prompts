My two roommates have been arguing for a week about something that happened on our block, and they asked me to referee and I honestly can't. What happened: a new ramen place opened. The first two people we know who tried it raved. After that basically everyone we know went, and all of them said it was great — including two people who later admitted to me they hadn't really liked it but assumed they must be missing something, since everyone else loved it.

Roommate A: this is just sheep behavior. The later people were copying the crowd, and the fact that some privately disliked it but said nothing proves the whole thing was irrational groupthink. Roommate B: no, each of them did the rational thing. The first two raves are real evidence; once two people independently rave, it's reasonable to go even if your own hunch is lukewarm, because your hunch is one weak signal against two strong ones. A: but thats exactly the problem — two early random reviews can lock in the choice for everyone, and then nobody's later experience counts, so a whole block gets convinced on almost no real evidence. B: that's a complaint about how information gets aggregated, not about whether any individual was irrational, and you keep sliding between those two things. A: if everyone's rational and you still end up with a block confidently stuck on a mediocre restaurant, then 'rational' is doing less work than you think. B: you want individual rationality to add up to collective wisdom; sometimes it just doesn't, and that's not a contradiction.

Referee it: go move by move, say who's right at each step, and tell me what each of them is missing. Is there a real difference between B's 'they were rational' and A's 'they were sheep,' or are they just redescribing the same thing? And one more thing I keep wondering about: if that very first reviewer had happened to dislike it, I bet the whole block would've ended up at a different restaurant even though the food is exactly the same. Does that possibility help one of them, or is it irrelevant?
