I've been sitting in a reading group on Bernard Williams's objection to impartial morality, and I need help with a reconstruction a friend offered. The objection, as we've been reading it, is that the utilitarian who saves his wife and then adds the thought that impartial morality permits partiality in cases like this is having one thought too many: the added thought does no work in moving him, and its presence betrays that his attachment to her is not, for him, what ultimately matters. My friend claims the objection proves far more than that, and I can't locate the break. Here is his argument, cleaned up:

(1) An agent acts from a ground project with integrity only if the project moves her without her first having to establish that the action is permitted. Where a licence must be secured before the project can operate, the licence is doing the work.

(2) Call a consideration *answerable* when the agent can cite it, if pressed, as what counted in favour of her action. Whenever an agent acts for a reason, that reason is answerable; that is just what distinguishes acting for a reason from being pushed.

(3) To cite a consideration as counting in favour of an action is to represent the action as, in that respect, licensed. So answerability carries licence with it: the agent who can cite her reason thereby represents the act as one she was entitled to perform.

(4) Now take the spouse who pulls her husband from the water, moved neither by duty nor by rule but simply because he is her husband. By (2) her love is answerable — she can say why she did it. By (3), citing it represents the rescue as licensed. The licence is therefore present in her case too, lodged inside the very thought that moves her.

(5) But a licence that is present is a licence that could have been withheld: she represents the rescue as permitted, and whatever is represented as permitted is represented as resting on a question that had, in principle, to be settled. That is precisely the structure Williams calls one thought too many.

(6) So no agent who acts for a reason acts with integrity; integrity would demand acting without reasons.

(7) But an agent who acts without reasons is not acting at all, only behaving. So Williams's ideal is either unrealisable or a sentimental name for unreflective compulsion.

I would rather keep (1) and (7) — integrity does seem to require that the project do the moving, and I don't want the ideal collapsing into compulsion — and give up most anything else, but I can't tell which step to surrender or what surrendering it costs. Which premise or transition should I reject? Say why it looked compelling, what rejecting it commits me to, and where your own diagnosis is likeliest to fail: what argument or consideration would move you to a different step. Keep the whole thing under 600 words.
