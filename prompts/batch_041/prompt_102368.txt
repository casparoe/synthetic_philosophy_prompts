Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems refute mechanism. I am a mechanist, but I do not want to say that human mathematical reasoning is inconsistent, and I do not want to say that mathematicians are infallible. My friend presented the following argument, and I have been unable to find its flaw. I would like you to diagnose it rather than defend either side.

1. Suppose human mathematical reasoning is the output of a machine M.
2. Any machine M is equivalent to a formal system F_M, whose theorems are exactly the sentences M can output.
3. By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, if F_M is consistent and strong enough for arithmetic, there is a sentence G_M that is true but not provable in F_M.
4. A human mathematician can recognize that G_M is true, provided she knows that F_M is consistent.
5. She does know that F_M is consistent, because F_M is supposed to capture her own mathematical reasoning, and she knows that her own mathematical reasoning is sound.
6. Therefore she can recognize a truth that M cannot output.
7. So M does not capture human mathematical reasoning.
8. Since M was arbitrary, human mathematical reasoning is not the output of any machine.

My friend says this is a clean proof of anti-mechanism. I think it proves too much. If step 5 is right, then for any machine that is proposed as a model of human mathematical reasoning, the human can step outside it and see its Gödel sentence. But the same move seems to apply to the machine that models the human's own reasoning about consistency. That appears to require the human to know the consistency of her own mathematical system, which is exactly what Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says she cannot prove if she is that system. So the argument seems to leave us with a disjunction: either the human is not a consistent formal system, or she is inconsistent, or she has some non-formal faculty of insight. I do not know which of these is the real conclusion, and I do not know where the argument goes wrong.

I would like a diagnosis. Which premise or step should I reject? Why did it look plausible? What does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up, and what do I lose by giving it up? I would especially like to keep the claim that a human mathematician can outstrip any particular formal system she can recognize as sound, and I would like to avoid the claim that human mathematical intuition is infallible. I am also unsure about step 2: is every machine really equivalent to a formal system in the sense the argument needs, or can a machine enumerate a set that is not the set of theorems of any recursively axiomatizable theory? If that distinction matters, please say how.
