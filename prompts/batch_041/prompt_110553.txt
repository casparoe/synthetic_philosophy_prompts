Ok so I'm working on a thing about operational definitions of intelligence and I keep coming back to the weird bit at the end of Turing's 1950 paper on machine intelligence. You know the one -- he's just spent the whole paper replacing the question of whether machines can think with the imitation game, and then right at the end he suddenly says, oh by the way, what about telepathy? He says the evidence for telepathy is actually pretty strong, and if it's real then the interrogator might be able to read the machine's mind, or the machine might be able to read the interrogator's, so you'd need to run the game in a room shielded from telepathy. Most people treat this as an embarrassment, a relic of the 1950s parapsychology craze, and just skip it. But I want to take it seriously as a test case. Analyze Turing's telepathy discussion as an episode in the history of the operational criterion of intelligence. What does the possibility of ESP actually do to the imitation game? Does it show that the test is measuring something other than intelligence -- some physical or paranormal capacity that humans happen to have and machines don't? Turing's fix is to shield the room, but does that fix work, or does it just gerrymander the test so that it can't be refuted by any empirical discovery about human capacities? And what does the episode tell us about the operationalist strategy in general: if you define intelligence as behavioral indistinguishability, are you always going to be vulnerable to the discovery of some new capacity that has nothing to do with thinking? I want the mapping in both directions -- what the imitation game explains about why Turing said what he said about telepathy, and what the telepathy discussion reveals about the limits of the imitation game. Don't just tell me it's a curiosity, and don't give me a reading list, just the argument.
