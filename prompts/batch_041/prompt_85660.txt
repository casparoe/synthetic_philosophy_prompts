I spent thirty-odd years as a fisheries biologist and since retiring I've been reading a lot of archaeology and philosophy, which for someone who likes a tidy model is a dangerous hobby. Here's what I keep going round on. Everyone from the popular collapse books to my old colleagues at the lab uses Easter Island as the cautionary tale: small isolated population, no way off, overshoots what the island can give, cuts the last tree, and down it goes — and the moral is that we're Rapa Nui and the island is the Earth. I've leaned on that one myself in talks. Its the petri dish with statues on it.

I'd like you to audit the comparison properly. Give me the correspondence first — what plays the island, the islanders, the trees, the soil, the moai, the rival clans, the isolation, and what exactly is supposed to stand in for the outside world — then where the mapping holds and where it tears, and which of the tears actually matter for the moral the story is being used to draw, namely that we have to change course or go the same way. Because here's my problem: I've since read a couple of archaeologists who say the whole tale is wrong. Rats and later European visitors did most of the damage, the population was still there and still functioning when the first European ships showed up, and the real wrecking came after contact, so that collapse is something we've retrofitted onto a society rather than something it did to itself. If theyre right, whats left of the analogy? Is the closedness of the island doing all the work, and is the Earth actually closed in the same sense? Someone also told me the honest definition of collapse is just a fast loss of complexity, which would make it nearly value-neutral — does that help, or does it just move the problem somewhere else?

And tell me whether the same move happens with the Maya or the Late Bronze Age, or whether Rapa Nui is the one case that has to carry the moral on its own. If you think a different comparison would do the job better, say what it is. One request: be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely fought over by the people who study it — I don't want a tidy consensus that isn't there.
