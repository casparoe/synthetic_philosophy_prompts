I teach the second half of a cross-listed upper-level seminar (philosophy and mathematics) on non-constructive existence proofs, and the running example for the unit is the strategy-stealing argument: you suppose the second player has a winning strategy, let the first player open with an arbitrary move and then impersonate that strategy, and conclude that the second player cannot have had one after all, the extra stone on the board being harmless because the game's winning condition is monotone in a player's own stones. My students can all recite the manoeuvre and none of them can tell me what it assumes or what it delivers; last year several were convinced it hands you the winning strategy, and one insisted it shows the first player wins in any game where the second player cannot. For this year's midterm I want one question that pulls the argument apart. I would like it to run on a game I can state in a few lines, so that nobody can answer it out of memory of the literature and so that nothing in the question depends on having read any particular paper: a Hex-style connection game on an n-by-n board, players alternately placing one stone of their own colour on an empty cell, the first player trying to build a chain of their stones from the top edge to the bottom edge and the second from the left edge to the right edge, where two cells count as adjacent if they share a side. Against that base game I want three variants. First, the same game played on a square grid with ordinary four-neighbour adjacency, where I want them to see that the argument now yields only that the second player cannot win, to say what further ingredient is needed before the first player's win follows, and to draw a completed position that is nobody's win. Second, a variant with a rule under which a player's own stones can be a liability — I have in mind a rule that a player whose stones contain a closed loop immediately loses — where they should pinpoint which premise dies and explain how a player's own extra stone could cost them the game. Third, an infinite board, where I want them to say what the argument now has to presuppose about determinacy. The question should close with the genuinely philosophical part: granted the argument works in the base game, does it give knowledge, and is the gap between knowing that a winning strategy exists and knowing one a real epistemic gap or merely a psychological one? I don't want the question to turn on whether students have read anything in particular, so everything they need should be stated in the question itself.

Please produce four things, in this order and under these headings. First, "The question as it will be printed": the four lettered parts with marks attached, totalling 25, together with the suggested timing for a 90-minute paper (roughly 300 words). Second, "Model answer": what a strong candidate would write, around 750 words, precise enough about the mechanism of the impersonation argument that a marker can see the difference between a candidate who understands why the extra stone must be harmless and one who merely says the proof is by contradiction. Third, "Marking scheme": the specific moves a top answer must make, each with its mark allocation, plus the two or three errors that should cost marks — I want to be able to tell apart the candidate who confuses the symmetry assumption with the monotonicity assumption, and the one who thinks determinacy is a rule of the game rather than a theorem about it. Fourth, "Where the model answer is most likely to fail": the places where it is oversimplified or possibly wrong, and which argument or consideration would change the verdict on any of the parts — in particular, if you think one of the three variants does not have the answer I am expecting, say so plainly rather than writing around it.
