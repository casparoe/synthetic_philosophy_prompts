A friend of mine in our reading group has been building this arguement about Adam Smith's impartial spectator and I can't tell if its a real problem or a trick. Here it is:

(1) For Smith, to call an action right is just to say that an impartial spectator would sympathize with the agent's motive and go along with it.
(2) But Smith also thinks the impartial spectator is something we build inside ourselves out of our experience of how actual people react to us — the inner judge is trained on real spectators, then corrected and generalized.
(3) Smith himself is the one who keeps insisting how crooked our actual reactions are: we blame a man more for the damage he causes by accident than for the damage he sincerely meant to cause; we admire the rich and the great over the wise and the good; we have far less patience for hatred and resentment than for kindness and generosity.
(4) So the inner judge is trained on crooked data, and whatever it approves of is public opinion with the obvious biases filed off.
(5) Which means Smith's theory can't even state his own criticisms of our sentiments. When he says it's base to admire a rich man over a wise one, he has to be appealing to some standard that isn't the corrected spectator.
(6) So either the impartial spectator is laundered public opinion, in which case Smith's ethics is conservative at the bottom, or it's an independent standard of reason that his sentimentalist machinery never actually reaches.

He says it's worse than it looks, because nothing in the argument is special to Smith. Hume's standard is also corrected sentiment rather than reason, so if the arguement goes through, sentimentalism in general is dead and not just Smith's version. I'd like to keep (1) and (3) if I can. Which step do you give up, why did it look fine at first glance, and what does giving it up cost me? American English please, and don't just tell me Smith was a sentimentalist — I know that. I want to know where this thing actually breaks.
