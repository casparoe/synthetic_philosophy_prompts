I'm giving the commencement address at my old college next month — small liberal arts place, I did cog sci there, and the provost roped me in after she read some blog post I wrote about Hofstadter. So it's a genuinely mixed room: the graduating class (a lot of them heading into ML, UX, consulting), their parents, and a good chunk of the psych and philosophy faculty who will definately know if I fumble the theory. About twelve minutes out loud, warm, a little funny, no slides, just a person at a lectern.

Here's the thing I actually want it to do, and this is the in-the-weeds part. I've been rereading Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, and I've become convinced that the usual takeaway — analogy is important, it's the engine of creativity — is way too soft. The strong claim is that analogy isn't a technique the mind occasionally reaches for; it's what categorization *is*, all the way down. Recognizing a chair as a chair, getting the word 'and' in a sentence you've never seen before, following a rule — all of that is already analogy to prior cases. I want the address to really commit to that, which means I can't just assert it and then tell a cute story; the speech itself kind of has to *run* on analogies, and I want the audience to feel the machinery turning rather than hear me describe it. And it still has to land for the parents who've never heard any of this, not just the cogsci kids.

I also want one darker thread woven through, because I think it's true and it's the actually-useful part for graduates. If categorization is analogy, then the same mechanism that hands you insight is the one that hands you stereotyping and self-confirming tunnel vision — and the real failure mode isn't *bad* analogies, it's *unmarked* ones, the comparisons you're running without noticing you're running them.
