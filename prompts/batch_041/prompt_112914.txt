I'm a novelist, about 60k words into a draft, and I keep hitting the same wall in revision: a character does something in chapter 12 that I swear is "in character," but my editor says it comes out of nowhere, and honestly I can't always tell who's right. I've been reading about Dennett's real patterns and the three stances -- physical, design, intentional -- and I want a procedure I can actually run on a scene to decide whether a bit of behavior is a real pattern in the character or whether I'm just projecting a pattern onto noise. My rough thought is that a pattern is real when it's cheaper to describe what the character does by naming the trait than by listing all the exceptions, but I don't know how to turn that into something I can do at my desk. So give me the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a load-bearing pattern versus a decorative one (a trait I've named but that isn't actually doing any compressing), when the honest verdict is "this is the design stance, I rigged it to get where I wanted," and the point where the procedure has to stop and hand it back to me as a judgment call. I need to run it in maybe twenty minutes on a scene, before I show it to my editor, and I've got no philosophy training so keep the jargon survivable. Then run it on three cases: (a) a lifelong miser refuses to lend money -- I'm pretty sure that one's fine; (b) a scene where my cynical narrator suddenly does something generous, and I want to keep it; (c) a character whose "trait" is really just me needing the plot to move. Be honest about the one where the procedure tells me to cut the scene I love, and tell me what the procedure definately can't do.
