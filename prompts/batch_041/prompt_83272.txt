I worked for forty years as an economic entomologist, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy in an attempt to understand what my former profession was actually doing. What I want is not an essay but a classification instrument: a decision tree for the ethics of insecticides and pest control, whose branch nodes are questions and whose leaves are positions.

Build the tree out of the logical space before naming anything. The upper branches should be questions a reader can answer without already knowing any of the positions: whether insect pain and suffering carry moral weight at all; whether, if they do, the unit that matters is the individual insect or the population, species, and ecosystem; whether sheer numbers alter what is permitted or instead reveal that the arithmetic has broken down; whether killing pests differs from killing wild animals, and if so whether the difference lies in intention, in necessity, or in the fact that agriculture is a practice one is already inside; and whether naturalness or ecosystem integrity has value independent of the welfare of the creatures that compose it.

Then locate the positions at the leaves, one sentence each on that position's most awkward commitment, using a name where one is in circulation and marking it unnamed where nobody defends it. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and any combination of answers that appears to be unoccupied, with a diagnosis of whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored. Keep the tree to a size one can hold in mind — on the order of six to ten leaves.

Sort by these cases rather than by self-description: a broad-spectrum spray that kills something on the order of a billion insects to bring in a harvest; a sterile-male programme that removes a pest species from a region; a crop engineered to kill only the insect that feeds on it; and a pest species whose young perish in vast numbers regardless of us, so that most individuals never reach the stage at which one would have thought their lives worth living. On the last case especially, I want it to be visible whether a position's verdict follows from its principles or embarrasses them.
