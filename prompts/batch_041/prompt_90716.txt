I chair the jury for the Alder Prize in Poetry Translation, and we are deadlocked. Our rules say only that the winning translation must best carry the original poem into English; they do not say what carrying across requires. I need a short written opinion to circulate before Thursday's final meeting, because the prize announcement is Friday.

The two finalists translate the same fourteen-line poem by a living poet writing in a language not all jurors read. The source is built on three simultaneous constraints. Every line contains exactly seven words. The first letters of the lines spell the name of the poet's brother, who drowned. And the poem avoids the letter that begins that name everywhere except in the acrostic. The surface is a calm description of a garden, but the acrostic and a pun on a source word that can mean both a garden plot and a burial plot make the poem an elegy for the brother. The committee's usual distinction between semantic fidelity and formal fidelity has collapsed, because the acrostic is both a formal feature and the bearer of the poem's reference.

Translation A renders the garden and the plot-pun precisely, line by line, in natural English verse, but abandons both the acrostic and the seven-word rule. Its readers get a moving poem about a garden with a submerged grief. Translation B preserves the acrostic and the seven-word rule, and its lines are metrically tight, but to do so it changes the garden into a harbor, drops the pun, and occasionally strains sense; it adds a translator's note explaining what was lost. A third submission, Translation C, abandons the source's words and constraints entirely and instead invents a new fourteen-line acrostic in English spelling a different name, arguing that this is the only way to carry across the poem's function rather than its content.

The committee is split three ways. Some argue that Translation A is the only real translation because it gives English readers the poem's meaning; the acrostic, on this view, is a formal game, not the poem. Others argue that Translation B is the only faithful one because the acrostic and the word-count are not decorations but the poem's skeleton: lose them and you lose the brother, which is the point; one juror puts it by saying that the acrostic is not a meaning but a performance, and a translation must reproduce the performance. A third group wants to give a special citation to C while awarding the main prize to A, on the ground that C is an adaptation, not a translation, and the prize should not reward it as one. We also have to decide whether to recommend changing the prize's definition of translation for next year, since this dispute will recur.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- who receives the prize, whether C receives any recognition, and whether the rules should be amended -- with reasons the jurors can actually argue about. It should engage the strongest case for the position you reject, and it should say what, if anything, the committee's own framing has gotten wrong. No citations or bibliography; reason from the case as described.
