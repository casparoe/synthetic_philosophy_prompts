I have just come back from a long argument with a friend about which animals are conscious, and I would like to settle what I actually think. Please act as an expert in the philosophy of mind and in comparative neurobiology — someone who knows the literature but is willing to reason from first principles rather than summarize it. Before you answer, brainstorm the space of candidate principles on paper; then think slowly through the cases; then give your considered view. Be explicit about where you are uncertain and why, and do not paper over the places where my verdicts pull against each other.

The argument started over dinner and ended with my friend saying I rank animals by how much they resemble me. These were the verdicts I defended.

1. An adult dog: conscious. Uncontroversial to me — it feels pain, fear, and something like joy.
2. A human newborn: conscious, even though it cannot tell me so.
3. An octopus: conscious, and I hold this fairly confidently.
4. A rainbow trout: conscious, I think. It avoids noxious stimuli in ways that look more than reflex, and it learns.
5. A honeybee: conscious, I lean yes — it navigates by learned landmarks, learns colours, and its behaviour looks integrated rather than mechanical.
6. A jumping spider: I genuinely do not know, and I kept changing sides mid-sentence.
7. A fruit fly: not conscious.
8. A nematode worm with a few hundred neurons: not conscious.
9. A mouse whose cortex has been removed, which still withdraws from noxious heat, still grooms, and still swallows food placed in its mouth: not conscious — a puppet, no experience at all.
10. A person who is completely paralysed but awake and aware: conscious.
11. A person in dreamless deep sleep: not conscious.
12. A plant: not conscious, despite responding to damage.
13. A whole bee colony, considered as one thing: not conscious, even though each bee in it is.

Two of these sit badly together, and my friend pressed exactly there. I deny experience to the decerebrate mouse, which behaves more like a suffering mammal than the bee ever does, while granting it to the bee, whose behaviour is comparatively thin. And I say yes to the bee, no to the fruit fly, no to the worm, without being able to say where the line falls or why it falls there rather than somewhere else. The spider embarrasses me for the same reason: it is in the same phylum as the bee, with behaviour at least as rich, and I cannot get a stable verdict out of myself.

Toward the end I tried to state a principle of my own: a creature is conscious just in case its nervous system integrates activity in recurrent loops, so that the state of the whole system — not a chain of independent reflexes — is available to guide flexible, learned, goal-directed behaviour, and the creature actually possesses a repertoire of such behaviour. My friend's reply was that I have smuggled in a cortex under another name, and that any criterion of this shape will simply reward whatever reminds me of a mammal. He offered his own: what matters is the capacity for unlimited associative learning — the ability to form arbitrary new associations, and then to learn relations among those associations, with no inborn ceiling on what can be combined.

What I want from you is this. First, state precisely the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts, with the specific case that motivates each clause. Second, give me your verdicts on cases I have not put to you: a crow; a shrimp; a sea slug; a honeybee while it sleeps; a silicon system that reproduces a bee's neural dynamics exactly; a creature with a richly recurrent brain that has been paralysed since birth and has never once behaved; a creature built as a chain of reflex units with no recurrent loops that nonetheless passes every behavioural test for pain; and a human patient with no cortical activity but an intact brainstem. Third, tell me honestly which of my original verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which I should therefore give up, and what I would have to add to keep each one — and whether keeping it is worth the cost. Fourth, say whether my friend's criterion does better than mine, and whether it is vulnerable to the same objection. Finally, tell me what my pattern of verdicts commits me to that I may not have noticed, and whether there is a simpler principle I have been avoiding because it forces me to give up the decerebrate mouse or the bee.
