I spent thirty-odd years in a geology department before I retired — stratigraphy and basin analysis, which means I've spent a lot of my life arguing about when things begin and whether the boundary is a discovery or a decision. Since retiring I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, badly, and I've joined a reading group at the local university that's working through the nationalism literature. Last month we did Hobsbawm and Ranger's The Invention of Tradition and Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities, both 1983, and a man in the group — retired diplomat, reads a lot of history, extremely confident — gave us what he called the bottom line. Roughly: both books show that nations are modern inventions rather than old communities; that national traditions are fabrications cooked up by nineteenth-century elites to manufacture consent and keep ordinary people in line (his example, which he said is the standard one, was tartan and the kilt); and that Anderson's real contribution is explaining how print media — newspapers and novels — let elites make millions of people imagine a community of strangers. Hence, he said, the question of how old nations are is basically settled: a couple of hundred years, and anyone who talks about ancient national identities is doing ideology rather than history. I nodded along, but I've learned in this reading group that the confident ones aren't always the ones to trust, and something about it felt like it was sliding two different things together.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through his summary and tell me which parts people who actually work on nationalism would accept without blinking, which are the harmless simplifications any good popularizer would make, and which are the ones that would make a specialist wince or that a defender of either book would object to. In particular: are 'imagined' and 'invented' doing the same work in those two books, or is he running two separate theses into one? And does the tartan example carry the weight he put on it, or is it a special case being generalised? Then the part I care about most: suppose we fix whatever is wrong with his summary — do his conclusions survive, or does the corrected version support something weaker, or stranger, than "nations are a couple of hundred years old and the rest is ideology"? A side question, and tell me if this is a bad comparison: in my old field, when the Jurassic begins is a decision by a commitee about a golden spike rather than a discovery, and everybody knows it, but nobody concludes that the rocks are fake or that stratigraphy is a con trick. I have a feeling my friend is using 'invented' in a way that this parallel wouldn't license, but I may just be pattern-matching from my own trade — if the comparison is useful, say what it does and where it breaks.

Finally, please be carefull about seperating what is settled among people who know this literature from what is genuinely contested. The reading group keeps giving me the impression that the modernist story is simply the consensus, and I'd rather not be handed a contested position dressed up as the settled one. Where you aren't sure, say so rather than smoothing it over.
