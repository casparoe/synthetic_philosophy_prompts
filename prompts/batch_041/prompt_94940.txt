Consider a jurisdiction that holds referendums frequently and treats the results as binding, but whose rules are conspicuously asymmetric, and I cannot tell what conception of democratic legitimacy the asymmetry commits it to.

Enacting is easier than repealing. A simple majority of those voting can enact an ordinary law by referendum, and the same simple majority can repeal an ordinary law passed by the legislature. But once a measure has been enacted by referendum, the legislature may not amend or repeal it at all, and a later referendum may repeal it only if that vote clears a higher threshold — say sixty percent of those voting, with a turnout floor — and only after two full electoral cycles have passed. A short list of topics is walled off entirely: no referendum, however popular, may alter certain enumerated rights or the rules of the referendum process itself. The status of a result also varies by topic: on constitutional questions the vote binds the government absolutely, while on certain other questions the same ballot yields only an advisory result that the legislature is free to follow or ignore, though in practice it always follows. And while the agenda is set from below, by petition, the wording of each question is drafted by the authorities.

I want an account of what this package presupposes about the people, their will, and the source of political authority. In particular: (1) Does the asymmetry between enacting and repealing commit the system to a single collective will that persists across time, such that an earlier majority can bind a later one — and is that coherent, or is it a dead-hand problem dressed in democratic clothes? (2) Does treating some topics as off-limits presuppose a distinction between the demos as sovereign and the demos as already bound by norms it did not choose, and can that distinction be drawn without circularity? (3) Does the advisory/binding split presuppose that legitimacy sometimes comes from the procedure and sometimes from the outcome, or instead that the electorate is competent on some questions and not others?

Then do the analytic work I actually need. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Ask whether a purely procedural account — legitimacy from fair aggregation and nothing more — could license the whole arrangement, or whether the repeal asymmetry forces a substantive commitment about which decisions are self-binding. Say where the practice is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would generate the same rules, and which observation would discriminate among them. Finally, suppose the implicit conception were replaced by a flatly majoritarian one — each generation's majority is sovereign and nothing it does binds a successor — and tell me what in this practice would have to change, and whether the result would still be a referendum democracy or something else wearing its name.
