I retired a couple of years ago from a career in atmospheric chemistry, and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy — badly, but enthusiastically. I've fallen in with a small reading group (one of whom did a philosophy degree decades ago and never lets the rest of us forget it), and last month we got onto the 'spectrum' or 'continuum' arguments you see knocking about: you take something like a very long life that is only a hair less good at each step, and the claim is that no single small step makes things worse, yet at the far end you've arrived at something plainly dreadful — so 'better than' can't be transitive after all. I've been trying to write a short piece for the group setting out my own take on it, and I'd like you to give me comments — mainly on whether I've got the philosophy right, and secondarily on whether the angle is at all interesting or whether I'm just reinventing something that everyone already knows.

Here's the arguement, more or less as notes. I claim the whole puzzle is an old friend from psychophysics dressed up in fancy clothes. In sensory work we've long known that discrimination has a threshold — the just-noticeable difference — and that if you build a preference-like relation with a threshold buried in it, failures of transitivity come for free: a tone can be judged no louder than a second, and the second no louder than a third, while the first and the third are obviously different. There's even a tidy formal home for this, the semiorders of measurement theory, where indifference is permitted to be intransitive. So, I want to say, the spectrum argument reveals nothing special about value; it's just the threshold structure of any discriminative relation, and once you see that, the alleged paradox evaporates — 'better than' is a threshold relation, threshold relations are routinely non-transitive, and there is no scandal and no reason to give up anything. 

Do you think that's right? Where I'd especially like pushback: whether the semiorder apparatus really is the right formalisation of what the spectrum argument claims, or whether I've quietly swapped one relation for another; whether 'no scandal' actually follows, or whether it just relabels the problem without dissolving it; and, if my diagnosis is off, what the cleanest honest statement of what the spectrum argument does establish would be. Please write back in American English, and don't bother sparing my feelings — I'd far rather be told the analogy is doing no work than be humoured.
