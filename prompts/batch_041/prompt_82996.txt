I chair the safety-standards review at a frontier AI lab: a standing committee of five that meets quarterly to decide which proposed safety measures the lab will actually adopt before its next training run ships. Our recommendations go to the board as the technical record, though the chief executive can overrule us and has. We are stuck in a loop. Every slate turns into the same argument — the safety team proposes a measure, the product team answers that it is an alignment tax we cannot afford, and someone invokes what we have taken to calling the competitiveness constraint: the claim that a lab cannot unilaterally absorb a safety cost when rivals who decline it will convert the saved capability into a decisive lead, and that a world where the lead goes to the less careful lab is worse than one in which we are all slightly less safe. No one in the room can reliably tell a genuine constraint from a negotiating position, so we relitigate the same measure three quarters running, then adopt it late, badly, and with an exemption for the flagship model.

I want a decision procedure we can actually run, in one ninety-minute meeting, on each proposed measure, without pretending to knowledge we do not have.

Build it on the following working taxonomy, which we have argued about for two years but never written down. Classify the cost of a measure by who bears it. A common cost is borne by every lab deploying at this scale — a mandatory evaluation that all of them run — so it creates no relative disadvantage and the competitiveness constraint is simply inapplicable. A unilateral cost is borne only by the adopter while rivals free-ride on its restraint, as when we forgo a data source they continue to use. A progressive cost falls hardest on whoever is furthest ahead, as when a capability threshold is crossed only by the frontier model.

The procedure should then carry these questions in order, each with a stated criterion for a sufficient answer and a stated point at which it stops:

1. Who pays — assign the measure to one of the three classes above.
2. Whether the cost is competitively decisive — this requires an explicit estimate of the relative capability or timeline gap against each named rival, together with an explicit statement of the uncertainty in that estimate.
3. Whether rivals would adopt the measure anyway — because it is also good for them, because it is a focal point they expect others to converge on, or because their own regulators will shortly require it.
4. Whether the measure carries offsetting competitive benefits — trust, recruiting, regulatory goodwill, reduced incident exposure — which must be netted against the cost rather than listed alongside it.
5. Whether adoption is reversible, so that a cheap-to-reverse measure can be taken now and revisited rather than deferred indefinitely.
6. Whether the measure is one that no lab can sensibly adopt alone, in which case the output is not a verdict but a referral to a coordination path — an industry standard, a regulator, a mutual restraint — and the procedure must specify what that path would have to look like to count as a real answer.

The procedure must be honest about two things. First, every claim about what rivals will do is a prediction rather than a fact, and the procedure must mark those steps as assumptions to be stated and defended rather than verified. Second, there is a point — I suspect it is the progressive-cost case — at which the remaining question is not factual at all but is how much relative position we are willing to trade for how much safety, and the procedure must stop there and hand the decision to a named human role rather than dress it up as a calculation.

Then run the procedure on three measures and show me its output: routing all chain-of-thought through a monitor, at a cost of roughly five percent on our internal benchmarks; refusing to train on a large scraped corpus that our two nearest rivals continue to use; and a training-compute ceiling that, by construction, binds only on the lab currently in the lead. At least one of the three should come out against what the committee plainly wants, and I want you to say so plainly rather than softening it.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. If I run it honestly, will it ever license the leader to be the least safe lab in the field — and if so, is that a defect in the procedure or a property of the situation we are in?
