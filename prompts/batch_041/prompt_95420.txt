I have a view about animals that I arrived at on my own and have never found stated in quite my form, though I suspect it is old. Its outline is this. Animals cannot be wronged. They are not the kind of thing that can have a claim against me, and their suffering, taken by itself, is not an evil; a deer that dies badly in the woods has suffered no injustice. What is wrong with cruelty to animals lies entirely in what it does to the person who commits it. It coarsens him, dulls whatever in him answers to the vulnerability of others, and that dulling is the whole of the injury. The duty, then, is one I owe to myself and to the people who have to live with me, and never to the animal. The same reasoning pulls me toward things that cannot feel at all: I own a two-hundred-year-old oak that drops limbs on my roof and shades my garden, and I cannot bring myself to have it felled for convenience, on the ground that a person who would do that is not a person I wish to be.

My brother says this position is absurd, and I have not been able to answer him. If the animal's pain counts for nothing in itself, he says, then nothing is forbidden me except losing my temper: I may keep a factory farm, drown a litter of kittens, or cut up a dog in a laboratory, provided I do it calmly, efficiently, and without taking pleasure in it. And since the pain is nothing in itself, the number of animals cannot matter either, so that the man who calmly kills ten thousand in an abattoir is no worse than the boy who torments one cat for fun. He believes he has caught me in a contradiction. I do not think he has, but I cannot say where his argument goes wrong.

Here is the decision I actually face. My daughter is fifteen. My brother-in-law — a man I love, and the gentlest person I know, with people and with dogs alike — has invited her to hunt deer with him this autumn. She would take the shot herself, and he would teach her to gut and butcher the animal. She wants to go. I have eaten meat all my life without giving it much thought, and I would eat this deer without hesitation. What I cannot settle is whether sending my daughter out to kill something for the pleasure of the day does something to her that my buying a chicken at the supermarket does not.

I do not want to be reassured, and I do not want to be told that animals matter after all or that they do not. I want to know what my own position requires of me. Specifically:

1. Is the view I have described coherent, or is my brother right that it collapses into a rule permitting anything to a man who keeps his temper?
2. What does it commit me to about the hunt? Does it yield a determinate answer, or does it merely tell me to consult my feelings? I would count the second as a failure of the view, not a feature of it.
3. Is the oak the same case as the deer, or a different one, and what does the difference reveal about the principle I am actually running?
4. If the position I have described is one that philosophers have defended under a name, what is that name, and where does my version depart from the standard one?
5. What should I do — and, if I do as you advise, what am I thereby committed to about the venison in my brother-in-law's freezer, the dog my neighbour keeps chained in his yard, and the meat counter at my supermarket?
