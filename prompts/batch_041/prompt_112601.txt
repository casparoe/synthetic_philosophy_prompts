3(a) Gibbard-Satterthwaite says that if there are at least three alternatives and at least two voters, then any voting rule that is strategy-proof must be dictatorial. A rule is dictatorial when there is some one voter whose top-ranked alternative is always the winner, no matter what everyone else reports. So with three or more options the only rule that can't be manipulated is one where a single voter gets their way every time.

3(b) Strategy-proofness means no voter can ever do better by reporting a ranking other than their true one, no matter what the other voters report, i.e. truthful reporting is a weakly dominant strategy. Equivalently, truthful voting is a Nash equilibrium of the game the rule induces: nobody can gain by lying, so everyone reports their real preferences and the winner is the alternative the rule would pick from the honest profile. Thats why the theorem hurts. Manipulability isn't a flaw of particular rules like plurality or Borda that a cleverer rule could fix; it is built into the setup, so any rule satisfying the theorem's conditions is either gameable or a dictatorship, and we're stuck choosing between manipulation and one voter deciding everything.

3(c) Two ways out. First, restrict the domain of allowed preferences.
