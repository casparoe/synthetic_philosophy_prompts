I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and in two weeks I'm presenting to a small reading group. Who's in the room: mostly people who build Bayesian models of how humans learn concepts (the "concepts are little programs, learning is inference over a grammar" crowd), two people who work on in-context learning in LLMs, and one person who runs categorization experiments on undergrads.

My thesis: Bongard problems — the puzzles where you get twelve figures split into two sets of six and have to say what separates them — don't test concept induction, they test something much narrower. My argument, roughly: on the standard problems, once you've fixed the vocabulary of features (count, shape, size, orientation, nesting, symmetry, touching, and so on), the intended rule is recoverable from the labeled figures by search, so a solver that already has the vocabulary can grind them out. Call those closed problems. The genuinely hard part of concept learning is getting the vocabulary — noticing that some property of a figure is a property at all — and in the standard problems that part is done for you by whoever built the puzzle. There's recent work where people bolt pragmatic constraints onto a visual description language to deal with exactly the ambiguity I'm pointing at, and I want to claim it still takes the vocabulary as given. I've also got ~30 of my own problems where I claim the vocabulary itself has to be discovered, plus a small pilot where 5 collegues solved them and two off-the-shelf models didn't.
