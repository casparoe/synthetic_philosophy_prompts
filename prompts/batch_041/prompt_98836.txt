I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one of the recurring proposals I find most compelling is the replacement of elected legislatures with randomly selected citizen assemblies—lottocracy, sortition, call it what you will. I would like you to write a short story that explores a problem I have not seen adequately dramatized: the endogenous pool problem. In most defenses of sortition, the legitimacy of the lottery depends on the pool of eligible citizens being fixed independently of the lottery's outcomes. But in any real implementation, the assembly will have some authority over the rules of future selection—over eligibility, exemptions, compensation, and the definition of citizenship. A first assembly can therefore shape the pool from which later assemblies are drawn, not by rigging the draw but by redrawing the urn. The randomness remains perfect; the representativeness quietly becomes an artifact of previous iterations.

Write a short story of roughly 3,000 to 5,000 words that stages this. Possible settings include a city that has run lottocratic assemblies for three generations, a planetary colony, or a digital polity where citizenship is itself a contested status. I would like the story to follow someone whose job is to audit the lottery—a sortition auditor, an election mathematician, a civil servant—who discovers that the formal fairness of the draw coexists with a systematic drift in who is eligible to be drawn. The story should not resolve into simple fraud or villainy; the interesting version is one where everyone acts in good faith and the drift emerges from reasonable decisions, path dependence, and the ordinary human tendency to optimize the rules one is allowed to touch. I am especially interested in the moment when the protagonist must decide whether to expose the drift, knowing that the exposure may discredit a genuinely fair procedure and hand ammunition to the procedure's enemies.

Consider one concrete mechanism the story might use, though you are free to invent a better one: the first assembly, wanting to avoid a permanent class of representatives, enacts a rotation rule—anyone who has served is ineligible for the next two cycles. The rule is passed in good faith. Over time, it produces a pool skewed toward people who can afford to take time off, or toward those who are willing to be drafted, and the lottery's fairness becomes a ritual that legitimizes a de facto selection process. The story should make the conceptual stakes vivid without lecturing. It can include characters who argue about the difference between statistical representativeness and descriptive representation, about whether legitimacy is a property of the draw or of the pool, and about whether a lottery that is fair at every step can be unfair in its trajectory. It may draw on ideas from mechanism design, the anthropology of ritual, and the sociology of institutions, but it should not require the reader to know any particular literature. No citations, no bibliography; I am looking for narrative, not a survey. The ending should be ambiguous but earned—an image or decision that leaves the reader with the problem rather than a slogan.
