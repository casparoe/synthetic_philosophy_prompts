I run curriculum design for a mid-size hospital's clinical training program — nurses, phlebotomists, a few allied health roles — and I keep running into the same fight with our educators. Every time something goes wrong on the floor, someone proposes a training fix: a module, a seminar, a video, a case-study session. And it's basically random which of those we pick, because nobody asks what kind of learning problem it actually is. I recently read about Dennett's four grades of creature — Darwinian, shaped by evolution; Skinnerian, shaped by reinforcement in the animal's own lifetime; Popperian, able to run the experiment inside its head so the hypothesis dies instead of the creature; and the fourth one, where the mind is loaded up with external thinking tools it didn't invent and couldn't have come up with alone — and I think that's actually the diagnostic I've been missing. But I need it as a procedure, not a lecture.

So: give me a decision procedure my educators can run on a single learning objective, in maybe fifteen minutes, without reading any philosophy. Something like — here's the objective, here are the observable signs of where trainees currently are, here's the intervention the grade actually calls for. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and hands off to me, and where it's just a judgment call wearing a procedure's clothes.

Some things I specifically need it to handle, because these are where we keep messing up:

- How do you tell a Skinnerian problem from a Popperian one from the outside, in a clinical setting? What do you actually watch or look at? I don't want the answer to be "ask whether they understand why" — everyone says they understand why.
- Can you ever skip a grade? My instinct is you can't teach someone to test hypotheses about a procedure they can't yet perform at all, but I want that stated as a rule with the exceptions marked, not as a vibe.
- I want a rule for deliberate demotion, and I think this is the part nobody talks about. There are things I want our staff to do automatically, without deliberation, under pressure, at 3am — hand hygiene, two-patient identifiers, the sepsis trigger. By this framework that's deliberately pushing a skill back down to Skinnerian even after they could reason about it Popperian-style. When is that legitimate, when is it infantilizing, and how do I tell the difference?
- And the fourth grade: we hand people checklists, dosing calculators, decision trees, our EHR's hard stops. A trainee who's fluent with those tools can look more competent than they are. I want the procedure to say what to do about tool-dependence — when it's a genuine achievement and when it's a masking problem — and how that changes what we assess.

Then run it on three objectives, showing your work at each step:

1. Hand hygiene compliance at the point of care.
2. Recognizing that a patient is deteriorating and escalating appropriately.
3. Using our weight-based dosing calculator for a high-alert drug.

One of those three should come out with an answer I won't like — I suspect it's the second one, because I've already spent money on a case-study curriculum for it and I'm attached to it, so if the procedure says that was the wrong instrument, say so plainly and tell me what I'm actually supposed to cut.

Also be honest at the end about what the procedure can't do. I know the grades aren't properties of a person — the same nurse can be Gregorian with the infusion pump and Skinnerian with a difficult conversation — and I'd rather the procedure have a line in it about scoping the grade to the objective than have it quietly pretend people are one thing. Tell me where it will misfire, and which of its outputs are really just me making a values call about what kind of practitioner I'm trying to produce.
