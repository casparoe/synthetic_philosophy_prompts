Okay so I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a book, and I've stalled on one character. He's a philosopher, retired, the kind of man who is generous in a way that somehow feels worse than being rude, and there's a dinner scene where he defends a position I only half understand — something about how knowing a thing entitles you to ignore evidence against it. My grasp of it, roughly: if you know P, then you also know that any evidence against P is misleading, since P is true and evidence against a truth is pointing the wrong way; and knowledge is closed under obvious consequences, so you know the counter-evidence is misleading; and if you know a piece of evidence is misleading you're entitled to let it sit there and not move you. He says this isn't a loophole, its just what knowledge is, and that it only looks like a paradox if you've already assumed knowledge has to be insecure. The thing that really needles me is that the principle seems to eat itself — he claims to know the principle, and by his own rule that would mean he gets to wave off any argument against it. I think he's a monster and I think the argument is a con, but I can't articulate why fast enough to write him a rebuttal that sounds like him.

So play him. Stay him the whole time. Sharp, not a strawman, happy to concede small things (he'll cheerfully admit he might be wrong about which cases count as knowledge) but never the main claim, and he has to be able to say why he isn't just rationalising, because that's the accusation he's heard his whole life. One paragraph a turn, talk like a person at a dinner table with wine in them, not like a journal. No names, no citations, no "the literature says" — he'd never, and I can't use any of it anyway. Don't break character unless I say stop.

I'll swing first. His license is unusable. Nobody can tell from the inside whether they know or just really, really believe, so "ignore it, I know" is a card everyone gets to play, and the wife who's certain her husband didn't do it holds exactly the same card as the person who's actually right about him. A rule you can only apply if you already know the answer isn't a rule, it's a permission slip with a diploma. And even granting the license — knowing a bit of evidence is misleading doesn't mean it shouldn't move you, because knowing isn't being certain, and he'd be the first to admit he's wrong sometimes. So where does that go wrong, exactly.
