ok so I'm writing a big feature on Molinism — the view that God knows, before deciding anything at all, what every possible free creature would freely choose in every situation it could be put in. middle knowledge, counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, the whole apparatus. my editor wants a poem to close the piece and I really dont want a rhymed paraphrase of the doctrine, I want an actual poem. heres the ask: a sestina. the six end-words should include the three things the Molinist says God knows — the necessary stuff, the counterfactuals, and what he ends up decreeing — plus three more that you pick and that earn their place. why sestina: the end-word order is fixed before a single line of it exists, and the lines still have to be written into that order, freely, one at a time. thats the thing I want a reader to feel without being told — the pattern settled in advance, and the choices inside it still being real choices. then the envoi, where the six words get crushed down into three lines, should arrive at the objection that kills a lot of people on this view: what makes those counterfactuals true, given that the situations theyre about never happen? what are they true in virtue of? dont answer it, just land there and let the poem stop on the problem instead of solving it. one more thing, and this is the part I care about most: give one stanza to the voice of a version of me who took the other job, the one whose choice is true of him in a world that never got made. subtle though — no names, no jargon, nothing that sounds like a philosophy seminar. someone whos read the debate should probalby recognize the middle knowledge problem sitting underneath it, and someone who hasnt should just find it a good poem. no footnotes either, my editor would kill me.
