I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and at work I've ended up running a small multi-agent thing: a few copies of the same model with different system prompts and seeds, all fed the same documents, told to argue it out until they agree on stuff like whether a contract clause is risky. I keep noticing they dont agree, and someone on my team keeps saying "they just see it differently, that's fine" and I keep thinking that is not actually fine.

The thing I can't shake is the Aumann agreeing-to-disagree result, at least my half-remembered version of it: if two agents are equally good at reasoning, have pooled everything they know, and each knows the other is equally good, they can't end up with different beliefs about the same question. Which I take to mean persistent disagreement after full information sharing is never a genuine difference of rational belief. It's a bug, or a hidden asymmetry, or one of them is sitting on information. So "they just see it differently" is always a cop-out.

Treat that as my position and tell me what would count against it.

I want the load-bearing assumptions, and for each one, what would have to be true for it to fail and what that world actually looks like. I already know about the shared-prior one, and something about agents needing to be logically omniscient, and I'm sure there's more I wouldn't know to ask about, including stuff that only bites for systems like mine. For each assumption, say whether the break is live for a real setup or just a logical possibility nobody hits.

Then rank them. Fatal to my position, merely awkward, or ignorable technicality. And for each, would someone who actually knows the result concede it or wave it off? I'm suspicious the honest answer is that the theorem is true and vacuous for finite agents, because nothing finite satisfies the antecedent — if that's where you land, say it plainly, don't soften it for me.

One thing I'd find genuinely persuasive, if it exists: a concrete case where two agents are as rational as each other, have shared everything, and still rationally disagree, with neither of them irrational or uninformed. If that can't be built, tell me why the impossibility is the whole point rather than a dodge.

And please don't lay out both sides and leave me to pick. Land somewhere — the position survives but means less than I thought, or it's a math result that says nothing about my setup, or something in between. Pick one, defend it, and say how confident you are and what would change your mind.
