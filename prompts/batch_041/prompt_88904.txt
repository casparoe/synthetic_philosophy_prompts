At the end of next month I am giving the closing toast at the retirement symposium for my dissertation advisor, a quantitative historian who spent forty years arguing that long-run political instability runs in long cycles — a slow buildup of immiseration, elite overproduction, and fiscal strain that ends in a rupture — and who, by the end, was considerably less sure of that than his reputation suggests. The room will be split: half modelers who have read every paper he wrote and will catch every reference, half historians who think the whole enterprise is curve-fitting with footnotes. Late in his career he arrived at a rule I want the toast to honor, and it is the rule that should constrain everything the toast is allowed to claim: a cycle is only real if a stranger can re-derive it from the same evidence without being told which century contains the crisis, and when I once asked him how many of his own cycle claims had survived that test, he declined to say. Please write the toast — five minutes, warm, landing for both halves of the room — so that it never asserts anything his rule forbids: no claim that history repeats, none that his models saw anything coming, none that the cycle is a law of nature, though the modelers should hear the respect in it and the historians should hear that he doubted his own machinery, and it should leave everyone with what the rule actually costs, that a cycle which cannot be re-derived blind is a description rather than a prediction. Continuous prose only, please: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter.
