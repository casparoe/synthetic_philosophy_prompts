In a seminar I attend on language and thought, a disagreement keeps returning that I cannot get to the bottom of. I do fieldwork with a small speech community whose language, so far as anyone has documented it, has no single word that translates 'promise'; speakers who want the idea use a cumbersome periphrasis involving an oath and a witness. A colleague concludes from this that the speakers lack the concept, and therefore cannot be bound by promises as we are. When pressed, he reaches for the maxim that 'you can't think what you can't say', as though it closed the matter. My suspicion is that this one sentence is doing several quite different jobs in his argument, and that at most one of them actually licenses the step from 'no word' to 'no concept' to 'not bound'.

I want a disambiguation rather than a verdict on his substantive claim about promises. Set out the distinct precise theses that 'you can't think what you can't say' could be expressing, and say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false. Among the distinctions I would like you to bring to bear: what 'say' ranges over (the absence of a lexical item, of a construction, of any means of communication at all); whether 'think' means entertaining a content, believing it, or knowing it; whether the impossibility is metaphysical, psychological, or merely practical, given paraphrase, coinage, borrowing, or the possibility that the concept is present but unlexicalised; whether the claim concerns an individual speaker or a whole community; and whether the operative notion is language specifically or conceptual scheme more generally. Then tell me which reading my colleague is most likely invoking, and whether any reading would in fact support his inference.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which reading, if any, actually supports the move from 'no word' to 'no concept' to 'not bound' — then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
