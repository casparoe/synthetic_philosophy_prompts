Im the chair of the ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital and we keep improvising on the worst death-determination cases. Our state law defines death as irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, or irreversible cessation of all brain function including the brain stem, which is fine until someone's on ECMO, or we're running a donation-after-circulatory-death protocol, or a family insists grandma isnt dead yet. I need a decision procedure the on-call intensivist can actually run at 3am without me: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and hands off to the committee or a judge, and where irreversibility is quietly doing all the work. Build it on the layering that actually matters here -- definition of death vs. criterion vs. the bedside tests -- and be straight about which steps are just hospital convention dressed up as metaphysics. Then run it on three cases: a patient on VA-ECMO with no intrinsic cardiac activity; a DCD donor five minutes into the standoff; and a patient in a persistent vegetative state whose family says she's already gone. One of those should come out in a way the family and probably the nurses will hate -- dont soften it. Finish by saying what the procedure cant settle and who has to sign off when it dead-ends (we use two attending signatures, for what its worth). Dont hand me a menu of options -- plant a flag at each fork and say why. First sentence should be the procedure's backbone, then the rest; no preamble.
