An argument I have been working through on my own begins with something completely ordinary and ends somewhere I cannot accept, and I suspect it proves far more than it should. It begins with the plain fact that a sentence is not just a list of its words and a chair is not just a heap of its parts; it ends by denying that anything is ever genuinely one thing. Here it is:

(1) Some things really are one thing made of parts — a sentence, a chair, a molecule.
(2) A whole is more than its parts; something has to make the parts into a single thing, or they stay a mere heap.
(3) Whatever does that unifying work is either itself one of the parts, or something added over and above the parts, or nothing at all.
(4) It cannot be one of the parts, since a part is just one more item that would itself need to be joined to the rest.
(5) It cannot be something added over and above the parts, because then we would have the parts plus that added item, and something further would have to join those into one thing, and then something further still, and so on with no end. The whole never actually comes together.
(6) It cannot be nothing at all, because if nothing makes the parts one, then they are a heap and not a whole.
(7) So nothing is ever genuinely one thing; there are only heaps. Nothing says or means anything — not a sentence, not a thought, and not the argument I just gave.
(8) But the argument above does seem to say something, which cannot be right.

I would much rather keep (1) than accept (7). The one escape I can see is to reject (5), insisting that the unifying item needs nothing further to attach it to the parts — but that seems to leave it optional, so that any random collection counts as a genuine whole, which is just as bad as (7). Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up actually cost me?
