I am a novelist — three historical novels, the most recent ten years in the writing — and in the spring I have been asked to give the closing plenary at a conference of social historians, where I intend to argue something I expect to go over badly: that the slow structures Braudel taught historians to see — the grain of a coastline, a rainfall regime, the price of a staple, the half-century decay of a currency, the northward creep of a crop — are rendered more honestly in fiction than in the monograph, because a novel can make a reader inhabit a duration that no argument can make her believe. My book spans forty years in a silt-laden river delta at the edge of an agrarian empire; its four viewpoint characters never meet, and what they share is not an event but a set of slow variables, so that each chapter carries a formal device I have come to call the under-ledger — a brief, unglossed column of figures (rainfall, a tithe, the ratio of two currencies) that no character ever reads and the plot never explains. The wager is that the reader should feel structure the way one feels weather, and not as fate; my editor, who would have preferred I write a spy novel, calls this the least commercial idea I have ever had.

The room will be mostly early modernists trained in microhistory and the history of everyday life, for whom the word structure has long sounded like an excuse for erasing the people they have spent careers recovering; two environmental historians who build climate series and will want to know what my rainfall column is actually a proxy for; one Braudel loyalist who has taught the three registers of duration for thirty years and will hear a novelist's appropriation; and a historian of the Annales itself who is allergic to structural vocabulary in any mouth but his own. Prepare my question-period prep sheet. I want the objections this room will actually raise, not the ones a literary festival would raise — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, phrased in their vocabulary rather than mine, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft.

Tell me what to concede at the outset rather than defend, which of my claims I should quietly narrow before someone narrows them for me, and which objections have real force and ought to change how I open. I should also say plainly that you are not to send me off to read anything: I don't know the historiography well enough to cite it and I would rather concede ignorance in the moment than bluff, so any reply that depends on a title or a name is useless to me. Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks — I have a suspicion about which it is, but I want it in your words, together with the best thing I can say if it comes.
