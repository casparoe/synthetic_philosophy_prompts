So I've been messing around with Hofstadter's typogenetics thing from GEB — the toy system where strands of A/C/G/T get read off as enzymes, enzymes bind to strands and do stuff like copy, cut, insert, switch to the complementary strand, and so on. The whole point, as I understand it, is that a strand can be read as a description (it codes for enzymes) and also be the thing those enzymes act on, so you can get a strand that, through its enzymes, ends up producing a copy of itself — a "self-rep." And Hofstadter frames that as the DNA/Gödel isomorphism: the self-rep is supposed to be the typogenetic analogue of a Gödel sentence that says something about itself.

Here's what I want explained, and I want you to be careful about it: is that analogy actually tight, or is it doing a lot of hand-waving? Specifically, in Gödel's construction the self-reference is achieved through arithmetization — you code statements about the formal system as numbers inside the system, and then a sentence ends up asserting its own unprovability. In typogenetics you don't seem to have that same move. You have a strand that codes for enzymes, the enzymes mechanically transform strands, and eventually you get two copies of the original. Nothing obviously "refers" to anything. So is the self-rep really self-referential in the Gödelian sense, or is it just a fixed point of a dynamical process — more like a quine than like a Gödel sentence?

And if it's more like a quine, what does that do to the claimed isomorphism? I've seen people say the typogenetics chapter is where GEB's DNA/formal-system analogy pays off, but I'm not sure whether the payoff is a real structural correspondence or just a suggestive vibe. Like, which parts map onto which: the strand onto a formula, the enzymes onto rules of inference, the ribosome onto the proof-checking procedure? And does the mapping break anywhere important — e.g., in a formal system deriving a theorem doesn't destroy the premises, but in typogenetics an enzyme acting on a strand can cut it up or delete bases, so the "theorem" gets consumed. Does that difference matter, or is it incidental?

Also: the self-reps I've seen tend to work by complementing the whole strand and then complementing the complement, which feels like a trick rather than a deep self-referential loop. Does that make them less interesting? I guess what I'm really asking is what the typogenetics example is actually supposed to show about self-reference and self-replication — whether the two are the same phenomenon, or whether typogenetics quietly equivocates between "self-replicating" and "self-referential." Walk me through where the equivocation (if there is one) lives, and what a cleaner version of the isomorphism would look like.
