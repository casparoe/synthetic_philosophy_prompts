I'm in a graduate seminar on historiography, and I've gotten stuck on an argument in Nietzsche's essay on the uses and abuses of history for life, the second of his Untimely Meditations, from the 1870s. My German isn't good enough to work from the original, and I don't trust my memory of any published English rendering, so what follows is my own plodding paraphrase rather than a quotation; part of what I want to know is whether the paraphrase itself is where my confusion starts.

As I understand him, Nietzsche distinguishes three ways a person or a culture can stand toward the past. There is a monumental mode, which seizes on a handful of great figures and deeds and holds them up as proof that greatness is possible again; an antiquarian mode, which reveres and preserves what one has inherited simply because it is one's own; and a critical mode, which judges the past, hauls it before a tribunal, and tries to break with it. The monumental mode, he says, is what the person of action needs, because action requires examples. But he also describes it as systematically deceptive: it falsifies the past by letting the grubby conditions and the failures drop away, it does violence to the actual record, and it is dangerous precisely to the strong and the great of the present, since it invites them to measure themselves against an idealized past and conclude that they are late and small. And yet he insists that forgetting, and this selective and admittedly unjust relation to the past, is a condition of life and action; a culture that tried to be purely historical and purely truthful would, on his account, be enfeebled.

What I can't get straight is how those two strands are supposed to fit together, and what they imply for the slogan that history is written by the victors. If monumental history is the history of the powerful, and if it is admittedly a distortion, does Nietzsche mean that all historical knowledge is just a projection of whoever holds power? That would make the slogan trivially true but also oddly flat, since on that reading even the slogan's critics would be writing in the service of some interest. Or is his point narrower and stranger: that there is a real distinction between history that is true and history that is life-serving, and that the two can come apart, so that the victors' version might be false while still being the version a healthy culture needs? I'd like help unpacking this, in particular three things. First, what work is the monumental/antiquarian/critical distinction actually doing, and why does Nietzsche think the monumental mode harms the very people it is for? Second, does his account of forgetting and of a selective, even unjust relation to the past commit him to relativism about historical truth, or only to a claim about the uses of history? Third, how would his position respond to the victors slogan — does it endorse it, reframe it, or show that it is asking the wrong question? I'm not looking for a reading list or citations; I care about the structure of the argument and where my paraphrase has smoothed over a real tension.
