For some years I have taught a graduate seminar on risk and rational regulation, and I keep a private ledger of my verdicts in precautionary cases — cases in which someone proposes to restrict a practice before the evidence is conclusive. I have never been able to see whether a single principle underlies the ledger, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. State it precisely, clause by clause, attaching each clause to the case that forces it; say what it implies for cases I have not listed; and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save.

My verdicts are these.

1. A regulator requires an additional chronic-exposure study before approving a new food additive, on the strength of a plausible but untested mechanism of harm. — Permissible.
2. A regulator blocks a promising therapy for a disease that is otherwise fatal and untreatable, on the same grade of evidence. — Not permissible.
3. A city replaces an aging water main before it fails, where the failure would be irreversible for residents and the replacement is cheap. — Permissible.
4. A city declines to switch to a cheaper pipe material because someone asserts that it might leach something, with no mechanism and no precedent. — Not permissible.
5. A worksite prohibits a solvent that employees handle daily, on plausible but unconfirmed evidence of neurological harm. — Permissible.
6. A regulator is asked to ban the retail sale of a consumer product to informed adult buyers, on exactly that same grade of evidence. — Not permissible.
7. A regulator declines to restrict a technology whose plausible, unconfirmed harm would fall on non-consenting bystanders, because the harm, if it ever occurred, would be trivial and reversible. — Not permissible.
8. A regulator restricts an activity whose plausible, unconfirmed harm would fall on future persons, who cannot consent, even though the harm would be reversible. — Permissible.
9. A licensing board forbids a competent adult from undertaking an irreversible personal gamble that harms no one else and that he has chosen with full information. — Not permissible.

I have tried to formulate the principle myself, and what I keep landing on is this: precaution is warranted when the proposed exposure is irreversible for the exposed even if reversible for the exposer, and it is not warranted otherwise. The thought behind it is that reversibility is not a property of an act but a relation — reversible for whom, and who holds the power to reverse it. I suspect the formulation is too neat, and that cases 5 and 6, which I cannot distinguish to my own satisfaction, will expose the problem. Please test it against every verdict above, repair it if it can be repaired, and if it cannot, tell me which verdict I should surrender and what the repaired principle then says.

There is one case I keep reversing, and I would like your principle, or its silence, brought to bear on it. A municipality must decide whether to permit a common agricultural spray whose plausible, unconfirmed harm would fall chiefly on children in the surrounding area; the harm would be reversible; the affected families are neither the beneficiaries of the practice nor the parties deciding it; and a substitute exists that is more expensive, the added cost falling on the growers rather than on the families. I cannot tell whether my hesitation tracks the children, the incidence of the cost, or the mere availability of a substitute, and I would like to know which of these your principle makes load-bearing.

Finally, apply the principle to a case I have not listed: a firm proposes to release an irreversible technology to a population that has consented to it in advance, where the harm is plausible and unconfirmed and would not surface until after the exposed can no longer change their minds.

If no single principle fits, make the trade-off explicit: give me the simplest principle that saves all but one verdict, then the more complex one that saves all of them, and name the additional clause and the price it exacts.
