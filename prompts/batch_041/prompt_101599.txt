At last month's workshop on the philosophy of economics, two of my colleagues fell into an argument about revealed preference that we adjourned without settling, and I would like you to referee it. The provocation was a graduate student who, having just learned that a utility function is merely a representation of a consistent pattern of choice, announced that he would henceforth choose deliberately intransitively — preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A — in order to demonstrate that he had preferences the theory could not capture.

One colleague said the stunt proved nothing. Utility functions do not name a quantity in the head, he argued; they are bookkeeping devices for coherent choice, and the student has merely chosen to be incoherent, which is to say he has no well-defined preference ordering at all. If anything, the student had made the theory's point for it: preference just is the pattern of choice, and where the pattern gives out there is no preference to speak of.

The other replied that this made the theory immune to the evidence that should count against it and, worse, made utility maximization vacuous. If preferring is just choosing, she said, then no one can ever choose anything other than what they most prefer, and the economist's normative apparatus — the claim that a policy raised welfare, that a choice went against someone's interest — collapses. We say she chose it because she preferred it; if the preference is just the choice, we have explained nothing.

He answered that the explanatory content lies not in the single choice but in the coherence of choices across situations — that someone who takes tea when coffee is on offer will not take coffee when the prices shift in a way that still leaves tea affordable. That is a substantive and falsifiable prediction, and not circular, since it constrains later choices by earlier ones. The student rigging his answers to be intransitive is exactly a falsifying case.

She said the inference from choice to preference quietly assumes that the agent knows what he is choosing. If I take the larger slice believing it to be the smaller, my choice does not reveal a preference for the larger slice; it reveals a mistake. So choice reveals a preference only against a background of belief, and belief and preference are states the choice does not by itself identify. The stunt shows the same thing: the student chose intransitively because he believed it would embarrass the theory, so his preferences may be perfectly consistent and his belief simply mistaken. You cannot read the preference off the choice.

He replied that one may attribute a belief along with the preference; choice then reveals the pair. Nothing in the theory forbids that, and the point was never to inventory the contents of the mind but to predict choice. The utility function remains what it always was, a representation of the ordering that shows up in behavior, and the fact that we must know what the agent took himself to be choosing in order to say what he preferred does not turn the preference into an extra hidden object.

She said that was precisely the concession she wanted. If the choice is compatible with any number of belief–preference pairs, then the pair is not fixed by the choice, and preference is doing work that choice cannot do; the mental state is indispensable, and the operationalist slogan that utility names nothing beyond choice is false. Once we admit beliefs and preferences as the things we really attribute, the interesting question — whether the student has a consistent preference he is thwarting or an inconsistent one he is expressing — cannot be settled by looking at his choices at all, which was the case he was sure was open and shut.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: say who is right about which specific point, rate each move, and note what both of them are missing — including whether the graduate student's stunt has any bearing on the theory at all.
