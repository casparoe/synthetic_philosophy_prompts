I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science who has been embedded for two years in a computational neuroscience lab, and part of my role is running the lab's monthly methods hour. Next month I have to take on the topic that keeps derailing our paper meetings: when a system we've modeled starts doing something we didn't predict, we can never agree on whether we're looking at the same mechanism under changed conditions, a different mechanism that happens to generate the same phenomenon, or our original mechanistic story simply being wrong. One colleague insists that if you can swap a component and the behavior survives, it was never the same mechanism; another insists that if no component can be swapped without losing the behavior, it was never a mechanism at all. The room is a dozen experimentalists and modelers -- quantitatively strong, philosophically allergic, and convinced that "same mechanism" is a phrase that explains itself. I don't need you to explain the concept to me; I have a rough destination in mind. I need a plan for how to teach it. Assume a whiteboard, fifty minutes, and no assigned reading.

Work out the order in which the ideas have to arrive so that the question "is this the same mechanism?" stops sounding trivial and starts sounding underdetermined. My own guess is that the sequence has to move from what a mechanism is (parts, activities, and how they are organized) to what a mechanism is for (accounting for a phenomenon against a contrast class) before the individuation question can even be posed in a way that has an answer -- tell me whether that dependency is right, and if the real hinge lies elsewhere, where. Specify the single carrying example that should do the work for the whole hour and why it beats the alternatives. I'm inclined to use a limp: a person walking with a limp after an ankle sprain versus a person with a visually identical limp from a neurological problem, on the theory that everyone can picture it, it has one salient behavior, and the parts are shared while the causal organization differs. Tell me whether that example will bear the weight, what it lacks, and what I should use instead if it won't. Then give me the misconceptions this audience will bring or form, in the order they will surface, and the precise point in the sequence where I should head each one off. I expect "same parts, same mechanism," "same behavior, same mechanism," "if it's a different mechanism then our first explanation was just wrong" (i.e., missing the possibility of mechanism variants), "levels of description are arbitrary, so the question is arbitrary," and the opposite error, "individuation is just definitional, so it's whatever we decide." Say where each comes from, and tell me which of them the limp example accidentally reinforces. Add the check I should run at the end that would show whether they can now pose the question properly rather than recite a distinction, and tell me at what point in the hour I should first let the phrase "the same mechanism" out of my mouth.

I'd also like the hour to end with something usable in our meetings, so evaluate the two-step rule I want to leave them with: first fix the contrast class -- what change are we explaining, relative to what reference behavior -- and then ask whether a single intervention on a shared component moves the reference behavior and the anomalous behavior in the same direction. Tell me what that rule quietly assumes, whether I should present it as a settled test or flag it as contested, and how much of that debate to expose at this level. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover in fifty minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing. Please write the plan in American English.
