Grading a stack of intro Indian-epistemology midterms and I want a second opinion on one before I commit to a number, so I don't have to walk it back when the student shows up at office hours. Heres the question:

Q3. Nyāya counts four pramāṇas. Buddhist epistemologists in Dignāga's and Dharmakīrti's line count only two, and they also deny that upamāna is a separate means of knowledge — the idea being that whatever the comparison case delivers is already delivered by perception, memory and inference. (a) Set out the Nyāya account of upamāna using the standard example. (b) Assess that reductionist line: does upamāna survive as its own pramāṇa, and does a parallel argument work for śabda? (c) A student in the seminar, Dev, claims that nothing arriving from a non-sentient device — a properly calibrated instrument, a printout from a machine — could ever count as śabda for Nyāya. Is Dev right, and what does your answer show about how Nyāya draws up its list?

And here's what the student handed in:

"Nyāya's four pramāṇas are perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna) and verbal testimony (śabda). Upamāna is the means by which we come to know the relation between a word and the thing it stands for. The stock case: a man is told that a gavaya is like a cow; later, in the forest, he sees an animal resembling a cow and forms the cognition 'this is the kind of thing the word gavaya applies to'. What produces this knowledge is the resemblance between the gavaya and the cow, together with the earlier instruction.

The reductionist says all of that was already available from perception (the animal in front of him), memory (the instruction) and inference, so nothing new is added and upamāna is not a separate means. I think this fails for upamāna but goes through for śabda. The point of the gavaya case is that what gets known is a convention — which word a community attaches to which thing — and no amount of perceiving a resemblance gives you that by itself. Testimony is different: it really does look like an inference from the speaker's reliability to the truth of what he says, and the two-pramāṇa scheme handles it fine.

Two consequences follow. First, since Mīmāṃsā holds that the validity of a cognition has to be established by a further external check, collapsing śabda into inference takes away their main reason for holding the Vedas to be authorless. Second, if śabda is defined as the utterance of a trustworthy person, then Dev is right that a machine printout isn't testimony; the Naiyāyika would have to file the knowledge we get from it under perception or inference, which means the list of four is doing less work than it looks."

Can you give it a grade out of 10 and then the specific comments you'd write — what's right, what's wrong, and what's merely arguable? I want the number to be defensible point by point, not a vibe.
