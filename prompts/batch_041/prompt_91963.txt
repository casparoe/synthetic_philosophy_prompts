I am a retired experimental biologist — thirty years in the field, mostly on the sensory and behavioural ecology of insects — and for the past four years I have been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than discipline. I have a half-formed idea that I would like turned into a short research proposal: two pages, for a small interdisciplinary fellowship that would buy me roughly nine months of half-time work. I would like you to write that proposal.

The idea, as well as I can put it at this stage. I have been reading the literature on teleosemantics — the project of explaining what it is for a state to have content by appeal to the biological functions of the states and mechanisms that carry it. I am sympathetic to the ambition and uneasy about the execution. The standard strategy, as I understand it, is to fix content by what a state or trait was selected for: a mechanism represents whatever it was selected to respond to, and it misrepresents when it responds in a way that departs from that selected-for condition. But I spent my working life in a domain where selectionist claims are almost never available with the precision the theory needs. I could rarely say what a structure had been selected for; I could say, with far more confidence, what it contributed to a capacity, and how it figured in a regulatory loop — what its set point was, what counted as an error, and how the error was corrected. I suspect the theory has been running together two quite different questions — what gives a state or structure a function at all, and what fixes the determinate content of a state — and that it has quietly assumed a selectionist answer to the first in order to secure determinacy for the second. I want to know whether a non-selectionist notion of function, built out of the internal normative structure of a mechanism (set points, error signals, the distinction between working and malfunctioning that is internal to a control loop), can carry the weight the selectionist notion has been carrying, or whether it collapses. My worry is that any mechanism has some set point, so the account may turn out to be too cheap — and I do not yet see how it would distinguish the content of a state from the mere reliable co-variation it stands in, or make room for misrepresentation.

What I want from the proposal:

1. A precise statement of the question, sharp enough that it has a determinate answer and could in principle come out either way. I am aware that "can X ground Y?" is usually too vague to be a question; please make it specific.
2. Why the existing positions leave it open — that is, why someone could accept the current state of the debate and still not know the answer. Be honest here: if the question is already settled, say so, and if it is really several questions, say that too.
3. The method: what I would actually do, in order, over nine months at half time. I would like this to be concrete — what gets read, what gets written, what gets tested against what — and to include at least one place where the argument is checked against a real biological case, ideally one in which the selectionist story and the control-loop story come apart.
4. What a result would look like in either direction, and what would count as the project failing rather than merely concluding negatively.
5. A realistic scope. I am one person with no formal training in the area, working part time. Tell me what I should refuse to attempt, and which part of the idea is most likely to prove either trivial or already done.

Two further constraints. First, do not give me a reading list or citations; I can find the literature myself, and I would rather the proposal be judged on its argument than on its bibliography. Refer to positions by their content rather than by name where you can. Second, I would like the response to be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it. I have already once mistaken a live disagreement for a received view, and I do not want the proposal to present a contested position as the consensus. If my framing of the current state of the debate is wrong, say so.
