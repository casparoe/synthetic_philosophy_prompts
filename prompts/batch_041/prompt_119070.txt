I am a second-year PhD student in cognitive science, and I have been asked to run a single ninety-minute session of our department's methods induction for incoming graduate students — a mixed group of psychologists, linguists, and computer scientists, none of whom has done any formal work on concept learning. My assigned topic is the induction of a concept from a handful of labelled examples, and I intend to build the session around Bongard-style problems: in the classic format, six figures on one side and six on the other, with the learner required to find the property that separates the two groups, given no further instances, no queries, and no feedback beyond the division itself. Do not explain concept induction to me; I understand the material well enough. What I want is a plan for teaching it. Specifically:

1. The order in which the ideas must be introduced, and why each depends on the last. Assume the students arrive with no vocabulary for any of this. Tell me which notions — hypothesis space, version space, a prior over hypotheses, underdetermination — earn their place inside ninety minutes and which I should merely name and defer to their own reading.

2. The one example that should carry the session. I am tempted by the easy opening, convex figures on the left and concave on the right, because everyone solves it in ten seconds; but I suspect it teaches them that the answer is always a property of the individual figure, which is precisely the misconception I later have to dislodge. Argue me out of it or into it, and if you would rather I used something else, design it: give me both sets, described concretely enough that I can draw them on a handout, and tell me what the example must contain — and what it must conspicuously lack — for the later points to land.

3. The hard point I actually want to reach, and the reason I volunteered for this topic: that the twelve figures never determine the rule, only a family of rules that agree on them; that what makes one member of the family the intended answer is that a human being finds it obvious; and that the format therefore measures a culturally and developmentally specific sense of obviousness while presenting itself as a test of pure reasoning. I want the students to feel the force of this rather than be told it. Where in the sequence does it belong, and what is the smallest move that makes them feel it? I have in mind a variant in which the rule is not a property of any single figure but a relation holding among the six figures of each set taken in order, so that no amount of staring at one instance can help. Advise me on whether that lands as a revelation or merely as a trick; if you judge it a trick, design a better one.

4. The misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I am already anticipating: that there is one right answer and that finding it is a matter of luck; that a feature merely correlated with the division is the rule; that the rule must be a detector of a feature rather than a relation (above, inside, more than, alternating); that a rule which fits one side need not be checked against the other; and that being able to state the rule and having found it are the same achievement. Add whatever I have missed.

5. How I will know by the end that they have grasped the idea rather than acquired a knack for the puzzles. I want a check that separates the two, and I want it to be something they do rather than something they say — ideally a single task that a student with only a knack fails and a student with the concept passes.

6. What I should refuse to cover at this level, and how to say so without leaving them feeling short-changed. The obvious candidate is the formal apparatus for comparing hypotheses by simplicity; tell me whether naming it and stopping there is defensible in this setting.
