My roommate Dana and I just spent two hours arguing about character traits and I honestly can't tell which of us is the confused one. She's read all the situationist stuff, I haven't, and somewhere in the middle she wrote this out on the back of an envelope and told me to find the bad step. Here it is the way she had it:

(1) A character trait is supposed to be a stable disposition. An honest person is honest across alot of situations, not just the ones where honesty is cheap. That's what separates a trait from a habit or a reflex.
(2) So if someone really has a trait, the trait and the behavior have to be reliably linked. Put them in any two situations that both call for the trait and by and large they'll do the same thing in both.
(3) But for any pair of situations that both call for, say, compassion, there's a pair of experiments where some tiny situational nudge flips the behavior. You're told you're running late. There's a stranger sitting right there. You're in a slightly better mood than usual. She brought up the divinity students who were on their way to give a talk and stepped over a guy lying in a doorway needing medical attention, because someone had told them they were late. Nobody's religious beliefs or personality scores predicted who stopped.
(4) So for any person and any alleged trait, there's a situation where the trait doesn't show up.
(5) A disposition that can be made to disappear isn't really there.
(6) So nobody has character traits, and virtue ethics is a description of a fiction.

Here's what's bugging me. I feel the pull of it, but the same steps seem to eat everything, not just traits. Every disposition has something that stops it from firing -- you can keep sugar from dissolving by wrapping it up. And her own situations seem to go the same way as my traits do: being in a hurry doesn't mean anything independent of what people do when they're in a hurry, so if the behavioral regularities are too weak to license a trait, they look too weak to license a situation-type either. When I said that she just shrugged and said fine, then there are no dispositions at all, and kept going, which I'm definately not convinced is an answer.

So: which step do I reject, and what does it cost me? I'd most like to keep (3) -- I think the experiments are basically right and I don't want to be the guy who explains them away -- and I want to keep "there are character traits" in some form, though I'll give up the idea that a trait has to show up in every kind of situation. What I don't want is traits so local that honest just means honest-when-the-boss-is-watching, and there's nothing left for virtue ethics to be about. Tell me where the argument actually breaks, why the bad step looked so plausible, and then price the repairs -- which one costs me the least and what exactly do I lose by taking it? And please don't send me to a reading list, I don't have the books here, I just want to know whether the envelope argument works.
