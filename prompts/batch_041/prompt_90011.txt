I'm an undergraduate in a course on collective responsibility and climate ethics, and for the satire unit I have to submit a parody in the form of a fake legal document. Please write roughly 600 words of a decision by a fictional international tribunal, in the deadpan register of a real administrative order: caption, findings of fact about one ordinary person's small acts and omissions, and a numbered disposition holding her personally liable for the whole of a collectively produced harm, at a sum she could never pay. The humor has to come from the jurisprudence being right rather than from jokes — the tribunal should reject a but-for test of causation in favor of something like a necessary-element-of-a-sufficient-set test, explain why the imperceptibility of her contribution does not reduce her share, treat her omissions as no less causal than her acts, and dispose of the usual defenses in turn: that the harm would have occurred without her, that countless others did the same, that the harm arises only past a threshold. Close with a short dissent reaching the opposite conclusion, so the document stages the dilemma rather than settling it, and add one clause extending the holding to voting, where a single ballot is found to be the cause of a government's entire policy. The target is the assumption shared by the personal-responsibility discourse and its excusers alike — that responsibility must be proportional to one's own causal contribution. A reader who knows the literature on overdetermination, thresholds, and the many-hands problem should find the piece fair; a reader who doesn't should still see what is being sent up.
