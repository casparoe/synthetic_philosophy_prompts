I am a PhD student in metaphysics writing on persistence, and I want to press a question about the endurance/perdurance dispute that I have not seen posed in exactly this form: whether persistence itself might come in degrees. The familiar contrast is between treating persistence as strict identity across time, which is all-or-nothing because identity admits of no degrees, and treating it as the holding of suitable relations among temporal parts, which is at least the sort of thing that could hold more or less fully. I would like the comparison of the two theories run through that lens, rather than through the standard inventory of objections.

Three things need addressing. First: is the idea of graded persistence coherent, or does it quietly equivocate between persistence and something else, such as overlap between distinct things or the continuity of a process? Second: if it is coherent, does it favour perdurantism, or can the endurantist absorb it, by locating the degrees in the properties a thing retains rather than in its identity, or in a relation other than identity, or by allowing intrinsicality itself to come in degrees? Third: there is a well-known reductionist line of thought on personal identity according to which what matters in survival, namely connectedness and continuity, comes in degrees and strict identity is not what matters; does that thought support one of the two theories, or is it orthogonal to the endurance/perdurance dispute, in which case the interesting question is whether that dispute was ever about what matters?

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each point should isolate one respect in which the two theories diverge or converge on this question and say which of them comes out better, and why.
