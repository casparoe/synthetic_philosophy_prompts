I am a novelist, and the book I am finishing turns on an argument I have put in the mouth of its protagonist, a set theorist, and have lately come to distrust. He offers it as a proof that the different sizes of infinity are an artifact of notation, and I need to know whether the scene in which he is answered is a scene in which he is answered or merely interrupted.

He argues as follows. (1) Any first-order theory in a countable vocabulary that has a model at all has a countable model; so if the standard axioms of set theory are consistent, they have a model whose domain contains only countably many things. (2) That model satisfies those axioms, and the axioms prove that there are uncountable sets; so within the model there is a set the model calls uncountable. (3) But every set in the model is drawn from the model's countable domain, so from outside, everything in the model — including the set it calls uncountable — has only countably many members. (4) Whether a set is countable therefore depends entirely on the model you are standing in, and there is no further fact of the matter. (5) If that holds for countability it holds for size generally, and Cantor's theorem becomes a fact about what a formalism can express rather than about sets. (6) So the hierarchy of infinities is not a discovery about a subject matter; it is a feature of our notation, and nothing more.

A colleague tells me the flaw is that the model's membership relation is not the real membership relation, and that the model simply leaves out the bijection that would witness countability. My character has an answer ready: he says this is not a correction but a restatement, since the real membership relation and the omitted bijection are themselves available only relative to a further model, and the regress never touches ground.

I would rather keep (1), (2) and (3) — I am assured the first two are theorems, and the third seems to me plain arithmetic — than keep (6), which is what my character wants to believe. Which step do I give up? Why did it look compelling enough to survive this long? And what does giving it up commit me to — what must I believe instead, and what do I lose by believing it? Answer in no more than four hundred words of continuous prose, without headings or numbered lists; I want to hear the reasoning in a voice, not as a table of options.
