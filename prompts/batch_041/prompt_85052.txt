A colleague at work claimed over lunch that when you can save either one person or five, saving the five is not obviously the right thing to do, and I have spent the week writing his position out as a proof so that I can find the bad line. I am a software engineer rather than a philosopher, so I have set it out the way I would set out a failing test case.

(1) If two people have equally strong claims on me and I can save only one of them, I have no reason to prefer either; I may save either, and tossing a coin is the fairest way to settle it.
(2) How strong a person's claim on me is does not depend on how many other people also have claims: if Alice's claim is as strong as Ben's, it is as strong as Ben's whether or not five others are drowning alongside them.
(3) So in a choice between saving Alice and saving five strangers, none of the five has a claim any stronger than Alice's; there are simply five of them.
(4) That there are five of them is a fact about a group rather than about any one of the five, and because it gives none of the five a claim stronger than Alice's, it cannot be a reason for me to prefer the outcome in which the five live.
(5) So I have no reason to prefer saving the five to saving Alice, and saving Alice is permissible.
(6) Nothing in the argument turns on how lopsided the numbers are, so saving one stranger rather than a thousand comes out permissible on exactly the same grounds.
(7) Nor does it turn on my not knowing which of the six I am: even if I might be Alice or one of the five, I still have no reason to prefer the five, even though a coin toss would give each of us a one-in-two chance of surviving while saving the five would give me five chances in six.

I would like to keep (1) and (2), and I can live with the coin toss; what I cannot accept is (6). Tell me which step to give up, why it looked innocent, and what else goes with giving it up — in particular, whether I am then forced to say that a surgeon may sacrifice one patient to save five. If instead you think the conclusion should be accepted, say what that commits me to. Finally, say where your own reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your verdict.
