So I'm a nurse on a surgical ward and I've been chewing on our pain protocol. Every patient gets asked to rate their pain 0 to 10 and we titrate the morphine to that number — teh number is the target, basically. An 8 gets a push, a 2 doesn't. But for people who can't self-report (dementia, babies, anyone sedated on a vent) we switch to a behavioral scale: grimacing, guarding, tensing, do they pull away. And everyone keeps repeating the line that pain is whatever the patient says it is, like the report settles the matter.

Then I read about these patients — pain asymbolia, I think it's called — who after a right parietal or insular stroke will say yes, the pinprick hurts, it's sharp, it's intense, but it just doesn't bother them. They don't flinch, sometimes they laugh, they say it isn't unpleasant at all. The sensation is all there and the awfulness is gone. (They also get hurt a lot, apparently, because they don't pull back.)

So what does our practice actually presuppose about why pain is bad? When we chase the number it looks like we're treating the intensity as the thing that matters, as if badness just is more sensation — more burning, more bad. But the asymbolia cases seem to drive a wedge between intensity and awfulness. Does the protocol secretly assume that pain's badness is baked into the sensation itself, or that intensity and unpleasantness always travel together, or just that killing the signal kills the suffering? Which rival story — bad because it's unpleasant, bad because it thwarts what you want, bad because it's a warning that your body is damaged — does it rule out, and which could it live with just as well? And does the behavioral scale for the non-verbal patients presuppose something else again, that the badness is really the distress and the wince rather than the report? Is the ward running two theories in one coat, or only one? And if we swapped in a view where the badness of pain comes apart from its sensory intensity, what would actually have to change on the floor — stop treating the number and treat distress instead, handle the asymbolia patients differently, or nothing at all?
