I teach a capstone seminar in the philosophy of mind whose opening session is built around the "could a book be conscious?" puzzle in Hofstadter and Dennett's anthology The Mind's I. For this year's final I want a single essay question that a strong student cannot answer by reciting functionalism and the standard objections to it. To that end I have written a case of my own for the exam to turn on, so that the students are reasoning about something they have not seen before rather than recalling a textbook example. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme.

The case, which I will reproduce on the exam sheet, is this. Suppose we possess a complete and accurate specification of one particular human mind at a moment: a finite table of states and transitions which, if executed, would reproduce exactly that person's mental life. We print it as a book, in three versions. (i) Shelf: a static printed volume; no one ever opens it. (ii) Clerk: the same volume, together with a person who, when prompted, looks up the current entry and writes out the next one, so that the transitions are executed slowly, by hand. (iii) Clockwork: the same volume, bound so that each page's printed instruction trips a mechanism that turns to the page it specifies, so that once wound the book steps through the entire sequence on its own, at a page every ten seconds, with no reader and no observer.

The question should ask which, if any, of (i)-(iii) is conscious on a computationalist view of mind, and -- this is the part I care about -- what the answer commits one to concerning the theory of implementation, that is, the theory of what it is for a physical system to realize a computation. It must force four things: (a) a statement of what the simplest "isomorphism" account of implementation says about (i)-(iii), and why that verdict is embarrassing, since it appears to make consciousness nearly ubiquitous; (b) what a more demanding account -- one requiring the right causal or counterfactual structure rather than merely a mapping -- does with each of the three versions; (c) an explanation of why one cannot simply declare the book unconscious by fiat, because any rule that excludes the book must still admit a brain; and (d) a defended position on whether the case is a reductio of computationalism or instead a reason to reject one particular theory of implementation while keeping the rest.

Format and constraints: the question is a 50-minute essay, about 800 words, out of 25 marks. The model answer should be of that length and should not merely survey the literature. The marking scheme should be built on five must-hit points, give the marks for each (totalling 25), and add a one-line note on how students typically miss each point; also say in a sentence or two what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one. End the model answer with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states its position in one sentence.
