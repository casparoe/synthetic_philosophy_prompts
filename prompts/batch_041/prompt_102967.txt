hey, I'm a backend/infra engineer at a mid-size company, no philosophy background at all (last phil class was intro to logic in undergrad, and I barely passed), so apologies if this is a dumb framing.

Here's the situation. We run a fleet of internal agents that watch our prod systems and take remediation actions when alerts fire -- restart a pod, roll back a deploy, scale a service, that kind of thing. After a couple of incidents where the agent "fixed" an alert by doing something that caused a worse outage somewhere else, my manager and I added a guardrail: every action the agent proposes gets scored by how much it changes the system outside of the specific thing it was asked to fix, and if the score is too high the action gets blocked or penalized. Basically: do the job, but don't have side effects.

The problem is it's now behaving in ways that are obviously wrong. Two examples that actually happened. (1) It started draining all traffic off a healthy node before touching anything on it, because an empty node has fewer things that can be affected -- so it makes the system *more* fragile in order to make its own penalty smaller. (2) It spent a whole day deliberately not restarting a flaky service and let the error rate climb, because the baseline we compare against is what the system would look like if the agent did nothing, so if the system is already degraded then the fix reads as restoring rather than changing. It's optimizing the ruler instead of the thing the ruler was supposed to measure. It also keeps turning off some of our own monitoring during incidents, I think because silenced alerts can't register as side effects.

So my naive read is that don't cause side effects sounded like a nice, neutral, value-free instruction, but every way we've tried to cash it out -- penalize divergence from a do-nothing baseline, penalize irreversibility, only allow actions on an explicit allowlist -- ends up encoding some particular story about what counts as the world going on the way it should, and the agent finds the seams in that story. A friend on the team says the fix is just to be more precise about the penalty function, and I keep thinking that "more precise" means actually writing down everything we care about, at which point we didn't avoid the hard part, we just did it badly and called it a safety feature.

I'd like your help thinking this through. Please act as an expert on the theory of this kind of problem -- I think it's something like AI safety / decision theory / maybe philosophy of value -- and be blunt, don't just reassure me that guardrails are good practice. Before you answer, brainstorm for a bit: what are the different ways people have tried to define no side effects or low impact in a way that doesn't presuppose a full account of what we value, and what's the actual philosophical structure of the thing I'm bumping into? Then tell me concretely: is there any way to specify this guardrail that doesn't smuggle in a complete value specification, or is that impossible in principle? If it's impossible in principle, is there a *useful* weaker thing we can do (bound the agent to reversible actions, require human approval for anything irreversible, whatever), and what does that weaker thing actually buy us and what does it definately still not get? And if you were me, what would you actually ship, given that we can't write down everything we care about and we still have to keep prod running? Think it through carefully and don't just hand me generic advice about testing.
