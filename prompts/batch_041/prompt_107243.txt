My roommate and I have been arguing about whether tables and chairs are real (she's been reading philosophy on her own and its gone to her head), and she has an argument I can't shake but also can't accept. Here it is:

(1) Either some things ever compose a further thing, or they never do.
(2) If they sometimes do, then some collections are objects and others are just collections, so there's a fact of the matter about where the line falls.
(3) That line has to be drawn by the collections themselves -- nothing outside a collection (who put it together, what we call it, what it's for) can make it the case that it's one thing.
(4) So the line is drawn by some feature of the collection: the parts being bonded, or in contact, or moving together, or cooperating, whatever.
(5) But take any feature like that and I can build you two collections differing only by a hair -- one grain fewer, one bond a shade weaker -- where obviously if one of them is a single object then so is the other. It's absurd that a table winks out of existence because you sanded off a molecule.
(6) So no feature can draw the line.
(7) So composition never happens: no tables, no chairs, no stars, no you and me.

I'm fairly sure (3) is right and I don't want to give it up -- I'm not going to say a chair exists just because we decided to call it one. But I can't swallow (7), and I have a nagging feeling the same reasoning would prove there are no clouds and no countries, which smells like a reductio. So where does this go wrong -- is it (5), is it the jump from (6) to (7), or is something smuggled in earlier? And whichever I give up, what am I buying into?
