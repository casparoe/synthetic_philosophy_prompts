I have been keeping a list of verdicts about which of two possible worlds is better, in cases where the worlds differ only in how many people live and how well their lives go. I would like to know what principle, if any, generates the list, which entries it forces me to abandon, and what it implies about cases I have not considered. Throughout, welfare is measured so that 0 is the line between a life worth living and a life not worth living, negative values are lives worse than nothing, and 100 is a very good life. I mean the numbers literally.

1. The same 1,000 people, all at 100, against the same 1,000 people all at 90. The first is better. No hesitation.

2. Alpha: 1,000 people at 100. Beta: 10,000 people at 10. Both come to the same total welfare, and I prefer Alpha without hesitation. I would rather have fewer people living well than many people living adequately.

3. Alpha-plus: Alpha, plus another 1,000 people at 2, with the original 1,000 entirely unaffected. I do not think Alpha-plus is worse than Alpha. Adding lives that are worth living, while making no one worse off, does not strike me as a loss; if anything it is a small gain.

4. Omega: ten billion people at 1. I think Omega is far worse than Alpha, not marginally but by a wide margin. I have heard this called the repugnant conclusion and I find it repugnant.

5. I know that one can get from Alpha to Omega by a long series of steps of the kind in (3), each of which looks harmless to me. So (3) and (4) cannot both stand as stated, and I do not know which to give up. That is one of the things I am asking.

6. Alpha-minus: Alpha plus 1,000 people at −5. Clearly worse than Alpha, and worse by more than Alpha-plus is better than Alpha. The asymmetry feels right: adding a miserable life is a cost; adding a barely good life is at most a small benefit.

7. Alpha-star: the same 1,000 people as in Alpha, all at 101. Alpha-plus-a-billion: the same 1,000 people at 100, plus a billion people at 1. I prefer Alpha-star. A single extra point for the people already here beats a billion lives that are only just worth living. This is the verdict I hold most firmly and the one that causes the most trouble.

8. Duration. Gamma: the 1,000 people of Alpha each live 100 years at 100, and then no one ever lives again. Delta: the same 1,000 people each live 10,000 years at 100. Delta is better, but nothing like a hundred times better. The hundredth year of a good life adds much less than the first.

9. Number of lives against length of lives. Epsilon: one person at 100 for a million years. Zeta: a million people at 100 for one year each. Both contain the same total amount of life at the same level, and I prefer Zeta. It matters to me that a million people get to have a life, not only that a certain quantity of life gets lived.

10. The case I keep going back and forth on: a billion people at 5, against the 1,000 people of Alpha at 100. My gut says Alpha, and says it firmly. But 5 is comfortably above the line, and there are a billion of them, so I cannot see what entitles me to Alpha.

My working guess at a principle: count each life by how far its welfare rises above the line at which life stops being worth living, sum over lives, and ignore how the lives are distributed. But that is totalism in disguise, and it delivers Omega, which I reject. I have also tried weighting each life by something that falls off sharply as its welfare approaches zero from above, but I cannot make the duration cases or the asymmetry in (6) come out right.

What I would like: the principle, or the small family of principles, that best fits (1) through (9); a precise statement of each clause and the case that motivates it; whether my verdicts pin down a level of welfare such that lives below it count against a world and lives above it count for it, and where that level falls; what the resulting view says about (10), and whether I should accept its answer or revise my gut; its verdicts on cases I have not listed, including whether the dead and the not-yet-born count, whether non-human animals count on the same scale, and whether a world of very many lives each only slightly above the line is ever preferable to Alpha; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the view cannot accommodate, together with the cheapest revision that would make the list consistent.
