I'm a third-year philosophy student and next week I'm giving a paper in our Justice and Borders seminar defending Dummett's take on immigration. The line I'm running is that Dummett's case isn't mainly a humanitarian one about refugees (though he cares about that) — it's that a state's power to exclude people from its territory is a coercive power, and coercion has to be justified to the people it's exercised against. Since would-be immigrants are people, you can't just point to the fact that they weren't born here, or that they'd change the culture, or that they might cost the economy a bit. The burden of proof is on the excluder, and it's a heavy one. The seminar is a mix: two libertarians who support open borders for economic reasons and think Dummett is too soft on state power, a communitarian who thinks states have a right to protect their way of life, a utilitarian who worries about wages for low-skilled domestic workers, and the tutor, who's a Kantian cosmopolitan but thinks Dummett's demands are unrealistically strong and that he underestimates the importance of political community. I definately want a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from these people, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they'd do, with a two-minute reply for each, a note on where the reply is weak, what I should concede up front, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Don't give me reading lists or citations — just the arguments and how to answer them. Also, if you think there's a tension between Dummett's anti-realism about truth and his confident moral claims about immigration, flag it, because I'm not sure how to handle that if it comes up.
