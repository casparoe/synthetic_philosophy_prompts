I've been roped into giving the commencement address at the small liberal arts college where I teach, and I'm stuck in a way I'd rather not be. Two things are colliding. First, I spent this whole spring teaching a seminar on the ethics of belief, Clifford versus James, and it's eaten my brain: Clifford thinks you're never permitted to believe a thing you don't have evidence for, and that's a moral failing, not just a sloppy one; James thinks that for a certain kind of question, one that's genuinely live for you and forced and important and that can't be settled by evidence either way, you're entitled to let your hopes make the call, and that refusing to decide is itself a decision. Second, last year's commencement speaker stood on that same stage and told the graduates to believe in themselves, to leap, to trust it would work out, and I sat there fuming because it's a blank check written to four hundred twenty-two-year-olds, and it's also, I'm fairly sure, the only actually useful thing anyone said to them all day. They have to pick a city, a job, a person, a life, this month, on evidence none of them has yet, and I'm supposed to stand up there and say something true about that.

So write me the address. The audience is the graduates, plus their parents (some religious, some very much not), plus the board and two trustees who'll be listening for whether I'm quietly undermining the school's values. It should be warm, it should get a real laugh at least twice, and it has to give them something they can actually use without my handing them the blank check. The one move I've come up with is a distinction I've been calling load-bearing beliefs versus weather beliefs: a load-bearing belief is one other people are standing on, so you owe it evidence, because when it gives way somebody else falls; a weather belief only changes how you carry your own life, and for those you get to go with what you need, because there's nothing to check them against anyway. My hunch is the graduates have been told every belief is load-bearing, and that's exactly why they're paralyzed. But I might be wrong about the whole thing, so if you can build the speech on a better distinction, do that instead and tell me why mine doesn't hold up.

Keep it under 800 words, and finish with a last line I can actually say out loud without sounding like a fortune cookie.
