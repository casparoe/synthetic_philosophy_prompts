I'm assembling a small anthology of poems about decision theory for a reading group that has spent the term on puzzles in which the agent's own perspective is part of the problem, and I want to close the session on the absent-minded driver with a poem that does some real work rather than merely gesturing at the puzzle. The setup I have in mind is this: the driver is on a road with two intersections, and at each one she may either take the exit or drive on. She cannot tell the intersections apart — at either one, she does not know whether it is the first or the second. Taking the exit at the first is worth nothing; taking it at the second is worth four; driving past both is worth one. Because she cannot distinguish the intersections, she has to behave the same way at both, so the plan that would obviously be best — drive past the first and take the exit at the second, for a payoff of four — is not a plan she can carry out, and the best she can actually do is to take the exit with probability one third at each intersection, which yields four thirds on average. What interests me is that the same piece of reasoning — if this is the first, drive on; if this is the second, take the exit — is both plainly correct and completely useless to her, precisely because it turns on a distinction she is in no position to make, and I would like the poem to register that without spelling it out.

So: a poem in her voice, somewhere between eighteen and twenty-four lines, in whatever form or non-form serves it, with one two-line refrain that appears exactly twice. The two appearances must be word-for-word identical, and the poem must be built so that a reader cannot tell from the refrain alone whether it marks the first intersection or the second. That indistinguishability is the whole point, and I want it to be a property of the poem rather than something the poem explains to us. Let a reader feel the arithmetic — nothing, four, one, and the compromise between them — without the poem becoming a word problem, and end on the gap between the plan she can state and the plan she can execute, rather than on a resolution or a consolation.

Please give me just the poem, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; if you want to add a sentence or two of commentary, keep it to a short paragraph of ordinary prose after the poem rather than a list of notes.
