I'm writing a short piece for a reading group on the Hart–Devlin debate and I'd like you to tear into it. Here's the argument in rough form — I havent written the prose out properly yet.

Everyone reads the debate as one question: may the law enforce morality? Hart says only to prevent harm to others. Devlin says a shared morality is itself part of the social fabric, so its erosion is a harm, and the law may step in to stop it. Fine. My claim is that this framing hides a split. There are two ways to be a legal moralist. Backward-looking: the act was wrong, therefore it may be punished — the wrongness is the reason, full stop. Forward-looking: the act may be restricted because restricting it keeps the shared morality from decaying. I want to argue Devlin is only ever the second, and that almost all the standard objections — you can't show a shared morality exists, disgust isn't a reason, the law shouldn't enforce mere opinion — are aimed at the first. If I'm right, the debate is a stalemate for a boring reason: the critics keep hitting a target Devlin isn't standing on.

The second half is where I get nervous. Forward-looking legal moralism has a symmetry problem. The mechanism is content-neutral: protect the prevailing morality, or it frays and something worse comes. But that argument seems to work just as well for a legislator trying to keep alive a morality I think is rotten. So the view either has to smuggle in a substantive standard for which moralities are worth conserving, in which case it's no longer really about conservation, or it bites the bullet and says any widely held morality may be legally propped up. I want to say it has to take the first horn, and that this quietly turns it into a liberal view — but I can't tell if that's a real result or just me redescribing Hart with extra steps.

Comments please. Does the backward/forward distinction actually do work, or am I relabeling something obvious? Is the symmetry problem as bad as I think, or is there a principled asymmetry available (say, only moralities that are already self-sustaining get to count as 'the shared morality', so the law is never the thing doing the conserving)? And where is the weakest link in the second half — I suspect it's the move from 'the law can't pick which moralities are worth keeping' to 'so it's liberal,' but I can't see the gap clearly.
