I'm building a teaching session around Fitch's paradox of knowability and I want a dialogue as the centrepiece. Write me a dialogue between two people: one who accepts the knowability principle — every truth is knowable in principle — and thinks it's the propper statement of anti-realism, and one who's a realist wielding Fitch's proof as a reductio against that whole position.

Make them actually walk through the proof together instead of trading slogans: the move where you take a truth that nobody knows, feed it through the knowability principle, and end up with the possibility of someone knowing that it's unknown — and why that's supposed to be impossible, since knowing a thing is unknown requires knowing it, which kills the "unknown" part. The realist should press that the anti-realist is therefore stuck with all truths actually being known, which is plainly false, so something has to give.

Don't let the anti-realist just wave it off. Have them try the real escape routes and argue about which one survives: denying that knowledge distributes over a conjunction (can you know p-and-q without knowing each of them?), restricting the knowability principle to the propositions you can consistently suppose someone knows — the ones where knowing them isn't self-undermining — and going intuitionistic, where the conclusion comes out weaker and stops being absurd. The realist needs a decent reply to each of those, not just a restatement of the reductio, and should push that the restriction looks ad hoc and makes the principle trivial.

They should really listen to each other, shift their ground a bit as the argument goes, and end somewhere unresolved but sharper than they started. Keep it tight — no scene-setting, seperate the voices clearly, just the argument.
