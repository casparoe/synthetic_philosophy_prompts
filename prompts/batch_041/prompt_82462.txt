I'm prepping a session for a small reading group on the weirder corners of epistemology, and I want to build it around a comparison of two paradoxes that I have a hunch are secretly the same paradox in different clothes — or maybe mirror images of each other. The first is the dogmatism paradox. The gist: if you know something, then any evidence that later turns up against it has to be misleading evidence, since knowledge can't be of something false — so you'd be entitled, maybe even obliged, to just wave that evidence away. Which is a bonkers conclusion, but the little argument that gets you there is annoyingly hard to wriggle out of. The second is the preface paradox. An author can rationally believe every single claim in her book, and also rationally believe — based on her own track record — that at least one of those claims is wrong. Same ingredients in both, as far as I can tell: knowledge or belief, the live possibility of error, and the question of what you're allowed to do with evidence. But they seem to pull opposite ways. One tells you you're so reliable you can blow off counterevidence; the other tells you you're so unreliable that you should expect to be wrong somewhere, and yet keep believing all of it anyway.

Here's what I want from you: a real comparison, not two encyclopedia entries stapled together. Are these two faces of one underlying problem about fallibilism, or do they actually come apart? Is the important difference just that the dogmatism case involves knowledge while the preface case only involves belief, and does that difference do alot of genuine work, or is it a dodge? And here's a wrinkle I want you to take seriously: try to mash the two together. Picture someone who knows a very large number of separate things — dogmatism says she may ignore any evidence against any one of them, while the preface-style reasoning says she should expect that at least one of them is false. Does anything survive that collision, and if so, what?

I'd also like you to test both paradoxes against a case where two people have exactly the same evidence, but only one of them counts as knowing — does the dogmatist get to ignore the counterevidence while the other person doesn't, and is that a result anyone can actually live with? Then tell me where each paradox really gets its grip, and whether there's a unified diagnosis of both or whether they need seperate treatments. I'm especially curious whether you can accept the preface conclusion (which seems obviously right) while blocking the dogmatism conclusion (which seems obviously wrong), without just gerrymandering the rules until the answer you wanted falls out.
