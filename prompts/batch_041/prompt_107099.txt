Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal experiences are caused by physical events but are themselves causally inert — effects that produce no further effects. The standard objection, commonly framed as the paradox of phenomenal judgment, runs roughly as follows: if experiences cause nothing, then my belief that I am now in pain, and my utterance that I am in pain, cannot be caused by the pain; so my judgments about my own experiences are not causally connected to the very facts they purport to report; and the epiphenomenalist's own assertion that experiences are epiphenomenal is just such a judgment. The view thus appears to saw off the branch it is sitting on.

I want you to argue the contrary thesis: that this objection is not fatal, and that a disciplined epiphenomenalist can answer it. Defend that claim under the constraints below, and be explicit about where the defense costs the epiphenomenalist something.

First, distinguish three things that the objection tends to run together — the phenomenal state itself, its representational content (what the experience is an experience of, or the way it presents things), and the physical state that both causes the experience and realizes whatever content it carries. Then argue that the epiphenomenalist may hold that it is the physical state, not the experience, that causes the judgment and confers its content, so that the judgment can be genuinely about the phenomenal state through its physical correlate while the phenomenal state itself remains causally idle.

Second, confront the sharpest version of the objection, the one that does not depend on any controversial theory of content. If epiphenomenalism is true, then a physical duplicate of you with the same brain events would make all the same judgments — that it is in pain, that it seems to see red — while having no experiences at all. The very physical state that causes your true judgment also causes the duplicate's false one; the cause is the same whether or not the fact the judgment is about obtains. Argue whether this makes the epiphenomenalist's phenomenal judgments unjustified, or whether a reliabilist or otherwise externalist account of justification lets her retain them.

Third, take up the demonstrative case, often thought to be the hardest: not the general judgment that one has experiences, but the particular judgment that this experience, right now, is such-and-such, where the judgment seems to depend on the very token the epiphenomenalist has rendered inert. Say whether your defense extends to it, or whether the paradox survives in this narrowed form.

Finally, draw out what your conclusion implies for the evidential status of first-person reports — whether they can be evidence for the existence of consciousness at all — and state plainly what remains genuinely troubling about epiphenomenalism even if the self-stultification charge fails.
