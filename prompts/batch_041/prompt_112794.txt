For a graduate seminar whose running question is whether the "problem of logical omniscience" names one problem or several, I need a constructed case rather than a discussion, and I want it built on an apparatus that is not the usual toy. Please use the following, making it precise as you go. Fix a formal language with atomic sentences; call the topic of a set of sentences the set of atomic sentences occurring in them; call a consequence topic-preserving when it has a derivation that uses no atomic sentence outside the topic of the premises, so that it introduces no new vocabulary; and call an agent topic-omniscient when her knowledge is closed under topic-preserving consequence.

Construct a single scenario, one paragraph, in which one finite agent — a person or a machine, nothing science-fictional — satisfies all of the following at once. She is fully closed under the consequence relation she actually faces, and closed under it for principled rather than merely accidental reasons. She is nevertheless not omniscient in the sense that the standard epistemic-logic closure principle demands. Her shortfall is not owed to any limitation of memory, time, or computational power, and not to her failing to grasp her premises. The same agent, at the same time, on the same information, gives opposite verdicts about whether she knows a specified proposition, and only one thing varies between the two verdicts.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. State the proposition, the consequence relation, and the two readings of knowledge; give each reading's verdict and the reasoning behind it; identify the structural feature that does the work. Then run the checks: say whether the relation you used is a genuine consequence relation (reflexive, transitive, monotone) and whether the case collapses into the familiar "awareness" or resource-bounded treatments of the problem, which I do not want it to be a relabeling of. Rerun the scenario with the one varying feature removed and report whether the agent becomes omniscient or not, and why. Finally, say how a defender of the idealization would resist the case — that knowledge simpliciter is closed under full consequence, so the agent's shortfall shows only that "knows" was being used loosely — and give the best reply to that defender, including an honest statement of where the reply is weakest. The case is for a paper arguing that omniscience is a modeling choice rather than a single defect, so a case that stipulates its verdicts rather than generating them from the specified closure conditions is useless to me.
