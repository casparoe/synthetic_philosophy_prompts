I have spent roughly a decade living in Germany and the United States, and one difference between the two places keeps pulling me back into thought. Where I lived in Germany, a rule was followed because it was the rule — even when no one was watching, even when the rule struck everyone as pointless — and ignoring it, however harmless, felt like a small betrayal of an order we all kept up together. Among the Americans I know, the instinct runs the other way: a rule binds only if it makes sense to the person following it, one's own conscience is treated as the last word, and quietly disregarding a senseless rule is not misconduct but good sense, sometimes even a small point of pride.

I am not after a description of national character, and I do not want a set of anecdotes. I want to know which of these two outlooks actually has the better of it as a moral matter, and I want the argument rather than the observation. A few things I would like you to take seriously along the way:

- Is there a real virtue in following a rule simply because it is a rule — a respect for the others who carry the same burden, or for the shared order itself — or is that only obedience dressed up, a way of being spared the trouble of judging for oneself? Underneath this, I suspect, lies a disagreement about what a person is: an autonomous chooser whose dignity lies in making the law for himself, or a participant in a shared arrangement whose dignity lies in holding up his end.
- When a rule is not merely pointless but unjust, does the case for following it collapse entirely, or does something of the rule-following virtue survive?
- The American instinct says that if I harm no one, the rule has no claim on me. Is that a defensible principle or a convenient one? Does it change if enough people reason the same way and the rule quietly erodes?
- The German instinct treats the person who follows his own conscience against the rule as a troublemaker, or worse, as someone taking advantage of everyone else's restraint. Is that judgment fair, or does it wrongly subordinate the individual to the collective?

Please write an essay that works through these considerations and reaches a clear verdict about which outlook has the better of it — or, if the honest answer is that the verdict depends on something, tell me exactly what it depends on and defend that. Do not settle on the comfortable note that each side has its strengths; I want an argued conclusion, even if it is a conditional one.
