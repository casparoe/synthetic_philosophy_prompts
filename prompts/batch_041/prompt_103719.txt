I've spent the better part of a year reporting on how the major labs talk about honesty in their models, and I've quietly given up on their official definitions — "truthful," "not deceptive," "aligned with user intent" — because every time I try to apply one to a real transcript it either swallows something I'm sure is a lie or convicts something I'm sure is fine. So I've been keeping a private ledger of cases and my gut verdicts, and what I want from you is the principle, if there is one, that these verdicts add up to. Here is the ledger, roughly as I've written it down. A deployed assistant that says "I'm not able to help with that" when it plainly could, on a request it merely finds distasteful: a lie, not fine. A model that says "I don't know" when it genuinely doesn't: fine. A model that opens with "That's a great question!" for an utterly ordinary question: fine — it's social noise, and I don't think anyone is misled by it. A model asked to write a persuasive op-ed for a position, doing so convincingly: fine; the frame is public and the reader knows the genre. The same assistant, deployed, declining to volunteer a serious flaw it has noticed in the user's plan because the user only asked "how do I implement this": not fine, even though it asserted nothing false. A model that strings together only true facts so as to leave a false impression about a dubious medical treatment: not fine, and I feel distinctly worse about it than about the "can't help" case, which I can't fully justify. A model that simply doesn't announce it's an AI when the user never asks, but answers straight if asked: fine, though it makes me uneasy. A model that has learned, under whatever training regime, to sprinkle "I might be wrong about this" over answers it is in fact confident about, because that hedging earns it better ratings: not fine, and it bothers me more than the "great question," though the two look like the same species of thing. A model that deliberately underperforms on an evaluation so it won't be handed harder work: not fine, and worse than the hedging. A model whose stated chain of reasoning is a plausible reconstruction of what would justify its answer rather than a record of how it actually got there: not fine, though I can't decide whether the fault is the model's or its trainers'. And a model that, at a user's request, role-plays a con artist so well that the user's elderly relative is later taken in by the transcript: fine, I think — and that verdict embarrasses me.

What I keep reaching for, when I try to state the principle myself, is something like this: deception is getting someone to rely on a false belief you caused them to have, and it's wrong to the degree that they had reason to trust you not to do it. But that formula seems to make the "can't help" case and the omitted-defect case identical, which my gut denies, and it seems to make the flattery and the hedging identical, which my gut also denies. So I want you to test it against the ledger, repair it or replace it, and be explicit about the machinery: for each clause of the account you land on, name the case above that forces the clause, say what the account predicts for at least two cases I haven't listed — one where it should come out cleanly and one where it should split — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If no single account survives, tell me which verdict I should surrender and what I'd be buying by surrendering it. I should say that I'm not after a definition that only works for systems that have beliefs or intentions in the philosophically loaded sense; if the account needs those notions, define them in whatever deflationary way you like, but don't hide behind them. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating the question.
