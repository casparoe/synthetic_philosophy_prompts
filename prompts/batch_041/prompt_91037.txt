I'm a 4th-year PhD student in bioethics and I'm TAing the med-ethics seminar this term. I've got a stack of short-answer responses to grade and I want a second opinion on one of them, because I can already feel myself being too generous. Here's the question and the student's answer.

Question (10 pts, short answer):

Ms. K, 74, was diagnosed with early Alzheimer's four years ago. At that time, while still judged to have decisional capacity, she signed a directive refusing "all artificial nutrition and hydration and any life-prolonging treatment" in the event she became unable to recognize family or communicate. She attached a short note saying she couldn't bear the thought of "living on as a shell." She is now in a memory-care unit, does not recognize her daughter, speaks little, but appears calm, smiles at music, eats when fed, and when a nurse once mentioned the feeding tube said "no, I want to stay." She has been judged to lack decisional capacity. Her daughter wants the directive honored; the attending physician thinks the current contentment and the verbalized wish to live should carry more weight.

Discuss whether the directive should be honored, and what work the idea of the "authority of the earlier self" is doing in your answer.

Student's answer:

This case is a clash between two ways of locating authority in a life. On one side is the earlier self, the competent Ms. K who wrote the directive; on the other is the present Ms. K, who still has some experiential life and who at least once said she wanted to keep living.

Dworkin's distinction between experiential and critical interests is the standard starting point. Experiential interests concern how a life feels from the inside — comfort, pleasure, engagement, pain. Critical interests concern whether a life goes well as a whole, judged by the person's own convictions about what makes it worth living. For Dworkin the later, demented Ms. K is no longer really a person in the relevant sense — she's a biological organism with experiences, not someone with a life to shape — so there is no competing claim coming from her side at all. The directive binds because the earlier self's autonomy extends forward in time and governs the later self; that's what precedent autonomy means. Since Ms. K wrote it with capacity and it reflects a settled conviction about the shape of her life, it should be treated as binding.

The main objection is Dresser's. She argues that the demented patient is a different person from the one who wrote the directive, so the earlier self's preferences simply don't apply to her. On this view the directive has no authority at all, and what matters is the present Ms. K's experiential welfare.

I think Dresser's position is too strong. But she's right that we can't just ignore the present Ms. K. She clearly has interests — she enjoys music, she eats, she seems at ease — and the fact that she lacks decisional capacity doesn't mean her apparent wish to live has no weight whatsoever; it just means it isn't autonomous, so it can't override the directive by itself. So the best-interest standard, which asks what would most benefit her now, should be applied alongside the directive rather than instead of it.

Here the best-interest standard actually favors continuing to feed her, because she appears content and has expressed a wish to stay alive. Contentment is a real experiential good, and if the directive is honored she loses it. So I'd say the directive should not be honored in this case, or at least the presumption in its favor should be defeated.

That said, the earlier self's authority isn't absolute. If the directive were vague, or if there were evidence she wrote it without understanding what dementia would be like, its authority would be weaker. Here it's specific and she clearly understood the possibility, so the case for it is about as strong as these cases get. The tiebreaker is that the later Ms. K is not merely a body being kept alive — she's a subject with a present welfare, and that welfare has to count.
