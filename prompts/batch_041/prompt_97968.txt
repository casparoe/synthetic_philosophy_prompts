I'm a philosophy PhD student who runs the logic section of our intro course, and the surprise exam is the one item on the syllabus that reliably gets students arguing in the corridor afterward. I'd like a satirical piece built on it: a formal memorandum, roughly 600 words, from a fictional school district's Office of Examination Integrity to its faculty, announcing a new policy governing "genuinely surprising" examinations. The voice should be deadpan bureaucratic. The joke is that the office takes the students' backwards-induction argument seriously enough to legislate around it, so it tries to guarantee unpredictability by publishing prior assurances that any exam will be unpredictable, apparently never noticing that the assurances are exactly what a clever student would use to predict it. Let the memo chase its own tail by clause: first banning exams whose dates can be inferred, then requiring the office to certify a given date's unpredictability, then addressing what happens when the certification itself is anticipated, and so on until the policy has quietly abolished the thing it was written to protect. I want someone who knows the paradox to laugh at how precisely it is being mangled — the role of the public announcement, common knowledge among ideal reasoners, why the induction seems to collapse at the final day and why that collapse is the contested step — while a reader who has never heard of it can still follow the bureaucratic absurdity. Keep it self-contained and give no citations or reading lists, since I can't assume the reader can look anything up. The target is the practice of treating the backwards-induction verdict as settled; invent the district and its officials, and name no real school or person.
