I run a lunchtime reading group at work — about ten of us, mostly backend and infra engineers, nobody with any formal philosophy training — and we've spent the last few weeks on the MU-puzzle and the jumping-out-of-the-system idea (jootsing, which I'm pretty sure Hofstadter coined, maybe in GEB or one of the later essay collections, dont hold me to it since I read him years ago and half of it is a blur). Anyway the group is doing a closing quiz next week, I'm writing it, and I want one essay question plus a model answer and a marking scheme. Here's my problem. Half the group can now recite the answer — count the I's, they're never a multiple of 3 — and they think they're finished, they think they solved it the same way you'd solve a hard sudoku. The other half keeps saying stuff like isn't this just Rice's theorem, or jootsing is just thinking outside the box right, which I'm fairly sure is missing the point in an interesting way but I cant articulate why well enough to grade it.

So what I want the question to force them to do is explain why that invariant argument is a jump out of the system rather than a smarter derivation carried out inside it — what you are actually doing when you step outside, and why no amount of cleverer rule-shuffling could ever get you there. I'd also like them to notice that an invariant can only ever rule something out and never rule something in, so that even a much better invariant couldn't show MU is reachable — reachability always has to be shown by actually building the derivation — and I want them to say something about what jootsing presupposes about having genuinely been inside the system first (our resident skeptic claims you could hand someone the invariant cold and they'd have jumped out without ever having been in, and I suspect that's wrong but I want the question to make them fight about it). Finally, push them on whether you can ever get all the way out, or whether every joots just lands you in a bigger system that you could joots again, forever, so that outside is always relative to something. Assume everyone knows the four rules and has already seen the standard solution — the difficulty should come from the why, not from the mechanics, so please don't make them redo the proof. Write the question, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (20 total), and for each point give me one line on the specific mistake that costs a student the marks — e.g. treating the invariant as itself a derivation inside the system, or thinking a different invariant might yet show MU is reachable, or flattening jootsing into generic creativity. It's a 45 minute open-book thing, and since they're engineers an abstract-interpretation-flavored analogy will land if it's the right one, but I dont want the question itself to be a vocabulary test.
