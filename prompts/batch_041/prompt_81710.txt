I'm in a graduate seminar on animal minds, and I keep bumping into a claim I don't know what to do with: that sentience comes in degrees, so that a fly has less of it than a mouse, a mouse less than a dog, and thermostats presumably somewhere near the very bottom. The part that stops me is the arithmetic. If sentience really is a matter of degree, then there ought to be some common unit in which all these degrees can be expressed — the way any two lengths can be put in the same units — and once there is a common unit it should make sense to ask how many insects it takes to offset one dog, or how many thermostats to offset one insect. I've even started sketching the scale in units I've been calling sents, with a dog at 400 and a mouse at 40, on the assumption that the numbers are the hard part and the unit is the easy part. But whenever I try to run the numbers something feels off: a thousand ants don't obviously add up to one dog, and I can't say why not without smuggling in something that isn't degree of sentience at all. So my question is how to set this up properly. What is the correct exchange rate between degrees of sentience across species, and what fixes it — is it something we discover or something we stipulate? Does the thermostat come out with a small but genuinely nonzero weight, or is there a true zero somewhere above it, and what determines where it falls?

I'd also like to know what happens at the threshold. A friend told me that if the scale is continuous then any cutoff for moral consideration is arbitrary, since there will always be a creature just below it that is indistinguishable from one just above, and I haven't been able to decide whether that is a serious problem or a trick of the framing. I suppose what I'm really after is how people who take degrees of sentience seriously do the bookkeeping: whether they think in terms of a single scale at all, and if not, what they use in its place.
