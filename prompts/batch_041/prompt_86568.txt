I convene the methods module in a graduate programme in consciousness science, and every year students arrive able to recite the menu of available methods while being unable to say which one they actually accept. I would like a single classification instrument — a decision tree, not an essay — that begins from a fixed commitment and sorts a student into a named methodological position. They should be able to run it on themselves and land somewhere specific.

Fix the root as follows. The student starts by accepting this claim: a science of consciousness should rest on data that are, in principle, available to more than one observer, and should not presuppose either that phenomenal experience exists or that it does not. Every node below the root must be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each must be answered by taking a stand on a concrete case rather than by declaring a label.

The tree must contain branch nodes corresponding to at least these distinctions, phrased as answerable questions rather than as names: whether the subject's report is treated as a window onto experience, as one more piece of behaviour to be explained, or as a fiction the theorist constructs for predictive convenience; whether the interpretation of that report answers to anything beyond the report stream itself — later self-correction, non-report measures, the subject's own endorsement of the reading — or to nothing; whether non-report evidence is allowed to bear on experience itself or only on the capacity to report; whether the method can and should bracket the metaphysics of experience, or whether neutrality is unstable and every method already commits itself one way; and whether first-person access is a trainable, cross-checkable skill or a confabulating process that cannot be calibrated.

Route the student through at least six of these cases: a patient who can move only their eyes and reports a full inner life; a patient who shows command-following neural responses but produces no report; a split-brain patient whose two hemispheres give conflicting reports; a person who confabulates a reason for a choice they demonstrably did not make; a person under dissociative anaesthesia who behaves as if in pain and afterwards reports nothing; and a machine that emits fluent first-person reports.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or be marked 'unnamed' where nobody defends it, together with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Aim for nine to twelve leaves, and ensure that heterophenomenology is among them. The tree must also find room for a position that rejects the root's publicity requirement and one that rejects its neutrality requirement; mark the edge at which a student has to leave the tree to reach these.

Finally, do two things. Note which combinations of branch answers have no occupant, and say briefly of each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Then flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to pull them apart.
