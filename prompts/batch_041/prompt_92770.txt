I host a small philosophy podcast, and the guest for an upcoming episode is a philosopher who has spent several years working on the problem of individual contributions to collective harms. I'd like a set of interview questions that engage her position at the level of detail the topic deserves, rather than letting her restate it.

Her view, stated crudely, has two parts. First, she grants the inefficacy objection in its strongest form: she agrees that a single person's emissions, meat purchases, or vote can make no expected difference to any outcome, and she refuses to rescue individual obligation with a pivotality or expected-value argument that she regards as an artifact of a particular model. Second, she denies that this dissolves the individual's obligation. She holds that an individual act should be assessed not as a difference-maker but as a constitutive part of a harmful practice — the relevant question being not 'would the harm have occurred without me?' but 'is my act part of the causal process that produces the harm?' — and she supplements this with a signaling mechanism: participating in a contested practice, even where the practice would persist regardless, contributes to its continued social acceptability, and that contribution is real though contingent. She takes this pair of moves to preserve individual obligations to reduce emissions, abstain from factory-farmed meat, and vote, without conceding any efficacy to the individual act.

Please draft nine to ten interview questions for this episode, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want the questions to press exactly where the position is weakest, and I want them sequenced so that her most natural deflection — that the objection presupposes the counterfactual test for causation — becomes progressively harder to use as the interview proceeds. Specifically:

- At least one question should press whether causal production without difference-making is a coherent basis for individual obligation, or merely a relabeling of the fact that the act is upstream of the harm.
- At least one should press the signaling mechanism: whether it is an empirical claim, whether the obligation vanishes if the practice would persist regardless, and whether grounding obligation in signaling makes morality hostage to contingent sociology.
- At least one should press proportionality, asking how the account avoids leveling the single consumer with the producer or the slaughterhouse owner when both are equally 'participants.'
- At least one should press the voting case, asking whether her account rescues a duty to vote and, if it does so on non-consequentialist grounds, whether that concedes the inefficacy objection in the climate and meat cases.
- At least one should press whether the account requires a collective agent, and if so whether it lets individuals off the hook by relocating responsibility to the group.
- At least one should press the motivational question: what reason survives the knowledge that nothing one does matters, and whether the account can avoid quietism without covertly reintroducing efficacy.
- At least one should press a circularity worry: that the account makes the wrongness of my participation depend on the wrongness of the practice, which itself depends on the harm the practice causes.

Close with a question about what evidence or argument could count against her view, and make that question one her earlier answers will have made hard to answer cheaply. The questions must turn on the actual content of the position; no questions about how she came to the topic, what she would tell students, or what she is working on next. Do not ask her to recommend reading or to name the literature — the point is to draw out the view, not to compile a syllabus.
