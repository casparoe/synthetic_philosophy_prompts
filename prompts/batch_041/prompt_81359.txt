A book I'm reading on decision theory summarizes the imprecise-probability program roughly as follows:

"Imprecise probability is the view that when you don't know enough to assign a sharp probability, you shouldn't pretend to. Instead of a single number you carry a set of probabilities for each event and report the lower and upper bounds. This is the formalism for Knightian uncertainty — the difference between a coin with a known bias and an urn whose composition you don't know. Ellsberg's paradox is the classic case: people prefer betting on a known 50/50 urn over an unknown one, standard expected utility calls this irrational, and imprecise probability vindicates them by saying that each act should be evaluated by its worst-case expected utility, that is, maximin. The cost is indecision. The deeper worry is that the set of probabilities is itself something you have to choose, so imprecision only relocates the arbitrariness; and since you have to act on some probability in the end, imprecise probabilities are really just a bookkeeping device for the sharp probabilities you'd use if you thought harder. On the plus side, the framework has a clean learning story: as evidence comes in your interval narrows, and learning something you took to be irrelevant to an event leaves its interval where it was."

How much of this should I trust? Please go sentence by sentence and say what a philosopher working on imprecise probabilities would accept, what is a standard simplification, and what is simply wrong — and whether the closing claim that the framework is just bookkeeping for sharp probabilities follows from whatever is accurate. I am especially unsure about the learning claim at the end.
