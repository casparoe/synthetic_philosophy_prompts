Some friends and I have been going back and forth for weeks about the reference class problem — the thing where any probability judgment about a particular case depends on which class of similar cases you count it as belonging to, and nothing in the logic itself tells you which class to pick. I've ended up defending a position I'll state as baldly as I can. There's a fact of the matter in each case, and it's fixed by a rule with three parts: condition on everything you actually know; among the classes compatible with that, take the narrowest one for which you have enough reliable data that further narrowing stops paying for itself; and the class has to be one you could have picked before you saw the outcome, so you can't gerrymander by result. I'll also say I don't think you're required to land on a single class — a weighted mixture is fine, but the weights have to come from the data and not from me. My friends think this is curve-fitting dressed up as a principle, that "reliable" and "enough" are doing all the work and are precisely what's in dispute, and that if I'm honest the rule never forbids any answer, it just makes me feel better about the one I already wanted. I used it last month to put a number on whether a particular one of my students would pass her nursing boards — I had her school's pass rate, her cohort's, her GPA band, her practice-test scores — and I got a number I beleive, which is maybe the problem.

So play it straight and tell me what would count against the view. I don't want the standard taxonomy of proposed solutions; I want the defeaters — the cases, formal results, arguments, and above all results from statistics or decision theory that should move someone who actually holds this, not just the objections critics like to make. What would I have to see for the rule to be refuted rather than merely under-specified? Does it predict anything its rivals don't, or is it pure accommodation? And be honest about the insulation worry: it looks like any counterexample I meet can be absorbed by saying I chose the wrong property or set the threshold wrong, and if that's true, is it fatal or is it just what any account of inductive inference looks like from the outside? One case that nags at me: if I apply the rule to self-locating questions, like my birth rank among all humans ever born, the narrowing never terminates anywhere principled, and I can't tell whether that's a defect in my rule or a fact about self-locating probability.

Rank the defeaters from the ones that should genuinely shake me down to the ones I can shrug off, and say which of them a defender of my position would themselves concede rather than just what an opponent would throw at me. Finally, tell me how confident you are in what you're claiming, and flag the single point in your answer you're least sure about.
