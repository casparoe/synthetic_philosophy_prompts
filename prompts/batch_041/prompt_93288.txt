I am a third-year undergraduate in a democratic theory course, and I would like help identifying a pattern that keeps surfacing in our readings on rotation in office and selection by lot. I can describe it, but I cannot find a name for it, and I am no longer certain whether it is one phenomenon or several.

Here is the clearest version of it I can give. A city council adopts term limits in order to break the hold of long-serving incumbents. A decade later the members are all newcomers, and the continuity the reform was meant to break now sits with the people who never leave: the council's chief of staff, the budget analyst, the parliamentarian, a former member who has come back as a consultant. The new members know they do not understand how the budget actually gets built, so they ask, and what they are told arrives as procedure rather than as preference. Nothing improper has occurred; the staff are competent and the members are sincere. But the body that was supposed to become more accountable now decides less than it did, and the people deciding are the ones nobody elected.

A second version, this time from the sortition material as I understand it. A citizens' assembly is convened by lot to settle a contentious issue. The participants are ordinary people, but the agenda, the briefing papers, the choice of expert witnesses, and the way the options are framed are set by a secretariat and a facilitation team, all of whom describe themselves as neutral. The participants defer, reasonably, and the recommendation that emerges is described by the participants themselves as the experts' option.

What strikes me is a second feature that I have not seen discussed alongside the first. The newcomers' ignorance is not only a cost. When something goes wrong, having relied on the advice one was given is a complete answer, and nobody holds the amateur responsible for not knowing. So there is a standing reason to remain inexpert and, more subtly, a reason not to ask who actually decided, since asking would dissolve the defence. The arrangement is stable because everyone, including the people whose authority it drains, prefers the fiction that the elected or selected members are the ones deciding.

So: does this have an established name? I have been told it is the iron law of oligarchy, and that seems close but aimed elsewhere; I am interested in what happens when the reform genuinely succeeds at removing the leaders. I am also unsure whether the two halves belong together -- the relocation of expertise to permanent staff, and the incentive to stay ignorant -- or whether the second is a separate phenomenon that tends to accompany the first. If no name exists, please coin one, define it, and mark its edges. Does it require a formal rotation rule, or does any arrangement with temporary decision-makers and permanent support staff count? Does it require that the staff's preferences diverge from the members'? Is it still the pattern if nothing ever goes wrong? And what would be a case that resembles it but is not an instance?

Tell me how confident you are in the identification, and single out the one point you are least sure of. End with a line \answer{...} containing the term you settle on -- the established one if there is one, otherwise your coinage -- together with a one-sentence definition.
