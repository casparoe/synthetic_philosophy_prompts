What Heritability Does Not Mean

Ask whether a trait is heritable and the answer usually arrives as though heritability were a property of the trait, like a melting point. It is not. It is a property of a trait in a particular population, at a particular time, across whatever range of environments the sample happened to include.

The statistic is a ratio: the share of the phenotypic variance in a sample that is statistically attributable to genetic variance. Almost everything worth saying about heritability follows from that one fact.

Consider house prices. Suppose that, in a given city, the size of the lot explains 40 percent of the variance in sale price. No one would read that as meaning that 40 percent of each house's price is caused by its lot, and no one would conclude that lot sizes are therefore irrelevant to making housing cheaper. The 40 percent is a fact about the spread of lot sizes in that city, not about the causal power of land. Sample only a subdivision where every lot is identical and the figure collapses toward zero — not because lots stopped mattering, but because there is no variation left to explain. Widen the sample to a region mixing rowhouse lots with ten-acre parcels and the figure climbs. The number tracks the sample, not the trait.

Genetic variance behaves like the lot. Its share of phenotypic variance depends on how much environmental variance the sample contains. Where environments are uniform, heritability rises even though nothing has changed about the causal role of genes; where environments are heterogeneous, it falls. Imagine a population in which everyone is exposed to the same diet, the same schooling, the same air. Environmental variance in a trait is now near zero, and the heritability estimate climbs toward one, even if the trait would shift for everyone the moment that shared condition changed. High heritability and high malleability are therefore perfectly compatible, and so are low heritability and low malleability. Heritability tells you nothing about what would happen if you intervened.

Two traps follow. First, a high heritability estimate licenses no inference about differences between groups. A trait can be highly heritable within every group while the difference between groups is wholly environmental: within a well-nourished population, adult height differences are largely genetic, but the difference in height between that population and a malnourished one is not genetic at all. Second, a high heritability estimate does not mean that any one person's trait value is mostly fixed by that person's genes. The statistic partitions variance across persons in a sample; it says nothing about what determines a single individual's value.

The twin study, the workhorse behind most of these numbers, rests on assumptions — that identical and fraternal twins share relevant environments to the same degree, that mating is random with respect to the trait, that genetic effects add up. Because twins are raised together, we can treat the shared-environment assumption as satisfied.

My own view is that heritability now survives mostly as a warning label: a reminder to ask which population, which environments, and to what end. The interesting questions concern reaction norms — how a trait responds across environments — and those cannot be read off a single ratio.
