I run model risk for a mid-sized UK pension scheme — about £4–5bn, so we can't afford our own quants but we're big enough that getting this wrong ruins people's retirements. Every few weeks something lands on my desk with a probability attached: a longevity model that says a buy-in saves us 3% over ten years, a credit model that says a counterparty is 99.5% safe, a climate scenario that says the deficit widens by X by 2050 with 70% confidence. The investment committee treats the number as if the number is the number. I don't think that's right, and I want a procedure I can actually run in maybe half an hour before each paper goes up, which tells me whether the probability in front of me is decision-grade or whether I should be applying some sort of ambiguity penalty to it — and how big a penalty, or at least how to argue for one.

Here's the raw material I've been chewing on, so you don't have to guess what I mean:

- The two-urn thing everyone trots out. Urn A has 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn B has 100 balls, red and black in some unknown proportion. Most people will take a bet on red from A over red from B, and also take a bet on black from A over black from B — even though one of the two B bets has to come in, so you can't sensibly rate both of them below their A versions if you're being consistent. The interesting bit isn't that people are silly, it's that they seem to treat the known-odds urn as worth strictly more, per bet, than the unknown-odds one.
- So people distinguish risk (odds you know) from ambiguity (odds you don't), and will often pay to avoid the second. There's a family of decision rules built on that: act as if the worst probability in your range is the true one and maximise the minimum expected payoff; or take a weighted blend of the best and worst case; or just bung a fudge factor on the discount rate. I don't need the maths, I need the discipline.
- The counter-argument someone always raises at committee: if you're making hundreds of small independent ambiguous bets, the ambiguity washes out, and being averse to it is a costly emotional tic. That seems right for the trading book and obviously wrong for a one-shot £500m buy-in, and I've no principled way to draw the line between the two.
- Also, and this is the bit that worries me most: sometimes ambiguity should make you *keener*, not less keen — if the downside is bounded, or if you're the one selling the ambiguous thing to someone else. I've caught our own papers doing this without noticing.

What I want from you: a decision procedure, in words, that my team and I can run on a paper. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no (or says: this number isn't usable, come back with a range and a story about where the range comes from), and where it has to hand the decision to the committee because the question is about our risk appetite and not about the numbers at all. Then run it on two or three realistic cases — a longevity hedge priced off a brand-new model, and a novel asset class with no long history — including at least one where your procedure tells me something I won't want to hear, because I'd rather find that out now than in the meeting.

Please be honest about where the procedure is just my prior dressed up as method, and say plainly what it can't do: cases where the ambiguity is about which model class is right rather than about a parameter, or where the committee is going to ignore it anyway, or where the "worst case in the range" isn't actually well-defined.

Answer in British English. Plain prose and a list of steps is fine, no diagrams, and don't assume I know the names of any of this stuff.
