Last night my friend Priya and I argued for two hours about whether the rulings she makes as chair of our worker cooperative's governance committee are principled, or a run of improvisations that only look consistent in hindsight. She designed the system, so she is the only person who could settle the matter, and she declined to. I am writing to you instead.

The arrangement, briefly. For two years the co-op has settled a subset of its internal decisions by market rather than by vote. On a question with several candidate options, we run contracts that pay out according to how well things go — measured either from our own books or from a quarterly member survey — conditional on each option being the one adopted. The option whose conditional price implies the better outcome wins, and the assembly is bound by the result. Payouts are in co-op credits redeemable against member services, so the sums at stake are small. One member insists on calling this futarchy, a term I gather comes from somewhere else. The committee rules case by case on whether a question is settled this way, and Priya writes the rulings.

Her calls so far:

1. Which of three inventory systems to buy, contracts resolving on three-year cost per item handled and on stockout rate. Market. 'There is a fact of the matter, and the people who do the work know things the vendors don't.'

2. Buy or lease the new delivery van, resolving on cost per mile over three years. Market. 'We will be able to check the answer against our own books.'

3. Amend the bylaws to add an asset lock and to name ecological repair as a purpose of the co-op. Assembly. 'There is no number to check, and it would bind members who are not here to bet with us.'

4. Create a class of associate members drawn from the neighborhood, entitled to attend assemblies but not to vote. Assembly. 'We have no number for whether a neighborhood feels included, and it would bind people who cannot trade.'

5. Switch to a cheaper supplier of packaging whose workforce is non-union and paid near the legal minimum. Market, over my objection that the question was really about what kind of co-op we are. 'The question before us is what we pay for boxes; we do not get to run someone else's business.'

I have two hypotheses about the rule she is running and a third that is my own worry rather than hers. First: she sends a question to the market when it concerns means to an end we already agree on, and keeps questions about ends for the assembly. Second: she sends it to the market when the outcome can be checked against a number we already collect. Third, mine: she will not let a market decide a question whose resolution is a quantity the traders themselves produce. That third one is what I would have expected to keep the van out of the market, and it did not, so perhaps she does not hold it at all. Tell me which of these the record actually supports, and whether rules I have not named fit it at least as well; and say whether case 5 means that people who cannot trade simply do not constrain her.

The case that has reopened all of this is next month's. A member proposes a participation dividend: each member would receive a monthly payment from surplus proportional to their attendance at the monthly assembly, funded by a small increase in the price of our retail line. The numbers already exist — we record attendance monthly and member income is on the books — so the procedure would ordinarily send it to the market, with contracts resolving on attendance over the following two quarters. I take it to be a means question, since we all agree we want attendance up. Priya, I suspect, will say the real question is whether we want to be a co-op that pays people to show up, which is a question about ends. And attendance is exactly the quantity the traders would be producing while their contracts were open, so the market would be a vote in disguise, or worse.

Predict her verdict. If you think she sends it to the market, say what measure she would insist on and why. Then tell me which rule she is in fact running, what each candidate predicts here, which fits her record best and what that fit costs her elsewhere, and which further case would tell the rules apart. I am also curious whether the point about the traders producing the number is decisive, or merely the ordinary circularity of any decision market and I have made too much of it.
