I teach a 90-minute session in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology — fourteen participants, all comfortable with probability but none specialists in Bayesian updating — and I want an activity that makes them feel from the inside that uncertain evidence is a real problem rather than a technicality. My sketch, which you should improve on: everyone holds a stake in tokens and must state a credence that a crate in a warehouse holds contraband, scored by a proper scoring rule so that honesty is their best play. Each participant then receives two glimpses — uncertain observations, each reported with a reliability figure (they are, say, sixty-five per cent sure the sound through the wall was a diesel engine). The house rule, announced in advance and applied mechanically, is that after each glimpse they set their credence in what they glimpsed to the reliability figure and re-mix using the conditional credences they held immediately before that glimpse, holding those fixed; it is probability kinematics, or Jeffrey conditionalisation, though the participants should not hear either name until the debrief. The order of the two glimpses is randomised per participant, publicly and verifiably, and the two glimpsed propositions must be dependent in their priors, or the divergence will not appear at all. I want the trouble to surface before the rule is named: each participant should find their own arithmetic impeccable, then discover on the wall chart that people given the same two glimpses in the other order ended up somewhere else, with no error anyone can point to — then a betting round across the two clusters. Give me the rules, the materials, a recipe for the numbers (and how to check on a spreadsheet in advance that the two orders really do diverge), the token payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Then the debrief: map each stage onto what the rule is doing and why the fixed conditional is what makes it more than ordinary conditioning, set out the available repairs and what each costs, and be frank about what the activity cannot settle — including whether order-dependence is a defect at all, and the harder case where the uncertain evidence is a conditional rather than a proposition. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. Please answer in British English.
