Having spent thirty-five years in an atomic physics laboratory and only recently taken up the philosophy of science in earnest, I keep running into something I cannot reconcile. In my working life we would trap a single ion, or prepare a single photon, run a sequence of pulses on it, and then write up the result as follows: the system was prepared in a particular state, and the preparation was confirmed by tomographic reconstruction, which returns a density matrix with error bars. We spoke without hesitation of the wavefunction of that one ion. We tuned our pulse sequences in order to shape it, we diagnosed a failed experiment by saying that the state had come out wrong, and in grant renewals we promised to prepare and manipulate states of a specified form. At no point did anyone in the group hedge any of this as a claim about our own information; the state was treated as a feature of the object in the trap, on a par with its mass or its charge, except that it could be damaged and repaired. And yet a number of the same people who ran those experiments will tell you over coffee that the wavefunction is not a physical thing at all, that it is a bookkeeping device for an agent's degrees of belief, and that anyone asking what the state really is has confused physics with epistemology. The oddity is sharpened by the fact that we also used probability distributions constantly, in the statistical-mechanical and thermal sense, and there we were perfectly comfortable saying that the distribution merely recorded our ignorance of a system that had definite properties all along.

What I would like is not a survey of the literature but an audit of the practice. What does it actually presuppose about the wavefunction? Is a working laboratory of this kind committed to the view that the state is a real, per-system feature of the individual object — a psi-ontic reading — or can a thoroughly epistemic reading license every move we made: the talk of preparation, the tomographic reconstruction, the error bars, the phrase "the state of the ion", and the verdict that a preparation failed? Where, precisely, must the epistemic reading begin to hedge or rephrase, and where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival views would serve it equally well? I am particularly interested in whether a sentence like "we measured the state of the ion and it agreed with what we had prepared" can be asserted at all under the epistemic reading without equivocation, or whether it has to be rewritten as something like "our expectations, revised, matched our earlier expectations" — and, if the latter, whether anything in the practice is thereby lost that a working physicist would notice. Finally, tell me what would have to change. If the epistemic reading is correct, what would a lab protocol, a paper, or a funding pitch have to look like in order to take it seriously, and is there any part of the enterprise — the calibration, the benchmarking, the talk of engineered states — that would have to be abandoned rather than merely reworded? I would like to know which of my old habits of speech were innocent shorthand and which ones were quietly importing a metaphysics I claim not to hold.
