Here is a variant of the Death in Damascus problem, though your answer should not depend on how that problem is usually treated. Dana lives in a city with two hospitals, Northside and Southside, and she will fall seriously ill tomorrow, when she must choose one of them. A forecasting service, built from a statistical model of her habits, her errands, her work, and her past decisions under pressure, has already issued its forecast of which hospital she will choose; over the years its forecasts of her movements have been correct without exception, and it exerts no causal influence on her whatsoever. Her deliberation, whatever form it takes, is itself part of the record on which the forecast was built. The city's single emergency vehicle will be dispatched to the hospital named in the forecast; patients who present at that hospital receive treatment, patients who present at the other do not, and Dana is never told what the forecast says. She has a third option: for a fee she can engage a broker who will decide her destination by a lottery that has not yet been run and whose outcome nothing about Dana can reveal. The forecasting service knows all of this. It knows that she may hire the broker, knows the broker, and knows the design of the lottery; what it cannot see is how the lottery will come out.

Write an argument for the following claim: Dana should pay the broker, and more generally, an agent who knows that a predictor's output will fix the circumstances she then reacts to has reason to spend real resources on severing the evidential link between her act and the prediction.

Your argument should (1) explain why randomization helps at all here, given that a Bayesian agent's value for a lottery over acts is ordinarily a weighted average of the values of the acts it mixes, so that mixing can never strictly improve on the best available act; (2) state the general condition under which buying unpredictability is rational, and why ordinary decisions fail to satisfy it; (3) say how much the broker's service is worth as a function of the forecast's accuracy, and what degree of randomization is best if the broker offers a dial rather than a fair coin; (4) confront the strongest objection you can construct, including the thought that a service accurate enough to warrant this much worry would also foresee the lottery, and answer it, stating plainly which assumptions about prediction and randomness your argument requires; and (5) close with two or three sentences on what the same reasoning implies for a person who knows that an accurate scoring model will set the terms she then faces, such as an applicant whose file will be ranked before she chooses how to present herself.

Give a single sustained argument rather than a survey of positions, and do not worry about citations: the case is fully specified above.
