I chair the ethics committee at a hospital network and we vote Thursday on whether to let our "learning health system" program launch as designed. The program pours every patient's records into a commercial AI partner's training set. Patients get a letter; if they don't respond in 30 days we count that as consent. The letter describes the data use as "supporting your care and quality improvement," which isn't really what the partner does with it, and it promises patients they can "withdraw at any time" even though once a record is baked into the model there's no realistic way to pull it out. Our general counsel says the consent is fine: they were told, they had a chance to say no, nobody put a gun to anyone's head. The dissenting members say a consent you can't actually act on isn't consent at all, it's just us deciding for them, and one of them keeps saying the real difference is between consent as a one-time waiver and consent as a standing authorization that stays live, and that this isn't just wordplay. I need the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. Please act like a philosopher who's actually thought hard about what makes consent valid, not a compliance officer, and start by getting clear on what consent is and what it's for before you land anywhere. Then give me a clear recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- with a concrete timeframe if you go the conditions route, and make sure you take the losing side's strongest point seriously instead of just reciting the four principles.
