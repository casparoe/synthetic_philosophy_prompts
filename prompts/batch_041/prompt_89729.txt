I've been reading rationalist and effective-altruist forums for the better part of a decade, and I've grown tired of the way moral uncertainty gets used as a conversation-stopper — a license to hedge, or an excuse to keep giving to the charity I already liked. I want a decision tree that sorts the actual positions rather than the slogans.

The starting commitment is fixed: the reader believes there is a fact of the matter about what one ought to do, does not know which moral theory is correct, and has credences spread over several theories. From there, every branch node must be a question the reader can answer without already knowing where it leads. I expect the tree to include at least these separators, though you should add whatever else the logical space requires: whether uncertainty itself gives you any reason to act differently from what your highest-credence theory prescribes; whether choiceworthiness can be compared across theories at all, and if not, whether only dominance-style reasoning survives; how the view handles a tiny credence in a theory that makes enormous demands; whether aggregation treats theories as voters, as bargainers, or as value functions to be averaged after some normalization; and whether the view licenses binding your future self against anticipated moral change. The leaves must include, at minimum, no-hedging (uncertainty gives no independent reason), dominance/maximality, maximize expected choiceworthiness, highest-credence-wins, a parliamentary or bargaining model, a scale-normalizing variant, and a maximin or pessimistic aggregation.

Two stipulative distinctions I want the tree to use, because I have not seen them carved cleanly:

1. credence-inert versus credence-responsive — a view is credence-inert if your credences about moral truth do no work in fixing what you may do, and credence-responsive otherwise;
2. action-hedging versus portfolio-hedging — changing what you do today, versus spending resources now to keep moral options open, buy information, or shape what you will later believe.

Four cases must appear as probes, and every leaf must be run against all four: a free Saturday, where theory A says volunteer, B says study to become more effective later, and C says rest; a one-in-ten-thousand credence in a theory that demands you give away everything you own; credences split between a theory on which insect experience matters enormously and one on which it does not matter at all; and the case where you expect to hold a different moral view in ten years while thinking that view will be no more likely to be correct than your current one, so the question is whether you act on today's credences, tomorrow's, or some hedge between them.

Deliverables: the tree itself, with each branch node labeled by its question and each leaf carrying a short name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment; a table of the logical space with unoccupied combinations marked and diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; a list of leaves the tree cannot separate, with a note on what further question would separate them; and a short note on which single branch does the most work. If a position has a standard name, use it, but do not attribute positions to authors, and include no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists — I would rather see "unnamed" than a guess. Write in American English.
