I am preparing a podcast interview with an environmental historian — not a real person, so you may fill in the specifics, but she must come across as a competent, coherent defender of one identifiable thesis rather than a composite of everyone who has ever written on the subject. Her thesis is that microbes, climate, and volcanic events are historical actors and not merely background conditions, and that treating them this way does not commit her to environmental determinism. She holds three claims in particular: that causal weight and agency are distinct, since an eruption that destroys a fleet can have enormous causal weight and no agency whatever; that what she calls reciprocal entanglement — human responses to a non-human cause altering that cause in turn, as when irrigation reshapes mosquito habitat, which reshapes malaria, which reshapes settlement — is the one place where the language of agency does explanatory work rather than merely rhetorical work; and that the determinism charge is a category mistake, because determinism is a thesis about law-governed predictability rather than about the magnitude of a cause.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press this position instead of inviting her to restate it. Include at least one question each on the causal-weight/agency distinction and whether agency adds anything that causal weight plus feedback could not; on the reciprocal-entanglement case, including what it would look like for a historian to get one wrong; on whether her view can still hold human agents responsible for outcomes she attributes in part to microbes and climate; and on how a historian distinguishes a constraint from a co-producer without already knowing the counterfactual. For each question, supply the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

Do not hedge. Take a definite line in each question, and close with a short note committing to which of her three claims you judge weakest and why, and to which single question you expect her to be unable to answer cleanly.
