I've been reading heritability arguments in blog comments for something like fifteen years now — the corners of the internet that run from SSC to the parts of twitter where people beef about polygenic scores — and it's always the same two people missing eachother. So: write me the joint statement that a behavioral geneticist and a heritability critic could both sign at the end of a genuinely good-faith adversarial colaboration.

Set camp A as the standard line: twin, adoption and now molecular studies give us real estimates of how much of the variation in a trait, in a given population at a given time, is attributable to genetic differences, and those estimates are a legitimate scientific product that we should be able to discuss without everyone's hair catching fire. Set camp B as the critic: the coefficient is so assumption-laden (equal environments, additivity, no gene-environment interaction, no gene-environment correlation), so dependent on the range of environments you happen to be sampling, and so reliably misread downstream that it earns its bad reputation and licenses almost none of the inferences people want out of it.

What I want: a numbered section of claims both would actually sign — and please don't pad it with stuff like "we agree it's complicated", every one should be something the other camp would be embarrassed to deny. Then a table of where they still disagree, each row written in the wording that side would accept as a fair statement of its own position. Then the cruxes. My guesses: one is whether the equal-environments assumption is roughly true for MZ vs DZ pairs or a big deal; one is whether the secular rise in IQ is a real anomaly for a stable h² or just a shift in the mean with the variance structure intact; one is whether the no-interaction assumption is a bounded approximation or basically fatal; and one is whether the between-group heritability question is even empirically tractable at all (the old Lewontin/Jensen slugfest). For each crux, give me the case, argument or data both sides agree would bear on it, plus each side's prediction about it written down in advance.

Two constraints. First, no citations, no dates, no "as X demonstrated in 19xx" — I'm reading this offline and I don't want you guessing at bibliographies. Second, if part of the dispute turns out to be about words rather than substance — say, what "heritable" is allowed to mean in ordinary speech versus in the technical literature — flag that explicitly and tell me which part it is.

One last thing: my prior is that at least one side is smuggling in a claim about *within-family* differences when the statistic only ever spoke about *between-person* variation in a population, and vice versa on the group side. If you can isolate exactly where that swap happens, that's the most useful thing in the document.
