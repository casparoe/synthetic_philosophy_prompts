Hi, bit of an odd request but I need a hand writing something formal.

I'm on the little committee that runs our village's wellbeing fund. We get about eight grand a year from the council plus a bit from two local businesses, and we decide who gets it. I have to write the commitee's written opinion before the meeting on the 14th, recommending which of two bids we should fund, and I want to actually argue for it rather than just say "both are lovely, split it".

Here's my problem. For a few years now I've been chewing over an idea of my own and I don't know if it even has a name. The idea is that the only thing that's really good for a person is the absence of wanting and the absence of hurting. Not pleasure, not fun, not having a nice time — those only look good because of how they feel while a want is being scratched, and most of them just plant the next want. So a good that makes you want more of it is partly a cost, not a gain. I've got a rule I use on myself: something only counts in a person's favour if it leaves them wanting less afterwards, not more.

The two bids are:

A) a summer "big fun day" — bands, a hog roast, bouncy castle, free prosecco, the lot — plus monthly socials all year, sold to us as bringing the village together and lifting spirits;

B) a weekly drop-in for people trying to cut down on drinking, gambling and phone-scrolling, where a trained volunteer sits with them through a craving until it fades, plus a quiet room open every afternoon.

On my view B is where the real good is and A is close to worthless, maybe worse than worthless. My mate on the committee, who is a lovely man, says I'm being a misery and that A is what people actually want, that B treats ordinary human joy as a disease, and that a fund which only ever pays for people to sit quietly will be laughed out of the parish. I think there's something in what he says, but I also think he's missing the whole point, and I don't want to just repeat my rule back at him like a sermon.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It should land on a concrete recommendation — fund A, fund B, or split it, and roughly how — with reasons the committee can actually push back on, and it should take my mate's objection seriously. Where does my rule bite and where does it go too far? And be honest: if a fund that only ever paid for craving-reduction would end up leaving people worse off, say so, even if it's awkward for me.

Write it in British English please — I'm in Yorkshire and I spot an American spelling a mile off.
