I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the historical Jesus, and we have spent the last few weeks working through the criteria of authenticity—embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, and the like. My professor regularly describes these criteria with an analogy: the sayings and deeds attributed to Jesus are like a mixture poured through a stack of sieves, and whatever survives all the meshes is the authentic material. The image is doing real argumentative work in the seminar. It is supposed to show that historical reconstruction can be disciplined and cumulative, that the criteria converge on a stable core, and that the resulting portrait is not merely a projection of the historian’s interests. But our teaching assistant offered a different version of the metaphor, saying the criteria are more like a coffee filter than a sieve: what we want is the liquid that passes through, while the grounds left behind are the later tradition. I cannot tell whether this is a harmless variation or whether the two images license opposite conclusions about what the criteria actually deliver.

Could you audit the analogy as carefully as you would a philosophical argument? I would like a mapping of what corresponds to what—the mixture, the meshes, the residue, the person pouring, the independent source material, the historian’s judgment—and then an account of where the mapping breaks. In particular, I am interested in whether a sieve can be independent of the material it sorts in the way the criteria are supposed to be independent of the tradition they test; whether the residue is really the target or merely what the criteria happen to retain; and whether the convergence of several criteria is evidence of authenticity or only of the criteria sharing the same assumptions. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion my professor wants—that the criteria can settle, or at least seriously constrain, who Jesus was—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the sieve image has been carrying that conclusion all along. If there is a better analogy for what the criteria actually do, I would like to see it stated and defended.
