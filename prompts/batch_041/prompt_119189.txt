I am a high school student working on a semester-long independent research paper, and I have been trying to understand how many individually unreliable judgments can be combined into one reliable judgment. The version I started from is the result usually credited to Condorcet: if each member of a group is more likely than not to answer a binary question correctly, and the members' judgments are independent of one another, then the probability that the majority is correct approaches certainty as the group grows, whereas a group whose members are each worse than a coin flip becomes steadily more likely to be wrong. My own version of the exercise replaced the judges with a handful of large language models that I asked the same questions and then combined by majority vote. Adding models helped for a while and then stopped helping, and on a few questions the majority was confidently wrong in a way that looked coordinated rather than random.

I do not want a reading list, citations, or the names of papers. I want to know where the genuine open questions are and which of them I should spend the semester on. Please give me a ranked agenda of roughly four questions, defended rather than merely listed, and tell me what I am getting wrong about the framing. I am especially unsure how much of the interesting work lies in the two words that do all the heavy lifting in the theorem — independence and competence — since each seems to me to mean several different things: independence of errors versus independence of judgments given the truth, competence as a stable trait of a person versus a probability that varies question by question, and perceived competence versus actual competence. I also suspect that when the judges are models trained on overlapping text, their mistakes are correlated by construction, so that the aggregate may be reporting the most common answer in the training data rather than converging on the truth — a poll rather than a jury. And I do not know what becomes of the whole framework when the question has no single correct answer, or when the goal is not accuracy but a decision that is good enough under uncertainty.

For each item on your list, please state the criteria you are ranking by and where those criteria conflict, explain why the question is upstream of the others, say whether someone with my resources can actually make progress on it without running large experiments, and identify what would move it up or down the ranking over the course of a semester. Please also flag any question that looks important but turns out to be a dead end because its answer would not change anything downstream, and any that I have not thought to ask about at all.
