A friend of mine, a scholar of Buddhist philosophy, and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether Nāgārjuna's four-cornered schema, the catuṣkoṭi, is simply a piece of non-classical logic that my own field has already formalized. The argument ended inconclusively, and I would rather settle it properly than by repetition.

I am a logician by training; my own research is on many-valued and paraconsistent systems. I am fluent in designated values and the separation of truth from designation, truth-value gaps and gluts, the strong Kleene tables, the four-valued matrix usually associated with Belnap and Dunn, supervaluational and fixed-point treatments of negation, the distinction between internal and external negation, and the apparatus of model theory generally — a semantics, a satisfaction relation, a set of values, a notion of consequence. What I am not fluent in is Madhyamaka, and that is where the difficulty lies.

My friend's position was that the four corners are, in effect, the four possible assignments of truth value to a proposition once one admits gaps and gluts, that the 'neither' corner is the gap and the 'both' corner the glut, and that nothing in Madhyamaka outruns what such a semantics can express — so that the whole business is informal four-valued logic dressed in soteriological clothing. I suspect he is wrong, but I could not say where the translation fails, and I would rather be told precisely than be reassured.

Please do two things.

First, the translation. Restate the Madhyamaka position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: the tetralemma as Nāgārjuna deploys it against the four possible positions on inherent existence; emptiness; the claim, much discussed, that the Madhyamika asserts no thesis of his own; and the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth. Go corner by corner, and be explicit about what each corner becomes in the formal setting — a value, a gap, a glut, the withholding of an assertion, the failure of a presupposition, or something else entirely.

Second, the residue report. Tell me what survived, what survived only under distortion and what the distortion was, and what had no counterpart at all. I want the specific pressure points addressed. Does the fourth corner behave like a truth-value gap, or like the refusal to enter the game of assigning values? Is the exhaustiveness of the four corners a theorem of the semantics or a substantive claim about the space of possible positions — and does the ease with which the logic defines a fourth value simply presuppose what Nāgārjuna is arguing for? Can the claim to hold no thesis be stated at all in a framework whose semantics assigns values to sentences whether or not anyone asserts them, or is it a claim about assertion rather than about truth and therefore invisible to the semantics? Does the two-truths distinction correspond to anything internal to a single semantics, or does it require a shift of language, metalanguage, or model? And what becomes of the warning, standard in the tradition, that emptiness itself must not be turned into a view — does that have any analogue in the formal setting, or is it exactly the sort of thing the formal setting cannot say?

End with the verdict I actually want. If the residue is small, say so, and tell me my friend is right and the remainder is rhetoric. If it is large, tell me whether that is a defect of my vocabulary — something a better logic would capture — or a sign that the Madhyamaka claim is less clear than it looks and is trading on a refusal to be made precise. Do not quietly import classical assumptions and present them as translation, and do not be polite about it. No citations or references are needed; I want the argument, not a reading list.
