I hold a position I would like you to attack rather than defend. Two agents who are epistemically rational, who share the same prior probabilities, and between whom all the relevant evidence has been pooled cannot rationally go on disagreeing about a factual matter; and it follows that when two people who appear to be peers do persist in disagreeing after full disclosure, that persistence is itself evidence that one of them has erred, not a neutral fact about the world. Aumann's agreement theorem is the backbone of this, and I take the theorem to have teeth well outside the formalism.

What I want is a map of the position's exposure. Start with the theorem's assumptions and tell me, for each, what work it is actually doing and how much of the position survives if it fails. Some I already suspect are load-bearing: a common prior; common knowledge that each party is rational; common knowledge of the other's posterior; information that can be modelled as partitions of a fixed space of possibilities; and updating by conditioning. For each, I want to know whether its failure is a merely formal escape hatch or a realistic way for sensible, well-informed people to disagree. And if the position is any good it ought to predict something its rivals do not; tell me what, if anything, that is, and what observation would falsify it.

Then press on something the theorem, as usually stated, seems simply not to cover, and which I have not been able to make up my mind about. Suppose two agents do share a common prior and do exchange everything they can, but they do not have the same space of possibilities in mind. One of them has a hypothesis the other has never entertained and cannot be brought to entertain without first acquiring the concepts needed even to state it; until then the second agent's probability space literally omits it. In such a case there need be no event on which their posteriors can be said to disagree, since they are not assigning probabilities over the same algebra, and yet we would ordinarily describe them as disagreeing, or at least as failing to converge. Tell me whether this is a genuine hole in the position or a confusion on my part about what the theorem claims. If it is genuine, say how deep it runs: does it merely limit the theorem's scope, or does it undermine the inference from peer disagreement to error?

I also want the escape routes enumerated and graded. If priors differ, disagreement is rational, but is there any argument that rationality requires a common prior, or is the common prior a modelling convenience dressed up as a norm? If the two agents disagree not about an event but about which evidence is relevant, about the partition itself, does the theorem have anything to say? And is there a version of the position that is true but empty, in which every real disagreement is explained away by denying that the parties were ever genuine peers with a genuine common prior? If so, say so plainly, and tell me whether that emptiness is a fatal defect or an acceptable feature of a formal result that was never meant to do empirical work.

I would find it most persuasive if you told me what a committed defender would concede, not only what the critics say. What is the strongest thing the defender must grant counts against them? Is anything here decisive, a case or a proof or a way the world could be, as opposed to merely awkward? Finally, rank the defeaters from fatal to trivial and tell me which of them, if any, would move you.

Answer in continuous prose. No headings, no bullet points, no bold or italics, no tables or numbered lists, just paragraphs, the way you would write a long letter to someone you respect.
