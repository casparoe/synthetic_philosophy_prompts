I spent most of last Saturday evening arguing with a friend about whether species are real, and we ended up talking past each other in a way I'd like to fix. I'd like you to write an essay that settles the question as far as it can be settled.

The disagreement, as best I can reconstruct it, is this. I hold that when a biologist says two populations belong to different species, there is a fact of the matter being reported — not a fact as crisp as "this rock is granite," but a fact about history and lineage structure nonetheless. My friend holds that "species" names a convention: a boundary drawn for convenience on a continuum of divergence, whose appearance of objectivity comes from the convention's being widely shared rather than from anything in the world. When I pressed him, he made three points I couldn't answer on the spot.

First: whatever criterion I propose — interbreeding, shared descent, ecological role, diagnosable distinctness — there are uncontroversial cases where it gives no verdict or the wrong one: asexual lineages, ring species, hybrid zones, genes that have moved sideways between distant branches. Every time I patch the criterion I seem to be choosing which phenomena matter rather than discovering a boundary. My reply was that these are cases where our knowledge is incomplete, not cases where there is nothing to know, but I could not say why that reply isn't just stubbornness.

Second: the word does administrative work — endangered-species listings, invasive-species regulation, the units in which biodiversity is counted — and those boundaries are often settled by committee vote. He says a concept whose edges get fixed that way is a tool, not a discovery. I don't think that follows, but I couldn't say crisply why not.

Third: he claims I'm confused about what kind of thing I'm calling real. He says a species is more like a particular historical entity — an individual with parts, dated and located — than like a class defined by shared properties, and that once you see this, "realism" about species stops meaning anything definite.

Here is what I'd like the essay to do.

- Separate the question whether there is a single best criterion for applying the word "species" from the question whether species are entities whose boundaries exist independently of our criteria, and say how the two questions relate. My friend and I, I now think, were running them together.
- Give the strongest version of the anti-realist position, not a version that is easy to knock down.
- Give the strongest version of the realist position. In particular, address whether pluralism about criteria is compatible with realism about the entities the criteria are meant to track — the way different procedures for measuring temperature are compatible with there being a fact about temperature — and address the arbitrariness of the species rank as one level among many in a nested hierarchy.
- Use the following case as a test. Imagine a chain of populations around a mountain range: neighbors interbreed freely, the two ends of the chain overlap and do not interbreed, and gene flow between the ends is only indirect. Now suppose a virus has carried a gene from one end of the chain into a lineage on the far side, where it has spread. Anti-realists will say the case shows the boundary is stipulated; realists will say it shows only that the criterion is crude. Adjudicate using the case, and say what the case would have to look like in order to favor the other side.
- Identify the crux — the point where the two sides genuinely diverge, as opposed to where they exchange slogans.
- Reach a verdict and defend it. "It depends on what you mean by real" is not a verdict. A qualified realism or a qualified anti-realism is fine, provided the qualification is precise enough that I could tell whether I agree. If you think the question is badly posed, that is an acceptable verdict, but then you owe an account of what the well-posed question is and why the original one keeps getting asked.
- Close by saying what would change your mind: which empirical discovery or which argument would move you to the other side, and where in your own reasoning you would expect the error to lie if you turn out to be wrong.

Roughly 1200–1600 words. Assume no access to the literature: argue from the considerations above and from general knowledge, and don't cite sources — I'd rather see the reasoning than a reading list.
