Ok so I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole on the whole "the first player can just steal the second player's strategy" thing, and I want to know whether I've got the underlying principle right, because a couple of my gut verdicts don't fit together.

Here's the list of verdicts I've accumulated.

1. Hex (the game where you try to build a chain across the board between your two sides). First player wins, and the reason is the stealing argument: if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could place some arbitrary opening stone and then play as if they were the second player, and the spare stone can't hurt them. I'm confident about this one.

2. Chomp (the chocolate bar one, where you bite off a square and everything up and to the right of it, and whoever is forced to eat the poisoned bottom-left square loses). First player wins by the same style of argument, and the argument is useless in practice: it never tells you what move to play. Confident.

3. Bridg-it (you draw little bridges between dots and try to link two sides). Same verdict, first player wins, same argument.

4. Nim with two heaps of one. First player loses. But Nim is symmetric, no chance, no draws, and both players have exactly the same moves available. So why doesn't stealing say the first player can't lose? I've been telling myself this is a weird edge case, or something to do with the fact that in Nim your move takes something away rather than adding something.

5. Tic-tac-toe. I used to think the stealing argument proved the first player wins, then I realised it only gets you a draw, and now I think draws kill the argument altogether. If the second player can force a draw, then the stolen strategy only gets the first player a draw too, so nothing is proved.

6. Misère Hex (same as Hex but you lose if you complete your connection). I figure the argument goes through unchanged, so the first player still wins.

7. Hex with a handicap where your opponent gets to place your first stone for you. I'd say that definately kills the argument, since you don't get to choose your opening move.

8. A game where the rules let you skip your turn. First player obviously can't be worse off, just skip and now you're the second player.

9. Maker-Breaker, where one player is trying to make a line and the other is only trying to stop them. Different jobs, but it still feels like the same argument should say the line-maker, moving first, wins.

So my tentative principle is: in any two-player game with no chance and no draws, where both players have the same options, the first player can't be worse off than the second. The reasoning being that whatever the second player could do, the first player could do it first, and having one extra peice on the board can only ever help.

Is that the right principle, or is a clause missing? Which clause is actually carrying the weight? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and are there any where I'm just wrong regardless of what the principle turns out to be? Also, what does it predict for cases I haven't thought about, like three-player games, games that can go on forever, or games with a random element? I don't care about the history of any of this, I just want the principle and to know where my intuitions are off.
