I'm the person who has to sign off on new internal AI tools at a mid-size logistics company — we ship industrial parts, about 900 employees, and I'm basically the closest thing we have to an ethics board, which should tell you something. Three teams have built agents that act on their own now: one triages and answers customer support tickets, one does storage cleanup on our cloud buckets, and one reorders parts from suppliers. All three have tool access to real systems, real money, real customer records. Every review meeting someone says we need to make sure the agent avoids side effects, and I nod and write it down, but when I sit down to actually build the sign-off form I realize I can't define the thing I'm supposedly checking for. If the storage agent deletes a folder nobody's touched in two years, is that the job or a side effect? If the ticket agent closes four hundred tickets in an afternoon with canned replies and our average response time looks great, has it helped or has it quietly shredded a record someone will need in six months? And my favourite one: if I make an agent timid — change nothing you weren't asked to change — it sometimes sits on its hands while something we badly needed done doesn't get done. Is that avoiding a side effect or causing a bigger one? I genuinely dont know, and I have to put a yes or a no on a form and live with it.

So please write me the procedure. It gets run by me and the team lead, not researchers, in about forty five minutes, before a deployment and again monthly once the thing is live. I want it as an ordered list of questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the process stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a human because the question turned out to be about values rather than engineering, and an honest marking of which steps are just judgement calls wearing a lab coat. One of my engineers keeps pushing a scoring idea — for each action the agent might take, score it by how much it shrinks the set of future options we still have, measured against a fixed list of things the company cares about, with doing nothing as the baseline — and I'd like the procedure to say straight out whether that works, or whether the baseline choice is doing all the work and just hiding our values in a spreadsheet. Run the procedure on all three agents and show me the output, including at least one case where it tells me to block something I'd rather ship.

And be straight with me about the limits. Where is this procedure most likely to misfire once we're actually using it, what would have to be true for me to throw it out and go back to gut calls, and if I handed you a complete description of everything we care about, would the whole procedure collapse into something trivial — and does that mean the exercise is confused, or just that any version of it is a proxy and I should stop pretending otherwise? Tell me which of those two you'd bet on and what evidence would move you. And please dont send me reading, I wont do it.
