You are a philosopher of epistemology with a specialty in the post-Gettier literature on knowledge, defeasibility, and higher-order evidence. I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the dogmatism paradox, and I want a dialogue that my students cannot reconstruct from a textbook summary of the paradox. Before writing, spend a few lines brainstorming: state the strongest version of each of the two positions below, and note the one or two points where you think the debate is most likely to turn. Then write the dialogue.

Both speakers are epistemologists who share a great deal. They both accept a knowledge-first framework, and they both accept that knowledge is the norm of assertion. Their disagreement concerns whether knowledge that P can be defeated by higher-order evidence, that is, evidence bearing not on P itself but on the reliability of the process by which the speaker came to believe P.

The first speaker holds that knowledge that P confers a kind of immunity on the belief. Since knowledge is factive, and since on this speaker's view a known belief is safe, any evidence suggesting that the belief was unreliably formed must itself be misleading; and it is not rationally required to reduce confidence in the face of misleading evidence. So once you know, you may rationally disregard the suggestion that your process was faulty.

The second speaker holds that this cannot be correct. Consider an agent who knows what she sees, and who is then told by a wholly trustworthy informant that she has been given a drug that impairs the very perceptual faculty she is relying on. The second speaker maintains that the agent is rationally required to reduce her confidence, even if she in fact knows, and even if the drug in fact leaves her perception unaffected. The second speaker will need to explain how this is compatible with the factivity of knowledge and with the thought that knowledge is not a matter of luck.

Anchor the exchange in one concrete case, whose non-epistemic facts both speakers accept, so that the reader can see exactly where their verdicts diverge. Neither speaker may be a strawman: give each the strongest reply available at every turn. Each must respond to the argument the other has just made rather than restating a position. The dogmatist should press the anti-dogmatist on what factivity and safety leave room for; the anti-dogmatist should press the dogmatist on what an agent is supposed to do when her own position gives her no way to tell whether the higher-order evidence is misleading. Let the dialogue end without a forced resolution if the arguments do not support one.

Then append a critical note of 150 to 250 words in your own voice, addressed to me. State which of the two positions you find more defensible after writing the dialogue, and say what would change your mind: name the specific argument or case that would lead you to the opposite verdict, and identify where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Do not pad the note with hedging; I want your actual assessment, with its weaknesses marked.

I am not asking for citations, reading lists, or a survey of the literature. If you invoke a named position, characterize it accurately; if you are unsure of an attribution, describe the view without naming it.
