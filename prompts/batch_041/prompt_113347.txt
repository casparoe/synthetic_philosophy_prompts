I'm in a three-person reading group on vagueness and we've been meeting for about a year and a half, and the same fight keeps coming back with nobody moving an inch. My friend is a supervaluationist: she says a borderline utterance like calling a particular pile of sand a heap is neither true nor false, because it comes out true on some admissible ways of sharpening the word and false on others, and that's the whole story. I'm the epistemicist, mostly because I can't see how you build a semantics out of the many-sharpenings picture without smuggling in a determinate set of admissible sharpenings, which just relocates the problem one level up. Our third member is a linguist who thinks we're fighting over words and wants us to stop. What we actually re-litigate isn't the sorites -- we all agree it's a bad argument and that heap has no cutoff any human could use. It's whether our agreement leaves the two views anything to disagree about. She says: at the borderline there is no fact of the matter about the pile with exactly N grains. I say: there's no fact that you or I or any possible creature could recover, but there's still a fact, fixed by the totality of our dispositions to use the word. She says: if it can't be recovered in principle by anyone, in what sense is it fixed? I say: in the sense that the word has a definite extension, so for every pile either it's a heap or it isn't, and nobody needs to be able to find the line. Round and round.

Intuition pumps haven't helped either. We trained a little classifier on labelled heap / not-heap examples to see which of us it would agree with, and it made things alot worse: the thing spits out a number for every input, so I say of course it does, that's what having a boundary amounts to, and she says that's a disanalogy, because the classifier has a physically definite decision surface while our word doesn't -- which looks to me like assuming the conclusion. Meanwhile all the standard objections are on the table and all inert: she has to live with the fact that a disjunction like either-it's-a-heap-or-it-isn't can come out true when neither half is, and with the suspicion that her admissible sharpenings arrive as a determinate set (so where did the higher-order vagueness she wanted go?); I have to live with a boundary that's arbitrary and unrecoverable, which she finds incredible and I find merely surprising. So my hypothesis is that the stall has nothing to do with borderline cases and everything to do with something we both assume -- maybe that the meaning of a predicate has to be fixed by facts about how it's used, which is precisely the premise I need permission to deny. Or maybe we're answering different questions under one heading, one about what makes an utterance true and one about what makes a classification defensible, or it's a verbal dispute about who gets the word vague. I don't want a verdict on who's right; I want a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement this actually is, what each of us would have to give up for the other to be right, and what would really count as progress here -- a formal result, say about whether a theory of vagueness can be stated in its own language, or whether higher-order vagueness is even coherently expressible; a distinction; or nothing at all.
