I am in a seminar on Augustine's moral theology, and we spent most of last week on his treatment of lying. The picture I came away with is that Augustine holds an absolutely strict position — lying is never permitted, not even to save an innocent life, not even to protect someone from a murderer — and that he defends this position across two treatises, the early On Lying and the later Against Lying, which he wrote partly in response to people who wanted to use deception to expose a heretical sect. What is bothering me is how he handles the biblical cases that everyone brings up: the Hebrew midwives who mislead Pharaoh about why the male infants are surviving, and Rahab, who hides the Israelite spies and sends their pursuers off in the wrong direction. I keep running into the claim that Augustine gets out of the difficulty by insisting that these women did not really lie — that they concealed something or answered in a way that was not, strictly speaking, a falsehood, and so the absolute prohibition is preserved. And I have also heard the opposite, that he was willing to call Rahab's act a lie and a sin while still praising her for her faith and hospitality.

Here is my confusion, and I think it is doing real work in how I am reading him. If Augustine defines a lie as saying something false, then his absolutism seems to commit him to saying that anyone who misstates a fact — a person who is simply mistaken, a person telling an obvious joke, a person who repeats bad information in good faith — has sinned, which is absurd. And if he instead keeps rescuing the midwives and Rahab by reclassifying what they did as not-a-lie, then the prohibition starts to look like a verbal trick: whenever a case comes along where the absolutism would be embarrassing, the definition of lying quietly contracts until the case falls outside it, and the view becomes impossible to falsify. So my question is whether Augustine's position has any principled content underneath the definitional maneuvering, or whether the strictness is an illusion maintained by gerrymandering the word lie.

What I would really like is an answer that separates these strands. Does Augustine actually define lying in terms of falsity, or in terms of something else — the speaker's intention, the will to deceive, a kind of doubling of the heart — and if it is the latter, what independent reason does he have for that definition rather than one that would have made the hard cases come out differently? Given that definition, is his handling of the midwives and Rahab a patch applied after the fact, or does it follow from commitments he holds anyway? And most importantly: if I wanted to test his absolutism rather than just admire its consistency, what would count against it? What kind of case, argument, or consequence should move someone who accepts his framework — and is there anything he concedes that already looks like a real cost, such as a situation where the honest course of action is clearly monstrous but he says one must take it anyway? I would also appreciate your telling me whether the charge of unfalsifiability has any bite here, or whether it is just the ordinary situation of any view that has a carefully drawn central term. Please answer in American English.
