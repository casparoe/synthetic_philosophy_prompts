Exam question:

Frege introduces the distinction between sense (Sinn) and reference (Bedeutung) to solve a puzzle about identity statements. (a) State the puzzle as Frege presents it. (b) Explain how the distinction is supposed to resolve it. (c) What does the distinction imply for a sentence containing a proper name that has no reference, such as 'Odysseus'? (d) Briefly state one objection to Frege's distinction.

Student's answer:

(a) The puzzle is that 'a = a' and 'a = b' seem to have different cognitive value. 'a = a' is trivial and knowable a priori, whereas 'a = b' can be informative and can extend our knowledge. For example, the discovery that the morning star is the evening star was a real astronomical discovery. But if identity is a relation between objects, then 'a = b' just says that an object is identical to itself, which is the same thing 'a = a' says. So identity cannot be a relation between objects.

(b) Frege's solution is to say that identity is not a relation between objects but between the signs or names. The names 'morning star' and 'evening star' are different signs, so 'morning star = evening star' relates two different signs, and that is why it can be informative. Frege then adds that the signs are related to objects through their sense. The sense of a name is the mental image or private idea that a speaker associates with the name. So 'morning star' and 'evening star' have different senses—different mental images—but the same reference, namely Venus. The difference in sense explains the difference in cognitive value.

(c) For a name like 'Odysseus', which has no reference, the sense is still there, but since the name has no reference, the sentence 'Odysseus was set ashore at Ithaca' expresses no thought at all. It is neither true nor false, and for Frege that means it is not a genuine statement.

(d) One objection is that Frege's account cannot handle indexicals. The sense of 'I' or 'today' changes with the context of utterance, so it cannot be the stable, objective mode of presentation that Frege's theory requires.
