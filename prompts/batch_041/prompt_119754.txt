I'm giving a paper at a joint classics-and-philosophy workshop on Plato's Laws, and I want a Q&A prep sheet before I finalize the talk.

My thesis: the drinking-party proposal in the opening books of the Laws is not, as it is usually read, a concession to human weakness — wine as a consolation for old age and a cheap way of catching out the sober hypocrite — but the earliest sustained attempt in philosophy to design an institution that measures character. On my reading Plato's interest in drunkenness is methodological rather than moralistic.

The argument, in brief. Plato's own psychology generates a problem he never quite states: virtue is a state of the soul, the soul is not observable, and ordinary social life gives the ambitious every incentive to simulate virtue, so a lawgiver who legislates for virtue has no way of knowing whether anyone actually possesses it. Wine solves this because it degrades the faculty — shame, the capacity for self-presentation — on which simulation depends. The supervised drinking party is therefore a stress test, and the institutional detail is what makes it a test rather than an ordinary drinking bout: the drinking is regulated, the company is selected, the proceedings are presided over by a sober and wise officer who functions as the nearest thing in the dialogue to a control, and the regime is age-graded, with wine withheld from the young, rationed to the middle-aged, and prescribed for the old. The Symposium is my counterexample that proves the rule: Socrates is the one man who can drink as much as he likes and remain exactly himself, and the dialogue treats that as the mark of a soul with nothing to hide.

I need the objections this room will actually raise, not the ones a general audience of Plato readers would raise. The room: (i) classicists, who read the Greek, know the sympotic and Dionysiac background, and regard the Laws as a late work of theocratic political engineering rather than philosophy; (ii) Plato scholars who work on the moral psychology of the Republic, think the Laws is a decline, and will hear every psychological claim in it through the tripartite soul and the Socratic thesis that no one errs willingly; (iii) a moral psychologist who works on character and situation, who will hear my stress test as situationism in ancient dress; and (iv) a historian of ancient dietetics, who will have views about what Plato's contemporaries thought wine does to a body.

What I want from you: the roughly eight objections I will get, ordered by how much damage they do to the paper rather than by how likely they are to be raised; each phrased as the person who will raise it would phrase it, in their vocabulary and out of their interests, in a sentence or two I could imagine actually hearing; for each, a reply I could deliver in two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weak. Tell me which of these I should pre-empt in the talk itself rather than leave for the question period, which are really one objection wearing two vocabularies, and what I should concede up front to buy credit for the rest. I will have twenty minutes of questions to survive. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly how much trouble I am in if it comes. No reading list and no citations — I know the texts and the scholarship; what I need is the argumentative shape.
