I'm putting togehter a short set of poems to close out a reading group on moral luck, and I want one built on the lorry driver case. Two drivers, same route, same skipped brake check, same moment of inattention at the same bend -- one child runs out, one doesn't. Write a poem in two sections of 8-10 lines each, where the second section is a word-for-word copy of the first except for a single line that changes. Everything else -- what he did, what he meant, what he'd do again tomorrow -- has to be identical, so that the whole divergence between the two lives sits in that one line and the reader can feel it isn't sitting in the agent at all. Dont have the poem say that out loud. The lucky one shouldn't be smug and the unlucky one shouldn't be self-pitying: I want the lucky one to sound like a man who knows he's the same man, and the unlucky one to sound like someone who can't get out from under something that is neither guilt nor ordinary regret about how things turned out, since he'd have to be someone else to have done otherwise. Keep the phrase "moral luck" out of the poem entirely. No title that gives the game away -- a road number or a date is fine. And please don't let the poem settle whether they're equally blameworthy; the two sections should leave that exactly as open as it actually is.
