I teach philosophy of science and have a colleague in the physics department who writes popular essays on probability and cosmology. He has just sent me a draft section on the doomsday argument, and because he knows I work on confirmation and anthropic reasoning, he has asked whether he has it right. Before I answer him, I would like an independent audit, since I suspect the passage is mostly correct but subtly off in ways that matter. Here is his summary:

"Here is the doomsday argument. Think of your birth as a random draw from the set of all humans who will ever be born. If our species is going to survive for millions of generations, then you and I are extraordinarily unlucky to find ourselves in the first sliver of the list — the odds of landing in the first thousandth are one in a thousand. It is vastly more probable that we are somewhere in the middle of the full roster, which means the roster is nearly complete, and human history is almost over. The argument rests on a single assumption: that you should treat your own position as a random sample from the observers, which is just the principle of indifference applied to your birth rank. Since that assumption is nearly impossible to deny, the conclusion — that we should expect extinction within the next century or two — is hard to escape. Some people object that we cannot identify the right reference class, whether it is all humans, all hominids, or all conscious observers, but this is a technicality; the result is robust across reasonable choices. The argument has also been tested: the same reasoning was used to predict how long the Berlin Wall would stand, and the prediction was correct, which shows the method works."

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me what a defender of the argument would accept as a fair statement, what would make them wince, and what is simply mistaken — distinguishing, where relevant, between errors that are merely loose phrasing and errors that change the argument's structure or its conclusion. I am particularly interested in whether the passage has conflated distinct arguments that happen to share a family resemblance, whether it has accurately represented what the argument assumes, and whether its final inference survives once the questionable steps are removed. Where a step is contested among people who work on this, say so explicitly and identify which positions are live; do not present a contested verdict as though it were the consensus, and tell me plainly which parts of the passage rest on things that are actually settled and which parts do not.
