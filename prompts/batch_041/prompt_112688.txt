My flatmate's doing an MA on art forgery and he's got this argument I can't shake, because I think it's wrong but every reply I come up with sounds like the reply he says I'll make. Here it is.

(1) If two things are indistinguishable from every possible perspective — you can look, measure, X-ray, run any test you like and never tell them apart — then there's no difference in value between them to appreciate.
(2) A good enough forgery is indistinguishable from the original by looking at it. That's what good enough means.
(3) So a good enough forgery has the same value as a work of art as the original does.
(4) If the original is worth more, the extra value has to come from its history: who made it, when, and whether they meant it.
(5) But history isn't in the object. You only ever learn it from a label, a signature, an expert's say-so.
(6) So whatever extra value the original has rests on the artist's reputation and on what experts tell us.
(7) Value that rests on reputation and expert opinion isn't artistic value — it's snobbery, or the market, or the aura of a relic.
(8) So originals aren't worth more than good forgeries. Insuring a canvas for millions while a perfect copy sits in the basement is irrational, and if you swapped every painting in the Louvre for a perfect copy overnight and burned the originals, nothing of value would be lost.

He says (8) is just the honest consequence and I'm being sentimental. I think it's insane — if the Prado were on fire and I could save either the real Goya or a copy made last week, I know which one I'd grab, and so would he — but I can't say why without sounding like I beleive in magic. What I'd most like to keep is (1) and (2); I really do think you can't value what you can't see. (7) is the one I want to be false.

Three things I keep chewing on. (a) A friend told me the drawing I'd been admiring was a fake and my whole experience of looking at it changed on the spot — same marks on the same paper, different experience — and I can't tell whether that's evidence about the work or just about me. (b) A painter I know made an exact replica of one of her own early canvases twenty years later, same hand, same materials, and signed it as a copy: is that one worth less than the one hanging in her mother's house, and if so why, since she made both? (c) There's a statue in a museum where the marble is genuinely ancient but the carving may be modern — the stone has one history and the image has another. And a forger once had to convince a court that his own best painting was a fake, because if it wasn't he'd of sold a national treasure to the enemy; he went down for the lie, not the brushwork.

So: where does this go wrong? If some step is a fallacy, name it and say why it looked fine at the time. If the argument is actually valid and something has to go, tell me which of (1)–(7) is cheapest to give up and what it costs me elsewhere — I'd rather not lose (1) or (2), but I'll pay if the price is stated. And please don't tell me the answer is that the original has an aura. That's his punchline, not mine.
