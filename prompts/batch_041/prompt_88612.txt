So I'm a backend engineer, no real philosophy training, but my roommate's a grad student and I've been tagging along to his reading group on infinity, and he keeps bringing up Thomson's lamp. I get the basic setup — lamp starts off, you flip the switch at 1/2, then 3/4, then 7/8, and so on, infinitely many flips before the minute is up, and then everybody argues about whether it's on or off at t=1. I think it's a great setup for a joke and I want to write one, but every version I come up with is just "haha infinite loop," which is exactly the lazy joke I'm trying to avoid.

Here's what I'm after: a short joke, maybe with a tiny scene (two devs, a code review, a standup, whatever — software is the setting I actually know, so that feels natural, but don't let the punchline just be "infinite loop" or "stack overflow," that's cheating). The punchline has to turn on the actual content of the puzzle. The thing that bugs me is that the flips never stop, so there's no last flip, which means nothing about the lamp's state at t=1 is forced by the sequence of flips — you could have it on or off at t=1 and not contradict anything that happened before, so the only way to get an answer is to just declare it by fiat. A joke that lands would be one where somebody tries to *derive* the final state, or insists it's definately one thing, and the comedy comes from the fact that it's underdetermined and any answer is a stipulation someone smuggled in. I'd also love a variant where somebody "fixes" it by adding one more flip exactly at t=1, so now everything is determined — but it feels like a swindle, because if you're allowed to do that then the original puzzle never had a real answer, it just had a missing stipulation. Extra credit if you can work in a second layer about what happens when you try to make it physical, like the switch needing some minimum time per flip, or the energy cost of flipping infinitely many times inside a finite window, since that's the other half of why the whole thing feels weird to me. Don't worry about citing anyone or naming papers, I genuinely don't care about that — I just want it to be funny to people who already know the puzzle.
