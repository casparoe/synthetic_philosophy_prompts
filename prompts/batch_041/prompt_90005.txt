In a dialogue from Gödel, Escher, Bach, a talking ant colony carries on a conversation, and readers are invited to take the conceit seriously: neurons are to a brain as ants are to a colony, so if a brain can think, why not a colony? A colleague of mine has adopted the analogy for a stronger purpose than the book's. He argues that the colony literally has a mind — it has memories in its pheromone trails, it makes decisions when it recruits foragers, it has a point of view — and from this he draws a moral conclusion: a colony has interests of its own, so destroying one is not merely killing ants but killing a mind, and the same reasoning should extend to cities and to the internet.

I want an audit of how far the analogy actually holds. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what is supposed to play the role of the neuron, the synapse, the signal, the brain region, the system's boundary, the single locus of experience — then tell me where the mapping breaks. Which features of a brain does the analogy quietly import that a colony lacks, and which features of a colony does it ignore that a brain lacks? Separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are idle differences. Finally: does the claim that the colony has a mind, and therefore interests of its own, survive once the breaks are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the argument all along? If there is a better analogy for what a colony is actually doing, say what it is.
