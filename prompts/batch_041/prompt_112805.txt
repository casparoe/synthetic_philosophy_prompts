Quick context before I ask: I'm a compliance officer at a mid-sized software company (roughly 400 staff) and a big part of my job is signing off on language before it goes out the door — privacy notices, security and uptime commitments, the code of conduct, support promises, and the occasional public statement after we've messed something up. Everyone treats this as a legal question and legal does get the final say, but most of the fights I actually have are about something else: whether a sentence commits us to a course of action, merely describes what we tend to do, authorises somebody to do something, or just signals a value we like. My colleagues and I genuinely can't agree on a case-by-case basis, and I've started to suspect we're disagreeing about what kind of act the sentence performs rather than about what it means. I need something I can point at in a meeting.

Please act as a philosopher of language who also spent a few years doing compliance work, and who knows the speech-act tradition (Austin, Searle, and the people who came after) well enough to use it without name-dropping. Think carefully about this before you write anything. Start with a short brainstorming section: which distinctions from that tradition actually earn their keep for someone in my seat — illocutionary force versus perlocutionary effect, felicity conditions, the difference between an act that misfires and one that goes through but is insincere, uptake, constitutive versus regulative rules, the special status of declarations that need an institution standing behind them. Say which you'll keep, which you'll drop as unusable in an hour, and why. Don't survey the literature, just make the cuts and defend them.

Then build me the actual thing: a decision procedure I can run on a single sentence of a draft in about ten minutes. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure lands on a definite verdict, and where it stops and hands the call to a person — me, or legal, or the relevant executive. Where you simplify a distinction to make it usable, keep the part that changes the verdict and bin the part that only interests specialists, and tell me which is which. Be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Constraints, so you can calibrate: I have maybe an hour per document, I have no philosophy background, my colleagues will push back and I need to be able to justify a verdict in one paragraph to a non-philosopher, and legal signs off last so I can't be seen to be freelancing. The stakes are that a sentence that reads as a promise but was meant as aspiration can create a commitment we can't keep, and a sentence meant to bind that reads as aspiration lets us weasel out of something we should have been held to — I care about both directions.

Then run the procedure on three sentences and show your working: (1) "We are committed to protecting your data." (2) "We will notify affected users within 72 hours of discovering a breach." (3) "Managers should aim to hold a weekly one-to-one with each direct report." At least one of your three verdicts should be one I won't like, and if the procedure says the first sentence is doing almost nothing, say so plainly and explain why, because I've been treating it as the load-bearing promise in the whole notice.

Finish with what the procedure can't do — the cases where it will misfire, the ones it can't settle at all, and where the whole exercise quietly stops being about speech acts and becomes a question of law or of what a reasonable reader would take from the text. Also flag any point where the theory and the practical answer come apart, so I know when I'm following the philosophy and when I'm just following the lawyers.

Write it in British English please.
