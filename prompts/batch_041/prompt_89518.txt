Two friends of mine, both committed utilitarians, got into an argument last week about whether their own theory can guide them at all. It started over a donation — one had given a modest sum to a deworming charity, the other said the money would have done more good spent on a bicycle — and it escalated.

The first said: it doesn't matter which of you is right, because the consequences of any act run on forever. Whether buying the bicycle sets off a chain ending in a famine or in a cure for aging, nobody can know; so 'perform the act with the best consequences' is not a rule anyone can follow, and a standard no one can apply is no standard.

The second said that confuses certainty with evidence: we maximize expected value, not guaranteed value, and the fact that we can't see the end of the causal chain just means we work with our best estimates, as we do everywhere. The first replied that expected value needs probabilities and there are none here — nobody can put numbers on the far future — and that once you allow even a tiny chance of an astronomically large outcome, the arithmetic is captured by whatever speculation you feed it, so you get either paralysis or whatever answer you were after.

The second then said: this proves too much. If uncertainty about distant consequences disqualified a standard of action, it would disqualify every standard — prudence, ordinary kindness, the golden rule — since all of them are supposed to be sensitive to how things turn out. Since that's absurd, the objection must go wrong somewhere. The first answered that the real lesson is that consequentialism is a criterion of rightness, not a decision procedure: it tells you which act was right once everything is known, not which act to perform now.

I can't referee this and it's affecting a real decision. Please adjudicate move by move — which claims are right, which are wrong, and what both of them are missing — and then say whether uncertainty about the far future really does undercut consequentialism as a guide to action.
