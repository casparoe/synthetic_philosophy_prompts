You've seen this fight before — you know the Historikerstreit literature and the later round of it, and you've sat through enough workshops to know what a room like this actually asks. Think it through before you write: first work out who's in the room and what each of them is committed to, then brainstorm every objection you can imagine them raising, then rank the objections, and only then start writing.

My thesis, roughly. Everyone treats the uniqueness question as a question about the event: whether the Holocaust has some property — industrial killing, racial ideology, total bureaucratic intent — that no other mass atrocity has. I want to argue that this is a category mistake. Uniqueness claims are really claims about explanatory indispensability: whether some particular explanatory apparatus is needed to account for the thing, i.e. whether an adequate account of the modern state's capacity for mass murder can be given without going through the Nazi case. On that reading the claim that the Holocaust is unique isn't a claim about 1941–45 at all; it's a claim about the shape of our explanatory projects, and it's indexed to a present, to what a given audience needs explained. So Nolte's comparability thesis isn't false so much as trivial — of course you can always find a comparison that holds along some dimension — and the interesting question becomes why the dispute felt so existentially loaded on both sides: an indispensability claim about an explanation doubles as a claim about who is required to keep telling the story, which is an identity claim, not a historical one. Second, smaller move: I want to say Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust is the weakest of the modern positions, because a theory that makes the Holocaust the apotheosis of bureaucratic rationality predicts it wherever bureaucracy and instrumental reason go, and therefore can't distinguish the cases — which was the one thing the book was supposed to do. I want to use Browning's Ordinary Men against it: the men of Reserve Police Battalion 101 were mostly not cogs in a rationalised machine, and the ones who were offered a way out and didn't take it were moved by peer pressure and something much more ordinary. If that's right, the modernity story and the ordinary-men story pull in opposite directions, and we've been teaching them as complimentary for thirty years.

The room: eight people, and I know roughly who they are. Two German-trained historians of the Third Reich in the functionalist tradition, allergic to philosophy of history and to anything that sounds like a thesis about German national character; one of them will say uniqueness talk is just a political slogan and ask me whether I'm for it or against it, which is precisely the question I don't want. A comparativist who works on Armenia and Rwanda and thinks uniqueness claims are an alibi for not doing comparison; he'll want to know what indispensable means operationally. An intellectual historian of the Federal Republic who knows the 1980s row cold and will catch me if I get the positions or the sequence wrong. A sociologist who teaches Bauman every year and will defend him, probably by telling me I'm attacking a 1989 book with 1990s evidence and that Bauman was making an argument about enabling conditions, not causes. A philosopher of social science who works on causal explanation and difference-making, and who is the only person in the room who can actually tell me whether my indispensability move is coherent or just a relabeling. The discussant, who works on memory and testimony, and who will put the ethical version of the question. And one person I can't place yet, which is its own problem.

What I need from you, in this order and roughly these lengths:
