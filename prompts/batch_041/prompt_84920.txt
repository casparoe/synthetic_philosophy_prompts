In my comparative-religion reading group the parable of the blind men and the elephant — retold in Buddhist, Hindu and Jain sources and now the stock illustration of anekāntavāda — is regularly used to license a conclusion: each blind man reports correctly on the part he has hold of, so each tradition's account is true for it, none can be measured against another, and anekāntavāda is at bottom a doctrine of tolerance on which criticism of anyone's view is out of place. I suspect the parable is doing the work rather than the doctrine. Set out the correspondences (what plays the role of the blind men and their reports, of the elephant, of the sighted onlooker; what corresponds to a naya, a standpoint-relative but valid partial account, and what to pramāṇa, comprehensive knowledge), then the places where the mapping fails — noting that on the doctrine the partial account is not false but valid as far as it goes, that the error Jain authors diagnose is taking a partial account as complete, and that the tradition does admit a knower who grasps the whole. My own guess is that the deeper trouble is structural: the parable makes partiality a matter of blindness and of a missing spatial part, whereas many-sidedness concerns aspects of one and the same object that hold at once. Test that guess, say which parts of the group's conclusion the parable can carry and which were smuggled in, and, if one exists, sketch an illustration that would serve many-sidedness better.
