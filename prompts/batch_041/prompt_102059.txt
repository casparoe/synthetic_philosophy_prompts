I have spent a week trying to reconstruct, for a graduate seminar, the Gödelian argument that the mind is not a machine — the line usually credited to Lucas and Penrose — and the version I have arrived at strikes me as proving too much. I would like to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs, because each step looks defensible to me in isolation, and the claims I would most like to keep are the two incompleteness theorems and the plain thought that I can know arithmetic is consistent.

Here is the argument.

(1) For any consistent formal system F strong enough to represent arithmetic there is a sentence G_F that F can neither prove nor refute, and G_F is true if F is consistent; moreover G_F is provably equivalent, within F, to the statement that F is consistent.

(2) A machine is a formal system, or implements one. Let M be a consistent machine that can do arithmetic, and let F_M be the system it implements.

(3) By (1), F_M cannot prove G_{F_M}, and G_{F_M} is true.

(4) We, however, can survey F_M's axioms and rules, recognize that they are all true, and so recognize that F_M cannot prove a falsehood — that is, that F_M is consistent — and therefore that G_{F_M}, being equivalent to the consistency of F_M, is true.

(5) So for every machine M there is a sentence that M cannot prove and that we can see to be true.

(6) So there is a sentence that no machine can prove and that we can see to be true.

(7) So our mathematical knowledge outruns that of any machine, and the mind is not a machine.

My worry is that the argument proves too much. Run the same reasoning on us. Let H be whatever consistent formal system best captures my arithmetical beliefs. By (1), H cannot prove the statement that H is consistent; by (4), I can survey H's axioms, see that they are true, and so see that H is consistent — a truth H cannot prove — so H fails to capture my beliefs and I am not H. So far that is only the intended conclusion, applied to me. But nothing in (4) is special to H: it licenses the same insight for any consistent system whose axioms I can survey. The argument therefore seems to imply that I can recognize the consistency of every consistent arithmetical system whatever, no matter how far beyond my survey, and that I cannot be mistaken in doing so. I know both that I can be mistaken about such things and that no finite being could certify the consistency of every consistent system. So the argument, taken straight, establishes not merely that the mind is not a machine but that we enjoy a consistency-oracle no finite being could have.

A friend says the mechanist has a cheap reply: hand the machine a reflection principle — the schema that if the machine proves a sentence then that sentence is true — and it will prove the consistency of F_M and hence G_{F_M} exactly as we do, so step (4) does not separate us from it. That seems to me only to postpone the problem, since the enriched machine then cannot certify its own consistency, and neither can I for the enriched system, and we are off on a regress; but I cannot tell whether the regress tells against the argument or against mechanism.

So: which step should I give up, why did it look so good, and what do I have to pay elsewhere if I give it up? In particular I cannot tell whether the real fault is in step (4)'s claim about what we can see, in the move from (5) to (6), or in how the machine is set up in (2) in the first place.
