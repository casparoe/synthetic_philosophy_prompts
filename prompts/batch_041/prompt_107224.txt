I am trying to determine whether the following pattern has an established name, because every label I try on it seems to cover something slightly different.

Two parties are locked in a contest that neither can win cheaply and neither can abandon cheaply, and yet neither has staked anything on the outcome that it would forfeit by stopping. My brother and I have spent two years and most of our mother's estate arguing over her house. Neither of us wants the house; each of us wants not to be the one who gave way. At any point either of us could have simply conceded it to the other and walked away, at no cost except the admission of having given way. The lawyers have now been paid more than the house is worth. When I ask him why it matters, his answer is always framed in terms of what it has already cost us; when I ask myself the same question, I give the same answer. It is as though the thing on the table becomes more valuable the more we both spend on it, and — this is the part I cannot fit to anything — his spending appears to raise its value to me as much as my own does. I see the same shape at work in two departments of the company I work for, each maintaining its own version of the same system, each waiting for the other to be the one whose project is cancelled.

I know the dollar auction, and this is not quite it: there the loser pays and the prize is worth a fixed amount, so the trap is that the price rises while the prize does not. Here the prize itself appears to rise, and nothing is forfeited by stopping. I also know the phrase "escalation of commitment," but what I am describing does not obviously involve defending a decision anyone made; neither of us chose this quarrel, and walking away would not require anyone to admit an error.

Does this pattern have a name? If it reduces to one of the things I have already mentioned, I would like to know precisely which features of my description are doing the work and which I am imagining. If it does not, please name it and define it, and tell me what falls outside it — in particular, whether it survives the substitution of a thing with a fixed market value, whether it requires two parties or can occur with one, what distinguishes it from a contest in which each side is merely waiting for the other to tire, and whether the fact that neither of us forfeits anything by stopping means the whole thing is really just stubbornness with a story attached.
