I'm writing a paper on the bootstrapping / easy knowledge problem and I want the spec first, before I commit to a line. Not the history, not who said what — I want to know what any account in this area has to actually deliver to count as adequate. So give me the requirements list. For each one, the case or consideration that forces it, and whether you think it's non-negotiable or just how one camp likes to set the table.

Here's the stuff I'm trying to handle. I look at a red wall and know it's red, with no prior justification for thinking my eyes work at all. I also know that if it's red, my eyes are working — and putting those two together to conclude my eyes are working seems like a step I can't give up. But when I sit there checking object after object, that one's red, that one's red, and then conclude my color vision is reliable, that's garbage reasoning, and it's the same shape applied over and over. Same for the thermometer I check with the thermometer, and the calculator I check with the calculator. Then some cases that are less literary: a pollster who validates her sampling method by running the same poll again and finding it lines up with what she expected; a model that reports how well calibrated it is based on its own confidence scores; a doctor who assesses her diagnostic reliability by reviewing cases she herself diagnosed; a person who gets a new sense — restored sight, or an implant that gives her reliable hunches about the weather — and over months learns to trust it by checking it against things she already trusts. I think she can genuinely come to know it's reliable, which makes me doubt that track records are automatically bad. And using modus ponens in an argument that modus ponens is valid doesn't bother me at all, so I doubt the problem is circularity as such. My hunch is that the bad cases are the ones where you already need the conclusion in order to take the premises at face value, but I dont know if that hunch survives the contrast between the color vision case and the new sense case.

Some candidate requirements to sort into real desiderata and prejudices: basic perceptual knowledge has to stay possible without a prior reliability premise, on pain of regress; reliability knowledge has to stay possible, or you've just got skepticism with extra steps; reliability knowledge has to not be cheap; it has to respect whatever difference there is, if any, between the two structurally identical inferences — the one from there being a hand in front of me to there being external objects, and the one from there being a hand in front of me to my eyes being reliable; it should be implementable, meaning it tells me what a system auditing its own outputs is and isn't entitled to conclude; and it has to say what's wrong with the bootstrapping inference specifically, in a way that doesn't also condemn the harmless cases above.
