My department's reading group has been grinding through the teleology/teleonomy literature for a couple months now, and last week two of my colleagues got into it in a way I honestly couldn't referee. Dara is a philosopher of biology; Tomas is a visiting postdoc who works on metaphysics and has a low tolerance for what he calls metaphors with tenure. The fight was over what they kept calling "free-floating rationales" — the idea that selection can build a system whose states and transitions track reasons (this is a good inference to make about where the nectar is; this is the right move if that shadow is a hawk) even though nothing in the system represents those reasons or entertains them. Dara's line is that the rationale is real and does explanatory work: it floats free of any reasoner, but it's still the reason the trait is there and the standard by which the trait gets evaluated. Tomas says that's just smuggling purpose back in through the side door — if nobody entertains the reason, then "reason" is a façon de parler, and what's actually doing the work is differential reproduction plus a pile of normative vocabulary we could and should drop.

Then it went a few rounds. Dara said the vocabulary isn't droppable, because we need it to say when a mechanism is functioning well versus malfunctioning: the frog's bug-detector that fires at falling leaves is getting it wrong, and that "wrong" is a fact about the mechanism and its history, not a projection. Tomas came back with what he called the falsifiability test — to say a system has a rationale for doing X, you have to be able to specify what state of affairs would show the rationale doesn't hold for it; otherwise you're just gerrymandering the reason to fit whatever the thing already does, and "X is well-adapted" collapses into "X happened." Dara said the frog case already meets his test. He said it doesn't, because the frog can't be corrected — there's no one whose mistake it is — and she said that's begging the question, since malfunction is exactly the case where you can be wrong with nobody home. By the end they'd drifted into whether any of this even applies to a thermostat or a river carving a channel, and Tomas said if it applies to those then it distinguishes nothing, and Dara said it applies to those but *differently*, and then the hour was up. Can you go through the arguement move by move and tell me who's right about what — in particular, is Tomas's falsifiability test a real criterion or a loaded one, and does either of them have a coherent way to say what's special about the cases that seem to matter (frogs, bees, maybe plants) as opposed to the ones that seem like a stretch (thermostats, rivers)? And tell me what both of them are missing, cause I suspect this is partly a verbal dispute and partly not.
