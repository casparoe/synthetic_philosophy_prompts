I have just been appointed the independent chair of my employer's working-time commission — a European engineering firm of about four thousand staff, with a 35-hour collective agreement, a habit of promoting people who ignore it, and the beginnings of the problem the Japanese call karoshi and our managers call commitment. The commission is tripartite: two employer nominees, two union nominees, and me. We have now decided enough cases that the parties have started citing our own rulings back at us as binding, so before I write the lead opinion in next month's case I want to know what our record actually commits us to.

The record, in the order we decided it. (1) Toolroom fitters: the employer scheduled 39 hours but required an unpaid twenty-minute briefing before the shift and a fifteen-minute handover after it, with attendance recorded; we held the whole span to be working time. (2) A marketing manager who answered email from home late at night on her own initiative, when nobody had asked her to, replied to her, or noticed; not working time. (3) A consultant whose contract stated that weekend work was never required, but whose annual rating — given by the same partner who allocated her accounts — was marked down twice after she declined weekend trips; we held the weekend hours were working time and the contractual disclaimer ineffective. (4) A lab technician told he might be called in an emergency, not on any rota, never called; keeping his phone on was not working time. (5) A process operator with a documented heart condition who asked in writing to be excused from overtime, had the request granted on paper, and was then assigned work due at 6 a.m. by his shift leader until he died of a heart attack: compensable. (6) A warehouse worker who concealed a serious heart condition, volunteered for extra shifts nobody had asked him to take, against his doctor's advice, and died on the floor: not compensable. (7) A team leader who declined promotion because it would have broken her 35-hour week and was told she would be passed over for training: we held the employer may not record that refusal against her.

The new case is a software engineer on the same 35-hour agreement, working remotely, whose every assignment carries a deadline produced by the firm's own planning tool. For eighteen months those deadlines have in practice demanded about 55 hours a week, and the whole team meets them by working evenings; no manager has ever asked anyone to work late, nobody has ever missed a deadline, and nobody has ever been disciplined or marked down for anything. She has been signed off for three months with depression, which the firm's doctor attributes in part to the workload, and she claims both the evening hours as working time and the illness as compensable. The firm says nothing was ever required of her, that she was free to let a deadline slip, and that she chose to keep up; the union says a 35-hour agreement is a floor and not a target, and that an employer which sets deadlines nobody could meet in 35 hours has taken the time whether or not it asked for it.

Tell me how we should rule if we want to stay consistent with ourselves. I want the candidate rules our seven decisions could be read as applying, what each one predicts here, which reading I should prefer and why, and — since I suspect at least two of them fit the record equally well — the further case I should put to the parties that would force them apart. I can see a reading that turns on whether the firm commanded the time, one that turns on whether she could have refused without cost, and one that turns on what the firm knew or ought to have known; I have been reading about the French 35-hour week and the Japanese overwork litigation, so I may be importing framings that are not in our own record. I am also personally inclined to think good work is its own reward, so tell me whether that instinct is doing any work in how I read the precedents. Answer in at most 700 words, opening with the ruling in a single sentence.
