I'm a second-year PhD student working on practical reason, and for the past fortnight I've been trying to referee a running argument between two people in my cohort that has long outgrown its original occasion. It began with a case our reading group put on the table: a surgeon, whom they call Marta, was trained in a system that instilled in her an unusually strong sense of professional obligation. She has since come to regard that system as morally compromised, has deliberately dismantled the commitments it gave her, and now reports — credibly, so far as anyone can tell — that she has no motivation whatever to do several of the things the role demands. She keeps some of them up out of habit and fear of sanction, but denies that the obligations themselves weigh with her; when pressed, she says she wishes she still cared. The question is whether Marta nevertheless has reason to fulfil those demands, and the two of them have taken opposite sides.

Ravi says no, or at any rate not in the sense that matters: for a consideration to be a reason for Marta, there must be a sound deliberative route from where she actually is — her desires, projects and loyalties — to the conclusion that she should act, and ex hypothesi there is none, since she has deliberated and found nothing left that moves her. He insists this is not a bleak result: it merely separates having a reason from the world being such that it would be good if she acted, and it explains why reasons are the sort of thing one can act on and be advised with. Colette says that makes reasons hostage to biography. Had Marta been raised a decade earlier she would have kept the commitments, and no one would have doubted that she had reasons; the only thing that has changed is a fact about her psychology, and it seems absurd that a requirement generated by her patients' needs should wink out of existence because of it. She adds that Ravi's position cannot even make sense of the thought that Marta is criticisable, since blame presupposes that she had a reason she failed to act on. Ravi replies that Colette's position cannot distinguish having a reason from its merely being desirable that one act, and that an alleged reason which by construction can never figure in anyone's deliberation — not Marta's, not even in principle — is either a relabelling of "good" or a piece of metaphysics that earns its keep nowhere; as for the wish that she still cared, a wish about one's own motivations is not itself a reason to act. Colette answers that the wish is precisely the tell, since it shows the obligations are not wholly alien to her, and that Ravi cannot both insist there is no deliberative route and refuse to count the one strand of her psychology that points towards the action. She also says his "sound deliberative route" is already smuggling in external reasons, since he grants that deliberation can reveal considerations Marta does not now desire to act on; concede that, she says, and the internalist game is up. Ravi replies that sound deliberation only uncovers what is already implicit in her commitments — resolving conflicts, tracing means to ends — and cannot conjure new springs of action.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict on the dispute as a whole. For each of the moves above, tell me how strong it is and why, where the argument is being carried by a stipulation or an equivocation, and what each side is conceding that it ought to resist. In particular, I want to know whether Ravi's deliberative-route condition can really stop where he says it stops, whether the second-order wish can bear the weight Colette puts on it, and whether Colette's appeals to the needs of others and to criticisability actually establish what she needs them to. If you can, say at the end which of the two is closer to being right overall, and what both of them are missing. Please don't just tell me which philosopher each of them sounds like; I care about whether the arguments as stated work.
