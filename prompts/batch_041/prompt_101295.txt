I am the rapporteur for a transitional justice commission whose amnesty jurisprudence has hardened into a body of precedent. Its mandate is to grant conditional amnesty for politically motivated offenses where the applicant makes full disclosure, but its actual rulings have not followed any single stated test. Its decided cases are these.

1. A low-ranking militia member who had personally killed two civilians received amnesty. He gave a complete account, named his immediate superior, said he acted to advance the movement's political aims, and expressed remorse. The family of one victim opposed the grant.
2. A mid-ranking commander who planned operations but did not personally kill was denied amnesty. He gave a complete account, expressed remorse, and said he acted for the movement's political aims, but the commission held that his command responsibility placed him outside the political-offense category.
3. A senior politician who financed a paramilitary group but did not order specific operations received amnesty. He gave a complete account, expressed remorse, said he acted to protect the state from a political insurgency, and disclosed assets and testified publicly. The commission reasoned that his acts were political in purpose and that disclosure, not punishment, was its aim.
4. A soldier who acted under duress was denied amnesty. He gave a complete account, but did not acknowledge that his acts were wrong, said he was merely following orders, and had no political aim; his offense was not part of a systematic campaign.
5. A prison guard who tortured detainees received amnesty after testifying. He gave a complete account, expressed remorse, and said he acted to extract information for the state's security policy; the commission found the torture was part of a systematic state policy. It noted that individual cruelty alone would not have qualified.
6. A rebel leader who gave a complete factual account but continued to justify the killing of civilians as legitimate was denied amnesty. The commission treated present acknowledgment of wrongdoing, not merely factual disclosure, as necessary.
7. A quartermaster who stole supplies for personal profit was denied amnesty. He gave a complete account and expressed remorse, but had no political aim and no connection to a systematic campaign.

The new petition is from a regional intelligence officer who designed a surveillance and data-fusion system that flagged individuals for detention. The system was part of a systematic state security program. He set the risk thresholds but did not select individual targets, interrogate anyone, or use force. He has given a complete factual account and named superiors. He expresses regret and concedes that the system caused serious harm, but describes his motive as professional duty rather than political conviction. Field units used the system's flags to detain people, some of whom died in custody. Victims groups oppose amnesty; the government argues that amnesty is necessary for stability.

What should the commission decide if it means to be consistent with its own precedents? Identify the closest analog among the earlier rulings. Set out the competing rules the pattern could be running, what each rule predicts here, and your best guess with reasons. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, state what principle the commission would be adopting with each available decision. Finally, name the further case that would best distinguish the competing rules.
