ok so this is maybe a wierd ask but here goes. i'm a junior and i run the satire page in our school lit mag, and my philosophy teacher got me obsessed with this position called epiphenomenalism, where your conscious experiences — the actual felt sting of pain, the redness of red — are completely real but cause absolutely nothing. your brain does all the work; the feeling is just along for the ride, like a shadow, or the whistle on a train that doesn't pull it.

what made me want to write about it is the objection my teacher calls the paradox of phenomenal judgment, which i think i get: if my pain doesn't cause anything, then my pain can't be what causes me to say ouch, or to believe i'm in pain, or to write any of this. my brain causes all that. so how do i ever know about my own experiences at all? the theory seems to quietly eat its own ability to be reported, believed, or even thought about. if you really believe it, your belief that you have experiences wasn't caused by any experience, which is pretty wierd.

i want this satire to actually be good, so here's the brief.

act like you're both a philosopher of mind (someone who really works on consciousness, not a vibes person) and a professional satirical writer. think it through carefully and brainstorm first — a few lines of notes is fine — then write.

the form: a court transcript. a deposition in a personal injury case. the plaintiff is an invented philosophy professor (not a real person, don't name any real philosophers or real books) who has published work defending epiphenomenalism. he's suing his former employer over chronic pain from a workplace accident and he wants damages for "pain and suffering." the defense attorney has actually read his papers and cross-examines him about them. deadpan court-reporter style: Q, A, a few "(pause)"s, no narrator, no winking, nobody in the room announcing how ironic this is.

here's what i want it to get right, so that someone who works on this stuff laughs instead of wincing:

- the professor should not be a strawman or an idiot. let him give the real replies, e.g. that the pain and the belief that he's in pain share the same physical cause in his brain, so the belief is a reliable report of the pain even though the pain itself did nothing; and that he never said the pain is unreal, only that it's causally idle.
- the attorney's pressure should be the genuinely hard part, not a cheap gotcha. two moves especially: (a) if the report is caused by the brain state and not by the feeling, the report would come out exactly the same if there were no feeling at all, so how is his testimony evidence of anything he feels? (b) what makes his belief about the feeling rather than about the brain state that produced the belief? a belief that never had anything to do with the feeling doesn't seem to be about the feeling at all. and (c) a bit where the attorney points out that a neuroscientist could have produced his whole testimony by poking the right neurons, which should visibly unsettle him.
- the comedy should come from the collision with the boring legal world: yes/no questions, "in your own words, sir," "i'll rephrase," "move to strike," the court reporter, a settlement number. the professor keeps wanting to give the careful version and the format won't let him.
- someone who has never heard the word epiphenomenalism should still follow it and laugh. don't lean on jargon or on any real names.

about 700-800 words, with a case-caption header like real transcripts have. end somewhere that leaves the reader honestly unsure whether the guy just destroyed his own lawsuit, made a real philosophical point, or both. and please don't make it mean — i actually find the view kind of beautiful, so it should read like the writer gets why someone would believe it.
