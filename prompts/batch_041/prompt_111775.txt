I've spent years reading the rationalist and AI-safety literature on Goodhart's law and specification gaming, and I've come to believe the standard framing — a proxy stops tracking its target once you optimize against it — quietly bundles together several distinct failure modes, and that until those modes are pulled apart, the interesting questions can't even be posed.

The partition I've been working with is this. (1) Statistical failure: the proxy is a noisy but unbiased correlate of the target, so selection on it moves you along the regression line; the loss is in expectation, not in mechanism. (2) Dynamical failure: the proxy is causally entangled with the target, so intervening on the proxy changes the target itself, often in the direction opposite to the one intended. (3) Constitutive failure: the proxy partly constitutes the target — there is no antecedent quantity it fails to track, because measuring and rewarding it is what brings the target into existence, and here talk of "divergence" is close to a category error. (4) Reflexive failure: the usual repair is itself a metric — measure the gaming and penalize it — which is in turn gameable, and it's unclear whether that regress terminates in something genuinely non-metric or merely relocates the problem one level up.

What I want is a dependency-aware agenda for this space, not a survey. Assume I'm one researcher with a year, a background in formal epistemology and some working knowledge of causal inference, and an end I actually care about: producing conceptual work that would change how oversight and evaluation regimes get designed, in AI training and in institutions alike. Which open questions here are most worth my year, in the sense that the most other questions depend on them? Please defend the ordering rather than just listing candidates, and don't bother with citations or a reading list — I can't check them and they aren't the point.

Structure your answer under these headings, in this order:

**Does the partition hold?** (~150 words) Before anything else, tell me whether my four-way split is doing real work or is a relabeling of the statistical/causal/adversarial distinction that already circulates. If it collapses, say into what, and which of the four survives as a genuine joint.

**The candidate questions.** (~500–700 words) Lay out the open questions worth considering, each stated crisply, with a sentence on what turns on it. I'd rather have eight sharp ones than twenty vague ones.

**The ordering, defended.** (~500 words) My top two or three, with the criteria you're applying — upstreamness, tractability for one person with my background, neglectedness, and how much downstream work would actually change if the question were settled — and an honest account of the trade-offs between those criteria where they conflict.

**What I should leave alone.** (~150 words) Questions that look central to the area but would be a poor use of my particular year, and why.
