I've been arguing for months now with a friend of mine who's doing a history PhD, about Arendt on Eichmann, and we're completely stuck — every time we meet up it's the same two moves and neither of us budges. Her line is that the later archival work basically killed the banality thesis: once you read the Argentina interviews and the stuff Stangneth dug up, Eichmann comes out as an unrepentant, boastful, genuinely enthusiastic antisemite and Nazi who was proud of his role, and that's just not the cliché-spouting thoughtless functionary in Arendt's book. So for her, Arendt got the man wrong, the concept falls with the man, and there's also something distasteful about a phrase that makes mass murder sound like a paperwork problem. My line is that yes, some of the sourcing was thin and I'm not going to die on the hill of Arendt's psychological portrait, but I don't think the idea stands or falls with one guy's motives, and she keeps attacking one claim and I keep defending another, and we never notice. It doesn't help that I have a vague memory that Arendt herself had said something about radical evil in the totalitarianism book and then later insisted evil isn't radical, only extreme — so which Arendt is even being refuted? — but I honestly can't remember well enough to make that into an arguemnt.

My hunch is that there are at least three different things wearing one name here: a factual-psychological claim about Eichmann (he wasn't a fanatic, he was thoughtless); a philosophical claim about what thoughtlessness has to do with doing evil at all, and what makes the specifically modern, bureaucratic version of it possible; and a moral-political fight about how she wrote about it — the tone, the passages about the Jewish councils, the whole question of what the trial was for. If that's right, then Stangneth-style evidence can be devastating for the first strand and totally irrelevant to the second, and my friend and I are just talking past each other. But I don't think that's the whole story either, because I suspect the word "banality" is slippery in a way that matters — is it supposed to be a property of the person, of the deed, or of the system? — and some of the dispute may be verbal in a non-trivial way, or may come down to what a concept like this is even for: a psychological hypothesis you could in principle test against archives, or a claim about judgment and responsibility that no archive could ever settle.

So: diagnose it for me. What kind of disagreement is this actually — empirical, conceptual, moral, or people answering different questions under a single heading — and which strands, if any, are genuinely settled and which only look settled because one side's evidence can't touch the other side's claim? Take the positions concretely enough that the diagnosis bites, and tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a reformulation of the thesis, a change of question, or nothing at all, in which case say why we should stop expecting these two camps to converge. No reading list please, I've read the book and the main criticisms, I want the diagnosis.
