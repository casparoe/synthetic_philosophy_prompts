People keep defending sortition with the same move and I want it audited properly. The move goes like this: a citizens' assembly picked by lot is basically jury duty writ large — we already trust twelve random strangers with a person's liberty, so trusting a few hundred of them with a policy question is a small, familiar step rather than a leap into the unknown. I'm sympathetic, but I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, and I've noticed that when I push on it the reply definately shifts: juries aren't supposed to be a representative sample anyway, they're supposed to be impartial, and that's the same thing the assembly is after. Is it? Please write me an audit:

- The mapping (table, ~200 words): what corresponds to what across the two — how members get picked, who sets the question they answer, what grounds their authority, what their decision binds, what representative means on each side, whether participation is compelled and how it's paid.
- Where it breaks (~300 words): the disanalogies that matter for the intended use (making lottocracy look like a small step), the ones that are merely cosmetic, and which is which.
- What it smuggles in (~250 words): features of the jury the analogy quietly imports that a citizens' assembly doesn't have — in particular, whether impartiality and descriptive representativeness are really the same good, and what happens to the argument if they aren't.
- Verdict (~250 words): does the small-familiar-step conclusion survive the audit, and if it doesn't, is there an analogy that would carry it better?

No reading lists or citations, just the audit.
