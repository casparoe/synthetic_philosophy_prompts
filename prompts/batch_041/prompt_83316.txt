I've been betting sports and playing poker for a living for about eight years now, so I think entirely in terms of edge, units, closing lines, Kelly fractions, drawdowns, risk of ruin, sample size, all that. I've never read a page of decision theory in my life, though I've picked up the log-utility / bankroll-growth argument secondhand from other gamblers, so I know it exists and roughly what it says.

Someone told me about the St. Petersburg game the other day — you flip a coin until it comes up tails, the pot doubles every flip, so the expected payout is infinite, yet nobody will pay more than a few bucks to play. I want you to restate the whole thing, and the main philosophical positions about it, in the vocabulary I actually use. One by one, as faithfully as you can. The positions I care about are: the one that says the game really is worth an infinite amount and my reluctance is just a bias; the one that says you should value money by its log, or by growth rate, rather than its face value; the one that says my utility for money has to be bounded somehow; the one that says expected value is the wrong decision rule altogether; the one that says the real problem is about comparing infinite quantities and the game is just a symptom of that; and the one that says the game is ill-posed because the payout can't actually be made.

Then give me the residue report. For each position: what comes through cleanly in my terms, what only survives as a distortion, and what has no expression at all in how I think. Don't just rename "expected utility" as "EV" and call that a translation — the interesting part is where the mapping breaks.

A few things I want you to be explicit about, because I suspect my vocabulary and the philosophers' aren't even talking about the same thing:

- I don't think I've ever seen a bet with an unbounded payout. Every market I've ever bet into has a cap somewhere, and there's a counterparty who has to be able to pay. Does my instinct that nobody would offer it and nobody could pay it actually solve the paradox, or does it just change the subject by describing a different game?
- The reason I turn a bet down is usually variance, not EV. A bet can be clearly +EV and still be one I won't take at any size, and some bets have finite EV and variance so bad they're unplayable. Am I right that this is a seperate objection from the philosophers' one, and can my vocabulary even state theirs?
- Everything I know is about repeated play — the long run, thousands of hands, alot of them. St. Petersburg seems to be one shot, and you pay to play each time. Does "just play it a lot" work here? If not, is that a real distinction or just a limitation of how I think?
- I've heard there's a result that you can build an even nastier game which breaks any unbounded utility function you might have, not just the money one. Where does that land in my terms — is there a gambling equivalent of it?

And at the end: is the stuff that doesn't translate a hole in my vocabulary, or a sign that the philosophers' question is less well defined than it sounds? I'd much rather you tell me my framing is missing something than flatter it.
