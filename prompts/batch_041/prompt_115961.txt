I'm hosting a film-and-philosophy night and we're doing Chaplin's Modern Times. I want to read it through Marx on alienation and exploitation, but not the lazy 'machines dehumanize the worker' version. Two things I'd like you to press on. First, the 1844 Manuscripts split alienation into four strands -- from the product, from the activity of labouring, from species-being, and from other people -- and I suspect the film really only dramatizes a couple of them; which ones, and does the Tramp ever get near the others? Second, Chaplin keeps making the machinery itself the villain (the assembly line, the feeding machine, the gears he vanishes into), whereas Marx insists the machine isn't the problem, the wage relation is -- does the film grasp that disctinction or does it quietly blame technology? And then the ending: the road, the gamin, the walk into the sunrise -- is that a Marxist resolution or just sentimental humanism with petit-bourgeois longings (a home, a hearth) instead of class consciousness? Give me a reading that's fair to both the film and the Marx, and tell me what the film shows about alienation that the Manuscripts can only argue for.
