I am working on a seminar paper about the dispute over whether knowing how to do something is a species of knowing that, and I want to build the central case myself rather than borrow one from the literature. The intellectualist side, in the version I am working with — the one associated with Stanley and Williamson — holds that knowing how to F is simply a matter of knowing a proposition: for some way w of F-ing, one knows that w is a way for one to F. On this view, the familiar complaint that skilled people often cannot say what they know is not a serious objection, because the way need not be known under a descriptive mode of presentation; the knower grasps it practically, as the way she is acting, and a practical grip on a way is not the sort of thing that can be put into words. The anti-intellectualist side, going back to Ryle's treatment of the intellectualist legend in The Concept of Mind, holds that knowing how is a different kind of thing altogether and is not constituted by knowledge of any proposition. I would like a single case, built to order, that puts maximum pressure on the intellectualist without begging the question against it, and I would rather be told that my specifications cannot be jointly satisfied than be handed a case that cheats.

The case must meet the following specifications. It should involve two ordinary adults and a single skill, described in no more than a paragraph; no science fiction, no exotic neurophysiology, no animals or infants, and no paralysis, injury, missing equipment, or other incidental obstacle that would explain the failure. The two people must be matched in every propositional respect I can think of: the same information, the same beliefs about the technique, the same beliefs about which descriptions of the technique apply to their own case, the same evidence, and the same opportunities. The only difference between them is that one can do the thing and the other cannot. The one who can must be at least as articulate as the other, and it must be clear that the difference is not a gap in information: the one who fails has been told everything, including a correct and complete description of the way the successful one moves, and can recite it, while the successful one's own spoken account of what she does is, as it happens, a false description of the way she actually moves. Avoid the stock examples that turn up in every discussion of this topic, and choose a skill whose execution cannot be captured by a short list of instructions.

After the case, do five things. First, state the verdict each side gives: what proposition, if any, the successful one knows, in what mode, and why the intellectualist is entitled to say that the unsuccessful one lacks the same knowledge. Second, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed — in particular, whether it collapses into the familiar insufficiency argument against intellectualism. Third, set out the practical-mode-of-presentation reply in your own words, say whether it rescues the intellectualist here, and say whether it is circular: that is, whether the relevant mode of presentation is available only to someone who already has the ability in question. Fourth, describe the strongest way for an intellectualist to attack the case — either by finding a proposition the successful one does know that I have overlooked, or by arguing that the unsuccessful one's deficit is itself a deficit of knowledge in the relevant mode — and say what would have to be true for that attack to succeed. Fifth, tell me which side, if either, the case actually refutes, and be willing to conclude that it refutes neither.
