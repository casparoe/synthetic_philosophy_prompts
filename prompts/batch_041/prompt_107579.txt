Write a dialogue between two members of a hospital ethics committee who disagree about what death even is. One is a neurologist: death is the irreversible loss of all brain function, brainstem included, because the brain is what holds the body together as one organism — circulation stopping is a sign, not the thing itself. The other is an intensivist who thinks that's a legal fiction, and that death has always really been the permanent loss of circulation; the brain criterion got adopted mostly to make organ donation tidy.

What I want them to actually fight about is a newer practice. A patient is declared dead by circulatory criteria — heart stopped, everyone's agreed not to resuscitate — and then a few minutes later surgeons put the body on a machine that pumps warm oxygenated blood back through the organs to keep them transplantable. Before they switch it on, they clamp or balloon off the vessels feeding the brain, specifically so no blood gets back up there. Which is the strange part: if the person's already dead, why do we have to block the brain? And if we're restarting circulation in the body, in what sense was it gone for good?

So have the neurologist argue that this shows circulatory death was never doing the work on its own — take the brain criterion away and you're just calling a corpse a corpse on a schedule. Have the intensivist push back that 'permanent' only ever meant 'we've decided not to restart it,' which is a call we're entitled to make, and that its the brain criterion that's the fiction, since those bodies stay warm and their wounds heal. Let them get into whether death is an event or a process we're just slicing, and whether either of them is quietly just rationalizing transplants. Both should be sharp, neither should fold.
