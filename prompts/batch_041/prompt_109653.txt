I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on the politics of working time — half the room are labour economists, the other half are humanities people like me who got invited for reasons nobody's explained, and it's a dinner so it needs to be warm and about seven minutes, not a lecture. The theme I've been handed is "what was Keynes actually promising us," and I want to do it properly rather than just trotting out the bit about fifteen-hour weeks. What interests me is the tension in the man. The young Keynes of his Cambridge set thought the point of life was a certain quality of experience — love, beauty, truth, the texture of your own and other people's states of mind — and cheerfully held that they recognised no moral obligations whatsoever; then the older Keynes looked back at all that and decided it was a fragile veneer over something much darker, with no real theory of action underneath, and that they'd been skimming along the surface without touching the currents below. And then there's the essay where he imagines a hundred years on and says that once the economic problem is solved we'll be faced with the real one: how to live wisely and agreeably and well, given that we've spent generations training ourselves to be purposive, to treat everything as a means to something else, and to prize money as a possession rather than a means. Write me the remarks, British English. It has to land for the economists without losing everyone else, and please don't let it turn into a seminar.
