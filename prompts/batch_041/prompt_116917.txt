I write a small newsletter for people who never studied philosophy formally but keep reading it anyway, and I want to try something in the form of a corporate credit-rating agency's methodology document, with a press release stapled on. The agency is invented — call it the Bureau of Blameworthiness or something better if you can think of it — and its product is a rating of a person's *standing to blame*. What got me onto this: a friend told me I had no business criticizing a colleague for something I'd done myself, and while I think she was onto something, I also think 'but you do it too' and 'you're wrong' are plainly not the same accusation, and I want a piece that keeps them apart instead of collapsing into either a shrug or a pile-on. So the document should be deadpan corporate throughout: a scope clause saying what the Bureau counts as blaming in the first place (believing someone did wrong in private, resenting them, telling them so, demanding they stop, agitating about them publicly — say whether these get the same rating and why); a schedule for the fault the blamer is alleged to share, distinguishing a fault that is the very same one from one that is merely similar in spirit; a relevance screen for whether the blamer's fault touches the norm being invoked at all; a temporal rule covering ongoing hypocrisy, long-abandoned hypocrisy, and the person who has stopped but only recently; a clause on whether an apology, a change of behaviour, or nothing at all can restore a rating; and an explicit note that a low rating is not the same as the complaint being false and does not, by itself, tell the person being blamed how to respond. Then a ratings scale from something like AAA down to D, two short worked examples (one high, one cut to junk), and a press release announcing a downgrade of an invented person, followed by a flat risk disclosure in which the Bureau concedes that under its own schedule very few people retain an investment-grade rating, that it has considered this objection, and that it has not revised the methodology. No real philosophers or books may be named anywhere — the Bureau cites only its own handbook, and I'd rather it never wink at the reader about how absurd it is. It's a satire of the practice of auditing who is allowed to complain, not of any actual person, movement, or argument, so the target should be recognizable to someone who knows this literature and still funny to someone who has never heard the phrase. Around 800 words.

One last thing: please add a short note to me, clearly outside the parody, saying where the piece takes liberties with the real debate or leans on a reading of it that people dispute, how confident you are in the philosophical claims the parody ends up committing the Bureau to, and which single one of those claims you are least sure of.
