For most of my career I worked with probabilistic and statistical models, and since retiring I have been reading in decision theory and epistemology. In a popular book on rational choice I came across a chapter that summarizes the common prior assumption and Aumann's agreement theorem roughly as follows: "Aumann proved that two perfectly rational agents who start from the same prior probabilities simply cannot disagree. Once each of them knows the other's opinion, reason forces them to the same belief, which is why the result is nicknamed the 'no disagreement theorem.' So whenever sensible people disagree, either one of them is being irrational or they must be working from different evidence; and this is precisely why economists treat the common prior assumption as the sensible default." Something about this strikes me as too strong, but I cannot quite locate where the slide happens, and I do not trust my instinct enough to know which parts are load-bearing. Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a decision theorist or game theorist would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I am especially interested in whether the theorem's conclusion really follows from the premises as stated, and whether the advice to treat the common prior assumption as the default is a consequence of the theorem or an independent commitment smuggled in beside it. Where the verdict depends on matters that specialists still argue about, say so explicitly, and keep what is settled distinct from what is genuinely contested rather than presenting a live disagreement as the consensus.
