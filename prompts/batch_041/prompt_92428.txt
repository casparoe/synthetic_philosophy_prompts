I am an undergraduate in a seminar on set theory and the philosophy of mathematics. For my final assignment, write a dialogue between two equally competent mathematicians: a classical set theorist who defends the axiom of choice as an indispensable existence principle, and a constructivist who rejects it as ungrounded. Center their exchange on a distinction I have been calling witnessed choice: a choice function is witnessed when there is a formula that provably selects an element from each nonempty set in a family, and unwitnessed otherwise. The classical theorist should argue that the axiom's real work lies in the unwitnessed cases and that refusing them changes the subject from set theory to a weaker theory; the constructivist should argue that unwitnessed choice functions are not objects we can meaningfully quantify over and that the classical theorist's indispensability is a pragmatic rather than an epistemic claim. Let them press each other on whether existence in the axiom is the same sense of existence as in constructive mathematics, and on what exactly would be lost if one dropped the axiom. They should respond to each other's actual formulations rather than talking past each other. No citations, bibliography, or historical references are needed; reason from the notions themselves.
