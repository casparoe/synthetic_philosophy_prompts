For a graduate seminar I'm giving on Schopenhauer's aesthetics, I've decided to end the term by pairing his philosophy of music with Thomas Mann's Doctor Faustus — a novel I've taught several times but never before from the Schopenhauer side — and I'd like your help in working out the reading before I commit to it in class. You will know the shape of Schopenhauer's claim: whereas the other arts, architecture and sculpture and painting and poetry, present the Ideas, the objective grades of the Will's objectification, music is the exception, since it copies not the Ideas at all but the Will itself, and so stands to the phenomenal world roughly as the Will stands to its manifestation; from this he draws the arresting consequence that music could subsist even if there were no world, but no world could subsist without music. He then reads the elements of music as a picture of the Will's structure — the bass and harmony as the lower objectifications, melody as the sustained, deliberate life of the Will itself — and treats music as the most metaphysical of the arts precisely because it speaks, as he thinks, in a language the understanding cannot translate into concepts. Mann's novel, of course, is saturated in the German metaphysics of music running from Schopenhauer through Wagner and Nietzsche, and its composer-hero Adrian Leverkühn arrives at a music that is at once the most severely intellectual — construction, calculation, the strict style — and, in the novel's own telling, demonic, bound up with a pact, with disease, and finally with the catastrophe of Germany itself.

What I want from you is a sustained critical reading of the novel through this doctrine, faithful in both directions. Does Leverkühn's music, and the novel's account of what music is and does — above all in the great conversation with the devil, and in the descriptions of his works and of the closing lament — confirm Schopenhauer's thesis that music is the direct expression of the Will, or does it quietly refute it by making music the most conceptual, the most constructed, and the most historically guilty of the arts? Where does Schopenhauer's metaphysics genuinely illuminate a scene or a character's choice, and where does the novel resist the reading, so that an honest critic has to admit the doctrine is being strained or inverted? I am especially interested in the tension between Schopenhauer's music as wordless and conceptless and Mann's music as the vehicle of a specifically German intellectual and moral predicament, and in whether Leverkühn's strict style is a fulfillment or a betrayal of the Will-metaphysics. Please do not reduce the novel to an illustration of the philosophy, and do not bend Schopenhauer to fit the plot; and end by telling me what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate about music that argument alone does not — what it dramatizes about music's relation to the Will, to feeling, and to the intellect that a treatise cannot. I will be teaching this to students who know both the novel and the Schopenhauer reasonably well, so you may assume the plot and the major scenes are familiar and need not summarize them; I want the reading and the judgment, not citations or a bibliography.
