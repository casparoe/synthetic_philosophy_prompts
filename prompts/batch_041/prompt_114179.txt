I'm about two-thirds through a novel set in a made-up country — I've been calling it Ravnia — and a lot of it turns on one song. The song is the ancient folk song of the place, the one everyone's grandmother supposedly knew, the one they belt out at the midsummer do. The catch is that it was written in 1889 by a schoolteacher. Nobody in the book knows that. As far as I can tell nobody in the country knows either. I need the actual text and everything I write comes out sounding like a bad hymn.

So, fussy list, sorry:

- It has to read old. River, mountain, a battle, an ancestor, the usual furniture.
- But the form has to be wrong for how old its pretending to be. Four square rhyming stanzas, the kind of thing a 19th-century schoolteacher with a piano and a temperance streak would knock out — not anything an oral tradition would actually produce. No long alliterative lines, nothing properly bardic. That's the whole joke: the song is 130 years old dressed up as a thousand.
- The refrain is the one part that should feel genuinely old. Rough, dialect, half-rhymed or unrhymed, a bit gnomic, grammatically not quite fitting the verses. The story is the schoolteacher lifted a real scrap of something — a line from a work song, a bit of a lullaby — and built his improving verses around it. The join should be obvious to someone whos looking for it and invisible to everyone else.
- Put one give-away detail in one of the verses. Something that could only exist after roughly 1850 — a printed book, a schoolhouse, a clock, a flag on a pole. Don't point at it. Anyone who knows the Hobsbawm invented-traditions stuff should spot it and grin. The neighbours shouldn't.
- The refrain should be first person plural, a "we" that has clearly never met. The imagined-community business — strangers singing themselves into a people.
- No lines announcing any of this. No "tradition", no "ancient", no winking at the reader.

Last thing, and it's the real reason I want a poem and not a paragraph: I want the repetition to do the work. By the time the refrain comes back the last time, I want it to feel like it's always been there — like the reader has known it their whole life — even though the poem invented it four stanzas ago. A reader who's paying attention should catch the poem manufacturing antiquity out of nothing but repetition; a reader who isn't should just hum along. If the poem can't do that, the chapter doesn't work.

16-24 lines, no title, and don't put the country's name in it.
