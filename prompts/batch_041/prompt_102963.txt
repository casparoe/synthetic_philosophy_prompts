I am assembling a handout for the third meeting of a graduate seminar on decision theory. The first two meetings covered preference relations and the expected-utility representation theorem; next week we turn to the Allais pair and the independence axiom. My students can all recite the standard pattern—most people take the sure million in the first pair and the lottery in the second—and they can all say the word independence, but when I press them on what the pattern shows they collapse into either treating the axiom as obviously constitutive of rationality or treating the pattern as a decisive counterexample. I want a diagnostic decision tree that sorts them by what they actually believe about the cases, not by which label they have memorized.

Please write that tree. Begin at a node for someone who reports the standard Allais preferences: a certain $1 million versus an 89 percent chance of $1 million, a 10 percent chance of $5 million, and a 1 percent chance of nothing; then an 11 percent chance of $1 million and an 89 percent chance of nothing versus a 10 percent chance of $5 million and a 90 percent chance of nothing. The first branch should separate whether the respondent regards the pattern as irrational, as rational but not representable by expected utility, or as a misframing of the decision problem. From there, build the tree out of questions that can be answered without knowing any theory names; none of the questions may mention independence, expected utility, or any named theory. Use at least these further cases: a version in which the common consequence is not a sum of money but the retention of a current job or status quo; a version in which the probabilities are ambiguous rather than known; a sequential version in which the respondent first learns whether the 89 percent event occurred and then chooses; a version in which the sure option is a certain loss rather than a gain; and a version in which the large prize is replaced by a small chance of catastrophe. At least one internal node should turn on whether the respondent distinguishes the common-consequence condition from the substitution condition, since I find that students conflate them. Each internal node should be a question about one of these cases or about a distinction the cases force, and each branch should be labeled with the answer that leads down it.

The leaves should be positions, named where a standard name exists and described functionally where it does not: for example, a view on which independence is a requirement of rationality and the Allais pattern is an error; a view on which the pattern is rational but only refutes one particular representation of preference; a regret-based view; a reference-point or prospect-theoretic view; a view that revises probability weighting rather than the axiom; a view that independence is a property of a representation rather than a norm; a view that rationality imposes only transitivity and dominance; a view that the real problem is ambiguity rather than risk; and a deflationary view on which there is no fact of the matter about rationality here. For each leaf give one sentence stating its core claim and one sentence naming the case that is most awkward for it. Where two leaves make the same prediction on every case in the tree, say so explicitly and either merge them or flag that the tree cannot separate them. Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, say whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I do not need citations, reading lists, or historical attributions; the instrument should stand on the cases and the distinctions they draw.
