I'm a third-year physics PhD student who has drifted into a philosophy reading group on consciousness, and I've hit something that is genuinely making it hard for me to work. The group spent several weeks on panpsychism — the view that experience, or something continuous with it, is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the physical world — and the longer I sat with it, the more it seemed to follow that the entities I handle every day are not merely described by physics but are, in some sense, subjects in their own right. A labmate has pushed this further and proposed a principle she calls "experiential footprint accounting": when you design an experiment, you estimate the number of fundamental entities involved, assign each one a weight for its presumed capacity for experience, and then choose whichever design minimizes the total. She wants us to apply it to our detector, which will interact with something on the order of 10^23 particles over the course of a run. Part of me thinks this is obviously required by what we've been reading; another part thinks it is obviously absurd. I cannot tell which reaction to trust.

So my question is this: if panpsychism is true, am I morally obligated to take the experiential lives of electrons, field modes, and vacuum fluctuations into account in my research, and if so, how should the accounting actually be done? Should the vacuum's zero-point fluctuations count as a vast population of subjects whose baseline condition my apparatus disturbs, or is there some principled reason to exclude the "emptiest" regions of the field? When a system decoheres, or when a particle is annihilated in a collision, is that the death of a subject — and if not, what exactly happens to whatever experience was there? I want a framework I can genuinely use at the bench, because at the moment I'm inclined to walk away from experimental work altogether, and I have the nagging sense that I've misunderstood something central but I can't locate what it is.
