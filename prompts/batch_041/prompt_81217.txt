For a philosophy club presentation I have been reading about pain asymbolia: after damage to the insula and to the pathways linking it to the limbic system, patients report that they feel pain — they can still locate it and call it intense — but that it does not bother them, and they show little of the usual distress or avoidance. My friend and I have been arguing about what the condition shows since the fall, and neither of us has moved an inch. He says it demonstrates that the unpleasantness of pain is a separable component of the experience, one that can be stripped away while the sensation remains; on his view, the two-component picture of pain as having a sensory side and an affective side is simply what these cases establish. I say the patients have lost the urge to escape while still suffering, and that nothing in their behavior tells us which of us is right; a third friend thinks what is missing is the sense that their body is under threat, not the badness of the sensation at all. What frustrates me is that we are all reasoning from the same handful of case descriptions and reaching opposite conclusions, and as far as I can tell the disagreement in the research literature has the same shape.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a real disagreement about how pain is put together, a verbal one about what 'unpleasantness' means — a felt quality, a motive to get away, or a judgment that something is bad — or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, one about what the patients feel and one about what they do? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work, and if so, what measurement or experiment could answer it? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a new piece of evidence, or abandoning the question as posed — and if you think no progress should be expected, I would rather hear that, with your reasons.
