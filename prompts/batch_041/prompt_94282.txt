I'm a 4th-year PhD student in cog sci/phil of mind and I run a reading group that's about to do the emergence/downward-causation unit, and I want to spend one session on Hofstadter's careenium — the frictionless table of little magnetic marbles that clump into bigger balls, where the low-level story is all jostling marbles and the high-level story is symbols gliding around and shoving each other. The question I want them to actually feel is whether the symbol-level shove is a real push or just bookkeeping, and I don't want to just lecture it at them.

Setting: ~10 grad students, 75 minutes, a seminar room with enough floor space to walk around in. Here's my rough plan, please fix it up and fill in the details.

Everyone is a sim. Each person gets a private card with a simple local rule that only refers to the nearest person within arm's reach and their color — stuff like step toward if red, away if blue, freeze if no one's close. No talking. Colors are on sticky dots so you can only see the state, not who. The rule distribution is fixed so clusters should form on their own. I stand at the edge and every so often apply a perturbation — call out a color and everyone in that state takes a step toward the wall. Clusters should form, drift, persist.

Then the symbol: one particular cluster shape that has reliably preceded the perturbation in the last few rounds. Two observers keep separate ledgers, one recording only sim-level events (who stepped, which rule fired) and one recording only cluster-level events (shapes, cluster movements). At the end each sim is asked whether anything pushed them besides their neighbor — they'll all say no. Then the two ledger-keepers each try to predict the next round using only their own ledger.

The moment I'm after: it'll feel obvious mid-game that the cluster made you move, and then the local-rule account turns out to be complete. Then I want to pair them off, half defending "the symbol did no causal work" and half "the symbol did work," each side having to say what would have to be different in the world for them to be wrong. Maybe a counterfactual round: rerun with one sim's rule changed deep inside a cluster, then rerun with the perturbation changed, and see which level predicts the outcome shift.

Debrief needs to map sims/simmballs/perturbation onto substrate/symbol/sensory input, and be honest that the toy is fully deterministic at the micro level so it stacks the deck toward the deflationist — the facilitator has to supply the argument for why real cognitive systems might not be like that. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I'm betting on people quietly coordinating and wrecking the local-rule discipline) and what part of Hofstadter's actual point this can't reproduce — I know it can't get at the strange-loop/self-modelling bit at all, but I want that said plainly so I don't oversell it.
