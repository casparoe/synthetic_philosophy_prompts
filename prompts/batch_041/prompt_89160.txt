I am a high school student working on a project that my history and biology teachers have let me combine, and I want to use an ecological model to think about the Classic Maya "collapse." I already have the historical background I need, so please do not send me a reading list or citations; what I want is a careful analysis that runs in both directions, between the case and the model.

The model is the adaptive cycle from resilience theory, associated with the ecologist C. S. Holling and developed with Lance Gunderson. It describes a complex system moving through four phases: a slow growth phase of rapid exploitation, a conservation phase in which connectedness and accumulated capital peak and the system becomes rigid and brittle, a brief release phase of creative destruction, and a reorganization phase that seeds the next cycle. The first two form the slow foreloop, the last two the fast backloop, and cycles are said to be nested across scales, a structure the theory calls a panarchy.

The case, as I understand it, is this. The Classic period runs to about 900 CE, and its final century is called the Terminal Classic. During the eighth and ninth centuries the great southern lowland cities, among them Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, and Copán, stopped erecting dated monuments and were abandoned, and the population of the Copán valley fell from a peak of some twenty-eight thousand to under a thousand within a few centuries. The leading explanation now is a series of severe droughts, probably combined with warfare and the failure of royal authority; some argue that what ended was a specific political institution, divine kingship, rather than the society itself. Meanwhile the northern Yucatán prospered, Chichén Itzá briefly built an empire across much of the Maya region, and Mayapán, Uxmal, and the highland states of the K'iche' and Kaqchikel flourished. Independent Maya states persisted until the Spanish took the last one, Nojpetén, in 1697, and millions of Maya people still live in the region.

What I want is three things. First, map the Maya case onto the four phases as tightly as the evidence allows: what, concretely, plays the role of exploitation, of conservation, of release, and of reorganization, and what in the archaeological record counts as evidence for each rather than mere narrative fit. Second, tell me where the mapping breaks. Does the theory have a principled way to draw the boundary of the system it is describing, when the Maya region was never one polity but a shifting network of competing states? How does it handle a driver like drought that is exogenous to the cycle? And is the identification of phases anything more than hindsight? Third, run the analysis the other way: what does this case show about the adaptive cycle itself? Is it a theory that predicts anything, or a description loose enough to accommodate any outcome after the fact? When the framework treats the northern and later Maya as a reorganization at a different scale of the panarchy, does that genuinely explain the case, or does it simply save the theory from a counterexample? And if the answer is that nothing collapsed, only reorganized, I would like to know whether "collapse" becomes a scale-relative term, and whether that makes it useful or empty. Please be explicit about which parts of your answer are the model speaking and which are you.
