I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about Hegel's master-slave dialectic, and I need help sorting out where her argument goes wrong. She laid it out like this:

(1) Self-consciousness is possible only through recognition by another self-consciousness.
(2) Recognition is genuine only when it is mutual—that is, when each party recognizes the other as a free and equal self-consciousness.
(3) In the master-slave relation, the master is recognized by the slave, but the slave is not recognized by the master as an equal; the slave's recognition is therefore one-sided.
(4) So the master does not receive genuine recognition and thus does not achieve self-consciousness.
(5) The slave, by contrast, through labor and the shaping of the object, comes to see himself in his work and thereby achieves a kind of self-consciousness.
(6) Therefore, the slave is actually in a superior position to the master with respect to self-consciousness.
(7) If the slave achieves self-consciousness through forced labor, then any condition of unfreedom that involves labor is conducive to self-consciousness.
(8) So slavery is beneficial to the slave, which is absurd.
(9) Therefore, one of the premises must be false.

I think (8) is absurd, but I'm not sure which premise to give up. I want to keep (1) and (2) if possible. Where does this go wrong, and what does rejecting the faulty step cost me? Please answer in American English.
