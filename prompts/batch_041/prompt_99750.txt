I host a small interdisciplinary interview series for a graduate seminar in comparative political theory, and our next guest is a political anthropologist who has spent roughly two decades doing fieldwork inside West African and Southeast Asian bureaucracies. Her book is a sustained attack on what she calls the universalist camp in anti-corruption work: her claim is that the global anti-corruption industry has quietly smuggled in a specifically modern, Western conception of the public office — one that presupposes a sharp separation between a role-holder's public duties and his private obligations, and that treats any flow of value across that line as a breach — and then mistaken that conception for a neutral, culture-free standard. She insists on separating three things I keep collapsing into one: (a) straightforward embezzlement of public funds for private luxury; (b) clientelism, which she argues can be a stable, predictable, and even accountable mode of representation, in which a patron who fails to deliver for his clients loses standing and can be punished at the next opportunity; and (c) the gift-giving, hospitality, and reciprocal favor that sustain the personal relationships through which anything gets done at all where impersonal institutions are weak. She grants that (a) is uncontroversially wrong, but claims that folding (b) and (c) into the same index as (a) is both descriptively distorting and politically self-serving, since it lets rich countries score well on a metric built to flatter their own institutional form.

I'd like eight to ten interview questions that press her rather than let her restate the book, with at least one question each on: the standing objection that clientelism substitutes private for public accountability and reliably hurts the poor, who lack the leverage to punish a patron; the so-called grease-the-wheels versus sand-the-wheels dispute over whether informal payments can ever improve outcomes in an over-regulated economy; whether her position collapses into a moral relativism she'd disown, or whether she can name a standard that is not merely local — for instance, whether a practice is transparent to those it binds, or whether its burdens fall on people who could refuse them; how she proposes to tell a gift that is constitutive of a relationship from a bribe that is merely disguised as one, and whether there is a fact of the matter there or only the participants' own understanding; and the collective-action problem, where if everyone is doing it, individual defection looks less like integrity than like self-harm. For each of those, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — I want the sequence to make her standard moves progressively harder to use, and I want to end on whether a genuinely non-parochial concept of corruption is even constructible, or whether the honest conclusion is that we should stop pretending the word names one thing.

Finally, close with a short note on where this line of questioning is most likely to fail: flag which of the questions presupposes a reading of her view she might plausibly reject, identify the one most likely to read as a cheap gotcha rather than a genuine press, and say which answer she could give that ought to make you abandon the sequence you have built.
