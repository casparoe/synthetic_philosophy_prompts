My father was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's a few months ago, and since then I have become the person who handles his affairs — I hold his power of attorney and I am the one his doctors call. Two years ago, while he was still entirely himself, I sat with him and a lawyer while he executed an advance directive. He was unusually explicit about it: he had watched his own mother disappear over eight years, and he said, in substance, that he wanted no part of the last stages. No resuscitation, no antibiotics for pneumonia, no feeding tube, and, if it came to it, that he would want his life ended rather than lived out in a ward where no one recognized him. The directive is binding on everything I can point to in a statute — resuscitation, ventilation, dialysis, antibiotics, artificial nutrition. What it cannot govern is the thing he cared about most. As I understand it, even in the handful of places that nominally allow a written advance request to stand in for a request made at the time, it is almost never honored in practice, and everywhere else the law requires that the person asking to die be competent at the moment of asking and be suffering unbearably at that moment. So the same man's considered, written, witnessed instruction is treated as authoritative about his body in every respect except the one he would have said was the point, and I am left holding a document whose main effect will be to keep him alive in precisely the condition he was trying to avoid.

Once I started noticing this, I noticed other asymmetries running in the same direction and some running the other way. A do-not-resuscitate order signed while competent is honored later, when the signer can no longer confirm it; so is a decision to donate organs; so, as far as I can tell, is a decision to stop eating and drinking, which I gather is lawful in most places even though it can be just as deliberate, and just as certain in its result, as an injection. Meanwhile the suffering that licenses an assisted death has to be traceable to a medical condition — the law appears to want dying to be a medical event with physicians as gatekeepers, rather than something a person simply decides. And the safeguards read as though the danger were a class of people rather than a property of situations: the elderly, the disabled, the depressed, the poor are treated as presumptively vulnerable and in need of protection from their own settled judgments, even when the particular person in front of the review committee has been clear for years.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the view of a person that this whole arrangement presupposes, not a survey of positions. Which theory of autonomy does it commit us to — that a person is only ever the self who is endorsing things now, or that a person is the author of a life and the author's earlier considered judgments carry authority forward, or something thinner, merely an apparatus for preventing harm? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? Are its parts even consistent with one another, given that we honor the incompetent patient's earlier refusal of a ventilator but not his earlier request for a death? Where is it philosophically underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license the same statutes? And then take a definite position: say which theory the practice actually presupposes, whether that theory can be defended, and what would have to change — in the safeguards, in the role assigned to physicians, in how vulnerability is assessed — if we adopted the rival view instead. Tell me plainly if my framing is wrong, and if the asymmetry I am complaining about turns out to be justified rather than merely convenient. I am not asking for a list of jurisdictions or statutes; I can look those up, and I would rather have the argument.
