I've drafted a short paper arguing that counterpart theory cannot preserve a certain intuition about de re modality, and I want a hard critical read of the argument rather than of the prose. I state both views below in my own words, so please work from what I've written rather than from anything you recall about the literature, and don't bother with references or reading suggestions — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography. Don't worry about whether the argument is original; assess whether it works.

The two views. Treat a possible world as a complete way things could have been. On the transworld-identity view, one and the same individual exists in more than one world and simply has different properties in different worlds. On counterpart theory, no individual exists in more than one world; each individual exists in exactly one world, and a claim about what could or could not have been true of a particular individual — a de re modal claim, as opposed to a claim about what is possible given how some description happens to be satisfied — is true just when some counterpart of that individual in another world satisfies the relevant condition. A counterpart is an individual in another world that resembles the actual individual closely enough, where "closely enough" is supplied by context. I work with the liberal version of the view, on which the counterpart relation need not be one-to-one, symmetric, or transitive; my argument turns only on its not being one-to-one.

The argument.

(1) Take two actual people, Ana and Ben, who are alike in every respect that bears on resemblance: same history up to a point, same traits, same dispositions. In some world w there is a single person, Cal, who resembles Ana closely enough to be her counterpart and also resembles Ben closely enough to be his counterpart. Because the relation need not be one-to-one, the liberal counterpart theorist cannot rule this out.

(2) Now take two claims: that Ana could have been a pianist, and that Ana could have been Ben. The first is unproblematic — Cal is a pianist, so a counterpart of Ana is a pianist. But the same machinery yields the second: there is a world in which a single individual is both a counterpart of Ana and a counterpart of Ben.

(3) The second claim is false. Ana and Ben are two distinct people; nothing about how the world might have gone makes one of them the other. The transworld-identity view cannot license it either, since a single world-bound individual cannot be identical with two distinct actual individuals.

(4) So the liberal relation overgenerates: it erases the difference between what could have happened to Ana and what could have happened to Ben. I don't see a principled repair. Forbidding many-to-one relations while permitting one-to-many is ad hoc, since resemblance is not one-to-one in either direction, and the one-to-many direction has to be permitted if the view is to handle fission and gradual replacement — among the cases it was built for.

(5) Conclusion: the counterpart theorist faces a dilemma. Permit many-to-one and the individual's modal profile is no longer determinate, since there is no stable set of counterparts that is hers; forbid it and the resemblance-based account of the relation is unmotivated. Either way the view cannot keep what the transworld-identity view keeps, and the choice between the views is not between a costly theory and a cheap one but between two different losses.

What I want.

- Is (1) coherent under the version I've described, and does the second claim in (2) really follow? Or does my reading of "Ana could have been Ben" equivocate — should the counterpart theorist say that claim is ill-formed rather than false?
- Is the dilemma in (5) exhaustive? Is there a third option, for instance relativizing de re claims to a context that supplies the counterpart relation, or constraining the direction of the relation by something other than resemblance?
- Is the move from (4) to (5) valid? "No stable set of counterparts" sounds like semantic context-sensitivity; "no determinate modal profile" sounds like metaphysical indeterminacy. Am I conflating the two?
- If the argument works, does it also tell against any view that relativizes de re modality — including a transworld-identity view that must appeal to some similarity relation to say when two world-bound individuals are the same individual? If so, is my conclusion really a point against counterpart theory rather than against a whole family of views?
