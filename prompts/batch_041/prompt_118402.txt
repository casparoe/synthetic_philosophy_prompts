Suppose I adopt Hofstadter's superrationality as a standing policy for one-shot interactions: in any symmetric game, choose the action that would be best for me if every player who reasons as I do made the same choice. What are the most important consequences of this policy for cases where the game has no symmetric pure-strategy optimum — for example a volunteer's dilemma in which exactly one of two equally placed people must act, and in which the only symmetric equilibrium is mixed? Address specifically: what the policy prescribes when "reasoning alike" forces identical choices and so rules out the randomization the situation seems to require; whether the policy is best understood as a decision theory or as a coordination device that quietly presupposes a shared randomization source; and what changes when my counterparty is merely similar to me rather than running the same procedure on the same inputs. Close with the one consequence you take to be most important for how I should actually act in such situations.
