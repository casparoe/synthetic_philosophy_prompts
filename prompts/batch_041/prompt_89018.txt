I host a small departmental podcast on philosophy of mind, and next month's guest is an early-career philosopher who defends a position she calls "mechanism-first functionalism." I want the interview to stay in the details of her view rather than retracing the standard map of the debate, so let me give you the three moving parts I need you to work against. First, she argues that the familiar case for functionalism — that the same mental capacity turns up in creatures with very different hardware — is weak, since it shows only that our taxonomies are coarse; the real evidence, on her view, is degeneracy: within a single organism the same capacity can be produced by different mechanisms at different times or after injury, with no change in the capacity's causal profile. Second, she proposes a condition on realization she calls "pathway-matching": a system realizes a functional role only if the distinct counterfactual dependencies in the role's specification are each tracked by a distinct causal pathway in the system. She offers this as the escape from the triviality objection — that any physical system whatsoever can be described as realizing some role. Third, when pressed on whether anything is left over once the role is fixed and the pathways match — phenomenal character, aboutness — she tends to reply that her questioner is describing the same fact at a coarser grain, and that there is nothing left over to explain.
