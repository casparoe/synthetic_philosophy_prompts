Last week a friend of mine who did a philosophy degree and is generally very confident gave me her take on disjunctivism and the argument from illusion, and I wrote it down almost word for word so I could think about it later. Here is what she said:

"Philosophers used to think that when you see a straight stick looking bent in water, what you're really aware of is a mental image, a sense-datum, because the stick itself isn't bent and your experience is exactly the same as it would be if it were. The argument from illusion is what proves this: since you can't tell the two cases apart, and in one of them there's no bent thing in the world, the immediate object of your awareness has to be something in your mind. So we never perceive the world directly — we only perceive our own ideas. Disjunctivism is the modern comeback. It says that perception and hallucination are two completely different kinds of mental state that just happen to feel the same from the inside. In the good case you're directly in contact with the stick; in the bad case you're not in contact with anything at all. That's why disjunctivists say the argument from illusion is simply invalid — you can't start from a case where you're misperceiving and conclude anything about the case where you're perceiving correctly, because the two have nothing in common. The upshot is that we can be certain of what we perceive, and skepticism about the senses is refuted."
