I am a first-year PhD student in philosophy of language, and I have spent the last month trying to get clear on David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals because they seem to bear directly on a formal model of belief revision I am building for a qualifying paper. My model treats an agent's credence in an indicative conditional as the agent's conditional credence in the consequent given the antecedent—what I had understood to be the Ramsey test—and I had also assumed that this was the only serious option left once Lewis showed that indicative conditionals cannot have truth conditions. A visiting speaker in our department told me that this is a muddle, but when I pressed her she said only that I was conflating the probability of a conditional with the probability of its truth, and that the triviality results are not an argument against truth-conditional semantics as such. Now I am stuck, because if conditionals do have truth conditions, then it seems to me that their probabilities must be calculated from the probabilities of the worlds at which they are true, and Lewis's proof would then show that those probabilities cannot generally equal the corresponding conditional probabilities. So either conditionals have no truth conditions, or the Ramsey test is simply false, and neither option seems to leave room for the ordinary probabilistic reasoning about conditionals that my model needs.

Could you explain exactly what Lewis's triviality results establish and, more importantly, what they do not establish? I would like to know which of my assumptions is the false presupposition, whether the results rule out all truth-conditional accounts of indicative conditionals or only a particular thesis about their probabilities, and how a defender of a truth-conditional semantics can avoid the triviality while still making sense of conditional credence. I am also unsure whether the results apply to counterfactuals in the same way, and whether the distinction between indicative and counterfactual conditionals is doing any work here or is just a distraction. A friend in the linguistics department has suggested that the proof only bites because it assumes the equation must hold for every probability function, including ones that assign zero to the antecedent, and that if we restrict attention to regular probability functions—or adopt a three-valued semantics on which the conditional is undefined when the antecedent is false—the problem disappears. Is that a genuine escape from the triviality, or does it merely relocate the difficulty? I would be grateful for a diagnosis that separates my genuine questions from the confusion that generated them.
