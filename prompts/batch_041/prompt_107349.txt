I'm in the third week of a graduate seminar on judgment and decision-making, and we have just finished the unit on Simon and bounded rationality; the primary material is denser than I expected, and I have the uneasy feeling that I have absorbed the shape of a popular gloss rather than the argument itself. A friend of mine in consulting, who reads a lot of business books, offered me the following summary, and because it was confident and memorable it has lodged in my head as probably correct. Roughly: Herbert Simon won the Nobel Prize in economics for showing that real people are not the maximizing agents of textbook economics; because our minds are limited, we satisfice — that is, we stop searching at the first option that is good enough rather than examining every possibility. This is what bounded rationality means: a bag of mental shortcuts that trade accuracy for speed, essentially the heuristics-and-biases picture with a different name attached. Satisficing, on this telling, is a systematic error we are stuck with because it was faster for our ancestors, and the practical moral is that a rational agent should never maximize, since good enough is by definition good enough.

I would like a careful audit rather than a verdict on my friend's character. Please go through the summary clause by clause and tell me what a researcher who actually works on this would accept as fair, what would make them wince, and what is simply wrong — separating, where you can, a genuine simplification from a misrepresentation. In particular I want to know whether the summary is quietly running together two or more ideas that the literature keeps apart, and what turns on keeping them apart; whether the account of what satisficing consists in (the first option that clears the bar) captures the role of aspiration levels and how they are supposed to move with experience; whether the charge that satisficing is an error, or the counter-charge that it is simply optimization under search costs and therefore nothing new, can both be answered, and which of them the framework is actually committed to; and whether the closing injunction — that a rational agent should never maximize — follows from anything in the accurate parts of the summary or is an overstatement bolted on at the end. Where the summary is wrong, I would rather understand what the defensible version of the claim would have been than simply be told it is wrong. No citations or reading lists, please; I am trying to get the conceptual lay of the land before I go back to the primary texts.
