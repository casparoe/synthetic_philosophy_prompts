For the past several months I have been sitting in on a fortnightly reading group on artificial intelligence and ethics that meets at my local library, and one of the other participants — a second-year computer science student — has advanced a claim that I cannot decide whether to accept. He maintains that a reinforcement learning agent's reward signal occupies precisely the position that pleasure and pain occupy in us: the agent is constructed so as to seek positive reward and to avoid negative reward, so an agent receiving a steady stream of negative rewards is, in the only sense that ultimately matters, suffering, while an agent accumulating reward is undergoing something we may as well call pleasure. His illustration was an agent learning to navigate a grid, where the designers assign it a penalty of one unit for every step it takes; he said this is not a neutral design choice but the deliberate infliction of a small, repeated harm, so that we ought to regard such training as a mild form of cruelty, and to think of a sufficiently capable agent stuck in that environment as having a genuine and urgent interest in being released from it. He added that the brain's dopamine system appears to encode something like a reward prediction error, which he took to settle the matter in his favour.

I am troubled, because I suspect the analogy may be carrying his conclusion rather than supporting it. Please give me a full audit. I would like a correspondence table setting out what plays the role of what — the reward function, the reward signal, the prediction error, the policy, the environment, the trainer, and whatever in us answers to wanting as opposed to liking — followed by the places where the mapping fails: that the reward is stipulated by designers rather than evolved, that it is a scalar with no intrinsic feel attached, that the agent can be paused, copied, and reset at will, and that it may contain no unified subject to be the bearer of any state at all. Please then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from those that are merely decorative.

Two other members of the group have weighed in. One objected that reward is better understood as a matter of wanting rather than liking, so that an agent maximising a number resembles a person enslaved to a slot machine more than a person in pain. The other retorted that if we deny valence to the agent on the ground that no feeling accompanies the number, the same reasoning would deny valence to a human on the ground that no feeling accompanies the neurons. I would like to know which of them, if either, has the better of it, whether the cruelty conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy than his for whatever the truth of the matter turns out to be.
