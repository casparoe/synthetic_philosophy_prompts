I'm a second-year philosophy student currently taking a module on consciousness and the metaphysics of mind, and we've spent the last fortnight on the case for treating 'qualia' — the supposed intrinsic, private, ineffable properties of experience — as an incoherent posit rather than a legitimate target of explanation. I'd like to try that position out against a case of my own, in conversation rather than in an essay.

Please play a committed abolitionist about qualia: someone who holds that talk about what an experience is like is not talk about an inner realm of properties at all, but a piece of folk theory we should replace rather than vindicate. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Constraints: (i) you may not concede that there is an intrinsic, non-relational property of experience over and above a creature's capacities to discriminate, react, remember and report; (ii) you may not simply declare my descriptions confused — you must offer a positive paraphrase of the reports in question and say what the person is actually reporting; (iii) you may concede that the reports are genuine data, and that there is a real phenomenon needing explanation; (iv) argue from the case in front of you rather than from the literature — please don't cite books or articles or tell me what other philosophers have said, and don't fall back on 'this is a well-known thought experiment'; (v) keep each reply to about three paragraphs of continuous prose, no bullet points, and write in British English. You may ask me at most one clarifying question, and only if you genuinely cannot proceed without it.

Here is my opening. A colleague of mine takes a drug for migraine prophylaxis. The first time she took it, the world went 'flat' — she said things looked like a photograph of a photograph. Her optometrist found nothing wrong with her eyes, but she did measurably worse on fine colour-matching and was slower on a same/different task, and the effect cleared as the drug washed out. That episode is easy for you: the flatness just is the impaired capacity. Some months later, on a second course, she reported exactly the same thing in exactly the same words and with the same insistence. This time, though, every test came back normal — colour matching, reaction times, memory for shades, wine-tasting, all indistinguishable from her baseline. She made no errors at all; she simply kept saying that everything looked drained. And it was not a tic. She predicted it would still look that way the following morning, and it did; she could tell me which objects would look worst, and she was right; and she was entirely unmoved when I showed her her own normal scores.

So, on your view, what is she reporting the second time? If you say the report is itself the whole of the difference, then she is describing her own tendency to use the word 'drained' while believing she is describing the sky — and I want to know why that redescription is better than taking her at her word. If you say she is reporting something, tell me what it is, in terms that don't quietly reintroduce the intrinsic property you have ruled out. And whichever horn you take, explain the asymmetry: why is she in a position to know this about herself in a way that none of her test results could confirm or refute?
