I am a novelist, and next month I am to give a two-hour craft seminar to MFA students on how to write what a character perceives. I intend to organise it around the philosopher's notion of cognitive penetration of perception — the claim that what a person believes, fears, or desires can alter not merely how she interprets what she sees but how it looks to her in the first place — and I want help planning the teaching rather than the content. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced for the idea to be intelligible at all, and say what depends on what. Nominate the single case that should carry the whole seminar: what it must exhibit and what it must be free of if the students are to grasp the difference between perception being genuinely shaped and perception being merely interpreted, and tell me whether the obvious literary example — a frightened character who sees menace in an ordinary street — can be made to do that work, or whether it will teach the opposite lesson. List the misconceptions my students will arrive with or acquire; I anticipate at least "it is just attention", "it means we see whatever we want", and "it licenses the view that each of us inhabits a different world", but I would rather hear yours. Specify where in the sequence each should be headed off and by what move. Close with one question I can put to the room at the end that would show whether the distinction has landed rather than merely been repeated, and tell me what I should explicitly refuse to settle at this level.
