I run a small municipal law clinic at the state university here, and I've somehow been roped into giving the after-dinner speech at our state associations centennial banquent next month. The room is going to be a problem. It's split clean down the middle: the big suburban towns want the association to endorse a plan for regional service districts (water, transit, emergency dispatch, the stuff where you genuinely do need scale), and the small rural towns hear "regional district" and hear "you're getting absorbed." Both sides have spent the whole year quoting subsidiarity at each other and meaning by it "the decision should be made by whoever I happen to be." I've got fifteen minutes, and I'd like to use them to hand both halves a version of the principle they can actually work with, without pretending it settles this particular fight for them.

The angle I want to take: tell me if I've got any of it wrong, but keep the correction to a couple of sentences, and then write the speech as if the corrected version is what I believe.

(1) Subsidiarity is two duties pulling opposite ways. A negative one: a larger body must not absorb what a smaller body can do for itself. And a positive one: the larger body owes the smaller one real help with the things the smaller one genuinely can't manage alone. American arguments about federalism are almost entirely about the negative duty; the positive duty is where the European, Catholic-social-teaching version of the idea lives, and it's why "just get the state out of it" sounds to some people in that hall like half a principle.

(2) Subsidiarity is not the same thing as decentralization. Decentralization is about where power sits; subsidiarity is about who should decide, and it isn't only territorial. It applies inside a level too: the county deciding something the town could have decided, the town deciding something a neighborhood association could have decided.

(3) The hard problem is who decides who decides. If the higher level is the one that gets to determine when the higher level may act, then the principle is advisory and nothing more. That's why the enforcement machinery matters more than the slogan: somebody has to be able to say no to the level that wants to act, and where you put that somebody decides most of the outcomes.

(4) There are two different triggers for when a higher body may step in, and people talk past each other because they're running different ones. The efficiency reading: the higher body may act when it can achieve the objective better, weighing scale and effects. That's roughly how the EU treaties are written, and it's worth noticing that the EU test explicitly asks whether the objective could have been achieved by the member states themselves, at the central or the regional or the local level, so even there it isn't just a states-versus-Brussels question. The dignity reading: the higher body may act only when the smaller one truly cannot act at all, because doing things through smaller bodies is a matter of respect for persons and not a matter of output. Those two triggers give different answers on nearly every real case, and I suspect my association has been having the European-versus-Catholic argument all year without knowing it.

(5) Fiscal equivalence: if the level that decides isn't the level that pays, then subsidiarity just becomes a way for somebody else to spend your money. The rural towns aren't wrong to ask who's on the hook for the regional district, and I don't want to give the speech without saying that part out loud.

So: fifteen minutes spoken, call it 2,000 words, prose only, no bullet points, something I can read off a page without stumbling. It has to give the pro-district people and the anti-district people each something real they could quote at a board meeting on Monday, and it can't sell either side out, so don't resolve the fight, and don't pretend the two duties in (1) never collide, because they do and everybody in that hall has felt it. No invented quotations from the association's founders or from anybody's grandmother, and no citations of any kind; I can't put footnotes in a banquet speech. American English, please.
