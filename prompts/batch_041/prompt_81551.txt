I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar on the safety and governance of advanced AI, with about sixteen participants, most of them philosophy students with a little technical background and none of them specialists in mechanism design or game theory. I want to open the unit on oversight with an activity rather than a lecture: I want them to feel, in their own decisions, both the pull of the proposal that a weak judge can supervise a much stronger system by watching two of its instances argue, and the specific place where that pull gives out. They should leave able to say what the proposal needs to be true of the judge, and able to name the failure mode they personally fell into.

The idea is that two systems are handed opposing sides of a question whose answer the judge cannot settle on its own; they argue; and the judge, who is weaker than they are and has only a limited ability to check what they say, decides which side is right. The hope is that a truthful arguer can expose a dishonest one, so the judge ends up tracking the truth without being able to verify it directly. I want the activity to put the participants inside that structure rather than describe it. Please design it around a dispute the judge cannot adjudicate from general knowledge, in which the two debaters have private access to the facts and the judge's only resource for checking them is a strictly limited budget — say a small number of tokens, each of which buys the judge one look at the source. Give me two rounds that differ in exactly one parameter, so that the first round comes out the way the proposal predicts and the second does not, and so that afterwards the participants can see the difference was a property of the mechanism rather than of who happened to be arguing. I would like the second round's failure to be traceable to something like the judge's sampling budget, the uncheckability of the decisive claim, or the asymmetry that leaves the honest side having to prove a negative; choose among these or invent a better one, but I want the mechanism, not the debaters' cleverness, to be what fails. The judge should be a participant, not me.

Do not let them hear the name of the proposal, or the vocabulary of scalable oversight, until the debrief. Give me the full runnable package: the roles (debaters, judge, and any observers or second judge), the materials I need to print, the rules, the payoff scheme (points or a prize will do; nothing should require real money), and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes. Then the debrief, which should map what actually happened onto the parts of the proposal — which features of the judge, the claim, and the arguer the outcome turned on — and should include the move of asking participants which side they would have bet on before the judge ruled. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, including the likelihood that participants treat it as an ordinary debate and reward rhetoric, that the judge spends more of the budget than allowed, and that the side defending the false claim simply fabricates rather than exploiting the loophole I built in. Finally, tell me what this activity cannot reproduce about the real proposal — I am thinking especially of the fact that the real scheme must make honesty the winning strategy rather than stipulating an honest debater, and of the gap between a human judge facing human arguers and the case that motivates the proposal — and what I will therefore have to supply in my own words at the end.
