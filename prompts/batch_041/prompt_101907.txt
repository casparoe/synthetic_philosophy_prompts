I keep a file of my own verdicts about tearing down old rules, customs, and institutions, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. My working maxim is roughly this: do not remove a fence until you can say why it was put there, and if the only reason you can find is a bad one, still do not remove it unless you are sure it is not quietly doing something else. I suspect that is two or three principles stapled together, and that my own cases pull them apart. My verdicts:

- A rule requiring a second vote on a constitutional amendment a year after the first: keep, though I cannot say what it accomplishes.
- A hospital's practice of a 24-hour on-call shift for new residents: abolish; it reads to me as hazing dressed up as training.
- A town's taboo against cutting the oldest tree in the square: keep, though no one remembers the reason for it.
- A firm's rule that only partners' names may appear on its letterhead: abolish; I can trace it to the founder's vanity.
- The parliamentary custom that the Speaker feigns reluctance when nominated: keep, though it looks like pure theatre.
- A rule added last year by a manager forbidding one employee from using the staff kitchen: abolish, though I have no idea what function it serves.

Two pairs bother me. The letterhead rule and the Speaker's reluctance look alike to me — both old, both ceremonial, both traceable to somebody's vanity — yet I keep one and abolish the other, and I would surrender the letterhead verdict before the Speaker one. And the constitutional second vote and the kitchen rule both have purposes I cannot state, yet age seems to decide them in opposite directions.

Give me the principle, or the small set of candidates, that best captures these verdicts; say which case motivates each clause; say what it implies about a fence that has not yet been built and about an old fence whose origin I can trace to something clearly bad; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon.
