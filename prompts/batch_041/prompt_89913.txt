I have been reading Franklin's account, in his Autobiography, of what he calls "the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection": thirteen virtues, each fixed by a short precept; a week of strict attention given to each virtue in turn while the others are left to their ordinary chance; a page ruled into seven columns, with a black spot entered for every fault found on the nightly examination; the whole course run four times a year. I am less interested in the list than in my own reactions to the project and to things like it, because those reactions do not fit together — and because I kept a version of such a chart myself for two years, which is probably why they bother me. I would like a principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures them. I would rather be told that one of my reactions is wrong than be handed an elaborate scheme that preserves all of them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Franklin's own project, as described. Admirable, and not merely quaint. But I suspect that what I admire is his willingness to report that he fell short, not the design.
2. A habit-tracking app that keeps a daily streak for honesty or kindness and resets the streak to zero on any lapse. Something is wrong here, and I cannot tell whether it is the tracking, the streak, or the reset.
3. A person who adopts a single absolute rule — never to say anything false — and keeps it even when a lie would spare someone considerable pain. Not admirable; I take the absoluteness to be the defect.
4. A monastic rule: written, shared, examined daily in common, with a superior who can impose a penance. Admirable, and without the misgiving I have about the app, even though the examination is just as systematic.
5. A person who quietly edits the list whenever he falls short, so that what he already does turns out to be what the list required. Worse than never having made a list.
6. A person who gives up the chart after some months but keeps most of the practices. Fine; perhaps better off than before.
7. A person who aims at a fixed threshold rather than at perfection — never cruel, always reliable — and holds to it. Fine, and it seems to me the same kind of thing as Franklin's project with the ambition filed off.
8. A person who succeeds completely: at the end he is exactly what the list demanded, with no lapse on record. Unease. I do not think I would want to know him.
9. A person who adopts the regimen for productivity, reputation, or discipline as such, with no interest in the virtues named. The conduct may be identical to Franklin's, but the project is not a moral one at all.

My tentative principle is this: such a project is admirable just in case a lapse is permitted to count as information about the person rather than as an injury to be managed. Case 5 fails that test outright. Case 2 fails it, I think, because the streak converts a lapse into a loss to be recovered. Case 4 passes because the penance comes from someone else. Case 9 fails because nothing about the person is at issue. But case 8 becomes a problem: a man with no lapse on record has nothing to learn from, and my unease at him suggests I am valuing something other than the learning. Cases 3 and 7 are a second problem, since both are fixed standards that do not aim at perfection, and I admire one and not the other; if the difference is not the absoluteness, I do not know what it is.

Please tell me which principle you would actually defend, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to cases I have not listed, and say what it implies for two or three further cases of your own construction. Then tell me which of my verdicts you would overturn. Give me your position rather than a menu of positions: if the best principle costs me case 4 or case 8, say so, and say why I should give it up.
