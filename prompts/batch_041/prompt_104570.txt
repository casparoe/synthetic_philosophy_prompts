I'm in a graduate seminar on vagueness, and the final paper has to argue for a definite position rather than survey the terrain. I want to defend epistemicism — the view that predicates like "tall" have fully determinate extensions that we simply cannot know — and to argue that supervaluationism is the weaker theory rather than the more humane one. Please construct that argument for me.

My main line is a dilemma about admissible precisifications. Supervaluationism says a sentence is super-true when it comes out true on every admissible precisification of the language; but then it owes an account of which precisifications are admissible. If that set is drawn sharply, the theory has sharp boundaries after all, just one level up from the ones it promised to avoid; if the set is left vague, then super-truth is itself vague and the theory cannot deliver the verdicts it was introduced to deliver. Epistemicism can answer the analogous question without embarrassment, because it never claimed there were no sharp boundaries — only that we cannot locate them.

Develop this, and make it concrete with a constructed predicate rather than the stock "heap" and "bald": take something like "roomy enough to be a comfortable study," where floor area, ceiling height, and daylight trade off against one another, and show how the dilemma recurs for its precisifications. Then take up the strongest supervaluationist reply — that admissible precisifications are already constrained by meaning, so no further boundary has to be drawn — and explain why I think that reply either relocates the boundary or begs the question. Around 900 words, thesis-first, no hedging.
