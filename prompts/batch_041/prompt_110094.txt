I'm a journalist who covers philosphy and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about lying — specifically about how strange Augustine's position starts to look once you take it seriously instead of treating it as a bit of historical rigorism. I need you to build me a thought experiment, and I want it built to spec rather than just a nice illustration.

Here's the target. Augustine thinks the thing that makes a lie a lie is the will to deceive rather than the falsity of the words — so on his view a person can say nothing but true things and still be lying, if what they want is for you to walk away believing something false. He also thinks no lie is ever permitted, not even to save a life or head off something terrible, though concealing the truth or keeping silent is a different matter. What I want is one realistic scene, set somewhere ordinary — a hospital corridor, a family kitchen, a small office, wherever — in which two things happen side by side and they're mirror images of each other:

1. Someone who is trying to deceive another person pulls it off while uttering only true statements. The true statements have to actually do the deceiving — not because the listener is a fool, and not because the speaker is a lawyer exploiting a technicality no normal person would catch. And the deception should be aimed at preventing something seriously bad, so the helpful-lie intuition is pulling hard.

2. In the same scene, someone else states something false, knowing it's false, but isn't trying to get anyone to believe it — and nobody does.

Then walk me through the verdicts. What does Augustine say about each of those two, and why, given that he's working from intent instead of falsity? What does a plain falsity-based definition say about each? Where exactly do the two come apart, and which single feature of my case is doing that work — if I deleted it, would the two views snap back into agreement? I'd also like to know what happens if I dial the stakes down: does the Augustinian verdict on the first person change if nothing much hangs on it? And if you can, tell me whether any defensible position would rank the two speakers the other way round, with the false-but-harmless statement coming out worse than the true-but-deceiving one.

Last thing, and be honest about it: how would a serious Augustinian try to wriggle out of the case? Not just that I've rigged it — I want the actual move he'd make and whether it costs him anything he cares about.

Keep the scene itself to a paragraph or two, and make both people the kind of people you'd actually meet. No brain scanners, no perfect predictors, nothing that only works because a philosopher stipulated it.
