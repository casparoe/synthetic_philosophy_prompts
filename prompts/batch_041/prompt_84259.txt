Six months ago, at forty-three, I was given a diagnosis of ADHD, and I have spent much of the time since reading advocacy material, sitting in support groups, and trying to work out what I have been told about myself. The refrain that recurs everywhere is that this is a medical condition like any other: no more a matter of willpower or character than diabetes is, to be medicated without shame and accommodated without argument, and that anyone who resists this framing is trading in stigma. I found the conclusion congenial, and I have repeated the diabetes comparison myself, in that spirit, more than once.

What troubles me is not the conclusion but the load the comparison is being made to bear, because the thing it is being used to describe does not sit still the way a pancreas does. Three things have happened to me since the diagnosis. First, my past has reorganised itself: decades of small failures I had filed under laziness or weak character now present themselves to me as symptoms, and I report them to my clinician in the vocabulary the clinic supplied, finding that I can answer every question on the intake form with an example. Second, the people around me have adjusted — my partner now expects me to lose things — and I have caught myself in recent months doing things I did not do a year ago and describing them in the terms the community uses. I genuinely cannot tell whether I have changed or only my description has. Third, the category itself is a moving target: my clinician tells me that by the standards in force when I was a child I would probably not have qualified, and my mother's generation had no such diagnosis to receive. A friend who reads philosophy says this is the sort of thing Ian Hacking wrote about, the mutual chasing of a classification and the people classified, and that it is why people argue over whether such categories carve nature at its joints. I do not want to relitigate that argument in the abstract. I want to know what it does to the analogy I keep repeating.

So please audit the comparison. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what in the diabetes case plays the role of the organ, the blood test, the symptoms, the patient's own report, the clinician's authority, the course of the condition across a life, and the community of the diagnosed — and mark where the mapping is exact, where it is loose but serviceable, and where there is simply nothing on the other side. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use I am making of the comparison, namely that the condition is real, unchosen, and entitled to accommodation, from those that do not. Say what the analogy quietly imports that the psychiatric case lacks, and what it quietly exports. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives: can I go on saying that it is just like diabetes, or does the comparison carry that conclusion only so long as I ignore the feedback loop? If it does not survive, what would be a better comparison — one that preserves the reality and the unchosenness while acknowledging that the classification acts on the people it classifies?

I am not looking for citations or a reading list. I want the argument, and I would rather have it plainly.
