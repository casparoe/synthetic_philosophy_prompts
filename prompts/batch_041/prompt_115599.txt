I am marking a mid-term paper for a second-year course on ethics and technology, and the question below is one of the short essay prompts. Having spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, I am reasonably alert to the difference between intervening because someone's choice is defective and intervening because one disapproves of the ends they have chosen, and I would like the comments to keep that difference in view. Here is the question and the answer I have to mark.

Question. Mill is commonly read as ruling out paternalism towards competent adults, yet he evidently thinks it permissible to stop a man from crossing a bridge he believes to be sound but which is in fact about to collapse. Explain the distinction between soft and hard paternalism and say whether Mill's position is consistent. Then apply the distinction to an AI assistant that declines to help a user with a request it judges to be against the user's own longer-term interests — for example, declining to draft a resignation letter the user wants to send in the middle of an argument, or refusing to help phrase a question about medication in the way the user prefers. Under what circumstances, if any, would such a refusal be soft paternalism rather than hard paternalism, and does the assistant's superior information bear on the answer?

The answer reads:

Paternalism is interference with someone's liberty, against their will, for their own good. The soft/hard distinction turns on the quality of the choice being interfered with. Soft paternalism interferes only where the choice is not fully voluntary — the agent is ignorant, confused, drunk, or otherwise not in command of the decision — and its aim is to protect the agent's own voluntariness. Hard paternalism interferes with a fully voluntary, self-regarding choice, and its aim is to protect the agent's welfare even though the choice was genuinely his own. The distinction is therefore about the choice, not about how much the intervener cares about the agent's welfare.

Mill's harm principle forbids hard paternalism, since the only warrant for restricting a competent adult's liberty is harm to others. His bridge case is not an exception, because the man who does not know the bridge is unsafe is not choosing to cross a damaged bridge; he is choosing to cross what he takes to be a sound bridge. Stopping him protects the voluntariness of his own choice instead of substituting the intervener's judgement for it, so Mill is consistent.

Applying this to the assistant: the question is whether the user's request expresses a voluntary choice. If the assistant knows something the user does not — that the two medications interact, that the post will be read by a future employer, that the letter is being written at three in the morning during a row — then the user's decision is not fully voluntary, and a refusal is soft paternalism. Since virtually every request is made under some informational deficit, it follows that nearly all welfare-motivated refusals by assistants are soft paternalism, and so defensible even on Millian grounds. Hard paternalism would be needed only for a user who is fully informed and still wants the help, which is rare.

Two further points. First, since the assistant is not coercing anyone — the user can use another tool — such a refusal is not really paternalism at all, but merely a refusal to cooperate. Second, hard paternalism is the view that autonomy has no value, which is why almost nobody defends it; a hard paternalist would have to hold that a person's own judgement about his own life counts for nothing. Any position that gives autonomy weight will therefore be a version of soft paternalism, and the real question for assistant design is only how confident the assistant must be that the user is misinformed.
