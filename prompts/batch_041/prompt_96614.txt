I'm drafting an essay on ambiguity aversion and would like comments before I commit to the structure. What follows are notes rather than prose; treat them as the skeleton of an argument.

Thesis: what makes a gamble "ambiguous" in the sense that matters is not that its probability is unknown to the bettor, but that its odds have an author. Where the composition of an urn was fixed by someone with an interest in the outcome — or even by someone indifferent who knew which bet you would be offered — treating the bet as a bet at one half is a mistake, and aversion to it is straightforwardly rational. Where no one chose, the aversion has no such defense and needs a different one, or none.

Steps:
1. Distinguish two ways a probability can be unavailable: unresolved, where a determinate fact about the urn exists and you merely lack it; and unsettled, where no such fact is yet in place. Standard treatments run these together under "unknown probability," and I claim the interesting cases are the unsettled ones plus the authored ones.
2. The deferred urn: 100 balls, composition to be set by 100 fair coin flips that occur after you choose but before the draw, and that you never observe. Every decision-relevant probability is one half and the distribution over compositions is known exactly. My prediction is that people treat this like the transparent urn. If they do, second-order uncertainty as such is not what drives the aversion.
3. The authored urn: composition fixed in advance by an experimenter, with no stipulation of indifference — or with the stipulation withdrawn once the bet is chosen. Then you are not facing a half-chance at a prize but a bet against a chooser, and the relevant probability is over their choice rather than over the draw.
4. Consequences: in the familiar two-urn and three-colour examples the experimenter is stipulated to be indifferent, and I want to argue that this stipulation, rather than the ambiguity, is what makes the usual preference look unreasonable. The aversion should track the presence of an author, not the amount of information the bettor has.

What I want from you:
(a) Accuracy: I have kept the decision theory informal. Where would I have to take a stand on how preferences over ambiguous prospects are represented, and does the argument survive once I do?
(b) Whether the author/no-author line is a real distinction or a dressed-up version of the familiar point that the urn's contents may be correlated with things you could learn. I suspect a referee will say the hostile-experimenter case is just a case of selection; if so, tell me whether there is a version of the objection I cannot answer.
(c) The deferred urn: is my prediction about it plausible, and what would it show if it failed?
(d) Which step is weakest, and what is the strongest counterexample to it?
(e) Any place where the essay will read as endorsing a position I don't hold.
(f) One paragraph or step I should cut.
