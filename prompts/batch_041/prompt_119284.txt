I sit in on a weekly reading group that started out on political theory and has drifted, over the past two months, into early modern philosophy; last week two of the regulars spent most of the session arguing about the cogito while the rest of us worked through the cookies. I would like you to referee the exchange for me, point by point, because I genuinely could not tell who was winning, and because I suspect part of the difficulty is that they were answering different questions. Neither of them is a fool: Mira has read a great deal of Hume and treats the whole argument as a grammatical illusion dressed up as a discovery, while Theo came in prepared to defend it as the one thing that survives the most radical doubt. Here, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it, is how it went. (1) Mira opened by saying the cogito is oversold: all it establishes is that the sentence asserting her own existence is true whenever she entertains it, which is a fact about the first-person pronoun rather than a finding about the world, since anyone who says "I" has already presupposed a referent for it; so the argument assumes what it sets out to prove, and it tells us nothing about a soul, a self, or even a subject that lasts longer than a moment. (2) Theo replied that she is reading it as a proof from premises when it is not one: the cogito is what remains after everything dubitable has been stripped away, and what remains is not a proposition one infers but an act one performs, since one cannot doubt that one is doubting; the charge of circularity therefore misses, because there is no inference in the offing to be circular. (3) Mira said that on that reading he has traded a proof for a performance, and that a performance establishes only that an act is occurring, not that an agent is performing it; she offered Lichtenberg's suggestion that we ought to say that it thinks the way we say that it lightnings, which she claimed is warranted by exactly as much as the first-person version is, and said he was reading a subject off the grammar of a verb. (4) Theo answered that doubting is something someone does, that an act requires a performer, and that the "I" the argument delivers is not a name, a body, or a biography but simply the thinker of these thoughts — still a real result, since it survives the imagined annihilation of the entire material world. (5) Mira said that the inference from act to agent is precisely the one the bundle theory denies, and that even if she grants him a thinker she has granted him one only for as long as the thought lasts: nothing in the argument rules out the self being a series of momentary flashes, and without persistence he cannot get anything resembling a soul. (6) Theo said she had conceded more than she realized, since a thinker grasped as thinking while the existence of every physical thing remains in doubt is already being grasped as something other than a body, and if the argument delivers the essence of the thing as thought rather than extension, the rest can be argued for on other grounds. (7) Mira said the essence claim is exactly where the trick happens: from the fact that she can doubt her body while she cannot doubt her thinking, it follows only that thinking is the part she cannot doubt, which is a fact about her epistemic situation rather than about what she is made of.

What I want is not a survey of what commentators have said about the cogito, and I do not need sources or references, but an adjudication of this exchange as it actually ran. Go move by move through the seven moves, rating each one on a scale from clearly mistaken through defensible-but-oversold to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Then tell me what each of them is missing that the other is not, whether any of the moves are in fact addressed to different questions — proof versus performance, epistemic certainty versus metaphysical essence, momentary subject versus persisting person — in such a way that both parties could be correct about their own question while talking past each other, and, where a move only lands against a weak version of the opposing position, say so explicitly. Finish with an overall verdict on the question I actually care about, namely what the cogito at its strongest does and does not prove. Please write in American English, and keep the verdicts concrete rather than hedging every claim into a draw.
