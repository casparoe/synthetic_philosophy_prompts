I am a probabilist by training — a statistician who works with measure-theoretic conditioning every day — and I have essentially no philosophical education. I keep bumping into a philosophical debate that I can only half-follow, and I would like you to carry it across into the vocabulary I actually use.

The debate concerns indicative conditionals and probability. There is an old and appealing idea — sometimes called Adams's thesis, or simply the equation — that the probability of a conditional of the form "if A then C" is just the conditional probability of C given A. It is motivated by the thought that we assess such conditionals by asking how likely the consequent is on the supposition of the antecedent, and it survives a great deal of everyday usage. Then there is a family of results, associated with David Lewis, showing that no probability function can satisfy that equation across the board: if the probability of the conditional always equals the conditional probability, then the conditional probability collapses into the unconditional one. So the equation cannot be literally true, and philosophers have argued for decades over what to give up.

Restate this debate for me in my own vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. By my vocabulary I mean: conditioning on events versus conditioning on sigma-algebras; regular conditional distributions and the fact that they are defined only up to null sets; disintegration; conditional expectation as a random variable rather than a number; the point that "the conditional probability given A" is a value of such a random variable, fixed by a choice of version, and that different versions need not cohere with one another; and the Borel–Kolmogorov phenomenon, in which conditioning on two descriptions of the same event gives different answers. I want the translation done honestly — do not quietly assume my framework's conventions and call the result a translation of theirs.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' problem map cleanly onto my machinery? Which parts do I have to distort to make them fit? And which parts have no counterpart at all in how I think about conditioning — for instance, if there is something about truth conditions, about assertion and assertability, about the difference between indicative and subjunctive conditionals, or about revising a belief state by adding a sentence to it, that my probability spaces simply cannot express. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it marks a genuine gap in my framework — something a better statistical treatment of conditionals would have to add — or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it sounded and dissolves once it is stated carefully. I am especially interested in the case where the philosophers seem to treat "the probability of the conditional" and "the conditional probability" as two well-defined objects that might or might not be equal, since I am not sure the second one is a well-defined object in my sense at all. I want to know whether that suspicion is the whole story, or whether a real problem is left over after my framework has had its say.
