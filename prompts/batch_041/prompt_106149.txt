I am a Year 12 student putting together a piece for my school's philosophy and poetry evening, and I would be grateful if you would write the poem for me. The subject I have been given is the old puzzle about a clay figure and the lump of clay it is made of: a potter shapes a lump into a figure; the figure and the lump occupy the same place and are made of all the same clay; a careless hand can squash the figure back into a ball, after which we say the clay is still there but the figure is not; and the clay was there before the figure was. I want a poem in which the form does this work rather than the vocabulary, so please write a pantoum.

I would like the pantoum to run to five quatrains, twenty lines, with the second and fourth lines of each quatrain returning as the first and third lines of the next, and the final quatrain closing the loop by taking up the third and first lines of the opening quatrain as its second and fourth lines. The returning lines should be the clay of the poem — the material that persists — and each time a line comes back it should mean something a little different because the quatrain around it has changed. Please make sure of the following:

- At least one returning line should be true of the lump when we first hear it and no longer true of the figure by the time it comes back, so that the words survive while the thing they described does not.
- At least one returning line should shift the other way, spoken first of the shapeless clay and afterwards of the figure, so that a reader can hear the same material taking on a form.
- The poem must never put the figure and the lump in two different places or treat them as two objects standing side by side; whatever it says about them, they must remain in the same place, made of the same stuff, at the same time. Nor should it simply declare them to be one and the same thing with nothing left over.
- The fact that the clay is older than the figure should be audible somewhere, and so should the difference in what each of the two can survive.

Please keep the poem free of philosophical jargon — no "constitution", "sortal", "coincidence" or "supervenience" — and let a reader who knows the puzzle recognise it from the shape of the poem rather than from any word in it. Keep the setting ordinary: a figure on a shelf in an art room, a hand, a bench, a kiln, rather than a gallery marble. Rhyme is not compulsory, but if you do rhyme, please keep to rhymes that hold when the poem is read aloud in a British accent. Someone who knows the puzzle should be able to point to the line that does the metaphysical work, so please add two or three plain sentences after the poem identifying those lines and what they are doing. Please write everything in British English throughout — spellings such as "colour", "grey" and "realise" — in the note as well as in the poem.
