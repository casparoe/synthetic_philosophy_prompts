I rewatched Inception last week and it sent me down a rabbit hole on the philosophy of dreams, and I found this old fight between Norman Malcolm (his book Dreaming, 1959) and Daniel Dennett (his cassette theory of dreams) that I can't stop thinking about. As I understand it, Malcolm basically says it's nonsense to talk about having experiences while you're asleep -- there's no "I am asleep" said by a sleeper, and all we really have are the reports people give when they wake up, so the dream isn't an experience that happened during the night, it's something more like the waking telling. Dennett then comes along and says fine, maybe dreams aren't lived through in real time at all; on his cassette theory the dream is more like a pre-recorded thing that gets loaded in and played at the moment of waking, or even just a confabulated memory, so there's no inner movie running in the sleeper's head for two hours.

The thing is, Inception seems to be built entirely on the opposite assumption. Dreams there are experiences in the fullest sense -- the team lives through them, hours pass inside a dream while minutes pass outside, the layers are synchronized so the kicks line up, people feel pain, get shot, age decades in limbo, and a whole emotional life happens down there. So I want a reading of the film through this debate. Does Nolan just take the naive view for granted and use it as a plot device, or does the movie actually undercut itself in ways that help Malcolm and Dennett -- like Cobb never being able to prove he's awake, Mal's certainty that her world was a dream driving her to jump, the whole story being told to us by someone whose only evidence is his own waking testimony? And what about the time stuff: the film needs dream time to be real duration that runs in parallel across layers, which looks like exactly what Dennett denies. Does the top at the end count as the film admitting Malcolm was right that you can't settle this from the inside?

I'm a backend dev, not a philosopher, so keep the jargon explainable, and don't just tell me the film "raises questions about reality" -- I want the actual points where it commits to a side and where it accidentally argues for the other one, scene by scene where you can. Also say which side, if either, the movie ends up dramatizing better than the arguments do.
