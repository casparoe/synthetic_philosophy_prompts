I retired from geology about four years ago — thirty-odd years of reading the rock record, which as I've realized lately is basically one long exercise in inferring a past nobody watched — and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy by hanging around a continuing-ed reading group at the local university. About a dozen of us: two grad students, a retired archivist, a couple of people who just like to argue. We get 75 minutes every other week, and next month I'm on the hook to lead. The topic I've claimed is how anyone knows anything about the distant past — the whole arc from Herodotus grumbling about which sources to trust and Thucydides insisting he checked things himself, down to the modern historian's craft. I don't want to stand up and lecture, though; I've sat through enough of those. What I want is a runnable activity, something we actually do for maybe 60 minutes with 15 for debrief, where the group has to *do* source criticism on a past none of us can go and check, and feels the particular vertigo of realizing there is no ledge outside the sources from which to check them against reality. That's the thing I keep circling in philosophy and can't quite get my head around, so I'd rather make everybody else feel it too.

Here's my rough sketch, and I'd like you to build it into something that actually runs — roles or rules, a timeline, materials, and a debrief. I want to hand them a dossier about an invented event in the past, so nobody can smuggle in what "really happened": I'm thinking a small colonial trading post or border town, something contested about two hundred years back — a massacre, a broken treaty, a disappearance, whichever gives the most room. The dossier should deliberately mix what I think it was Bloch, in a little book on the historian's craft, who called intentional evidence (things written to persuade you — a diary, a petition, an official report, a hostile letter, a sermon) with unintentional traces (a shipping ledger, a burial register, a map with marginal scrawls, a midden full of rubbish, a parish tax roll), so the group discovers for itself that the material nobody meant as testimony is often the most reliable for the narrow questions it can answer and completely useless for the ones they care about most. Include at least one source whose bias is strong and predictable, so they can learn to use a hostile witness as evidence rather than discard him; at least two sources that agree because one quietly copied the other, so they can trip over the difference between corroboration and non-independence; and one document that says nothing at all about the central question but is decisive about something adjacent, to make the point that evidence is relative to the question you're asking — I think that one's Collingwood, but don't lean on the name. Have them work in small groups toward three lists (established, probable, merely reported) plus a one-paragraph consensus account, and then at around the 40-minute mark have me hand in one more document that undercuts whatever they've settled on, so they watch the consensus come apart.

In the debrief I want you to map each beat of the activity onto the underlying issue — proximity versus bias, the argument from silence, the regress that every source is itself just another claim about the past — and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I already suspect people will over-trust the vivid first-person diary, treat the official report as neutral, and declare a claim settled because two sources agree when really there's only one source wearing two hats. Be honest too about what this activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking: I don't think a two-hour game can really make you feel the *scale* of the evidentiary gap over two centuries, or the way real historians work for years inside one archive until the sources start answering questions they weren't asked. Anyways, the group is sharp but they're not specialists, so keep the invented material self-contained enough that nobody needs to look anything up.
