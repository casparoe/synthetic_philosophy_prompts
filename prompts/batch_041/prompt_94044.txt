I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and I've internalized the standard picture well enough to be suspicious of it: that rationality is coherence, that coherence means having a complete and transitive preference ordering which some utility function can represent, that the proof is the money pump — give an agent with a preference cycle, or merely a gap in its ranking, enough patience and it will trade itself into a position it strictly prefers less — and that therefore a rational agent maximizes expected utility. What I want is a piece of sustained satire that takes this picture more seriously than its defenders usually do.

Write the employee onboarding handbook for Compleat, a fictional life-design and consulting firm whose entire product rests on the coherence argument. The genre is the corporate handbook: a welcome letter from the founder; the firm's single stated value; a section explaining the method; an FAQ in which client objections are answered; a short appendix of graduation criteria; and a closing note from the legal department. Roughly 800 words, deadpan and earnest throughout — no winking, no editorial asides, nothing that breaks the voice. Number the sections.

The comedy has to come from the content, not the format. Every joke should be one a decision theorist or a philosopher of practical reason would recognize as getting the argument right — the kind of reader who has actually thought about money pumps, the von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage frameworks, and the difference between a consistency constraint on a set of preferences and a practical requirement on an agent. In particular, let the handbook's own earnestness expose the soft places: that the money pump presupposes what the chooser will do at each step and so can be refused by someone who sees the sequence coming; that completeness is the axiom everyone quietly argues about; that a ranking over the small worlds an agent can actually describe is not obviously a ranking over the grand world it lives in; that escaping a sure loss is a weaker demand than maximizing expected utility; and that some goods may be genuinely incommensurable, so that leaving a gap in one's preferences might be the honest response rather than a defect to be repaired. Do not announce any of these as objections — the handbook should keep recommending its program in perfect good faith, and a careful reader should feel each crack open on its own. A client testimonial and the legal note are your best chances to let the seam show without ever naming it.

Keep it legible to someone who has never read a word of decision theory, and let the fictional founder carry the firm's voice. Invent the founder and any clients; do not quote or name real living people, and do not include citations or a reading list.
