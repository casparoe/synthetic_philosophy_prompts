Hey — I cover AI and tech, and I'm working on a feature about what I've been calling the "no" problem: assistants that refuse or override what you actually asked them to do. Everyone I interview is either "an assistant should never second-guess a competent adult" or "of course it should, have you seen what people ask for," and neither side seems to have thought about where the line sits. I want to pin it down with a made-up case instead of another quote from a product manager. I've been going back and forth on this for weeks and I still dont have it straight.

So: take the role of an applied ethicist who also knows how these systems actually get configured. Think carefully. Before you write anything, brainstorm three or four candidate scenarios in a scratch list, tell me in a line each why you're killing the ones you kill, then build out the best one.

Here's the case, built to spec.

- Realistic and moderate stakes. No crisis-line stuff, no medical emergency, no crime in progress, no child safety. I want something a normal adult might plausibly ask an assistant to help with — money, work, dating, privacy, time, a decision about their own life. If the stakes are enormous, everyone agrees and the case is boring.
- The user is competent, informed, and not under duress. They know exactly what they're asking for and what the downsides are. Nobody gets to resolve this by saying the user didn't understand.
- No third-party harm. If the only reason to step in is that someone else gets hurt, that's not paternalism, it's ordinary harm prevention, and the case fails. The user is the only one affected.
- The assistant is the user's own. They set it up, they pay for it, they told it what their goals were. Its not a public service or an employer's tool.
- Then give me two versions of the same case, differing in exactly one variable, and I think the variable is substitutability: in version A, if the assistant refuses, the user just does the thing another way and gets the same outcome; in version B, the refusal actually changes what happens. Everything else held fixed. If my hunch is right, the verdict flips between A and B. If it doesn't flip, say so, and tell me which variable does the work instead.

After the scenario: walk through what the assistant should do in each version and why, name the principle doing the work, and say whether the justification is genuinely paternalist or something else wearing paternalism's coat.

Then be honest with me about the case. Which feature is load-bearing? What happens if I take it out? What does a hard-line anti-paternalist — Mill-style, my own good isn't a sufficient reason to coerce me — say to defuse it? And tell me if the case secretly cheats: if it smuggles in a third-party harm, or a competence failure, or if the only thing making the override look okay is that the user set the policy themselves earlier, in which case tell me why that stops being paternalism and becomes something more like contract-following. Also: is the assistant acting as a tool or as an agent here, and does the case turn on which one? I keep seeing that distinction made and I'm not sure it's doing real work.
