My friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about whether the American or the European attitude toward economic inequality is the more defensible one. Her case for the American attitude was cleaner than I had heard it put: the American question, she said, is whether you can rise; the European question is whether you can fall; no society can secure both, and the attitude that keeps the ladder open has chosen the thing worth choosing. I argued for something closer to the European view, while conceding that it is a family of views rather than a single one. What ended the argument was a sentence she offered as though it needed no defense at all: "equality of opportunity, not equality of outcome." I found that I could not say what precisely it was claiming, and I suspect that neither could she.

I would like the sentence taken apart rather than explained. Set out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, as a menu, running from readings so thin that no one would deny them to readings substantial enough to be worth fighting over, including any on which the sentence is simply false. For each reading, say what it commits its holder to, whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and whether it actually supports her conclusion — that the American attitude is the more defensible one — or is neutral between the two attitudes, or tells against her. Two things I especially want from the list. First, whether the contrast between opportunity and outcome survives inspection: if opportunity is itself something a society distributes, and if any claim about how much opportunity a society affords has to be cashed out in terms of what its members end up with, then the sentence may be contrasting a thing with one of its own measurements. Second, which reading the phrase carries when it is used as she used it, to end a conversation rather than to open one.

I want to bring this back to her as a list she can recognize herself in, so keep the readings distinct and say what each one costs.
