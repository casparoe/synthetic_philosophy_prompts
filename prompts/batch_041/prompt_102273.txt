Here's the question (worth 20 points):

"Treat death here as defined by the Uniform Determination of Death Act framework, under which, roughly, a person is dead if there has been either irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain including the brainstem. (a) What distinguishes the neurological criterion from the circulatory criterion, and how are the two related? (b) Is a patient in a persistent vegetative state dead under the neurological criterion? Explain why or why not. (c) What does the dead donor rule require, and are the two criteria interchangeable when we apply that rule to organ donation? Justify your answer."

And here's the student's answer:

"(a) Both criteria are trying to capture the same underlying idea, which is that death is the irreversible loss of the organism's ability to function as an integrated whole. The circulatory criterion is the older cardiopulmonary standard: death is when the heart and lungs stop and can't be restarted. The neurological criterion says death is when the brain, including the brainstem, has irreversibly stopped functioning. In a typical brain-death case the patient is on a ventilator, so the heart is still beating and oxygen is still reaching the tissues, but the brain can no longer integrate anything, so the organism as a whole is gone. In a way the neurological criterion is just the circulatory criterion seen from the other end: the brain dies because it stops getting blood, so the two are describing the same event, just reversing the order of explanation.

(b) A persistent vegetative state patient has lost the function of the cerebral cortex, which is where consciousness and the person live, while the brainstem keeps them breathing and cycling through sleep and wakefulness. Since the neurological criterion is really about the person and not about mere biological housekeeping, and the person is gone in PVS, I'd say such a patient is dead under the neurological criterion, even though families and the law usually go on treating them as a living patient out of sentiment. The Harvard report was written with exactly these cases in mind.

(c) The dead donor rule says you can only take vital organs from someone who is already dead, so the donor has to satisfy one of the two criteria first. That's why donation after circulatory death makes you wait a few minutes after the heart stops before proceeding: you have to be sure the cessation is irreversible, since a heart that restarts was never really stopped. As for whether the criteria are interchangeable, I think they are. Whether you use the brain or the circulation, you're picking out the same event in the patient, and that's why the same people qualify under either one. The difference between them is really procedural — the neurological criterion lets you stop the ventilator right away, while the circulatory criterion makes you wait around."
