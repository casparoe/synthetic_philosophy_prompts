I teach philosophy at the community college here, and I grew up in this town: my father spent thirty-one years on the floor at the mill. In June the local asked me to give the after-dinner speech at its centennial dinner, and I have been putting it off because I cannot find a register that does not insult somebody at the tables. The facts are these. The mill closed in 2019. The site is now a data centre. The operator has hired about sixty of our members, most of them former machine operators, to do data annotation: they sit in what used to be the toolroom and grade pairs of model answers, thousands a day, and those grades are the training signal for the systems the company sells. The programme is called Skills Transfer. There is a plaque in the lobby listing the annotators as knowledge contributors. The branch leadership negotiated the arrangement and is proud of it; a sizeable minority holds that the union sold the members' judgement for wages and a plaque; both will be in the hall, along with families, two councillors, and the site manager, who is at my table. The men who worked with my father will be there, and they have not come for a lecture.

The spine I want is Marx on machinery, in my own words, and I would like the speech to be built around a sentence from the Grundrisse that I keep returning to — that the specific mode of working "appears directly as becoming transferred from the worker to capital in the form of the machine, and his own labour capacity devalued thereby" — but without the speech itself containing any quotation from Marx. I am not going to read Marx aloud at a union dinner, and I would not want to attribute a phrase I cannot check. Three of his claims should do the structural work: that when a tool becomes a machine the skill is not destroyed but moves out of the worker into the apparatus, where it belongs to someone else and the worker is left tending it; that under capital machinery does not shorten the working day but lengthens and intensifies it, since the machine exists to make labour cheaper rather than lighter; and that once production rests on stored-up social knowledge — what he calls the general intellect — labour time stops being a sensible measure of wealth, which is at once capital's crisis and everyone else's opening.

These claims should constrain what the speech can and cannot say. It cannot console the room with the thought that their skills live on in the system, because that transfer is precisely the thing being described. It cannot console them with the thought that there will always be a human in the loop, minding the machine, because Marx's own picture of the worker who supervises the process instead of performing it is a picture of demotion, and this room will hear it as one. Nor can it fall back on retraining as the answer, since the retraining here consists of surrendering, one judgement at a time, the tacit knowledge that made these people good at their old jobs. I need something that survives all three prohibitions and still gives the room a reason to be in the hall together.

Write the speech, about eight minutes long. It should not take sides in the branch's quarrel over the deal, and should not pretend the quarrel is not happening. It should be warm to the families and the old members without being nostalgic, and it should not flatter the company. Begin with the speech itself: no preamble, no framing note, and nothing after it.
