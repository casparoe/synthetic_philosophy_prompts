I am a high school student serving on the organizing committee of my school's philosophy society, and I have been asked to conduct the on-stage question-and-answer session with our visiting speaker next month. Our guest is a visiting ethicist who has spent the term defending a distinctive position about war, and I want my questions to engage the position itself rather than invite her to restate it.

As she has explained it to our committee, her view has four parts. First, soldiers who fight for an unjust cause act impermissibly when they kill, so the moral equality of combatants is false at the level of permission. Second, those same soldiers are not blameworthy, so the equality survives at the level of culpability. Third, the reason they are not blameworthy is not the familiar one that they could not have known their cause was unjust; she rejects that excuse as both empirically shaky and philosophically beside the point. Her reason is instead that responsibility for a war belongs to the collective and its political leadership, whose instruments the soldiers are, so that the wrongness of what they do is real but the guilt for it is not theirs. Fourth, she holds that a state's authority to conscript, order, and deploy its soldiers is independent of the justice of its cause, so an unjust state may permissibly require its soldiers to do what those soldiers are not themselves permitted to do.

Please draft eight to ten questions for the session, ordered so that the pressure builds rather than scatters. I need at least one question on whether combatants who act impermissibly but blamelessly are still liable to be killed in self-defence, and what her answer implies about the just side's killings; at least one on whether the collective-responsibility move does not simply transfer to the just side's soldiers and dissolve the asymmetry she wants to keep; at least one on how a state can have authority to command acts that are impermissible for the person commanded; and at least one that forces her to say what, if anything, would change in the conduct or the judgment of an actual war if she is right.

For every question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and say briefly what that likely answer is, so that I am not improvising in front of an audience. Please leave out anything about her career or how she came to the topic.
