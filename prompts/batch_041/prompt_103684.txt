I am a high school student who has been reading about consciousness on my own ever since my philosophy club spent a few weeks on the mind–body problem, and I keep running into a question I cannot settle by reading alone: when is a mental state actually felt — when is there something it is like to be in it — as opposed to merely being processed and put to use? I have written down my verdicts on a set of cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. (1) A patient with damage to his visual cortex can point at and even guess the orientation of a bar shown in the blind half of his field, yet sincerely reports seeing nothing: I say there is no experience there — he is using information he does not feel. (2) A word flashed and immediately masked so that I cannot report it, though it briefly speeds up how fast I read a related word afterward: no experience of that word. (3) The gorilla video everyone shows in psychology class — I am busy counting passes and never notice the gorilla: no experience of the gorilla. (4) At a loud party I am following one conversation and suddenly catch my own name spoken in another: there I think something did flicker into experience. (5) Watching the two images in binocular rivalry trade places, I experience the switching itself. (6) While I am asleep and dreaming I am having experiences, even though I cannot report any of them until I wake up. (7) The refrigerator hums all day and I never notice it, but in the instant it switches off I feel as though I had been hearing it the whole time — I want to say it was felt, just not attended to.

The best single rule I have come up with is something like this: a state is felt exactly when its content is broadcast widely, made available at once to many systems — memory, language, planning — the way one theory I read describes a global workspace. That rule handles (1) through (4) and (6) nicely: the blindsight information and the masked word never get broadcast, so they are not felt; my name gets through, so it is; dreams are broadcast internally even without report. But it seems to get (5) and especially (7) wrong, and (2) also bothers me, because the masked word clearly reaches several systems — it changes how I read a later word — so by the broadcast test it should count as felt, and yet I am sure it is not. So I suspect either the rule needs an extra clause, or one of my verdicts has to go, and I cannot tell which.

What I would like is this: state the principle, or the small set of rival principles, that best captures my verdicts, say precisely which clause is doing the work in each case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot save. Then apply it to cases I have not listed — a person whose brain implant forces a stimulus to be broadcast everywhere even though he insists he feels nothing; a patient who acts as though he sees perfectly but denies any experience of what he sees; an infant, or a dog; and a patient who was under general anesthesia and later shows priming to words spoken in the operating room. I do not need citations or a reading list — I would rather have the reasoning. And please separate what is genuinely settled among people who study this from what is still contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
