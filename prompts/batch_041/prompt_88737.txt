ok so my friend and I got into it last night, two hours on the couch, about whether history is locked in. His line: QWERTY, VHS, the usual — once a mediocre standard wins early it wins forever, so the present is a pile of accidents and nothing about it was inevitable. My line: half of that is just saying history happens, and the other half is a claim about switching costs dressed up as a claim about fate. We didnt settle it. So this morning I wrote up my position to see if it actually holds together, and I want you to tear into it. Below is the arguement, compressed. Tell me where it breaks.

The claim I want to defend: "path dependence" is three or four seperate claims wearing one coat, and the whole argument about lock-in is really an argument about which one is in play.

1. Contingency. Small early differences get amplified. True almost everywhere, and mostly boring — it says nothing about whether the outcome was avoidable or whether it was worse. Chaos is not lock-in. People reach for butterfly effects when what they mean is regret.

2. Lock-in proper. Positive feedback: the more a standard gets used, the better it gets relative to its rivals, so one of several live possibilities absorbs the system and stays. This is the only one that earns the phrase, and even here "locked" is a claim about switching costs, not impossibility. Expensive to leave is not the same as impossible to leave, and the two get run together constantly.

3. Lock-out. My addition, and the part I trust least. Sometimes the alternative isn't expensive to reach, it's gone — not costly but unreachable, because the skills, supply chains and institutions that would have carried it have themselves decayed. You can't buy your way back to a road that no longer exists. If that's a real category it's doing work that switching costs can't.

4. Lock-step. The outcome was overdetermined: many different starting points, same destination. Convergence. Here "would it have happened anyway" gets answered yes, and path-dependence talk is simply the wrong diagnosis. I suspect most cases people cite are actually this.

Then a distinction I think everyone muddles. In materials, hysteresis means the loop doesn't close — push the thing up and back down and it doesn't retrace, the state depends on the direction of travel. That is not lock-in. A hysteretic system can be perfectly willing to return; it just returns by a different route. Lock-in is about the cost of the return trip, hysteresis is about the shape of it. Different animals, same word in a lot of papers.

And a gloss from a friend who does econophysics: all of this is just whether time averages and ensemble averages coincide. Replay the same history a thousand times — if the outcomes pile up in one place you have convergence and one trajectory tells you about the whole ensemble; if they scatter, your trajectory is one draw and "would it have happened anyway" is ill-posed. I like this a lot. I cannot tell whether it's a genuine sharpening or just a fancy restatement of "history matters".

What I want from you: first, accuracy. Are those four actually distinct, or does lock-out collapse into lock-in with a very high switching cost? Is lock-step even a species of path dependence, or have I defined it out of existence? Does the ergodicity gloss earn its keep? Second, give me a real case for each of the four boxes and tell me which box is empty — I'm fairly sure one is. Third, the suboptimality worry: does path dependence matter at all if the locked-in state is as good as the one it displaced? I keep wanting to say no and I keep suspecting that's wrong. And say whether the QWERTY-versus-Dvorak fight, which I take to be a fight about mechanism rather than about counterfactuals, looks any different under my scheme. If some section is just me relabeling the obvious, tell me to cut it. Dont send me reading lists, I'm offline for the week.
