Two colleagues of mine, Mira and Dev, spent most of a long train ride arguing about a case Mira had constructed, and I never worked out who had the better of it; I had no stake in the dispute and still don't know which of them I believe. The case was this. A radiologist glances at a scan and is confident there is no fracture. In one version this is routine — if she is wrong the patient is simply re-scanned and little follows — while in the other version she is deciding between sending the patient home and sending the patient to surgery, so that a mistake could kill someone, though her own career and interests are untouched either way. The visual evidence is stipulated to be identical in the two versions.

Mira said the radiologist knows there is no fracture in the routine version and does not know it in the grave version, on identical evidence, because whether one knows depends on what is practically at stake in being wrong. Dev said that cannot be right: with the evidence held fixed the epistemic state is the same in both versions, and what varies is only whether it is correct to *say* 'she knows' — a matter settled by the context of whoever is doing the saying, not by anything in the radiologist. Mira replied that this reduces knowledge to a question of vocabulary, whereas the radiologist herself, if she later grasped what had been riding on the scan, would take back her earlier 'I know' — and one takes back a claim because it was not true, not because it was impolite to have made. Dev answered that people retract true statements for many reasons, chiefly to disown the consequences of having made them, which shows only that the norms of assertion bend with stakes and not that knowledge does. Mira then offered a case in which the two views come apart: the radiologist, safe at home, says of her past self 'I knew it was nothing,' while a malpractice reviewer, whose own position is on the line, says 'she did not know.' Dev's view, she said, makes both remarks true of one and the same state, which turns the reviewer's accusation into a fact about which of the two is speaking; her own view makes the reviewer simply mistaken.

Please adjudicate the five moves. Give me a numbered list of exactly five items, one per move, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; in each item say how the move fares and what, if anything, it leaves out.
