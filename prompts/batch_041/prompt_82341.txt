So I've somehow agreed to run next month's session at our department's interdisciplinary reading group, and the topic I got handed is "Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and what they do and don't show outside mathematics." The room is mostly philosophers of mind, a couple of cognitive science people, one guy from the ML side, and me — basically nobody who's done real logic, but everybody has heard the name and thinks they know what it means. Two of them will definately tell me it proves you can't know everything or that any system of thought has holes, and one is going to say, ten minutes in, that it proves minds can't be machines (you know the argument I mean). I've got 75 minutes and a whiteboard and I do know the theorem well enough — so don't explain it to me. Plan how I explain it.

What order do the pieces have to come in so that by the time I actually state the theorem it feels inevitable rather than like a magic trick? My instinct is: provability as a purely syntactic game first, before truth gets anywhere near the room; then a machine that just spits out strings from rules; then the diagonal move, the sentence that says of itself that it has no proof; then, and only then, the step out to "true in the standard model"; and the analogizing to minds, law, science last of all, if at all. Tell me where that order is wrong, what has to come before provability that I'm not seeing, and exactly where the consistency assumption has to enter so it doesn't look like an arbitrary fine-print condition. Second, the carrying example: I want the whole thing to hang on one toy formal system — a little string-printing machine with two or three rules that the room can hold in their heads — so tell me what that toy has to have (enough arithmetic in it? a way of talking about its own strings? effectively checkable rules?) and what it must *not* have, or it'll do the wrong work. Also tell me whether the second incompleteness theorem earns its place in the sequence or should be one of the things I say I'm deliberately skipping, along with the arithmetization of syntax and the diagonal lemma proof.

And the traps, because I think this is where the whole session lives or dies: the word "complete" colliding with Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order logic — I'd bet money that at least two people in that room have those fused into one thing — the assumption that the theorem applies to "any system," so constitutions and ethical codes and natural languages all get swept in, and the slide from "unprovable in this system" to "unknowable" or "so mathematics is broken." Map each of those misconceptions to the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off, say which ones I should refuse to settle at this level rather than pretend to, and give me one thing to do in the last ten minutes that would actually tell me whether the idea landed — something where they have to make a call about a fresh case they haven't seen, not just nod along.
