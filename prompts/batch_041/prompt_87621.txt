Hey — I'm the student council president at my high school and I got asked to give a short speech (like four minutes) at the rally outside the district office next Wednesday night, the night before the teachers vote on whether to authorize a strike. So the crowd is going to be a wierd mix: a few hundred teachers, alot of whom have done this before and will visibly groan at anything that sounds like a bumper sticker; parents who are honestly freaked out about who's watching their kids if there's a walkout; students like me; and I'm pretty sure the local paper is sending somebody. Everything I write comes out either as "we support our teachers!" (which says nothing) or as me lecturing adults about labor law, and I need it to be neither.

Here's the thing that's actually bugging me and I want the speech built around it. My friend Maya keeps saying a strike is just a bunch of people each individually deciding not to show up, and since the union gets the raise for everybody anyway — including the teachers who cross the line — the ones who strike are basically chumps and the ones who cross are just being rational. My dad says the opposite, that the ones who cross are free-riders living off other people's risk. I don't think either of them has the facts wrong, and I don't think shouting "solidarity" answers it, because that's the word I'm trying to pin down, not the answer. Mr. Alvarez in social studies said the whole legal idea is that what gets protected is acting *together*, not one person refusing by themselves, and he also said a strike is more like a sentence than a decision because it's aimed at somebody — which I don't fully get, but I think it's the seed of the thing. So what I want the speech to actually do is say something precise about what a strike is, one action or a million actions, and whether the right to organize is something a person has or something a group has (a right to assemble seems like it only means anything when there's a bunch of you), and then make that land for this exact crowd without turning into civics class.

Couple more things: nothing in it for the parents can be a promise I can't keep, and please don't put a quote from some famous person in there, because if the reporter asks me who said it I will definately get it wrong. Just write the speech.
