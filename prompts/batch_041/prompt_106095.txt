A friend who works in synthetic biology and I have been going back and forth for months about what it means to say that something has a function, and I keep coming back to the etiological account — the view that ascribing a function to a trait is really a claim about its history, namely that the trait was selected for the effect in question (or, where artefacts are concerned, that it was designed to produce it). It seems to me to get something right that a purely causal-role account misses: it explains why malfunction is possible, and why a function ascription can be normative rather than merely descriptive. But I have grown suspicious that it is more exposed than its defenders admit, and I would like a proper map of that exposure rather than the textbook objections. What should actually move someone who holds the view? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, tell me which ones the etiological theorist would themselves concede and which they would simply wave away, and finish with an honest verdict on how much of the account survives and where it needs revision rather than abandonment.

I would like you to work through a few pressure points in particular. There is the case of a trait that is maintained by selection now for a purpose other than the one that explains why it spread in the first place — does it have two functions, one, or no determinate function, and is any of those answers tolerable? There is the case of a trait with a clear and beneficial effect but no selection history whatsoever: a metabolic pathway inserted into a bacterium by a designer, a structure that arises reliably whenever the organism develops in a novel environment, or a feature present in the very first generation of a lineage. Does the account give the right verdict here, and if it has to reach for a designer's intention in order to do so, has it quietly become a different account of function altogether? There is the first-generation problem more generally, and whether the account can distinguish a function from a mere effect in such cases without going in a circle. And there is the worry that tying function to selection history makes function ascriptions hostage to facts that working biologists neither know nor care about when they attribute functions in practice — whether that is a defect in the account or merely a divergence of interests is something I would like you to adjudicate rather than assume.

Finally, I would like you to say what the etiological account predicts that the causal-role account does not, and whether some of the apparent counterexamples are really just cases where the two accounts are answering different questions rather than competing over the same one. If it turns out that the etiological account has been insulated by qualification, say so plainly and say whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Answer in British English.
