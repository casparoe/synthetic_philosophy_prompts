I'm a doctoral student in intellectual history trying to pin down what the modern discipline of history quietly commits itself to. Consider a familiar package of professional norms: historians are trained not to judge the past by present standards and to produce scholarship that stands on its own rather than serving current causes; yet the same departments and funders now reward "impact" and public relevance; the antiquarian impulse survives in digitization projects that aim to archive everything; and the discipline still tells a civic story about why historical knowledge matters. What conception of the relation between historical knowledge and life does that package presuppose — does the anti-presentism norm commit historians to the antiquarian ideal of knowledge detached from present needs, and can that ideal be reconciled with the demand for relevance and with the critical use of history to break the hold of the past? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark where it is underdetermined, so that several accounts of history's value would license the same norms. Then say what would have to change in how history is actually practiced if the presupposed view were replaced by the thought that historical knowledge is justified by what it does for life. Keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among historians and philosophers of history, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
