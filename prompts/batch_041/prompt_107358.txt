I have no formal training in economics or philosophy. I have been working through decision theory on my own for several years, and I now run a small study circle for people at roughly the same stage as me — fluent in the formal machinery of expected utility theory, but not specialists. For our next session I want to end with a written exercise, and I would like you to produce the whole assessment package: a single essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The exercise must not be answerable by reciting the standard story about the sure-thing principle and the certainty effect. To make sure it cannot be, I have built it around two pairs of lotteries whose numbers do not appear in the usual treatments, so the candidate has to run the argument rather than remember it. Build the question around these, and keep the prizes and probabilities exactly as I give them.

First pair (prizes $0, $400, $2,000):
Problem 1 — A: $400 for certain. B: a 90% chance of $400, a 9% chance of $2,000, and a 1% chance of $0.
Problem 2 — C: a 10% chance of $400 and a 90% chance of $0. D: a 9% chance of $2,000 and a 91% chance of $0.

Second pair (prizes $0, $1,000, $2,500):
Problem 3 — E: $1,000 for certain. F: an 80% chance of $2,500 and a 20% chance of $0.
Problem 4 — G: a 50% chance of $1,000 and a 50% chance of $0. H: a 40% chance of $2,500 and a 60% chance of $0.

Treat the following as the intuitive responses the exercise is about: A over B, D over C, E over F, and H over G.

I want the candidate to confront four things, roughly in this order. First, that each pair of choices, taken together, violates the independence axiom, and to demonstrate this by decomposing the lotteries into mixtures rather than by gesturing at the sure-thing principle. Second, that the explanation people usually reach for — that a sure gain is being treated as somehow special — can carry the first pair but not the second, because in Problem 4 both options are risky; so the candidate has to say what is actually doing the work once certainty is gone. Third, that there is a genuine difference between the claim that independence fails as a description of how people choose and the claim that it fails as a requirement of rationality, and to take a position on each, engaging with the strongest defence of the axiom — that the component shared by the two options is irrelevant, so letting it sway the choice is an error — instead of simply declaring that people are irrational. Fourth, and this is what I care about most, whether any principled repair keeps all four intuitive choices while salvaging something recognisably like independence, and what that repair costs.

Please write the question as I would hand it out, with the four problems stated, and do not name the paradox or the axiom in the question itself — I want the candidate to identify what is going on. Pitch it so a strong candidate finishes in about forty-five minutes. Then give me a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit points; for each point, give the marks it carries and one line on how a candidate typically fails to earn it. Do not let the model answer depend on naming particular authors, papers, or dates — several people in the circle will be working with nothing in front of them.
