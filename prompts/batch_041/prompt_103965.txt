I work in the emerging-risk team of a mid-sized general insurer, and a growing share of my job is putting a defensible number on exposures we have never written before: a new class of injury claim arising from warehouse automation, a product-liability exposure from a battery chemistry that has been in the field for only eighteen months, a one-off liability from a construction method nobody in the market has yet insured. I have roughly two days per case, no scope for bespoke statistical modelling, and whatever I produce has to survive sign-off by the chief actuary with the regulator looking over our shoulder. The recurring difficulty is never the arithmetic; it is choosing the class of past events from which to take the frequency at all. Take the battery case: I could draw on all lithium-ion incidents, on all battery incidents in warehouse settings, on incidents involving this specific cell format, on this manufacturer's earlier product line, or on industrial fires generally, and the frequencies those pools give me differ by two orders of magnitude. A senior colleague's rule of thumb is simply to use the narrowest class with at least a hundred observations, but that strikes me as smuggling in a statistical convention while quietly ignoring relevance. I recognise this as the reference-class problem wearing a suit, and I know the classical answers in outline — take the narrowest class for which you have reliable data, take a homogeneous class, take the widest class that still contains the event — but I have never seen them turned into something a working analyst can actually execute under deadline.

Please write me that procedure. I want the questions I should ask, in order, when I have to fix a reference class for a novel risk; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the tests I should run to decide whether a class is too narrow to be reliable or too broad to be relevant; the point at which the procedure stops and tells me to use a class and stop second-guessing myself; and the point at which it hands the decision back to a person because the question has become one of what we are willing to be held to rather than one of fact. It must be usable by someone who knows statistics but not the philosophy, so tell me which distinctions from the theory have to survive the simplification and which can safely be dropped, and be candid about the ways the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on two of my cases: the battery-chemistry exposure, where I have eighteen months of data covering a class of roughly forty incidents, and a first-of-its-kind claim from a construction method where the only usable pool is "all large construction liability claims". For at least one of these I expect an outcome I will not like — a recommendation to use a broader, duller class than the one the underwriters are pressing for — and I would rather hear it now than at sign-off. Finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do, and where I should simply document my judgment and move on. Please write the whole answer in British English.
