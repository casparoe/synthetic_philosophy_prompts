When cochlear implants were approved for use in young deaf children in the late 1980s and early 1990s, parts of the Deaf community opposed the practice, arguing that deafness is not a defect to be corrected but the basis of a language and a culture. Treat that controversy as a test case for the naturalism/normativism debate about health and disease, and run the analysis in both directions.

Begin with what each side implies about the case. On the naturalist side, take Boorse's biostatistical theory, on which a disease is an internal state that reduces some functional ability below species-typical efficiency, so that whether deafness counts as a disease turns on a biological fact rather than on anyone's values. On the normativist side, take the view that ascribing disease is an irreducibly evaluative act, so that the Deaf community's objection is not a confusion about biology but a substantive claim about what is worth wanting. Then determine, for each account, which features of the controversy it explains and which it distorts. The naturalist owes an answer to the charge that a statistical norm is doing the work where a biological norm is claimed; the normativist owes an answer to the charge that the community's objection is itself a factual claim about what the ear is for. Bring in the distinction between a biological impairment and the social disadvantage that may or may not attach to it, and say whether that distinction tracks the naturalism/normativism divide or cuts across it.

Finally, say what the episode shows about the debate itself: whether it confirms one side, embarrasses both, or exposes a case neither anticipated. Press the counterfactual — if a signing environment neutralized every disadvantage of being deaf, would the naturalist still classify deafness as a disease, and would that verdict be a reductio of naturalism or a clarification of it? Say whether a hybrid view, on which a disorder requires both a biological dysfunction and a value-laden judgment that the condition is harmful, handles the case better than either pure position, and where the two pure positions are simply silent, say so rather than making them speak.
