My dad died three weeks ago, he was 79, and the last four years of it were vascular dementia. I'm giving the eulogy at the memorial on Saturday and I'm stuck, so I'm asking for help. Since he went into the hospice I've been reading Dennett's thing about the four kinds of creatures more or less on repeat — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — and honestly it's the only frame that's made sense of what I watched happen to him. So I want the eulogy built on it. Here's my problem though: about half the room are his old colleagues and grad students from the ecology department where he taught for thirty-five years, and they'll clock exactly what I'm doing the second I say "Skinnerian". The other half are my mum's side — cousins, aunts, people who've never read a word of it — and they'll glaze over if I turn a funeral into a seminar. My brother has also already told me he doesn't want "the philosophy bit", he thinks it's cold, that I'll end up eulogising an idea instead of our father. He might be right, so I need you to make it not cold.

What I want: the speech itself, about four or five minutes when spoken, with the four levels as its actual spine, not decoration. Darwinian first — the newborn, the reflexes, whatever he showed up with. Then Skinnerian — the boy who learned by trial and error, touched the stove once, all the small skills he just tried until they stuck. Then Popperian, which for me is the heart of it — the man who could run a possibility inside his own head and let it die there rather than having to actually live it out, the scientist who'd argue himself out of a plan over breakfast. Then Gregorian, the top rung, the one with words and the tools of thought, who could take a whole discipline's worth of thinking-tools and make them his own — the teacher, the man who got those tools into other people's heads, mine included.

Then the hard turn, which is really why I need this and not just a nice poem: he came back down. Words went first, then the planning, then he was back to trial and error, and at the very end back to the reflexes — he'd squeeze your hand, turn towards your voice, that was it. The eulogy has to be honest about that, everyone in that room watched it and I won't paper over it. But I want it to also say something true that isn't just bleak: that the top rung isn't really the individual's, that the thinking-tools he installed in other people are still running, that a Gregorian creature's mind is partly made out of other people's minds and so outlives the body that carried it — so his own head emptying out last doesn't mean the thing he built is gone. If that's a fair reading of Dennett, use it; if it isn't, say so and don't force it, I'd rather it be honest than neat. And if there's a rung where the whole ladder metaphor breaks down, tell me that too, because I'll be standing in front of people who'll notice.

Two last things. It has to land for my brother and the cousins as well, so no jargon without a plain-English gloss the first time it appears. And please write it in British English — he was from Bristol and it would sound wrong any other way. No quotes or citations in it obviously, it's a speech, not an essay, and definately no "as Dennett says" bits, it should sound like me talking about my dad.
