You are an archaeologist who also works in the philosophy of science — someone who has spent a decade in the trench and also writes on the logic of inference. I am preparing a methods chapter and I need the specification before I can write the defence of it.

The subject is inference from material remains to past beliefs and institutions: not whether people herded cattle, but whether they worshipped, redistributed, buried with intent, held property, or answered to a central authority. Write the desiderata that any theory of that inference must meet — the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the empirical results it cannot contradict — with the consideration behind each requirement, and with a judgement about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp.

Fix the purpose first. The theory must be good enough to defend a claim about past beliefs or institutions before an adversarial panel: rival archaeologists, a heritage regulator, and members of a descendant community whose ontology may not contain the categories the claim uses. A standard that only licenses talk among specialists is too weak for this.

Begin by brainstorming: list every candidate requirement you can generate, including ones you expect to reject, then prune. Say what you pruned and why — the pruning is where the philosophy is, and I would rather see the rejected candidates than a tidy list that pretends they never occurred to you.

Build the list around at least these cases, each of which is a real shape of evidence even where the details are invented:

1. A waterlogged lakeside deposit holding more than three hundred complete but mostly undecorated vessels, with almost no food refuse, ash, or broken bone; the excavators want to call it a ritual offering.
2. A burnt room whose floor is covered by sixty near-identical unfired clay figurines, laid face down in rows; the excavators want to call the building a shrine.
3. A large, centrally placed building with monumental walls, no hearths, no domestic debris, and rows of standardised storage jars; the excavators want to infer a redistributive institution backed by a central authority.
4. A cemetery with almost no grave goods; the excavators want to infer an egalitarian society.
5. A site where all organic material has decayed, no texts survive, and radiocarbon ranges span two centuries; the excavators want to say nothing at all, and are accused of refusing to interpret.

For each requirement, name the case or the general consideration that generates it, and say whether it is a demand of the evidence — formation processes, equifinality, preservation bias, chronological resolution, the underdetermination of meaning by form — or an import from a theoretical camp, such as a demand for strict testability, a demand that interpretation recover meaning, or a demand that the result be usable by non-specialists.

Rule explicitly on the following distinctions, keeping or collapsing each with reasons: what people did versus what they intended versus what they believed; individual belief versus shared or institutional belief; ritual versus mundane; emic versus etic categories; absence of evidence versus evidence of absence; a pattern in the ground versus the label we hang on it.

Then identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say exactly where the conflict bites. Finally, name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate theories, and what it eliminates.

Constraints: no citations, no reading lists, no names of scholars — I am testing the reasoning, not the bibliography, and I would rather have a well-argued requirement than an attributed one. Write the desiderata as a numbered list, add a short table marking each requirement as non-negotiable or camp-specific, and keep the whole thing under 1,500 words.
