My old development-econ professor has firm views on why particular countries are rich, and I've been keeping a list. Britain's industrial revolution: not coal and not empire, he says, but secure property and merchants with a seat at the table after 1688. Botswana: not the diamonds, plenty of countries have diamonds — it's that the post-independence elite didn't plunder and kept the rules fairly open. North versus South Korea settles it for him: same land, same people, different rules. But then he'll turn around and say tropical Africa was poor in 1900 because of malaria, tsetse and bad soils, and that no institutional tweak would have fixed that; and that Switzerland and the Netherlands got rich off rivers and mountain passes and only later grew institutions to match; and that the places that were richest in 1500 are mostly poor now, so geography can't be the deep cause. He also gets annoyed if you ask what would have happened to Britain without coal — you can't rerun history — even though every explanation he gives sounds like exactly that kind of claim.

New case he hasn't ruled on: a landlocked tropical country with a copper boom since 2004. Corruption indices put it near the bottom, property rights are a joke, the ruling party has held power since independence. Income per head is up fivefold since 2004, but four in five people still farm and the schools and clinics haven't improved much.

What's he going to say about why it's richer, and will that square with the rest of his list? Give me the rules his earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with, what each of them predicts here, which rule I should bet on and why, and the one case — real or made up — that would force him to choose between them. Also tell me whether it matters that he's measuring richness by income per head rather than by how the typical person lives. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate my question.
