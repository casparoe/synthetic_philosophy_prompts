Bertrand's paradox is usually described as a refutation of the principle of indifference. Ask for the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and the answer comes out as 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether one randomizes over endpoints, over the chord's distance from the centre, or over its midpoint within the disk. Since a principle that yields three incompatible answers to a single question cannot be a principle, I am tempted to abandon indifference for continuous quantities and to keep it only for discrete cases such as a fair coin or a well-shuffled deck, where it appears to do honest work. Yet I cannot state what the relevant difference is supposed to be, since the same notion of equal likelihood seems to be at work in both places, and I would rather not give up the symmetry arguments that let me say anything about a coin before I have tossed it. Where has my reasoning gone wrong?
