I'm a science journalist putting together a long feature on John von Neumann's last, unfinished book about the brain — the short manuscript he was preparing from a lecture series when he died in 1957 and which appeared the following year as The Computer and the Brain — and for the magazine's website I want to run an interactive flowchart, "Which picture of the brain do you actually hold?," instead of another two thousand words of my own prose. The reason I keep returning to that book is that it doesn't so much argue for a thesis as leave a handful of questions open, and those questions are still the fault lines. Is the nervous system's signalling digital or analog? A neuron's output is all-or-nothing, yet almost everything feeding into it — receptor potentials, the graded integration along a dendrite — is continuous, so both descriptions are available and the physics doesn't force the choice. Is the brain's "code" a small alphabet of symbols or something more like a statistical property of a vast population of noisy units? How does a machine assembled from slow, individually unreliable parts produce behaviour reliable enough to keep an animal alive for decades? Is memory kept at addresses, or smeared across the network so that it can be recovered from a partial cue? And is the brain's "logic" the Boolean logic of switching circuits, or a different logic altogether? I spent a dinner last month being told by a computational neuroscientist that the whole "brain as computer" framing is obsolete, which only convinced me the disagreement is worth mapping properly.

What I'd like from you, then, is a decision tree — or, at the points where the branches genuinely don't nest, an explicit taxonomy — whose internal nodes are questions a reader could answer without knowing which position they lead to, each phrased as a concrete case or claim rather than as a label, and whose leaves are the positions themselves. Start the tree from something almost everyone would grant — that the brain is made of slow and individually unreliable components and yet reliably produces adaptive behaviour — and branch from there. I need the reader sorted on at least these axes: the unit of computation (the single neuron, the synapse, the cortical column, or a statistical property of a population); the representational medium (discrete spikes, continuous rates, spike timing, or an irreducibly mixed scheme); the source of reliability (deliberate error-correcting redundancy, statistical averaging over very many units, or an architecture that merely tolerates failure without correcting it); and whether "computation" is the right category at all — I want a leaf on which someone says the question is malformed, and I want it reachable through the same tree rather than tacked on at the end. Assume the reader is a smart generalist who has never encountered any of these debates, so no node may presuppose a term of art.

For each leaf, give the position's name if it has one and, if nobody has bothered to name it, say so and describe it anyway, followed by one sentence on the commitment its holder would find hardest to defend. Mark every combination of answers that nobody occupies and tell me why you think it is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored — and flag any two positions the tree cannot separate rather than manufacturing a distinction that isn't there. Eight to twelve leaves, please, and no citations or reading list: this is a sidebar, and I can't hang footnotes off a flowchart.
