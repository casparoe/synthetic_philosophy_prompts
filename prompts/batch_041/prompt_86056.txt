I run a fortnightly reading group for history grad students and we keep circling the same fight: someone says the First World War would have happened anyway, someone else says that isn't even a meaningful claim, and a third person says the question is badly posed because there's no such thing as *the* cause of anything. I want to stop haveing this argument and start sorting people instead, so could you write me a 'which theory of overdetermination do you actually hold?' quiz? Nine or ten questions, each one a concrete case rather than a question about labels — the three arsonists who each independently torch the same house; two snipers firing at the same instant where either shot would have killed; a double-prevention case (someone jams the signal that would have stopped the train); a cause that's an absence (the garrison that never came, the warning that was never given); a structural cause like the Black Death shifting the bargaining power of labour, where you can't point to any one decision; the Sarajevo assassination and the question of what a Europe without it even looks like; a case where two histories of the same event, one blaming a king and one blaming a harvest, are both defensible; and a chaos case where the initial difference is so tiny that re-running the counterfactual lands you in a completely different world. Two or three answer options per question, phrased so the reader can't tell which position they're buying into. (We've been reading a lot of counterfactual history lately, the 'what if the Armada had landed' sort of thing, which is probably why everyone's so het up about this.)

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves. I need at least these to come out: the pure counterfactualist, for whom only a difference-maker deserves the name of cause, and who therefore thinks overdetermination is a philosophers' artefact because preemption always dissolves the ties; the INUS/network view, where causes are nodes in a sufficient set and overdetermination is simply normal; the contrastivist or pluralist, for whom 'the' cause is relative to a contrast and the whole puzzle is verbal; the pragmatic selectionist, who thinks there's one causal structure but 'the' cause is picked out by our explanatory interest, Weber-style value-relevance; the structuralist, for whom individual actors and events are surface phenomena and the real causes are relations of production or demography; and the regularity or covering-law person, for whom singular causation in history is either not real or just a compressed lawlike generalisation. Also make room for the narrativist who thinks historical explanation isn't causal at all — and if that position turns out to be indistinguishable from one of the others given your questions, say so rather than inventing a question to rescue it. At the end, tell me which answer combinations nobody occupies, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves the quiz can't really separate.
