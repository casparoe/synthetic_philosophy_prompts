I grew up in a town called Kettle Creek (pop. 3,900, one traffic light, and it blinks), and next Thursday we vote on whether to repeal a building ordinance that's been on the books since 1908. It says every new building on Main Street has to have its ground-floor door raised at least two feet above the sidewalk, with steps up to it. Nobody knew why. Contractors have always hated it, and in the last decade it's become the thing that keeps our two cafes and the pharmacy from being accessible to anyone in a wheelchair, so there's now an actual coalition -- the accessibility people, the new families, the coffee guy -- who want it gone. The old-timers want it kept, mostly because they're sick of being told that everything their parents did was stupid. I'm the one who found out why its there, so the historical society asked me to give the speech at the dinner the night before the vote. Sixty people will be there, the council vote is 4-3, and the lumber dealer's great-grandson is one of the four, so this could actually matter.

Here's what I found. The 1908 minutes record the vote but not the debate, so for eighty years the standard story was "old-timey flood prevention" or "they were just odd." Then a letter turned up in the county archive from the lumber dealer who proposed the ordinance. Main Street really did flood almost every spring, that part was true, and raising the thresholds kept water and silt out of the shops. But he also owned the only lumber yard in town, and that same spring he'd taken delivery of a boxcar of short boards he couldn't sell any other way, and he wrote the specs. So the fence had two reasons, one good and one that was basically a racket, and the flood reason died in 1931 when the levee went in. There's also a thing nobody planned: those stoops turned into the town's living room. The whole social life of Main Street happens on them. And everyone tells the 1974 story, where the town tore down the covered bridge because it was "useless," and then spent the nineties building a fake one for the tourists. My grandmother, who sat on the zoning board for thirty years, used to say that a rule which survives fifty years of people wanting it gone is carrying information, and a rule which survives fifty years because nobody ever wanted it gone is just carrying inertia.

Please write me the speech -- six minutes, spoken, for that room. It has to do real work with the fence idea without turning into a civics lecture: maybe three people there have read any Burke and nobody's heard of Chesterton, so the argument has to live in the specifics above rather than in names. Be honest about both halves of what the letter shows -- the real reason and the racket -- and about the difference between "there was a reason" and "the reason still holds," and about the fact that the people who paid for the fence and the people who pay for it now are not the same people. The keepers should come away feeling the town honored its dead by finally going and finding out what the fence was for; the repealers should come away feeling they aren't being called vandals for noticing that the steps are a wall. For what it's worth I lean toward repeal, but I don't want a repeal speech -- I want one I could read aloud to the people who'll be furious at me. And it should land on a specific proposal, not "let's have a conversation," one that at least one side is going to hate. Don't use the words "tradition" or "values" at all.
