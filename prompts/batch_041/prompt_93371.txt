In a seminar on the problem of evil, the discussion of Leibniz's thesis that this is the best of all possible worlds has reached a familiar impasse. The critic says that the quantity and intensity of horrendous suffering, especially suffering that seems to serve no compensating good, makes it unreasonable to think that a good and omnipotent being would actualize this world rather than some better one. The defender replies that we cannot survey the whole of history, that many goods may depend on the possibility of evil, that creaturely freedom may be necessary for certain goods, or that the best world may be one in which some evils are permitted for the sake of a larger narrative or the development of virtue. Each side knows the other's moves; the critic treats the appeal to unknown goods as ad hoc, the defender treats the critic's judgment as an unjustified global inference from a local perspective, and neither moves.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, not another round of theodicy. My hypothesis is that the impasse is not fundamentally about the amount of evil but about the formal structure of the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds: whether best means a maximum in a total ordering of worlds, whether the set of possible worlds is totally ordered at all, whether God must actualize a maximum rather than merely a world than which no other is better, and whether compossibility constraints—the conditions on which possibilities can coexist in one world—make the relevant set of worlds something other than a simple menu. Is that right, or is the real disagreement axiological (about what makes a world good), modal (about what is possible), epistemic (about what we can know of the whole), or verbal (about the word best)? If it is a mix, say which component is doing the stalling.

Then I want an account of what would count as progress given your diagnosis. Could a formal result about the ordering of possible worlds settle it? Would a distinction between a best world and an unsurpassable world, or between a maximum and a supremum, dissolve the impasse or relocate it? Would the debate change if we separated the question of whether this world is the best from the question of whether a good God could have a sufficient reason to actualize a non-best world? Or is the disagreement at bottom a clash of starting intuitions about what an acceptable response to evil looks like, in which case no argument of the kind either side is offering should be expected to move it? Be concrete about which positions in the actual debate your diagnosis fits, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress.
