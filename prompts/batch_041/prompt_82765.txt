I am a novelist, some way into a historical novel whose central figures left almost no first-person record behind, and I have arrived at a difficulty that is at bottom conceptual rather than technical. I would like you to help me work it through. Please take the part of an expert in historiography and the epistemology of archives — someone who has thought hard about how records come to exist, and who has spent real time in the reading room — and, at the same time, someone with a novelist's ear for what a metaphor is quietly doing when it has been smuggled into an argument. Think carefully before you commit to anything. Begin by brainstorming: set out for yourself what the analogy I am about to describe is being asked to carry, and only then start to answer.

The situation is this. The people I am writing about were, for the most part, people whom the institutions around them had reason to count and to charge, but not to let speak. What survives of them are entries in registers, a handful of bills of sale, a few court depositions in which their words appear only as answers to questions someone else had chosen to ask, in categories someone else had chosen. Nothing in their own hand; nothing addressed to a future reader; no diary, no letter kept on purpose. And the silence is not random — it falls precisely where a society had no institutional need to write.

My editor, who is a good editor, keeps invoking a picture I have come to distrust. The archive, she says, is a mirror: it reflects what was there. If the people I care about are missing from it, that is not the archive's doing; it is because they did not write anything down. From this she draws a rule for the novel. I may write only what the documents support, and an invented interior life is a falsification — a sentimental one at that, since it puts words in the mouths of the dead and calls the result history. A mirror cannot lie, she says, so my additions can only be lies.

I suspect the picture is wrong, but I want the suspicion tested rather than flattered, because the conclusion is not obviously absurd: there is something real to the charge of sentimental ventriloquism, and I do not want to answer it with a shrug. My own candidate is that the archive is less like a mirror than like a sieve, or a filter with a very particular mesh — but I am not confident that this replacement is any better, and I would rather have the audit than the alternative.

So, please:

1. Give the mapping explicitly. A table of correspondences would suit me well: what plays the role of the mirror, of the thing reflected, of the light, of the frame, of the hand that holds it, of the viewer. Say what would have to be true of archives for the mirror picture to get a grip at all.

2. Say where the mapping breaks, and then sort the breaks. Some will be decorative; some will bear directly on the work my editor is making the analogy do. I want the second kind separated from the first, and I want to know why each one matters, or does not.

3. Tell me what the mirror quietly imports that the archive does not have. I have my own list — passivity, completeness, a single source of light, no curator, no shelf — but I would like yours, and I would like to know which of the imports are doing the real work of licensing her conclusion.

4. Then the verdict. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does her conclusion survive — the conclusion that documentary fidelity is the only honest course and invention is falsification? If it survives, say so plainly and tell me what I owe it. If it does not, tell me exactly which step fails.

5. Finally, if the mirror is the wrong instrument, what is the better one? Test my sieve against whatever you propose, and say what the better analogy licenses, and what it forbids, that the mirror did not. I am not looking for a licence to invent whatever I please; I am looking for the right description of the constraint I am actually under — and, if it turns out that invented interiority is a claim of some kind, for the right account of what it is a claim about.

Throughout, please keep the question of what an archive is separate from the question of what a novelist may do. I suspect my editor's argument turns on running the two together, and I would like to see whether it does.
