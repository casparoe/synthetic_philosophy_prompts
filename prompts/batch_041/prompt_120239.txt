I am a type designer working on a variable font for a smartwatch with a 1-bit e-ink display. The lowercase a is giving me trouble. I want a single-story geometric a: a nearly perfect circle with a short stem on the right, no closed counter, and a terminal that does not quite meet the bowl. My collaborator says that at small sizes, in strings such as ao, ad, and alarm, it reads as an o with a smudge. If I add the features needed to make it unmistakably an a, she says, the letter will no longer fit the austere style of the rest of the alphabet. She wants to replace it with a conventional double-story a, which would be legible but would break the font's visual logic. I think there is a way to keep the single-story form and still have it count as an a, but I cannot articulate the principle. The watch uses the font for safety-critical words such as alarm, cancel, and start, so legibility is not a trivial concern. I need advice on how to decide whether a given glyph is still an a or has crossed into being a different mark, and on how to weigh that against the demand that the whole alphabet share one style. Please give me a way to think about this that does not simply assert that legibility matters or that style matters. I am also unsure whether the answer changes when the same glyph has to work across a variable weight axis, from hairline to black.
