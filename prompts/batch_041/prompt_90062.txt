My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Max Black's two spheres, and I came away dissatisfied with both of us. He holds that the identity of indiscernibles is plainly false: two spheres, alike in every respect, alone in an otherwise empty universe, are still two things, so there must be some difference between them that no property captures. I could not refute him, but I could not shake the suspicion that he was treating "two things" as more innocent than it is. What I want from you is not a verdict on the thought experiment itself but an account of what a real, working practice commits us to.

The practice I have in mind is the way physicists count states for systems of identical particles. Asked how many ways a system of two electrons can be arranged, the standard treatment counts the arrangement and the arrangement with the two electrons exchanged as one and the same possibility, not two. This is not offered as a bookkeeping convenience: it is built into the formalism, and it is what makes the observed statistics come out, from the exclusion principle to the behavior of helium. Yet the same physicists will say without hesitation that there are two electrons in the orbital.

So: what does this practice presuppose about identity and individuation? Does it commit physics to the identity of indiscernibles, or only to the weaker claim that there is no bare thisness, no haecceity, over and above the properties? Could my friend, who insists that the spheres are two even though nothing distinguishes them, give a consistent account of why exchanging the electrons makes no difference, or does his position collide with the practice? Where is the practice underdetermined, such that a bundle theorist, a structuralist who says particles are nothing but nodes in a pattern of relations, and someone who denies that quanta are individuals at all might each explain the very same practice in their own terms? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, and tell me what would have to change — in the physics, or only in the metaphysics — if we adopted a robustly haecceitist picture on which each electron carries its own primitive identity.
