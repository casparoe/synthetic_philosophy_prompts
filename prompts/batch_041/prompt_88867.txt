You are a political philosopher whose specialty is the ethics of public office, and what follows is conceptual groundwork rather than a literature review. Work carefully and deliberately. Begin with a short set of notes, a paragraph or so, in which you say what you take the phenomenon of "dirty hands" in politics actually to be and which of the cases below you count as instances of it; then write the deliverable.

I am writing a chapter on the ethics of office, and before I commit to a line of argument I want the specification: what any adequate theory of dirty hands would have to deliver. Fix the purpose of the theory as follows — it must be strong enough to guide an office-holder's deliberation in advance, under uncertainty, and not merely to redescribe a decision after the fact. A requirement that can be met only in hindsight is, for my purposes, a failure.

Here is the material I have collected. Some of it is standard; some of it is mine, and I do not yet know whether it is a distinction or a confusion.

1. The core case. An official does what ordinary morality condemns — tortures a suspect, orders a killing, lies to the public to hold a coalition together — and we want to say both that it was wrong and that it was the right thing to have done. A theory must either make that combination coherent or explain why it cannot be had.

2. The dirt must be more than a cost. An official who spends the treasury to save lives is not dirty-handed. So the theory must say what the residue is — guilt, a stain on the record, an outstanding debt — whether it is dischargeable at all (by punishment, confession, resignation, penance), and, above all, to whom it is owed.

3. The theory must separate dirty hands from its neighbours: moral dilemma, regret over a permitted act, ordinary wrongdoing under temptation, "lesser evil" reasoning in which nothing is dirty because the arithmetic works out, political realism in which morality never applied, and the private person who does the same deed.

4. If the wrong is done in the citizens' name, by their agent, why are the politician's hands the dirty ones rather than ours — and if ours, why may she be punished for an act she performed on our behalf? I cannot tell whether this is a genuine constraint on a theory or an artefact of the metaphor.

5. One familiar demand is that the politician must pay a price: be punished, lose office, at least suffer. If a theory requires that, it seems to say the act was wrong after all, and then it owes an account of why it was nonetheless right to do.

6. The variable to isolate. The same torture, and the same failure to extract anything, differs morally depending on whether the official took a genuine risk for a real end and whether she took pleasure in it. A theory must capture that difference or explain why it is irrelevant.

7. The mundane range. A mayor who misreports a budget to prevent a panic; a health minister who rations a drug by a rule she knows will kill someone identifiable. A theory that covers only ticking bombs and wars has narrowed its subject out of existence.

8. The ex ante demand. The official cannot know the consequences and cannot be sure the wrong will pay off. Whatever the theory licenses must be usable before the fact.

What I want back: a numbered list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as either non-negotiable or the preference of one camp. Then the part I most want — whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which. Then, briefly, which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard positions (consequentialist, absolutist, virtue-theoretic), and whether any requirement on your list has been smuggled in rather than found.

Be willing to push back rather than to comply politely. If "dirty hands" turns out to name no phenomenon distinct from a hard moral dilemma plus the regret that attends it, say so and tell me what is left of the task. If my purpose — a theory usable in advance by a real official — is incoherent, or renders the specification trivial or impossible, say that instead of producing a list that pretends otherwise. Mark your confidence, especially on the incompatibility claim, which I expect to be the most contestable thing on the page, and do not soften the verdict to be agreeable.
