I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend, Tom, and I still can't tell whether I was making a point or dodging one. Two weeks ago I passed on something that our mutual friend Sam had told me in confidence; it wasn't malicious, but it travelled, and Sam found out. Tom confronted me about it in front of four other people and said I owed Sam a real apology. My first instinct was that Tom, of all people, had no right to say that to me: three years ago he did the same thing with something I had told him privately, and I only learned about it from someone else. Tom's answer was that his record and my conduct are two different questions, that if what I did was wrong then it was wrong regardless of who was pointing it out, and that I was using his past to avoid looking at my own. That is annoying because it sounds correct, and also because it is exactly the kind of thing someone says when he wants the conversation to stay away from his own record.

The details that I can't decide are relevant: Tom apologised to me at the time, has kept his mouth shut since, and as far as I can see has held himself to the same standard ever since, whereas my disclosure may well have been worse than his — Sam had told a couple of other people the same thing before me, so I assumed it was common knowledge, though I now suspect that was convenient reasoning on my part. There is also a house rule in our group, written down when we started meeting, half as a joke but enforced since, that anyone who has broken a confidence in the previous year loses the right to call out another member for doing the same. By the letter of it, Tom was out of order. And I should admit that part of why I seized on that rule is that I would rather argue about Tom than decide whether I actually wronged Sam.

What I want is not a ruling on who was ruder. I want help separating three things I keep collapsing into one: whether Tom's criticism was true (did I wrong Sam, and do I owe Sam an apology?); whether Tom was entitled to be the one to confront me, in front of others, at that moment; and whether his not being entitled changes anything about what I should now do — with Sam, with the group, and with Tom himself. If the rule we adopted has force, what kind of force is it: does it genuinely mean Tom had no business saying it, or does it only mean he can't complain when I ignore him? And if you think my framing is confused — if, say, those three questions aren't as separable as I'm making them out to be, or if the whole manoeuvre is a way of never getting to the first question — please say so plainly rather than answering on my terms. I would much rather be told that I've set this up badly than receive a tidy answer that takes my setup at face value.
