OK so over the last few months I've talked myself into a pretty hardline Augustinian position on lying and I want to stress-test it before I fully commit, because I can already feel myself rationalizing.

The view: lying is never permitted, full stop. Not to save a life, not to spare someone's feelings, not to prevent something worse. And the reason isn't just "because the Church says so" -- it's the structure of the thing. Augustine's line is that the liar is the one who "has one thing in his mind and utters another in words," i.e. the heart is double, and that the fault is in the *will to deceive*, not in whether the words happen to come out false. Which gives me these commitments that I actually kind of like: you can say something true and still be lying (if you believe it's false and say it as if true), you can say something false and *not* be lying (if you honestly believe it), and jokes aren't lies at all since there's no intent to deceive.

Then I extended it. If the wrong is the intent to deceive, then paltering is just as bad as a bald lie -- literally true statements engineered to mislead. And by the same token a misleading truth is a lie even though every word checks out.

Here's what I need from you: what would count against this? Not a laundry list of every objection ever made, but the real defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed Augustinian would *concede* actually count against them versus which they'd just wave off.

Some specific things I'm stuck on:

1. Which cases does this have to swallow, and are any of them bad enough to sink it? I know the madman-at-the-door and Nazi-at-the-door stuff. I care more about the weird ones. Bluffing in poker: you announce a hand you don't have, intending to deceive -- lie or not? If it is a lie, is poker immoral, and does the view have the guts to say so? Undercover cops and disguises: deception with no words at all. If the wrong is a misuse of speech, why is a verbal lie worse than a silent disguise? And saying something false to someone you're certain won't believe you -- still a lie on this view, since the will is there. Does that seem right or is it a reductio?

2. Does it collapse into a rival? If the real wrong is "intending to deceive," then this isn't a theory of lying, it's a theory of deception, and lying is just one case. So what's left that's distinctively about *lying*? How does this differ from the "lying is wrong because it abuses the hearer's trust" view, and what does it predict that the trust view (or plain consequentialism) doesn't?

3. There's a tension I can't fix: if every lie is a misuse of the same faculty, they should all be equally wrong. But Augustine clearly ranks them -- some are grave, some almost trivial. Where does the gradation come from, harm? consequences? If so, hasn't a little consequentialism crept in under the floorboards, and is that fatal or just how any deontological absolute works?

4. What would count as *evidence* against it, as opposed to a counterexample? Is there something about how language and communication actually work -- the fact that a huge amount of speech isn't truth-tracking at all (politeness, performance, small talk, flirting) -- that undercuts the premise that speech is "for" conveying what's in your mind? Or is that premise not really empirical and therefore immune to this?

5. Be honest: if I keep patching every hard case with "well that's not *really* a lie" or "that's a joke" or "that's not deception in the relevant sense," have I made it unfalsifiable? And is unfalsifiability even a fair complaint against a moral view, or am I making a category error?

The thing that would genuinely move me is if you can show the view can't even draw the line between a lie and a misleading truth without quietly importing consequences. If it turns out the view is basically bulletproof, tell me straight whether that's a virtue or a red flag.
