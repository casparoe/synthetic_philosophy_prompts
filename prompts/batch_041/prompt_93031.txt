I want to rehearse an objection against the factored-cognition picture of deliberation — the idea, associated with work on iterated amplification and neighboring alignment agendas, that a hard deliberative task can be handled by decomposing it into many small, largely independent subtasks whose outputs are then recombined — and I want to argue against someone who actually holds the view rather than against a caricature. Please play a committed defender: someone who treats factored cognition not merely as an engineering strategy but as a defensible idealization of what good deliberation is, who knows the standard replies, who will concede a point when it is genuinely conceded, and who will not retreat into "it is only an idealization" as a blanket shield. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each turn to a short numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no preamble or summary, and in each point indicate whether it is something the view's sympathizers broadly agree on or something that is itself contested among them. My opening: the decomposition is supposed to be where the hard work of deliberation gets done, but identifying which subtasks a question decomposes into just is the hard work — deciding that a messy practical question is really a question about expected consequences plus a separate question about fairness is already the substantive deliberative achievement, and it is precisely the move your picture never explains, since every bounded step you permit is downstream of it. So the idealization appears to relocate the very thing it was meant to model. How does the defender answer that without simply positing an oracle for task-identification?
