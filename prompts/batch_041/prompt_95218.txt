I run a 90-minute session for sixteen graduate students in a philosophy of mind and applied ethics seminar, and we keep circling the insect sentience question without getting anywhere. Some of the group think the probability that insects are sentient is low enough that their interests can be set aside; others think the scale argument settles the matter. I want an in-class activity that makes the group feel the force of the scale argument from the inside, before anyone names it, and that does not depend on any particular empirical estimate or on a debate about whether insects really are sentient. By the scale argument I mean the thought that a very low credence that a single insect is sentient can still generate an enormous expected amount of morally relevant experience once it is multiplied by the number of insects affected; I want participants to run into the difference between a low probability and a low expected moral weight, and to see why the second does not follow from the first when the population is large enough. The activity should also expose the hidden steps that make the argument contestable: the move from credence in sentience to credence in morally relevant sentience; the move from per-individual expected value to aggregate expected value; the assumption that welfare units are comparable across taxa; the opportunity cost of diverting resources from clear cases; and the threshold problem, where if sentience is all-or-nothing rather than a matter of degree, a small change in credence about the threshold can swamp the arithmetic.

Set it in an ordinary seminar room with sixteen participants and ninety minutes, no real money, and only cheap materials such as index cards, tokens, and a whiteboard. Assume the participants are intelligent but not specialists in decision theory; if the activity needs a short primer on expected value, include one, but do not let the primer state the scale argument or name the conclusion. You may use toy numbers to make the arithmetic transparent—for instance, a policy affecting one trillion insects with an initial group credence of one in ten thousand that a single insect is sentient, compared with a policy affecting one million chickens with a credence of one. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not name philosophers or theories; the activity should be intelligible to someone who has never read the literature. The rules, roles, and payoffs should arrange for participants to commit to a low credence, allocate moral resources on that basis, and then discover what their own credence implies in aggregate; the idea should show up in what happens, not in what the facilitator says afterwards. Include a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the structure of the scale argument, says what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and is frank about which feature of the scale argument the activity cannot reproduce—for example, it cannot settle whether insects are sentient, and it may make the underlying credences look more precise than they are.
