The guest is someone I've had on before who defends a fairly unusual line on precommitment, and I want to get properly into the weeds with her rather than let her do the standard tour. Her view, roughly: Ulysses contracts and constitutional entrenchment are not the same kind of thing at all, and almost everyone who writes on precomitment conflates them. The Ulysses case, she says, is one agent binding her own future self; a constitution is a rule binding other people. And her claim is that you can't get the second out of the first — the self-interest story that makes the rope round the mast rational does no work once the person tied up is somebody else.

She also has a distinction she keeps making, between entrenchment that is "self-limiting" and entrenchment that isn't. A rule that raises the cost of changing rules is legitimate, on her account, only if it entrenches the very procedure by which it can itself be revised, so that it can't lock the door behind itself. Everything else she calls a ratchet, and she treats ratchets as illegitimate more or less across the board.

One more thing she does that I want to press on. Whenever anyone brings up Odysseus's crew, she says the crew isn't really a case of precommitment at all, because the crew aren't binding themselves to anything — they're the machinery that does the binding, and the wax in their ears stops them hearing the sirens whether they like it or not. But it seems to me the crew is doing all the enforcement work, and every real constitution needs its crew as well: police, courts, officials who are themselves bound by rules they never individually agreed to.
