I host a reading group that has spent the better part of a year on theories of well-being, and I want to build a diagnostic instrument rather than another summary of the usual taxonomy. Please write a decision tree that leads a participant from a starting commitment to a named theory of well-being, where every branch node is phrased as a question someone could answer without already knowing which label it leads to.

The tree should be grown from the logical space, not assembled from a list of names. Work out first which distinctions can vary independently, then locate the actual positions in that space, then say what the unoccupied combinations are and why nobody is there: is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored? Where two leaves turn out to be indistinguishable by any question a person could answer, flag the pair rather than paper over it.

I want the experience machine to be the entry point, but as a family of cases rather than the one case everyone has already rehearsed. For that I propose a decomposition you should build on. Call the thing a reality requirement, and distinguish three strengths it can take: veridicality, where what you experience must be true of some world; causal contact, where the world must actually be doing something to you and not merely a simulation feeding you; and authorship, where the outcomes you seem to bring about must really be yours to bring about. These can come apart, and most of the interesting positions differ precisely in which strength they demand, or in demanding none.

Please include at least these cases, which I have invented for the instrument and which you should not treat as standing for anything in the literature:

- The dial: a machine whose realism is adjustable in one dimension only, from vivid-but-false up to experiences that are true of the actual world, with causal contact held fixed at zero throughout.
- The understudy: a life in which everything you seem to do is really done by a perfect double acting on your behalf, while your stream of experience is accurate, uninterrupted, and exactly what you would have had if you had done those things yourself.
- The ledger: at the end of an ordinary life you are shown a complete and accurate record of what actually happened to and around you; the record reveals that your causal influence on events was nil, though every experience you had was veridical.

Use these to force apart views that usually get lumped together as simply wanting contact with reality.

I also want a second axis, because I think the first one is where most people stop and it is not where the real disagreements are. The second axis is the temporal and aggregative structure of well-being. Distinguish momentary from whole-life assessment, and distinguish additive from holistic aggregation, so that a view can hold that a life is better than the sum of its moments (or worse) without that being an afterthought. Some leaves on the first axis combine with several options on the second, and I want the tree to show which combinations are actually defended and which are merely consistent.

Required leaves, at minimum: experiential hedonism; desire satisfactionism in both an internalist and an externalist form; an objective list theory that includes contact with reality as one of its items; a perfectionist view; a hybrid that requires experience plus causal contact but not authorship; an authorship view; and the deflationary option on which there is no determinate fact about which of these a person's well-being consists in. If you think a required leaf is best split, split it and say why; if you think two of them cannot be separated by any question a person could answer, say so.

Keep it to roughly ten to twelve branch nodes and no more than a dozen leaves. Each leaf should carry the position name and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. The audience is a rationality meetup: people who have read some philosophy but not much, who will answer honestly about cases and dishonestly about labels if you let them. So no node may ask anything like whether pleasure is the only intrinsic good, or whether desire satisfaction is internalist; every node has to be a concrete case or a concrete verdict, phrased so that the participant discovers their position by answering rather than by recognizing it. Write it in an indented outline, with the splitting question at each branch and the unoccupied and inseparable cases discussed in a closing section.
