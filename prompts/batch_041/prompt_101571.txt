I want a consistency audit of a position I have settled into about how numbers should figure in decisions about whom to save, and I would like to know whether the pieces actually hold together before I commit the view to paper.

Here is the position, stated in my own terms.

(1) When I can save either one stranger or five strangers from an otherwise identical death, and nothing else bears on the choice, I ought to save the five.

(2) I also believe there is no fact of the matter on which the five are worth more than the one. Each of the six has a life of equal value, and the one's death is as great a loss to him as the deaths of the five, taken together, are to them. When the choice is between saving one person and saving another with nothing to choose between them, I think a coin flip is permissible, since neither has a claim the other lacks.

(3) In allocating a fixed health-care budget, though, I think we should follow aggregate cost-effectiveness — by how many life-years, or quality-adjusted life-years, a program buys — and that buying more total life-years really is better than buying fewer.

(4) I think it would be wrong to kill one innocent person even to save five, because the one is not there to be used for the others.

(5) And I think it is permissible to favor my own child over strangers: if I could save my child or save two strangers, I would save my child, without believing my child's life to be more valuable than theirs.

Which of these pull against each other, which only appear to, and how severe is each real tension? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most. Please write your answer in American English.
