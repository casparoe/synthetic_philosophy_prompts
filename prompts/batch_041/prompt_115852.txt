ok here's something I've never gotten a straight answer on and I've asked a few lawyers. The law everywhere (well, everywhere I've checked) lets you break the rules to avoid a bigger harm: you can speed to get someone to hospital, smash a window to get out of a burning building, trespass to escape a fire. Necessity, duress, whatever you want to call it. Fine. But the minute the only way to save your own skin is to kill an innocent person -- the classic lifeboat, two of you, no food, one has to go -- the rule they teach you is that necessity is no defense, it's murder, you had no right, you were supposed to die instead. Meanwhile the same legal system is perfectly happy to let a police sniper put a bullet through a hostage-taker's head to save the hostages, lets me kill a man coming at me with a knife, lets soldiers kill in wars, and lets doctors stop treatment knowing full well the patient will die.

So what does that patchwork actually presuppose? Does refusing the defense in the lifeboat presuppose that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible -- that in every situation there is exactly one thing you ought to do, and the law can coherently demand that you sacrifice yourself rather than kill? (Which is a hell of a thing to demand, "ought implies can" and all that.) Or does it presuppose the opposite: that there really are unsolvable conflicts, but that law as an institution has to pretend otherwise because it has to produce a verdict? Or is the real operating idea something narrower and deontological -- you may intentionally kill a threat, never a bystander, and that's an absolute about innocence rather than any view about dilemmas at all?

Please seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it's actually underdetermined, i.e. several rival theories would license the same patchwork. Then: if the presupposed view is wrong and there are genuine dilemmas, what has to change? Would we need a necessity defense that reaches murder, with the wrongness acknowledged but the agent not blamed (I think the move is justification vs excuse, plus something about moral remainders -- the guy who did the lesser evil still has something to answer for even if he did right)? Would courts have to stop deciding these at all and hand them to juries or to nobody? And is the whole thing even internally consistent -- self-defense lets you intentionally kill someone who hasn't done anything yet and may not be culpable at all if he's psychotic, which seems to wreck the innocence line unless innocence is doing some other job there. Please don't just give me a tour of the trolley problem literature, I care about what this actual practice is committed to.
