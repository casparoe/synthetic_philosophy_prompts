I would like a poem to close a graduate seminar on the hole argument: the problem that arises when you treat the points of spacetime as genuine individuals and then notice that a transformation which does nothing at all outside a certain region and shuffles points around within it carries a model of general relativity to a different model — one that agrees with the first on everything outside that region. The form should do the argument's work rather than merely illustrate it, and the poem must not use the vocabulary of the debate: no "manifold," no "diffeomorphism," no "substantivalism," no "Leibniz equivalence." It may keep the word "hole" in its ordinary sense, as a place in a landscape.

Write exactly five numbered sections, in blank verse, no more than six lines each. The reply should consist of those five sections and nothing else — no title, no preface, no note afterward.

Section 1 introduces two neighbouring places and the pattern spread across them, and makes plain that each place already has its own identity and its own load of properties, over and above the pattern — the assumption that sets the problem going. Sections 2 and 4 tell the same stretch of history twice, with the two places exchanged: whatever section 2 says of the first, section 4 says of the second, and neither section may contain any asymmetry — no privileged vantage, no detail that fits only one of the two arrangements — that would let a reader tell which telling is the original and which the permutation. Section 3 is the intervention itself: doing nothing whatsoever outside the hole, every point beyond it keeping its name and its load, and busy only within, rearranging the points while carrying the pattern along so that the pattern comes out untouched. Section 5 sets the two verdicts side by side and refuses to settle between them: one voice holds that two distinct possibilities now sit where there was one, so that the account of the world fails to say which of them is actual; the other holds that there was only ever one story, told twice, and that what the argument exposes is not indeterminism in the world but a mistake about what those places were ever supposed to be.

The line "nothing has changed" must occur verbatim at least twice: once where it is plainly true, because nothing in the pattern has changed, and once where it is the very thing at issue, because whether the world itself has changed is precisely the question. Let section 5 end in a way that leaves both senses of that line available to the reader. Someone who has taught the argument should find every step of it in the poem; someone who has not should still find a poem.
