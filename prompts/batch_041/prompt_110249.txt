I am a philosopher of religion beginning a three-year research fellowship, and I want to spend it on the open problems in the Molinist account of providence that actually matter, rather than on the ones that merely attract attention. Please give me a defended agenda.

Some background, so that we are talking about the same thing. Molinism distinguishes God's natural knowledge of necessary truths, His free knowledge of what He has chosen to create and decree, and a middle knowledge between them: God's knowledge of the counterfactuals of freedom — of what each possible free creature would freely choose in each set of circumstances in which it might be placed. Because the choice is free, its content is not settled by God's decree; because the knowledge is knowledge, the counterfactual is true prior to the decree. The standard difficulties are four: first, the grounding objection, that nothing makes a counterfactual of freedom true if the creature in question never in fact chooses; second, the objection that if God infallibly knows what you would do in circumstances C, then in C you could not do otherwise, and so the choice is not free; third, the charge that middle knowledge cannot be reconciled with divine simplicity, immutability or atemporality; and fourth, the soteriological argument, that if God could have placed every person in circumstances in which they freely accept Him, it is hard to see why He has not.

I would like the agenda organised around a hypothesis of my own, which I ask you to take seriously and then press hard against. The hypothesis is that the grounding objection and the objection from inability to do otherwise are less fundamental than they appear, and that the question on which everything turns is prior to both: whether a would-counterfactual about a libertarian free choice can be true at all. If the free choice is genuinely undetermined by its circumstances, then perhaps the most that can be true is a might-counterfactual — that the creature might freely do A in C — and middle knowledge as normally stated would collapse into a weaker claim about what God knows to be possible. On this view the real work lies in the semantics of conditionals applied to free antecedents rather than in theology, and the grounding objection is a symptom rather than the disease. I may be wrong about this; if so, I want to know precisely where the argument fails.
