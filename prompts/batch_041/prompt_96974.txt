Bit of a weird ask, but bear with me. I somehow got roped onto the ethics panel at work — I do ops, I'm definately not a philosopher, it's a long story — and every meeting goes the same way. Someone lays out a pile of facts (incident numbers, survey results, whatever) and then says some version of 'so we ought to do X'. I always want to say: you can't get an ought out of an is. And then someone says 'sure you can, watch this' and pulls out something about promises, or about a heart and it's job of pumping blood, or about how the rules of chess mean you shouldn't walk your king into check, and everyone nods and moves on and I sit there feeling like either I'm wrong or they cheated.

So I'd like you to settle it with an actual case rather than a general discussion. Build me one scenario — mundane, the kind of thing that could happen in an office, a hospital or a kitchen, no sci-fi, no gods, no aliens, one paragraph max — in which a genuine ought is validly derived from purely descriptive premises. Constraints, because otherwise it's too easy:

- No premise may contain a moral or evaluative word. Test: if you'd have to explain the word to a child using good, bad, right, wrong or should, it's out.
- The conclusion has to be a real ought — not the instrumental kind (if you want to be healthy you should exercise) and not a prediction about what people will disapprove of. Something unconditional, about what someone actually has reason to do.
- Every premise gets stated. No hidden bridge premises, no 'and obviously'.

Then do the work: write the derivation out formally, tell me which single term is carrying the norm, and what happens if I swap that term for a purely descriptive synonym — does the inference survive? Tell me which feature of the case is load-bearing and what happens if you take it away. Then give me the strongest reply from someone who thinks the gap is real: what do they say the case actually shows?

And one last thing I keep confusing myself about, keep it short: is the is-ought gap the same claim as the naturalistic fallacy, or a different one? If it's different, what does the difference actually turn on?

The reason I care is I want to know whether my objection is a rule of logic or just a quirk of how our vocabulary works — that's what the case is supposed to decide, so don't fudge it. And no reading list please, just the argument.
