I am a retired geophysicist — thirty-odd years spent fitting hazard functions to earthquake catalogues — who has spent the last several years sitting in on graduate seminars in philosophy. A colleague in the philosophy department and I are co-teaching a graduate seminar on decision theory and catastrophic risk, and I have been asked to contribute the take-home examination question for the session on nuclear deterrence and brinkmanship. I would like you to draft that question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

Some background on what I want the question to do. I have grown impatient with the way this topic is usually taught, which invites students to recite a handful of well-worn paradoxes about credibility and commitment without ever having to make the reasoning work. So I have built a small toy model of my own for them to reason inside. The question must be self-contained with respect to it, and students will not be permitted to consult anything, so nothing may depend on their having read any particular literature. Please do not ask for citations, reading lists, or attributions: the students cannot look anything up, and I do not want them guessing at references.

The model, which I have been calling the shared-hazard crisis, runs roughly as follows. Two states are locked in a crisis over a stake they both value. Each chooses an escalation posture — a level of alert, readiness, delegation of launch authority, forward deployment — and I write these as h(A) and h(B). The postures are not weapons; they are hazards. So long as neither state backs down, a general war occurs as the first event of a Poisson process with rate h(A) + h(B); if it occurs, each state suffers its own cost of war, which need not be symmetric. Backing down forfeits the stake to the other side; holding on costs nothing directly except through the accumulating hazard; and there is a common discount rate, so a long crisis is worth less than a short one. The structural features I care about are these: the hazard is shared, so each state's posture raises the risk faced by both, while the benefit of escalation — the other side's increased willingness to back down — accrues to the escalating state alone; and the postures are chosen simultaneously, with no binding commitment available.

I want the question built around three tasks, though you should feel free to reorganise or add a fourth part if it improves the discrimination of the examination.

First, reconstruct within the model the mechanism by which a partly uncontrolled hazard makes a coercive threat work: explain why a state that could not credibly intend to start a war might nevertheless gain leverage by raising h. I want the students to show that the leverage comes from the shared hazard rather than from any intention, and to say what goes wrong with the argument if the hazard can be perfectly controlled by whoever raises it.

Second, establish the externality — each state's posture imposes a risk on the other that it does not internalise — and from this derive the condition, in terms of the slopes of the two best-response maps, under which a unilateral reduction in one state's posture reduces total expected risk, and the condition under which it does not. I want them to see that the sign of the answer is not settled by the concept of deterrence but by a magnitude that has to be argued for, and to say what evidence would bear on it.

Third, take seriously and then assess a temptation of my own. Writing the probability of war as the product of a crisis-onset rate and a conditional escalation probability, I keep wanting to assert that total risk is conserved — that measures reducing the frequency of crises are paid for by more dangerous crises, leaving the product roughly fixed. I want the students to say whether anything in the model makes that a theorem, what additional assumptions would be needed to make it one, and what a falsifiable version of the claim would look like. Part of what I am after is the distinction between a conceptual claim and an empirical regularity, which I am still learning to draw.

Please write the examination question as it would appear on the paper (roughly 150–250 words, with clearly numbered parts), then a model answer of about 700–800 words, then a marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want five or six must-hit points, each with a mark value summing to 30, plus a one-line note on how a typical candidate gets each one wrong — the sort of error that should cost marks rather than merely being inelegant. The students are a mixed group of philosophers and security-studies people, some comfortable with a little algebra and some not, so the question should be answerable in prose but should reward a candidate who can set the problem up formally. The examination is ninety minutes, closed book, with no citations expected or wanted.
