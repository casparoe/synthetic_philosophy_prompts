Back in the mid-60s Joseph Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA at MIT, the program with the DOCTOR script that mirrors your own sentences back at you as questions the way a Rogerian therapist would, and the part of the story I keep coming back to is that his own secretary — who had watched him use it and knew exactly what it was — asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it on her own. That shook him, and he wrote later that he hadn't realised how little exposure to a fairly simple program it took to get quite normal people into a kind of delusion about it.

Treat that episode as a case study for behavioral criteria for mind — the general idea that what a system does is what you go on when deciding whether there's a mind behind it, with Turing's immitation game as the sharpest version of the idea. Not a history of ELIZA and not a survey of the standard criticisms of the Turing test. What I want first is the mapping, done properly: who the interrogator is, what they're actually being asked to judge, what counts as the machine's behaviour in this case, and what's absent from the setup that Turing's version has — the interrogator is trying to catch the machine out, there's a clock running, and underneath the whole thing sits the older party game with a man and a woman where one of the two is supposed to be deceptive. Then run it the other way and tell me what the case does to the criterion. Weizenbaum thought the attribution was a mistake, obviously. But on the stronger reading of a behavioral criterion — the one where the outward performance isn't merely evidence of a mind but part of what we mean by attributing one — his secretary applied the criterion correctly and his discomfort is just a mechanism-snobbery that the criterion was built to make irrelevant. Is that right? Does ELIZA vindicate the criterion, refute it, or show that it was never doing what its friends and its critics both assumed? And say where the thing goes quiet: the point of the game is that you never have to look at the machinery, so what does the case look like once the machinery is the most salient fact in the room?

Worth folding in briefly, because it seems to be the same shape of problem: the 2014 episode where a bot posing as a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy convinced about a third of the judges and was announced as the first machine to pass, with Turing's forecast — that an average interrogator would have no better than a 70% chance of getting it right after five minutes — being converted after the fact into a 30% pass mark. Is that the same failure as ELIZA or a different one, and does a criterion that gets met or not depending on how charitable and how sharp the judges happen to be tell you anything about the machine at all? Finish by saying what a defender of behavioral criteria should give up and what they should hold on to.
