So I'm taking this course on famine and food politics — it's cross-listed between the development econ people and the political theory people, which is either the best or the worst thing about my semester, still deciding — and last week's seminar blew up over the claim that famines basically don't happen in democracies, or at least not in ones with a free press and real elections. One guy in my section kept insisting that famines are never natural disasters, they're political crimes, full stop, and the drought or the blight is just the occasion, the excuse. Another girl pushed back pretty hard and said that's a slogan that's quietly become unfalsifiable, that sometimes the harvest genuinely does collapse and no amount of political will conjures calories out of thin air, and that the whole framing writes off chronic hunger, which definately kills way more people than the acute famines that get the documentaries. The bell cut them off and I've been chewing on it ever since.

Can you write me a dialogue between two people who take those two sides — and please don't make either one a strawman, I actually want to see which one has the better of it. The first should be the entitlement-and-accountability type: famines are about who can command food rather than how much food physically exists, the natural shock is a trigger at most, and what really decides things is whether the government and the press have any incentive to react. The second should be the skeptic: that the entitlement story can re-describe literally any outcome as a failure of entitlement and so risks becoming unfalsifiable; that some real famines involved the actual destruction of the food itself, not just people's claims on it; that the no-famines-in-democracies pattern is a thin correlation confounded by wealth, roads and urbanization and only holds on a narrow definition of famine; and that a country can hold elections, have a noisy press, and still lose huge numbers of people to slow malnutrition and quiet excess mortality. I want them to actually respond to each other's points rather than alternate monologues — concede where the other lands something, press where they don't, and ideally end up somewhere neither of them started.

To stop it from turning into a summary of stuff everyone already knows, have them work through a case I'm making up, and use these details: a small middle-income country, twenty years of competitive elections, a handful of papers and a lively online press but the outlets are owned by two urban families, a marginalized minority concentrated in a dry northern province that swings almost no seats, a three-year drought that genuinely cut the northern grain harvest by around half, a government export ban meant to keep city bread prices down (which crashed farmgate prices up north and made farmers stop planting), a fuel-subsidy cut demanded by the country's lenders, and a trucking cartel that controls the only road south. Acute deaths spike in the north. I'd like the entitlement person to explain why this still isn't a counterexample — the grain was sitting in the south, the ban and the cartel did it, and the elections prove nothing because the minority isn't a constituency anyone needs — and the skeptic to say the harvest really did halve and that the story is now doing unfalsifiable work. Toward the end I want them to disagree about what would actually settle it: what would count as a real counterexample for each of them, and whether famine should be defined by excess mortality, by some threshold, or by something more like a social crisis of destitution where people sell their tools and livestock and split up their households. If either of them brings up the Irish blight or Bengal or the Great Leap Forward, I'd much rather they argue about what those cases show than swap statistics, because I know I'll get the numbers wrong. No footnotes or reading list, just the argument — maybe 2-3 pages, whatever the conversation needs.
