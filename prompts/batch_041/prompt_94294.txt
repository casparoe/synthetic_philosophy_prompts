I spent about thirty-five years as a vision scientist — psychophysics, contrast and motion, nothing you'd have heard of — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy of mind, which has turned out to be alot more fun than I expected. Anyway I've gotten stuck on the timing of experience, and I've ended up convinced of something I'd like you to try and take apart.

The thing I'm convinced of is this. Take the color phi phenomenon: you flash a dot in one place, then a dot of a different colour in another place a fraction of a second later, and people report a single dot that travels from the first spot to the second and changes colour partway across. So the experience seems to contain the colour of the second dot before the second dot has even been shown. There are roughly two ways to tell that story. The Orwellian one says you really did experience things in the wrong order and the brain then goes back and edits the memory, so you'd swear the order was fine. The Stalinesque one says nothing was ever experienced in the wrong order — the brain waits, constructs the whole travelling-and-changing-dot episode after the fact, and hands that to consciousness as a finished product, so nothing was ever falsified because there was never a record in the first place. I think the Stalinesque story is right, and I think the Orwellian one is a worse theory for the same data: it posits an extra stage (a genuine experience, then an edit of it) that does no explanatory work. This is roughly Dennett's line as I understand it, and I've swallowed it.

So: what would actually count against it? Treat this as a position to be attacked, not one to be explained back to me. I want the observations, experiments, arguments and intuitions that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd really be, and I want you to keep the fatal ones apart from the merely awkward ones. Be honest about which of these a committed Stalinesque person would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only the Orwellians would throw at them. And tell me straight whether the position risks anything at all, or whether it's been quietly insulated into unfalsifiability by qualification — and if it has, whether that's a real problem for a view of this kind or just the price of doing business in this area.

Two things I want you to fold in. First, I have a half-formed idea I haven't seen written up anywhere, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than pat me on the head: maybe there are two separate revisions happening, one of what was there (colour, position) and one of when it was there (order, timing), and the two can come apart — so you could get a case where the content is untouched but the temporal label gets rewritten, or the other way round. If that's even coherent, it doesn't map onto the Orwellian/Stalinesque split, which lumps the whole thing into a single edit. Is that a genuine third option, or does it just collapse back into one of the two? And what would I have to see in a lab to tell the difference, given that I can just as easily imagine this idea being unfalsifiable as the view it's meant to challenge?

Second, tell me what you'd expect to be the strongest discriminator and why — I'll say up front that I'd be most moved by something from a patient population, or by a case where the two accounts genuinely make different predictions about what a person can report or do, and least moved by yet another thought experiment, because I've had my fill of those. If you think that preference of mine is itself a mistake, say so.
