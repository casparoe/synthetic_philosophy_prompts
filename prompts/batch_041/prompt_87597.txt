Consider the position that Turing's treatment of extrasensory perception in his 1950 paper on the imitation game is not an eccentric aside but a load-bearing concession: it reveals that he took the imitation game to be an empirical criterion whose validity could be undermined by discoveries about the world—specifically, that if telepathy is real, an interrogator might identify a machine by means other than its verbal performance, so success in the game would not establish thinking. On this reading, Turing's operationalism is conditional and revisable rather than a definition of thinking. What would count against this reading? Sort the evidence and arguments by how decisive they would be, distinguishing what would merely make the reading awkward from what would refute it. Which parts of the reading would its defenders themselves concede as problems? And does this interpretation make Turing's position unfalsifiable, or does it expose it to empirical defeat in a way that is a strength? I am especially interested in whether the ESP discussion should be read as a serious methodological worry or as a rhetorical device to disarm a rival objection.
