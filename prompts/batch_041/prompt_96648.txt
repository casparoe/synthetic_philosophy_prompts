I teach a small upper-level seminar on mental representation, mostly senior philosophy majors with two cognitive science students who keep the rest honest, and I've just finished marking a midterm that has left me annoyed at my own question-writing. I asked them to explain how teleosemantics is supposed to naturalize content, and every answer was the same recitation: proper functions, consumer systems, misrepresentation is possible because functions can fail. Not one of them had ever considered whether the machinery actually delivers a determinate content in a case where the answer matters, which is the only place the view earns its keep. So for the final I want a single essay question, worth 25 marks and answerable in ninety minutes, that can only be passed by someone who has genuinely worked through the indeterminacy problem — the one where the theory's own resources let you say with equal textual support that a state represents the distal cause, the proximal stimulus, or whatever the organism needs. The question has to be unpassable by asserting that teleosemantics is the most naturalistic option on offer, or by appealing to what the creature 'really' represents; those are the moves I want to cost marks.

I'd like the question built on a case of my own, which I'll give you here because none of them will have seen it and I don't want them pattern-matching onto the frog. Call the creature a glimmerfish: it survives by snapping at anything small, bright and drifting in its visual field, and in its native waters such things are almost always edible plankton. The snapping mechanism is triggered by a simple detector whose firing is caused by moving highlights and which in turn causes the snap, and this arrangement is what keeps the fish fed. It also fires at sunlight glinting off the surface and at scraps of plastic, which the fish cannot digest. Now, the essay question should force the candidate to do four things: assign a content to the detector state on teleosemantic grounds and defend the assignment; show that at least two of the standard disambiguating strategies they will reach for — maximum specificity, the distal-most reading, the consumer's-need reading, the reading that fixes content by what the organism's survival explanation actually cites — come apart on this case rather than converging; say whether that shows the account fails to naturalize content or only fails to settle it, and why that distinction is not a dodge; and finally say what the most defensible concession is for someone who wants to keep the programme. I also want the plastic-snapping worked in, since the theory needs misrepresentation to be possible and it is genuinely unclear whether the glimmerfish misrepresents anything at all here.

What I need back is the exam question itself, phrased the way I'd hand it to students, then a model answer of at most 650 words, then a marking scheme of five or six named must-hit moves with marks attached summing to 25, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong — I want the scheme to separate a student who actually uses the indeterminacy to do philosophical work from one who merely names it, and a separate line for the answer that treats the whole problem as a terminological quibble. Keep the entire deliverable — question, model answer, and scheme together — under 1,300 words; I'm pasting it into a shared document and my co-instructor will not read past two pages.
