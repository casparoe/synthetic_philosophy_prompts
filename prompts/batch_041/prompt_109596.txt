I host a small podcast on decision theory and practical rationality, and next week's guest is a philosopher — an invented composite rather than a real person — who has spent years defending the claim that Pascal's-mugging cases are a genuine reductio of expected value maximization rather than a curiosity to be dissolved. Her line is that the theory rests on a false premise: that any nonzero probability, however minute, can be multiplied by any finite value, however vast, and still yield a determinate quantity that a rational agent is bound to maximize. The mugger — a stranger who offers an astronomically large reward, or threatens an astronomically bad punishment, on a probability far below anything we would ordinarily credit — is supposed to expose that premise, and she holds that the repair must be either a floor on the probabilities we are permitted to take seriously or a ceiling on the values we are permitted to trade against them. She is fluent, has heard the standard objections, and has a stock reply to most of them: any rule that refuses the mugging also refuses ordinary long-shot prudence, and she maintains that this is a cost her opponents have never properly priced. I do not want the episode to be forty minutes of her restating the view.

Act as an experienced interviewer and a philosopher of decision theory. Begin with a short private brainstorm — a list of the pressure points where this position is most vulnerable and where it is strongest, not part of the final answer — and then draft eight to ten interview questions that turn on the actual content of the view rather than on its reception. Each question should be answerable in a few minutes on air rather than being a disguised essay prompt, and each should come with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I am not left improvising when she reaches for the stock reply. I need at least one question distinguishing the mugging from Pascal's wager proper — whether the wager shares the flaw or escapes it, and what exactly the disanalogy is supposed to be; at least one on whether the right repair is a probability threshold, a bounded utility function, or an outright restriction of expected value reasoning to cases with well-behaved distributions, with her asked to say which of the three she would abandon first and why the others are worse; and at least one on the principle that seems to be doing the real work — that for any finite good there is some sufficiently small probability of a sufficiently large good such that taking the trade is required — asking whether she thinks that principle is independently plausible or merely an unnoticed consequence of expected value maximization that only became visible once the mugger arrived. Sequence the questions so that they build, ending with one that is hardest to deflect: ideally a question that forces her to say what would count as evidence that the mugging indicts her preferred repair rather than the theory she rejects, or what she would have to observe about actual deliberating agents to conclude that her fix is not merely a stipulation. Close with a question about what would change her mind. Please give a one-line note after each question on what it is designed to expose, and do not ask her for reading recommendations or citations — I want the substance, not a syllabus — nor spend any questions on how she came to the topic.
