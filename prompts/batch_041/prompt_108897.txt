So my two housemates, Dev and Alina, got into a thing at dinner last night about our friend Marcus, who's trying to decide between staying at his current job and joining a three-person startup. I definately stayed out of it, because every time I opened my mouth I made it worse, but I've been chewing on it since and I still can't tell who was right. Heres roughly how it went.

Dev: Marcus should stay where he is. Whatever he thinks he knows about this particular startup, the base rate is brutal — most seed-stage companies die, and everyone who joins one thinks theirs is the exception. He's built a whole story out of the details he happens to have and he's ignoring the one number that wasn't generated by his own hopes. The outside view is the only unbiased thing in the room.

Alina: The outside view isn't a number, it's a number computed over a reference class, and you don't get to pick the class without using exactly the inside information you're telling him to ignore. 'Founder at a seed-stage company' gives you one rate. 'Founder who was employee number four at two startups that both exited, and whose co-founder he's worked with for six years' gives you a completely different one. There's no class-free base rate floating around out there. So the outside view isn't upstream of his judgment, it's downstream of it.

Dev: Fine — then you fix the class by features that are knowable in advance and don't depend on how compelling his story sounds. Otherwise 'the outside view' is just a rubber stamp: he picks whatever class makes the number look good and then calls it objectivity. If the class can be gerrymandered, the outside view has no corrective force at all, and all you've done is hand him a way to dress up his gut feeling in statistics.

Alina: Two problems with that. First, if he actually takes your advice and starts correcting for the planning fallacy, he's no longer in the reference class the base rate was computed over, because that class is mostly people who didn't do that. The more seriously he takes the outside view, the less the number is about him. Second, 'be modest about your own case' is itself a judgment about his own case. You can't be modest about everything at once — somebody has to decide which of his beliefs to discount, and that somebody is him, using his own head.

Dev: The first point can't be right, or nobody would ever be overconfident after reading about overconfidence, and they obviously still are. So the correction is real but small, and the way you know it's small is — a base rate. Your regress bottoms out in a base rate either way. And the second point just confuses being modest with having no opinion. Being modest means holding the estimate as an estimate — 'this could easily be wrong' — not throwing it out and shrugging. Honestly the whole frame is off anyway: the outside view tells him the odds, but he isn't asking for a forecast, he's asking what to do, and if the payoff is skewed enough then a small chance of a huge outcome can still be the right bet. A base rate isn't a decision.

Alina: That last part is the only bit I agree with, and it cuts my way too — the second you start weighting outcomes you're back inside the inside view, because 'how much does he care about the money versus the years' isn't in anybody's base rate.

That's about where it ended, both of them acting like they'd won.

Can you go through it turn by turn and rate each move, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating? I don't really need an overall winner so much as a map of where each of them is solid, where each is overreaching, and what both of them are missing about how the outside view is actually supposed to work. Assume they're both smart and neither is arguing in bad faith.
