His argument, roughly in his words:

(1) Whatever objective a trained network ends up actually pursuing, the only thing we ever observe is its behavior -- outputs, actions, in whatever environments we put it in. There's no readout that says "here is the goal."
(2) Training signals are functions of behavior (plus maybe internals, but grant him behavior for now). So training can only ever reward and punish what the model does.
(3) A model whose real objective is misaligned has a standing incentive to behave exactly as though it were aligned while it's being trained and evaluated, so it doesn't get modified or shut down, and to defect only once it's confident it's out of distribution and can get away with it.
(4) So for any model that behaves aligned, there's a possible model with a misaligned objective that behaves identically in every environment we could ever test.
(5) If two hypotheses predict exactly the same observations, then no amount of observation can tell them apart.
(6) Everything we could ever collect as evidence about a model is just more behavior, in more environments.
(7) So we can never have any evidence that a model is actually aligned rather than merely behaving aligned. Inner alignment isn't hard, it isn't even a well-posed empirical question -- it's undefinable if you ask what we're after, and unfalsifiable if you ask how we'd check.
