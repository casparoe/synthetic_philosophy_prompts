Here's the tangle. Present bias is standardly modeled by making the discount factor steep between now and the next period and flatter after that (the beta-delta thing, where beta < 1 hits everything that isn't now). An agent like that reverses her preference as an option gets closer: on Sunday she prefers salad on Friday, on Friday she prefers cake. Now the classical akrasia case is supposed to be somone who judges A best, all things considered, and does B anyway. But a beta-delta agent always does what she most prefers at the moment of choosing -- she never acts against her own present preference. So in what sense, if any, is the model a model of akrasia at all?

I want: (1) whether these are even the same phenomenon or two different ones that got conflated -- diachronic preference reversal vs. a synchronic conflict between judgment and desire; (2) whether the fix is to identify the agent's "real" preference with her long-run or second-order preference rather than her present one, and what that move costs -- does it just redefine akrasia into something the model could already do, or does it smuggle in a standard of what she "really" wants that she herself need not endorse? (3) whether sophistication helps: is an agent who correctly predicts her own future reversal always better off than a naive one, or can accurate self-knowledge make things worse, and by what mechanism? and (4) whether commitment devices (the Ulysses-contract trick) only make sense if there's a single self persisting across the time slices -- if the self is just a series of slices, whose preference gets to bind whom?
