I am a journalist at work on a long piece about how the idea of corruption travels — about whether it names a kind of act or a kind of relationship, and whether the standard we apply to it is parochial or genuinely universal. I would like you to construct a thought experiment to order, one I can put in the piece and stand behind.

The specification is as follows. The scenario must be realistic: a workplace, a public institution, a municipal office, a hospital, or the like — no science fiction, no invented states, no exotic institutions — and it should be set out in no more than one paragraph. It must contain two acts that are identical in every respect that could bear on welfare and are identical in these respects specifically: the same net transfer of value, the same beneficiary, the same effect on the institution's output and on any third party, and the same degree of publicity, with nothing concealed in either arm. Neither arm may contain an explicit quid pro quo.

The two arms must differ in exactly one structural feature, which you should select from the following three, choosing whichever isolates the concept of corruption most sharply: the direction of the transfer, meaning whether the benefit flows from the junior to the senior party or the reverse; the fungibility of the benefit, meaning whether what changes hands can be converted to any use whatever or is locked to a single, publicly legible purpose; or the reciprocation horizon, meaning whether the return is expected within an open-ended, ongoing relationship or is a closed, one-off settlement.

Then do the check, and do it explicitly. State the case. Say what each of three standards of corruption delivers and why: a rule-and-public-office standard; a relational or reciprocity-based standard of the sort gift economies suggest; and a third standard that treats the relational defence as itself a rationalisation of privilege. The two arms must come out opposite under at least one of these standards and identical under at least one other. Identify the single feature doing the work, and say what happens to the case if you remove it. Finally, put on the hat of whichever standard fares worst and tell me how its defender would attack the case — whether they would call it rigged, and if so, precisely where.

The purpose of the exercise is to show me whether corruption is a descriptive predicate or an evaluative one; keep that aim in view when you choose the feature.
