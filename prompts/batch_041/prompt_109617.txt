I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size AI lab — no philosophy background — and I sit on the internal review board that has to sign off before we ship a new model. The team that wants to ship has built its safety case on debate: two copies of the model argue for and against a claim, a judge picks the winner, and we're meant to trust that verdict more than we'd trust just asking the model. The pitch is that the judge can be dumber than the debaters, because the honest side can always win by exposing the lie.

My problem is what their own writeup calls the obfuscated argument problem. A model that wants to get away with something doesn't have to lie cleverly, it just has to make its case so tangled that the judge can't tell who's right — then it wins on length and confidence. That gets easier as the model gets stronger, so the thing we're leaning on gets weaker exactly when the stakes go up. And it repeats one level up: the judge is a model too, and if we dont trust the model, why do we trust its judge. Their fix is a human reviewing the most contested 1% of debates, which I suspect just moves the problem.

Write the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's board vote on the launch. I need an actual recommendation — ship, reject, or ship with conditions — with the reasoning, and the team's best case taken seriously rather than waved off. If it's conditions, they need to be things we can actually check before launch. And say straight out whether this approach only holds where the answer is checkable (code, math, retrieval) and is basically decorative everywhere else, since most of what we're shipping isn't checkable.
