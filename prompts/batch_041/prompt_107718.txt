I have been trying to find a name for a defence I keep meeting in formal modelling, and I cannot tell whether I am circling something established or something that needs to be coined. In physics the false assumption normally comes with a knob: a frictionless surface, an inviscid fluid, a population taken to be infinite. A real quantity in the target — friction, viscosity, system size — is set to zero, so one can say what the model is a limiting case of, argue that the quantity is small, and gesture at the size and direction of the error. Many assumptions in economics and the social sciences have no such knob. Nothing in the world is nearly such that a government can commit to a policy and never revisit it, or nearly such that a contract is complete; the assumption is not the end of a continuum of more realistic cases but a different object altogether. Yet the same phrase — that it is only an approximation — is offered in justification of both kinds, and when such an assumption is dropped and the result changes qualitatively rather than slightly, that is waved away on the ground that the model was never meant to be realistic.

Is there a name for this distinction, and for the mistake of defending the second kind of assumption with the vocabulary appropriate to the first? If there is not, please coin the terms and mark the boundaries: how does this differ from the usual contrast between idealisation and abstraction, from robustness analysis, and from the familiar claim that the realism of a theory's assumptions does not matter so long as its predictions are accurate? What I chiefly want is a criterion for telling, before one relaxes an assumption, which kind one is dealing with, and what it means for a result when the assumption cannot be relaxed by degrees. Please answer in British English.
