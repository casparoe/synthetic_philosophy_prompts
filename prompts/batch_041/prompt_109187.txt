I am a novelist, and I have a problem of the following kind, which I would rather settle before I finish the book. My protagonist is a woman who was right once, in a way that cost her everything, and who has since organized her life around a single conviction: that her brother did not do the thing he is imprisoned for. She will not be talked out of it. The novel's question, or one of its questions, is whether that refusal is integrity or a species of self-flattery, and I had assumed the answer would be a matter of character. Then a friend who teaches philosophy handed me an argument that appears to make it a matter of logic, and I cannot find the seam.

Here it is as I have written it out.

(1) If she knows her brother is innocent, then he is innocent; knowledge is not knowledge of what is false.
(2) If he is innocent, then anything that tells against his innocence misrepresents the world. Let us call any such thing misleading.
(3) So if she knows he is innocent, everything that tells against his innocence is misleading.
(4) No one reasons well by being moved by a consideration she has established to be misleading; to be moved by it is a failure of reasoning rather than a sign of open-mindedness.
(5) So if she knows, she is entitled to set aside every consideration against his innocence, whenever it appears.
(6) Nor is it a mistake to fix this in advance: she may resolve now that she will set such considerations aside later.
(7) But then there is nothing left for further inquiry to do. If she keeps looking, she will turn up either considerations for his innocence, which change nothing, or considerations against it, which she has already set aside. Her belief is settled before she looks.
(8) So a person who knows has no reason to keep asking, and cannot rationally change her mind; and by any ordinary standard for knowledge, a great many people who are wrong will count as knowers and be licensed in the same way.
(9) That cannot be right. A juror who is certain of a verdict must still sit through the defense. A scientist who is certain of a result must still read the failed replication. My protagonist's refusal may be admirable or monstrous, but it is not compulsory.

Where do I give the argument up? I would like to keep (1) — I cannot make sense of knowing what is not so — and I would like to keep (4), since the whole force of calling something misleading is that it ought not to move you. There is also a question about my character's own self-understanding: she claims no certainty, only that she is right, and she concedes that she is mistaken about all sorts of things. Does the argument need her to be certain, or does it run just as well for someone who admits she might be wrong? And does it matter that what she claims to know is a person rather than a fact — that her conviction is not a proposition she could write on a card but something closer to an intimacy with a life?

Tell me which step does the work, why it looked innocent until pressed, and what rejecting it would cost — whether the price falls on my character, on the notion of knowledge, or on the idea of evidence. If more than one step can be cut, rank the cuts by expense, and say which one a defender of the argument would find least forgivable. I would also like to know whether the surviving steps can be kept, or whether the structure comes apart once the seam is found.
