Write a dialogue between two forecasters arguing about Aumann's agreement theorem, built around a concrete case so they have actual numbers to fight over. The setup: there's one biased coin, and it's common knowledge that it was drawn at random from three coins with biases 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7, one of each. Forecaster A flipped it ten times in private and posts 0.62. Forecaster B flipped it ten times in private and posts 0.41. Both are Bayesians, both know the setup, neither has seen the other's flips.

A treats this as a scandal: Aumann's theorem says two agents with a common prior can't have common knowledge of each other's differing posteriors, so somebody here is either irrational or lying, and the honest move is for each to shift toward the other and keep shifting until they meet. B thinks A is mistaking a theorem about an idealization for a fact about the world: the common prior is a substantive assumption nobody can check from the inside, common knowledge of each other's rationality is harder still, and if all you ever get is mutual knowledge -- finitely many rounds of "I know that you know" -- then the theorem doesn't apply and persistent disagreement between two honest Bayesians is just the normal case rather than a paradox.

Have them actually press each other. A should have a real answer when B asks which assumption he thinks fails, and whether either of them could tell that it fails from the inside. B should have a real answer when A asks whether "the idealization never quite holds" is an arguement that the disagreement is fine or just a way of changing the subject. Both should be sharp; neither wins by being dumb. Around 700 words.
