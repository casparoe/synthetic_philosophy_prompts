Over the past several weeks a colleague and I have been having the same argument, and I have concluded that it needs to be written up properly rather than settled over coffee. The subject is Otto Neurath's picture language: the system he named ISOTYPE, International System of Typographic Picture Education, developed from the mid-1920s at the Social and Economic Museum in Vienna under the earlier name Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics, and carried on after he was forced out of Austria in 1934, first to the Netherlands and then to England. Its best-known rule is that quantity is shown by repeating a standardised pictogram rather than by enlarging a single one, each symbol standing for a fixed number of things, so that a city with twice the unemployment of another gets twice as many figures; perspective was avoided for the same reason. A person Neurath called a "transformer" took the statistical material and turned it into a sketch for what he called a "self-explanatory" chart, with an illustrator drawing the pictograms to the same rules. My colleague's position is brisk: this is graphic design with a manifesto attached, nothing can be read off a chart that does not first have to be learned, the claim of universality therefore reduces to the claim that a convention can be taught, which is true of every notation ever devised, and the transformer is a translator, that is, exactly the human intermediary the project was meant to do without. I think he is about half right, and that the half he is wrong about is where the philosophical interest lies.

My half-formed idea is that we are arguing in the wrong place. Both of us keep asking whether the charts are really universal, and the honest answer is that they are not in the sense that matters: they have to be learned, they were always accompanied by verbal captions, and Neurath himself seems to have accepted that the thing could never be a fully developed language, calling it instead a "language-like technique". What is distinctive about ISOTYPE, I now think, is the placement of the interpretive labour. It does not sit with the reader, who is supposed to need none; it sits upstream, with the transformer, who decides what the salient comparison is, what the baseline is, which categories are lumped together and which separated, and how far to simplify before the picture stops being faithful. None of those decisions survives into the finished chart, which is precisely what makes the chart look self-explanatory. So the question I want to ask is whether a chart can be self-explanatory in the sense the method required, given that everything that makes it informative was fixed by an interpreter whose reasoning is invisible in the artefact; and if it cannot, whether that damages only the rhetoric of the project or whether it shows that self-explanatoriness was always the wrong aspiration and the right one is something weaker, such as recoverability by an appropriately placed reader. I should also like to keep a second thread if it does not make the project unwieldy: Neurath's philosophy of science was hostile to the idea of knowledge ordered as a single foundational System and preferred an encyclopaedic, mosaic, non-hierarchical picture of it, and I want to know whether a single standardised international symbol set is in tension with that or is simply its pedagogical face.

Please turn this into a short research proposal: a term paper of about eight thousand words, to be written over one semester, for a course on the history and philosophy of scientific visualisation. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, and say what kind of answer would count — I suspect it is conceptual rather than empirical, and I want you to be explicit about what settling it could even mean here. Explain why the existing treatments of ISOTYPE leave the question open, given that I have already conceded the standard point that the system was not a universal language; the proposal should not rest on that concession but should show what it leaves untouched. Set out the approach, and why it is better than the two obvious alternatives — treating ISOTYPE as a case study in the history of graphic design, or treating it as an episode in the Vienna Circle's politics of enlightenment. Say what the argument would look like if it succeeded and what would count as failure, be honest about which parts I can do without archival work, and tell me whether the encyclopaedism thread should stay in or be cut and saved for a second paper.
