Construct a case in which a belief comes out true on William James's account of truth — true because it is expedient in the way of belief, borne out by the way it conducts the believer through experience — and not true on C. S. Peirce's, which ties truth to the opinion that inquiry is fated to converge on.

Constraints: ordinary contemporary life, no exotic devices, no religious belief or afterlife, and no self-fulfilling belief (believing it must not be part of what makes it true). The believer should be epistemically diligent rather than credulous, and the belief should be doing real work — nothing else would let this person's life hang together. Inquiry, though, must not converge on it, and the case should make plain whether that is because inquiry would run the other way or because the question admits of no convergence at all. One paragraph.

Then walk through it: state the belief and the evidence bearing on it; give James's verdict and Peirce's; and say which Peirce is committed to — that the belief is false, or that it is not a candidate for truth at all — with the reason drawn from his realism about truth and his account of how opinion settles. Identify the single feature doing the work, the one that pulls expediency and convergence apart, and say what happens if it is removed. Close with the strongest objection available to the side that comes out worse, and with an honest note on what the case has stipulated rather than shown: the point is to test whether these two pragmatisms can be prised apart by construction rather than by exegesis, so a case that asserts the divergence instead of generating it fails.
