I'm a high school student in a philosophy elective, and I'm writing a paper on guilt and shame. The textbook version of the contrast goes like this: guilt attaches to what you did, shame attaches to what you are; guilt can be felt alone in a room, shame needs an audience; so guilt is the more mature and more autonomous moral emotion, and a culture that leans on shame is leaning on something more primitive. I'm not satisfied with that, partly because it treats emotions as though a culture could simply pick them the way a school picks a mascot, and partly because the shame side always gets illustrated with the same handful of images (public humiliation, exile, gossip) while the guilt side gets all the sympathetic cases. The paper has to argue whether one of these is actually the better moral emotion, and whether "choosing" one is even something a culture can do.

What I want from you is one example, worked through in detail, that puts pressure on the standard contrast. Concretely: invent a single small-scale situation — a school honor code, a family dealing with a theft, a workplace where someone has been blamed for a mistake that was not entirely theirs, a neighborhood responding to vandalism — in which a shame-oriented response and a guilt-oriented response give different answers to who owes what to whom, and in which the shame-oriented response is the one that looks more like a genuine moral relationship between persons rather than a matter of managing reputations. Be explicit about who feels what and about what, since that is where I keep getting confused: whether the difference between the two responses is really a difference between two emotions, or a difference in who is watching, in whether the wrongdoer is readmitted to the group afterwards, or in whether the wrong is treated as a debt or as a stain. Then give me a second, much briefer case that cuts the other way, so I can judge whether the pattern is a feature of the emotions themselves or just an artifact of the cases I happened to pick.

Please don't spend space on citations or on the history of the debate; my teacher wants the example and the reasoning, and I have no way to check sources anyway. Finish by telling me what would change your mind about the verdict your example supports — which further detail of the case, or which counterargument, would push you to say the other response was better after all — and where in your own reasoning you'd expect the weakest link to be.
