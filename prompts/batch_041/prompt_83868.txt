Having spent a career building and validating instruments for quantities that do not announce their own values, I have spent the first years of my retirement reading political theory and the philosophy of the social sciences, and I keep arriving at the same irritation: the current argument about democratic backsliding is conducted almost entirely as a dispute about verdicts when it is really a dispute about measurement, and the measurement decisions are the ones least often stated. I would like a piece of sustained satire that makes that irritation legible.

Write a press release, roughly 600–700 words, from a fictional consortium of universities and advocacy organizations that publishes an annual democracy index. The release announces this year's revision of the methodology and the reclassifications that follow, and it should remain deadpan from beginning to end — no winking, no invented absurdities, nothing the real genre would not contain. The comedy should come from the technical choices visibly producing the verdicts: the threshold at which a polity crosses from 'impaired' to 'backsliding'; whether a decline reversed at the next election still counts, and for how many cycles; whether the unit of analysis is the national executive or the whole polity, subnational governments included; whether formal-legal compliance can offset a substantive deficit in contestation; and what the consortium does when its coders disagree by more than the year-on-year change it is reporting. Invent the consortium and its indicators rather than borrowing the names of real indices, keep the invented instrument internally consistent — if a score change is reported, the arithmetic should work — and close with a short FAQ in which the consortium answers the obvious objections (that the voters re-elected them, that its region is being graded on a curve, that the previous government did the same thing) in the flat procedural register such documents use. Let the illustrative cases be the familiar ones, thinly disguised if you prefer: a Central European member state, its larger neighbor whose illiberal turn was reversed at the polls, a long-governing state on Europe's southeastern edge, and the United States. A specialist in comparative politics should find the piece fair rather than a strawman, and a reader who is not one should still follow what is at stake. Please do not write it in my voice; I am commissioning it, not appearing in it.
