I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for, and it bugs me. Someone defends a view on which a certain thing genuinely exists but does nothing — it's along for the ride, causally speaking. Fine, no problem there. But then the same person gets asked how we come to think about that thing, talk about it, write papers about it, and the explanation has to be done entirely by something else, with the thing itself just sitting there being true of us. The first time I noticed it was with conscious experience: a philosopher says experiences are real but make no difference to anything anyone says or does, and then has to account for why people like him keep confidently announcing that experiences are real and have such-and-such a character. Lately I see the same shape elsewhere — people who say the self is an illusion, but then describe the illusion as uncannily accurate about the very self it's supposed to be an illusion of.

Is there a name for this predicament in general — a theory that puts something in the world and at the same time makes it unavailable to explain our talk about it? I don't think "self-refutation" is it, because nothing strictly contradicts itself; the account is usually internally consistent, just oddly tidy about which side gets to do the explaining. People keep telling me this is just the hard problem, but the hard problem seems to be about why there's any experience at all, whereas mine is about why we report it, and those feel different to me. If there's no established term, I want one that covers both the experience case and the illusion cases, and that tells me where the edges are — does it kick in whenever an entity is denied causal powers, or only when the entity is the sort of thing we're supposedly directly acquainted with?
