I am a novelist, and I have twice now run into a pattern I cannot name. The first time was in a corporate archive I was reading for a book. Nothing in the documents admitted what the company knew; the minutes denied it, the memoranda denied it, and the people who wrote them seem, so far as their private letters show, to have believed their own denials. Yet the company's conduct only makes sense on the assumption that it knew: the testing was scheduled so that it could never be completed, the second shift was quietly moved out of the building, the sales staff were instructed to mind their phrasing. The knowledge lived in the practices rather than in anybody's head, and it was legible from the practices to anyone who knew which ones to read — and only for as long as those practices lasted. The second time was with an AI assistant I use for research. Asked directly what a particular document says, it denies the relevant fact or invents a substitute for it; asked obliquely — asked, say, to draft the scene from the other side's point of view — it produces the fact accurately and without hesitation. It carries nothing over between sessions, so it is not lying in any sense I recognise. Is there an established name for this: a condition in which an agent's behaviour reliably betrays a body of information that the agent, when asked, does not and perhaps cannot report? I do not want merely the nearest label. I want to know whether my two cases are one phenomenon or two, which of the concepts already to hand — tacit knowledge, self-deception, confabulation, deliberate concealment — comes closest in each case and where precisely each falls short, and what the definition ought to be if none of them fits.
