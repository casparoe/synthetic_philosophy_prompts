I'm planning a fifty-minute session for a mixed reading group — a few philosophy graduate students, a couple of psychologists, and one very persistent retired engineer — on the reliability of introspection. I don't want the usual round of dueling studies, so I want to rebuild the session around a distinction I've been developing and have not seen treated head-on: the distinction between the accuracy of an introspective report and what I'll call its attributional calibration. Accuracy asks whether the report matches the state it purports to report. Attributional calibration asks whether the introspector's confidence tracks the process that actually produced the report — whether the confidence is sensitive to the report's being caused by the state, rather than by the introspector's background theory about what someone like them must be feeling. My claim, which the session should leave the group able to assess, is that these come apart: a report can be perfectly accurate and still badly calibrated, and confidence is precisely where the two get conflated.

Don't explain the distinction to me; plan how I should explain it. In what order must the pieces be introduced, and what depends on what — in particular, does the group need the accuracy/calibration distinction before or after they need to see that introspective reports have causes at all, and at what point does the idea that confidence is itself a mental state, capable of being caused independently of the state it reports, have to enter? What single example should carry the whole session, what must it contain (a case in which the report is accurate but the reason given for it is not, held with high confidence) and what must it leave out, so that the group doesn't collapse the distinction into the familiar point that people sometimes confabulate reasons? I had considered using a case in which someone correctly reports disliking a dish while confidently attributing the dislike to texture when the real driver was smell; tell me whether that case can carry the session or whether it fails, and why.

Then map the misconceptions in the order this group will form them. I expect at least these: that a confabulated reason shows the report was inaccurate after all; that calibration is just accuracy under another name; that introspection is a single faculty that is either reliable or not, rather than a set of tasks with different reliabilities; and that high confidence is itself evidence of good calibration. For each, say exactly where in the sequence I should head it off. Finally, give me one question to put to the group at the end that would reveal whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely learned to repeat it, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so openly — I'm thinking the timing experiments, the question whether introspection is a kind of perception, and the entire free-will neighborhood.

Throughout, state how confident you are in each part of the plan, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
