I have been puzzling over Avicenna's flying man argument on my own, and I keep getting stuck at the same point. The setup, as I understand it, is a person created fully formed but suspended in the air, with his limbs kept apart from one another and his eyes covered, so that he perceives nothing at all — neither his own body nor anything external. Avicenna says that such a person would still affirm that he exists, and would affirm this without affirming anything about his limbs, organs, or body; from this he concludes that the self he affirms is not the body but an immaterial soul.

I would like a careful explanation of what this argument can and cannot establish. Three things in particular bother me. First, what exactly is the gap between showing that someone's awareness of himself does not depend on his body and showing that the thing he is aware of is not his body? Does Avicenna need the stronger claim, and does the argument actually deliver it? Second, the man is described as suspended in the air and as unable to touch anything — does that not already place him inside a spatial, bodily frame of reference, so that the thought experiment quietly assumes the very thing it is meant to prove? Third, if he is to affirm that he exists, does he not already need the concept of existence, and where could that concept come from in a state with no experience whatsoever? Please weigh these objections, tell me which is the strongest and which can be answered, and say what, if anything, the argument still shows even if the objections succeed.
