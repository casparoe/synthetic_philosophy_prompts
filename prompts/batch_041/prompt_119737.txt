I run the evidence desk at a mid-sized development finance institution. Roughly a dozen times a month, a consultant, a country office, or an academic partner sends us a claim of the form "X explains why region Y is poor," where X is an institution, a geography, a culture, or a historical event, and asks us to fund a programme on the strength of it. These claims almost always rest on a historical natural experiment — a boundary surveyed in 1897, a forced-labour system abolished in 1932, settler populations that differed from one district to the next — and they arrive with a regression and a mechanism story attached. My staff are economists and social scientists, not historians. We have about two hours per claim, no realistic access to archives, and no capacity to re-estimate anyone's regressions; the country director signs off; a bad call means money wasted, or a programme that does damage in a place we don't understand.

Write me a triage procedure we can actually run on such a claim. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with a no, the different points where it stops with a verdict of "we cannot know from this evidence" rather than a verdict of "false," and the points where it must hand the decision to a person because what remains is a question of values or politics rather than of history. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and keep the whole thing usable by someone who has never read the historiography.

Two things to build on or overrule. First, our current draft is three questions: is there a correlation, is there a plausible mechanism, and could we act on it. I suspect this is too permissive, but tell me precisely where it goes wrong. Second, our unit has a rule I would like you to assess: a historical correlation licenses a policy claim only if we can name the mechanism and show that the mechanism's inputs still exist in the place where we would be intervening. Tell me whether that test is sound, what it would wrongly reject, and what it would wrongly accept.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases: (1) a claim that a colonial-era land-tenure regime explains a present-day regional income gap, supported by a discontinuity across the old administrative boundary; (2) a claim that culture, measured by survey responses on trust, explains why firms in one region underinvest; (3) a claim that a cash transfer we are considering will have long-run effects, argued by analogy to a historical episode. At least one of the three should come out "cannot know," and I would rather it were the one I am most attached to.

No reading list or citations, please; I want the procedure and the reasoning behind each step, not the literature. At the end, tell me how confident you are that this procedure would survive contact with our actual docket, and name the single step you are least sure of.
