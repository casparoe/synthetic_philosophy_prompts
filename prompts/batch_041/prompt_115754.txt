Question

In the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, al-Ghazālī examines the claim that the connection between what is habitually taken to be a cause and what is habitually taken to be its effect is necessary, and he denies it.

(a) State precisely which thesis about the fire-and-cotton case al-Ghazālī rejects, and which thesis, if any, he affirms about the relation between the two. (3)

(b) Some interpreters hold that the seventeenth discussion is a dialectical argument directed at the philosophers' claim that the opposite of the customary connection is impossible, and that it establishes only that the opposite is possible, not that occasionalism is the true metaphysics. Assess this reading on the basis of the structure of the argument. (3)

(c) What follows from al-Ghazālī's position for (i) our ability to predict and to act on the regularities of nature, and (ii) the possibility of miracles? (2)

(d) Does the seventeenth discussion commit al-Ghazālī to the view that human beings exercise no causal power of their own? Explain. (2)

Student answer

(a) The thesis al-Ghazālī rejects is the philosophers' claim that the connection is necessary, that is, that once the cause is present the effect must follow and cannot be prevented. He does not deny the conjunction. We do observe the cotton burn when the fire touches it, and he grants this; what he denies is that the necessity of the connection is either perceived or demonstrable. We perceive only the succession, and the succession could in principle be otherwise. Because the connection is not necessary, God can produce the burning without the fire and can prevent it while the fire is present, and it is this that makes the miracles of the prophets possible. The discussion therefore establishes that occasionalism is the correct account of the world: God is the sole cause of every event, and created things make no causal contribution of their own.

(b) The dialectical reading holds that the argument aims only at the philosophers' claim of impossibility, and so establishes merely that the opposite of the customary connection is possible. This cannot be right. Al-Ghazālī is not making a point about what the philosophers can prove; he is asserting that the connection is not necessary, and that is a claim about the world rather than about our evidence. Since the opposite of the connection is possible, and since God is omnipotent, it follows that God alone brings about every event, and the reading is refuted.

(c)(i) The position does not make prediction impossible. God's action is regular, and it is because he habitually brings about the burning when the fire is present that we may expect it to happen again; the customary course of nature is reliable in practice even though it is not necessary. (ii) Miracles are possible, since the course of nature is not fixed and God may act otherwise.

(d) Yes. If God is the sole cause of every event, then the movements of a human body are no more the agent's own doing than the burning is the fire's. Al-Ghazālī accordingly denies that human beings have any power over their actions, and it follows that no one is responsible for what God brings about through them.
