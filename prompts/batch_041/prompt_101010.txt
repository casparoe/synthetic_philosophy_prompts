I have spent the better part of a year in a small working group that has talked itself, against its own early instincts, into supporting a deliberate radio broadcast to a few nearby stars — not because we expect an answer, but because the refusal to speak has come to seem to us a kind of unexamined cowardice. We are drafting the case for it now, and the one argument that keeps stopping us cold is the dark forest. The picture is by this point a fixture of every serious discussion of the Fermi paradox: the galaxy as a forest at night, thick with hunters moving silently among the trees, each civilization a crouching figure with a raised gun, every transmission a signal flare, and our own long silence therefore not a puzzle to be explained but a survival strategy to be preserved. The name comes from Liu Cixin's 2008 novel and the idea has been recycled, in looser forms, throughout the long argument over whether we should be broadcasting at all. It is a good story. That is exactly what worries me: I can no longer tell whether the argument persuades me because it is sound or because it is a story, and I have a talk to give on this in six weeks, so I need to know which.

Audit the dark forest for me as an analogy rather than argue about it as a policy. I want the mapping made explicit before I judge it. In the forest picture, what plays the role of the hunter, the gun, the footsteps, the darkness, the scarcity of game, and the silence — and which of those elements is actually carrying the argument? Then tell me where the mapping breaks: which features of a literal forest have no counterpart in the galactic case, or have a counterpart that behaves quite differently. Then tell me what the analogy smuggles in — which of its conclusions are really assumptions borrowed from the source and quietly installed in the target, about how cheap a first strike is, about whether intentions can be known, about whether resources are scarce, about whether travel and communication run on the same timescale. Finally, the question I actually care about: does the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that we should stay silent — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion the whole way? Separate the disanalogies that matter for this particular use from the ones that don't, and if the picture fails, tell me what would be a better analogy, or whether the whole business is beyond analogy.

Be direct with me. If the way I have posed this is confused — if the dark forest is not really an analogy in the sense that can be audited, or if the "stay silent" conclusion is already dead for reasons that have nothing to do with the analogy, or if I am asking the comparison to do work that only a probability estimate could do — say so plainly rather than producing a tidy table anyway. I would much rather be told that the framing is wrong than receive a polished answer to the wrong question, and I would rather find out now than in front of an audience. Mark clearly where you are reasoning from general principle and where you are repeating a standard line you cannot verify without a source; I have no use for confident attributions I cannot check, and I have deliberately given you no way to look anything up. Please write in American English.
