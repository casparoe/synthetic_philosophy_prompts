Write a dialogue between two metaphysicians — an endurantist (three-dimensionalist) and a perdurantist (four-dimensionalist) — who have set themselves to work through a case that neither has met before.

The case is this. A particular house is dismantled over the course of a year, with care: every timber, brick, tile, and nail is labelled and stored. For the next forty years the parts remain in a warehouse, intact but arranged as no house at all. At the end of that period the same parts are reassembled on the original foundations, in the original arrangement, and the result is indistinguishable from what stood there before. Nothing was ever destroyed at any point; only the arrangement was interrupted. The question the two must settle is whether a single house persisted across the whole episode, or whether there were two houses, or one house with a forty-year hole in its career — and, more to the point, what each of their theories is entitled to say about it and what it costs them to say it.

In the dialogue:

- Each character must derive a verdict from their own machinery rather than announcing one: the endurantist from the claim that the house is wholly present at each moment of its existence, the perdurantist from the claim that it is a four-dimensional whole composed of temporal parts.
- The endurantist must say whether the house is wholly present in the warehouse during the forty years; if not, they must say what makes the house that was dismantled the same house as the one rebuilt. The perdurantist must say whether the storage stage belongs to the house at all; if it does, they must face whether that commits them to scattered objects; if it does not, they must say what makes the two surviving stretches of the house one object rather than two that merely resemble each other.
- Each must concede the point at which their own view has to import something beyond its native resources — a causal condition, a sortal, a convention about arrangement — and say honestly what that import costs.
- Each must say whether the gradual case, in which the house stands throughout while its parts are replaced one at a time, is easier or harder for their view than the gap case, and why the two cases do not come to the same thing.
- The exchange must be genuine back-and-forth: each character should engage the other's actual arguments, grant what has been scored against them, and press hardest where they think the other has helped themselves to something unearned. Neither should be a strawman; make both as strong as you can.
- Close with each character stating what would have to be true for them to abandon their position, and where they take the other's view to be not false but incomplete.

Roughly 1,200–1,800 words.
