I want you to reconstruct the position Kant would most plausibly have taken on the split-brain cases, which of course postdate him. Kant treats the transcendental unity of apperception as a condition on anything's counting as a representation for a subject at all, rather than as one more empirical fact about minds; commissurotomy, together with the dissociations, confabulations, and divergent reports it produces, appears to leave us with a single organism running two partly independent streams of access. Would Kant have to describe such a patient as one subject whose empirical unity is merely impaired, or could his framework accommodate two subjects in one body? Be explicit about where his commitments actually settle the question and where we would be extrapolating past anything he wrote. Then extend the case: if the hemispheres were reconnected by a prosthetic bridge allowing each to report, after a delay, on the other's percepts, would that delayed cross-access satisfy the condition Kant places on unity, or would he insist that no arrangement of empirical access could supply what he is after — and what would the answer reveal about whether his unity of apperception is a description of how minds are actually put together or a formal requirement on anything that is to count as a mind at all?
