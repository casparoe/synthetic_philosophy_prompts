I am a novelist, and I have reached the stage of a book at which its moral architecture has to be settled. The book follows a woman — I will call her Ilse — through a year in which she becomes persuaded that the small contributions she makes to large and distant harms are either trivially wrong or not wrong at all: the flights, the meat, the ballot she casts in a district her party cannot lose. She acts on this persuasion for a while, and then finds that she cannot sustain it, and the novel has to know whether her collapse is a failure of nerve or a discovery. Two accounts of her situation are competing for the book's foundation, and I would like you to compare them and come down on one side.

Call the first the arithmetic account. On this view an act is wrong, where it is wrong, because of the difference it makes: it adds its own increment — a quantity of carbon, a number of animals, a probability of being pivotal — to a harm, and its wrongness is proportionate to that increment. Ilse's acts are wrong, but very slightly so, and the slightness is real rather than a trick of perspective; as the crowd of contributors grows, her share shrinks, and at some point it shrinks to nothing worth deliberating over.

Call the second the participation account. On this view the increment is a distraction. Ilse's acts are wrong not for what they add but for what they are: exercises of a practice whose continuation does the harm, and endorsements of that practice by someone who understands what she is doing. On this view the wrongness of her flying does not diminish as the number of other flyers rises; if anything it sharpens, since her act is now also a ratification of what everyone else is doing.

I want the comparison pressed at a point where the two accounts are forced apart. Consider two of Ilse's acts. The first is a vote in an election that will in fact be decided by a single ballot — hers. The second is a flight taken in the knowledge that it will make no measurable difference to a harm that is diffuse, cumulative, and without a threshold. The arithmetic account seems to make the vote a grave act and the flight a negligible one; the participation account seems to make them, if anything, continuous with each other. Say which account handles this pair better, and why, and whether the account that loses on this pair can be repaired without quietly collapsing into the other.

A few constraints. I do not want a survey that lays the two accounts side by side and leaves the choice to me; I am asking you to take a position, defend it, and state plainly where it is weakest and what it would cost me to build the novel on it. Take the two accounts as I have stated them, and where a term is doing more work than it can bear — endorsement and practice are the obvious candidates — say so, and either repair it or argue that it can stand. And because the book's whole interest lies in Ilse's inner weather rather than in the metaphysics, tell me finally what each account implies about what her relapse would mean: whether a person who cannot hold to the arithmetic account has failed to be rational, or has merely been unable to live as a rational person must.
