I've talked myself into the procreation asymmetry: coming into existence can be bad for the person who comes to be, but it can never be good for them, so there's a reason against creating someone who'll suffer and no matching reason to create someone who'll be happy. I also want to push it further than I usually see it pushed — I think it's really a claim about reasons rather than value: harms and benefits don't just weigh differently, they generate reasons of different kinds, which is why you can't trade them off against each other. What would count against that? I don't just mean the usual charge that it's status quo bias dressed up as intuition. I mean which cases, which counterintuitions, and which neighbouring commitments it stands or falls with. Does the different-kinds-of-reasons version force me to say something about harms that don't generate reasons at all (posthumous ones, say), or about benefits that seem to generate exactly the reasons harms do (rescue cases)? What do I have to give up if I'm wrong? And be honest about wether the view can be refuted at all, or whether it's been quietly insulated by making nonexistence incomparable — because if nonexistence is incomparable, I don't see how the bad half survives either. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them defenders of the asymmetry already concede, rather than just listing what critics throw at it.
