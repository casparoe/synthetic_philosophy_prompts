I'm working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, the bit in Part II about how fortune affects our moral sentiments, and I keep getting tangled in it. Heres the gist as I understand it: whether an action deserves praise or blame depends entirely on the intention it came from, so someone who meant well deserves the same credit whether or not things worked out. But our actual feelings dont behave that way. We're grateful to whoever actually helped us, resentful at whoever actually hurt us, and someone who tried to do harm but was stopped by a lucky accident strikes us as less blameworthy than a person whose identical intent went through. Smith treats this as a disorder in our sentiments, then says it isnt altogether useless, since it keeps us from being too pleased with ourselves and makes us more cautious, and nature seems to have planted it in us on purpose.

What I cant work out: does he think the intention-only view is the truth about desert and our luck-tracking feelings are just a useful error, or does he think the luck-tracking feelings are also right about desert? And if the point of the irregularity is its good social effects, has he quietly switched from a question about what people deserve to a question about what feelings are useful to have? Help me get clear on whether these are two consistent strands in him or one strand with a confusion in it. Keep it focused, I dont need a survey of the secondary literature.
