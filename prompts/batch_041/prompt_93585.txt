I edit a documentary series on the ancient Mediterranean with a colleague who runs what she calls her independence test on every claim that goes on screen. Her recent calls: she cut a claim that a besieged city's commander sold the gates to the enemy, because it rested on a single writer who had every reason to blacken the commander's name; she aired a claim that a celebrated victory was won by a ruse the victors' own descendants found shameful, on the strength of two writers a century apart, neither of whom shows any sign of having read the other; and she cut a wholly colourless detail about grain shipments that only one writer reports. Next week's segment turns on a claim that a treaty was broken by the very side that preserved the record of it — a claim that embarrasses that side and that appears in two writers who never read each other, but who are both, on inspection, plainly retelling the same lost chronicle of the war.

Predict her verdict, say what rule her track record actually encodes, name the competing rules that fit that record just as well and say what each predicts here, give your best guess with your reasons, and name the one further case that would tell the leading candidates apart.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
