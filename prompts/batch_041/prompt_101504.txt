Ethicists standardly distinguish two categories of act that sit on opposite sides of the line between the required and the forbidden, and the apparent symmetry between them is more misleading than illuminating. On the upper side lies the supererogatory: an act that morality does not demand — one is free to omit it without wrongdoing — yet that is genuinely admirable, the saint's or the hero's act, praiseworthy precisely because it was optional. On the lower side lies what is sometimes called the suberogatory: an act that morality does not forbid — one is within one's rights to perform it, and one may not be compelled or blamed for performing it — yet that is nonetheless morally objectionable, the sort of thing a decent person would not stoop to.

Please compare these two categories. I am interested in the structural problem they jointly pose, not in a survey of who has said what about them. Address at least the following. First, can a single ordering of acts — say, a scale of moral value together with a threshold of requirement — accommodate both categories, or do they pull the deontic framework in incompatible directions? Second, are the two asymmetries really one asymmetry? If the supererogatory shows that being better than one's duty does not entail being required, does the suberogatory show that being worse than the merely permissible does not entail being forbidden — and are these the same point viewed from opposite ends, or two distinct claims? Third, is suberogation best understood as a genuine third category, or merely as the shadow of supererogation — that is, as nothing more than the failure to perform an optional good? If the latter, then every omission of a supererogatory act would itself be suberogatory, and the distinction threatens to collapse; say whether it does. Fourth, which of the two categories sits more comfortably with a broadly consequentialist axiology, and which with a broadly Kantian one, and why.

To keep the discussion concrete, test your account against this pair of cases. In the first, a passerby wades into a flooded river at considerable risk to herself to pull a stranger from a wrecked car; no one could reasonably have demanded it of her. In the second, a man in a nearly empty train station watches an elderly stranger struggle with heavy luggage up a staircase and, though he has ample time and no competing obligation, walks past without offering a hand; no rule forbids this and no one may punish him for it, yet the act seems beneath him. Say what each case establishes and whether the same theoretical machinery can handle both.

Conclude with a verdict: are supererogation and suberogation two irreducibly different phenomena, or two descriptions of a single underlying structure — the gap between what morality requires and what morality values?
