John Stuart Mill spent most of his working life in the East India Company's London office, and in his writings on government he defended a form of foreign rule over what he described as uncivilized or semi-civilized peoples as a legitimate, indeed necessary, stage on the path to self-government. This position sits awkwardly beside the doctrine of On Liberty, where he argues that the only justification for interfering with a person's liberty is to prevent harm to others, and that the value of free discussion rests on our fallibility. Write an essay on the following controversy: was Mill's defense of British despotism in India a coherent extension of his liberal and utilitarian principles, or an unresolved contradiction in his thought? In answering, address at least the following: (1) how Mill's distinction between civilized and non-civilized peoples functions as a condition on the applicability of his liberty principle; (2) whether his epistemic arguments for free speech and against the suppression of opinion apply with equal force to the administration of a colony, and if not, why not; (3) whether the utilitarian standard can justify tutelary despotism without collapsing into the very paternalism Mill rejects in On Liberty; and (4) whether Mill's practical stance—his defense of the East India Company against the transfer of authority to the Crown after the 1857 uprising—is best explained by his theory or by institutional loyalty and the belief that expert administration was more competent than direct rule. Reach a definite verdict on whether the position is consistent or inconsistent, and justify it. Do not just summarize Mill's texts; construct the strongest version of each side and then argue for your conclusion.
