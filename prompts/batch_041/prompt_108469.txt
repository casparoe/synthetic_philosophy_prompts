I retired last year after 30-odd years in analytical chemistry, and I've been reading alot of philosophy since — evenings, usually with a whisky, so be gentle with me. Here's what's nagging at me.

A friend of mine, an economist, heard me going on about this Twin Earth business and said he couldn't see what the fuss was about. His line: meaning is money. A twenty isn't worth twenty because of anything in the paper; it's worth that because a whole community treats it as worth that, and there's an authority at the top who stamps it and stands behind it. Words work the same way, he says. "Water" gets its meaning from the community that uses it, and from our having settled on the local clear stuff as what the word is about. So Twin Earth doesn't show that meaning is out there in the world — it shows meaning is a social currency, a convention, one we could in principle have arranged differently. Which deflates the whole externalism thing, he thinks: not a discovery about the mind, just a description of a custom.

I don't trust it, but I can't say why, and I spent my working life being suspicious of arguments that feel like instruments that haven't been calibrated. So: take the analogy apart for me. Lay out the correspondence properly — what plays the role of the paper, the mint, the exchange rate, the counterfeiter, the person holding the note — then show me where the mapping breaks, and say which of the breaks actually matter for what he wants to conclude and which are just decoration.

Two things I keep chewing on that won't fit into his picture. First: in 1750 nobody on either planet had the faintest idea what was in the stuff, and we still want to say the two of them meant different things by their word — no convention, no recognition, no agreement was doing any work there at all. Second: I read somewhere about a patient who's never opened a medical book, but who still means by "arthritis" exactly what the doctors mean, so when he's sure it's flared up in his thigh he's simply wrong, rather than speaking his own private language about a different ailment. That second one seems to sit much more comfortably with the money picture than the first does, and I'd like to know whether that's a real distinction or whether I'm fooling myself.

Also, is there an analogue of counterfeiting, and does the picture need one? And what would a better analogy be — one that keeps whatever is right about the community business without quietly importing the part where value is whatever we say it is? And give it to me straight: does his deflationary conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the money picture carrying that conclusion the whole time?
