"The puzzle of promising is that a few words seem able to change a person's normative situation. My aim is to show that no single account explains bindingness, and that a promise's grip and its reach come apart.

Hume gives us the most economical starting point. His account of fidelity is usually read as conventionalist, but I think this misreads him. What Hume actually holds is that we have a natural sentiment of fidelity to our word, and that the convention of promising merely gives that sentiment a determinate object and a rule to follow. Fidelity is therefore a natural virtue in Hume's sense: the convention is scaffolding, but the obligation would exist without it, because the sentiment does. On this reading the grip of a promise comes from the sentiment rather than from the practice.

Conventionalism also struggles with reach. If my obligation is generated by the practice, it looks as though only those who are party to the practice can be wronged by my breaking my word, so a promise made to a small child or to a stranger outside the community would have no reach at all. That seems to me a decisive objection, and it is why I turn to expectation-based accounts.

Scanlon's principle of fidelity is the best worked-out version of the expectation view. On his account the wrong of breaking a promise consists in having voluntarily and intentionally led the promisee to expect that I will do the thing, knowing that she wants the assurance, and the principle is one that no one could reasonably reject. But the obligation only really bites once the promisee has altered her position in reliance on my words; before that point I have not done anything to her, and so she has nothing to complain about. This is what gives a promise its reach.

Kant answers differently again. For Kant the bindingness of a promise is grounded in the will of the promisor: in making a promise I must be able to will my maxim as a universal law, and a maxim of making promises I do not intend to keep cannot be universalised without destroying the practice of promising itself. The grip of a promise is thus a matter of self-legislation rather than of social convention.

If grip and reach come apart, the case of a promise made to a dying person who never learns of it is instructive. She never forms the expectation and never relies, so on Scanlon's view no wrong is done. Yet the promisor still seems to have a reason to perform. I conclude that promises bind in virtue of the promisor's will, and that the promisee's expectations matter only to whether she has been wronged, not to whether the promisor is bound."
