I teach a 200-level course titled "Stoicism Ancient and Modern," and I am currently grading the midterm essays. Below are the essay question and one student's response. Please assign the response a grade as a percentage out of 100, and give me the specific comments that justify it; I need to be able to defend the grade if the student appeals it.

Midterm essay question:

"Stoic authors, ancient and modern, speak of a 'dichotomy of control.' In lecture we distinguished two readings of it. On the causal-power reading, the dichotomy sorts events according to whether my effort can bring them about. On the attribution reading, it sorts them according to whether they are properly credited or debited to me as my own doing — whether, in the strict sense, they are mine. The two readings agree on the paradigm cases (a judgment I form; tomorrow's weather) and diverge in between. In an essay of no more than 800 words: (1) state the dichotomy as Epictetus presents it and say what falls on each side; (2) using the Stoic notions of preferred indifferents and of acting with a reservation, explain how a Stoic can pursue health, friendship, and political ends without counting their attainment as part of her good; (3) say which of the two readings the text better supports, and whether the dichotomy, so read, makes the Stoic objectionably passive in the face of injustice."

Student response:

"Epictetus opens the Enchiridion by dividing things into those that are up to us and those that are not. Up to us are our judgments, our desires, our aversions, and our impulses — that is to say, our own actions. Not up to us are the body, property, reputation, and office: whatever we do not ourselves do. The point is practical. If I put my happiness in the first category, nothing that happens to me can take it away; if I put it in the second, I have handed it to other people. In modern terms, the things up to us are the things we are able to bring about, and the things not up to us are the ones that lie beyond our reach.

This is why the Stoics call health, wealth, reputation, and friendship preferred indifferents. They are goods — real goods, which is why the Stoic pursues them — but goods of a lower rank than virtue, and not fully within our power, so the Stoic holds them loosely. The reservation is the technique for holding them loosely. When she sets out to heal a patient or to pass a law, she appends a condition, that she will do it provided nothing stands in the way. That condition is a reminder to herself that the plan may be blocked, so that when it is blocked she is not surprised and not devastated. It is, in effect, a way of keeping her expectations honest.

Which reading of the dichotomy does the text support? The attribution reading. Epictetus is not mainly interested in how much I can accomplish; he is interested in what is mine. A tyrant can chain my body, but my judgment is not his to command, and that is the difference that matters to him.

On the passivity objection: I think the charge is fair but not fatal. Because the outcome of a political struggle is not up to me, the Stoic is not required to secure it; she is required only to make the attempt. And since the attempt itself is often beyond her power, she may legitimately decline to act where success is unlikely and the cost to herself is high. This is not numbness — the sage still has the good emotions — but it is a real limit on what the dichotomy asks of her."
