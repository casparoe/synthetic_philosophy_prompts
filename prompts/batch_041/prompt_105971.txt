My dissertation advisor died in March and I'm giving the eulogy. She spent her whole career on Rawls and religion — her line was that everyone reads the later political stuff as a retreat from his early religious seriousness, and that this gets it exactly backwards: he never stopped thinking that a whole society can only be held together by one shared faith if the state props that faith up, which is why he wanted religion kept out of public reason, not because he thought any less of it. She was a churchgoer her entire life, which most of her colleagues never knew. Half the room at the memorial will be philosophers who know the public-reason debates inside out; the other half are her family and her congregation, who knew her as a devout woman and not at all as a theorist. Write the eulogy, seven minutes or so. It has to be honest about the tension between the faith and the philosophy without dissolving it into either "she was really a believer all along" or "her theory quietly replaced her religion," and without any vocabulary that will lose the family. The one thing I really want it built on, not just mentioned: her own view that the Rawlsian rule isnt a rule about what you may believe but about what can serve as a shared premise — she used to say the test is whether you'd be willing for the person across the table to reject it and still be owed an answer.
