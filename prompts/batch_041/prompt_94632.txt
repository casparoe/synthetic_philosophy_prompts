Write an essay on whether ambiguity aversion is a rational response to uncertainty or a bias, and defend a verdict. Anchor the argument in the following four cases; they are constructed for this essay, so treat them on their merits rather than as textbook examples.

Urn A: one hundred balls, exactly fifty red and fifty black, which you may count for yourself.

Urn B: one hundred balls, each red or black. Before you entered the room a fair coin was tossed: on heads the urn was filled with a hundred red balls, on tails with a hundred black ones. The coin has since been destroyed, no record of it was kept, and no one knows how it landed. The composition of the urn is therefore unknown to everyone, but the second-order probability that a randomly drawn ball is red is exactly one half.

Urn C: one hundred balls, red or black, produced by a process about which you know nothing at all and about which you can form no justified distribution.

Urn D: one hundred balls, each independently coloured by a mechanism you fully understand, except that the bias parameter governing the mechanism was itself chosen — after seeing your instructions, and with an interest in your losing — by another agent, rather than drawn at random.

In each case you must commit to a single gamble: a prize of £100 if a ball drawn at random is red, nothing otherwise; or the same prize if it is black. In the standard Ellsberg pattern, most choosers prefer either colour from Urn A to either colour from Urn C, and the aversion attaches to the urn rather than to the colour: they dislike betting on red and on black from the same ambiguous urn. Assume that pattern holds for you.

Your essay should:

- state the orthodox objection to this pattern as precisely as you can — the argument from the reduction of compound lotteries, the sure-thing principle, and the claim that a probability is a probability however it was generated — and say exactly where it bites hardest;
- set out the strongest available defences of the pattern: that choosers care about the source or pedigree of the uncertainty and not merely its magnitude (sometimes called source preference, or the competence hypothesis); that an unverifiable probability estimate is itself evidence that the description of the gamble may be incomplete or wrong; and that in a world where descriptions sometimes fail, robustness rather than expected-value maximisation may be the better policy;
- decide which, if any, of those defences survives scrutiny, and in particular whether your verdict should differ between Urn B (where the generating mechanism is fully specified) and Urn C (where it is not), and how Urn D should be treated;
- say what follows for an institution rather than an individual: for instance a trustee choosing between a portfolio whose risks can be estimated and one whose risks are genuinely unmodellable, or a regulator weighing a precautionary rule against a rule based on the best available estimate;
- reach a conclusion and defend it, rather than surveying the positions.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. In particular, do not present as consensus either the claim that ambiguity aversion is straightforwardly irrational or the claim that it is straightforwardly rational, and flag where your argument depends on contested empirical claims about how people actually choose or on contested normative claims about what rationality requires.

Write in British English.
