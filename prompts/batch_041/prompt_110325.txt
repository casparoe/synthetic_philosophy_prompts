I'm a philosophy grad student working on normativity, trying to get a draft into shape, and I'd like you to act as a referee for a journal that publishes on reasons and rationality. Give me comments, and be honest about which parts are dead on arrival. I care most about whether the central argument actually works; less about prose. Please start by brainstorming the strongest objection to what I'm about to say before you write the comments themselves.

The gist: everyone separates reasons that are reasons for an attitude in the right way from reasons that merely make it useful or desirable to have the attitude. The demon who will torture you unless you believe that 2+2=5 gives you a reason of the second sort, not the first. The standard framing -- and I'm just borrowing it, not claiming originality -- is that right-kind reasons bear on whether the attitude is correct or fitting, and wrong-kind ones bear on whether having the attitude is good for you.

My move is that this framing quietly assumes the attitude has one fixed constitutive aim. Take an attitude with a feedback loop between the attitude and its object. If I trust you and my trusting you makes you more trustworthy, then the reason "trusting you will make you trustworthy" looks state-given at the start and object-given by the end -- exactly the thing the distinction is meant to sort cleanly. Same with a stock that rises because analysts believe it will rise, or a shy person who becomes brave because everyone keeps telling them they're brave. Call these loop cases.

First claim: loop cases aren't wrong-kind reasons that got promoted to right-kind; they show the right/wrong-kind line is only defined relative to a fixed point of the loop, so any account that treats the distinction as a property of the reason itself is incomplete. Second claim, weaker: a fitting-attitude account of value can survive loop cases only if it says what fixes the fixed point, and I don't think anyone has said.

So: (1) Is the distinction still doing work if it's relative like this, or have I just redescribed the problem in fancier words? (2) Can you construct a loop case where the reason is clearly wrong-kind and stays that way, which would kill my argument? (3) If you think I'm right, what's the cheapest repair -- an error theory about the distinction, or a shift to reasons-relative-to-a-purpose? Be blunt about which of my moves are just known moves wearing a hat. Don't bother with citations, I'll handle those, and please don't just tell me it's interesting -- I need to know where it breaks.
