I have been following discussions of Gödel's modal ontological argument for years, and I keep noticing that almost every treatment stops precisely where the interesting part begins: people either restate the axioms and the derivation, or they wheel out the objection that existence is not a predicate and call it a day. I would like something that lives past that point.

Write a dialogue between two interlocutors who share far more than the usual opponents do. Both accept the argument as a valid derivation in higher-order modal logic with S5. Both accept that existence claims can be treated as second-order. Neither is a Kantian, and neither is a naive theist who wants to win by assertion. The dispute is entirely internal to the argument.

Speaker A defends the argument and is willing to accept that its axioms imply what is usually called modal collapse — the result that there are no contingent truths, so that necessity, actuality and possibility all come to the same thing. A does not treat this as a reductio; he treats it as a Leibnizian conclusion that a serious theist should be prepared to pay for.

Speaker B accepts the derivation and accepts that the axiom set is consistent, but holds that collapse is a genuine reductio and that the axioms smuggle the conclusion in.

I want the two of them to work through the following, in this order, responding to each other rather than talking past each other. Each should concede at least one point where the other lands a real blow.

1. Collapse. Is the loss of all contingency fatal to the argument, or does it only show that the argument is unavailable to a theist who insists on a free creation and real creaturely alternatives? A should get to argue that the objection's real target is the objector's prior commitment to brute contingency; B should get to argue that collapse destroys the argument's usefulness, since premises that are themselves necessary cannot be entertained, weighed and then accepted.

2. A line of thought I have been chewing on and want stress-tested. In Gödel's system, Godlikeness turns out to be an essence of any Godlike being, and any property of a Godlike being follows from Godlikeness, which is itself stipulated to be positive. It looks to follow that the positive properties are exactly the property set of a single maximal individual. If that is right, then the structural axioms — closure under necessary implication, and exactly one of a property and its negation counting as positive — may already manufacture a maximal being out of any complete, implication-closed family of properties, with the entire substantive weight resting on the stipulations that Godlikeness and necessary existence are themselves positive. Have B press this as hard as it can be pressed, and have A give the best available answer rather than a dodge.

3. Uniqueness. Does the argument deliver one God, or only that some Godlike being exists in every world? If uniqueness needs an extra premise — an identity of indiscernibles, or haecceities counted among the positive properties — say so plainly, and let the speakers disagree about whether that premise is available to them.

4. Repair. If one wanted to block collapse, the options on the table seem to be: restrict which property-constructing operations the metatheory permits, weaken the definition of essence, or weaken the positivity axioms. Are these principled repairs or ad hoc rescues of a proof that has been caught doing something it should not? Both speakers should be concrete here, and neither should pretend that patching is costless.
