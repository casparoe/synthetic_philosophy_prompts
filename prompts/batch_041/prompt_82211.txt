I am a retired physiologist — forty years in respiratory medicine, with a good deal of epidemiology along the way — who has spent the last three years attending a philosophy seminar. Of the many disputes I have encountered there, one has struck me as genuinely intractable, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The dispute is over whether omissions can be causes. Our group divides cleanly on it, and neither side has moved in a year.

One side holds that they obviously can. The nurse who fails to notice a falling oxygen saturation, the gardener who does not water the plant, the clinic that does not stock the vaccine: in each case the outcome counterfactually depends on the omission, and since counterfactual dependence is what we are tracking when we make causal claims, the omission is a cause. Nothing about an absence makes the relevant counterfactual undefined; the counterfactual is perfectly well defined for an event that never occurs.

The other side holds that an omission is not the sort of thing that can stand in the causal relation at all. Causation, they say, is a relation among events — happenings, occurrences, things that take place — and a failure to water a plant is not an event; nothing happens. To call an omission a cause is at best shorthand for something else: a claim about responsibility, a claim about what would otherwise have happened, or an explanation that answers a "why" question without identifying any mechanism. They add the familiar reductio: if absences count as causes, then the plant's death was caused not only by the gardener's inaction but equally by the sun's failure to explode and by every other absent condition whose presence would have saved it, which suggests the notion has gone slack.

I notice that each side's arguments seem to presuppose its own conception of what causation is, so the exchange never quite lands. I cannot tell, however, whether that is because the dispute is verbal or because there is something substantive underneath. I also notice that the cases that divide us most sharply are not simple omissions but ones where a prevention is itself prevented — the colleague who would have administered the antidote was kept out of the room, and the keeping-out is an event while the non-administration is not — and I cannot tell whether that asymmetry is doing real work or is an artefact of how the examples are described.

My hypothesis, offered for testing rather than endorsement, has three parts. First, that the two camps attach the word "cause" to two different relations — production, on the one hand, and difference-making on the other — which coincide on ordinary positive cases and come apart precisely on the hard cases, and that this is why nothing moves. Second, that this verbal element is not the whole story, because a real question sits beneath it: whether difference-making is itself a relation among things in the world, or whether attributing an outcome to an omission always requires an expectation — what the nurse was supposed to notice, what a competent gardener would have done — in which case such attributions are irreducibly normative rather than descriptive. Third, that some of the stall comes from a question that is not about causation at all but about absences: whether an absence can be a term in any relation, and whether a thing that did not happen can do explanatory work. If the third part is right, the omission debate may be a special case of a more general problem, and not specifically a dispute about causation.

There is a further wrinkle from my own field which I would like you to take seriously rather than dismiss as confusion. In epidemiology I was taught to think of an outcome as arising when a set of component factors co-occur, no one of them necessary on its own; a cause is then any factor whose removal from the set would break it, and on that understanding a missing factor is a cause in exactly the same sense as a present one. We reasoned this way daily, and no one found it paradoxical. If our usage is coherent, then perhaps the philosophical dispute is about token causation — this death, here — while the scientific usage concerns type causation, or perhaps the philosophers are asking about the metaphysics of the causal relation while we were asking about intervention and explanation. I would like to know which, and whether the difference matters.
