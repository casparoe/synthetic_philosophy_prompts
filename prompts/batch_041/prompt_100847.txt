I've been trying to work out what I actually think about conditionality in the welfare state, and I've decided to go about it the way I go about most things now -- by cataloguing my gut verdicts on cases first and looking for the principle afterward, rather than picking a theory and defending it. I should be clear that I'm not asking what the law is anywhere, or what any particular agency does; I want the moral structure underneath my own reactions, and I'm willing to be told that some of them can't be kept. So: a healthy adult who quits a decent job to surf and then draws unemployment -- sanctioning him and requiring work seems right to me. A laid-off worker who turns down a job paying half his old wage and is then cut off -- that seems wrong. A man claiming for a relative who died two years ago -- prosecuting him is clearly right, and the fact that he was poor doesn't move me at all. A woman doing casual undeclared work while claiming, reporting none of it -- I feel much less strongly about sanctioning her, though it's the same species of dishonesty. A single mother whose childcare falls through and who misses a mandatory appointment -- sanctioning her is wrong. A man with a drinking problem who skips the treatment program attached to his claim -- also wrong. A woman who won't take a job requiring Saturday work, on religious grounds -- I don't think she should be sanctioned. A man who won't take that same job because it's beneath his qualifications -- I think he should be. A young man who refuses a job two hundred miles away because he doesn't want to leave his friends -- sanction him; a woman who refuses the same job to care for a disabled parent -- don't.

I can feel at least two of these pulling against each other, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a defect in me or a real distinction. The Saturday case and the qualifications case look like twins -- both are refusals of an available job for a personal reason -- yet I want opposite verdicts, and the only difference I can name is that one reason is one I'd expect a stranger to respect and the other is one I wouldn't, which feels like it's smuggling in respectability rather than desert. A friend of mine, who spends too much time on the rationalist blogs, suggested I stop asking whether people deserve their benefits and start asking what the sanction is doing -- whether it's a price we're charging for a choice or a punishment for a character -- but that distinction doesn't survive the surfing case, where I'm happy to charge the price and equally happy to call the man a freeloader. What I'd like from you is the principle, or small set of principles, that actually generates these verdicts; where a principle has to grow complications to save all of them, say so and tell me which verdict is carrying the weight; and then apply whatever you land on to two cases I haven't listed -- a claimant who fails a literacy requirement he could have met with effort but never had any reason to want, and a claimant who lies about a disability he really does have -- so I can see whether the principle is recognizably mine or just tidy.
