I'm a staff writer covering science and ideas, and I also host a long-form interview podcast that sits somewhere between journalism and philosophy; the show's premise is that we spend ninety minutes on a single argument instead of ten minutes on a book. Next month's guest is a philosopher of mind who has spent her career defending a version of panpsychism — the thesis that experience, or at minimum some proto-experiential property, is among the fundamental features of the world rather than something that shows up only once brains get complicated enough — and who has lately been insisting that the view carries ethical consequences, which is exactly why I booked her. My listeners can follow the metaphysics of consciousness well enough, but almost none of them have thought through what it would mean for ethics if the metaphysics were true, and I do not want ninety minutes of her restating the position. Please draft ten to twelve interview questions, arranged as a sequence rather than a list, with a follow-up under each one keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. No generic openers, and nothing that amounts to asking her to explain her own view to us.

There are three things I specifically want her pressed on. First, valence: she holds that phenomenal experience and hedonic valence — the felt good-or-bad dimension — are separable, so that the micro-experiences she posits are affectively neutral, meaning there is something it is like to be one of these entities but nothing that is either pleasant or unpleasant about it. I want a question that forces her to say what work affectively neutral experience can do in an ethical argument at all, given that most people's reason for caring about consciousness is that it is the seat of suffering, and then the follow-up she has earned, which is that moral status and welfare are two different things and that a subject can be owed consideration without having interests that can be set back. Second, what her critics call the decombination problem — the mirror image of the familiar combination problem, and the price she accepts for her preferred variant, in which the cosmos as a whole is the one fundamental subject and finite minds like ours are derivative or partial rather than additional: if that is right, does the ethical patient become the cosmos, and what would it even mean to wrong something whose experience is not organized around a boundary, a body, or a future? I would like that thread pursued to the point where she has to choose between a cosmic patient that is genuinely morally considerable and a cosmic subject whose experience is so unlike ours that calling it a patient is a category mistake. Third, what I've been calling moral-status inflation: if experience is everywhere, the number of subjects in any room is enormous and unbounded, and I want a question about whether aggregation across micro-subjects is even coherent — whether the utilitarian arithmetic she presumably finds absurd at the micro level can be quarantined without giving up the claim that micro-subjects matter.

One more note about her style: when the ethical questions get sharp, she tends to reply that she is a metaphysician and that these are questions for the ethicists, which is fair as far as it goes but has a way of ending the discussion. Please build the sequence so that this move becomes progressively harder to make, and close with a question about what she would count as evidence against the ethical extension of her view, as distinct from evidence against the metaphysics. Keep the whole thing self-contained: assume that neither she nor I can look anything up, so any technical term you use should be spelled out inside the question itself rather than gestured at by name, and do not send me to any literature — I want the questions to do the explaining. Write in American English, in a register I can read aloud on air: precise but not stiff, and each follow-up should make clear what it is responding to.
