I am writing a novel set in the 1660s, and one of my characters — a provincial magistrate who has been reading Pascal — delivers the following argument in a chapter I am now revising. I need to know whether he is right, and if he is not, which of his steps fails, because the scene's irony depends on the answer. He argues as follows.

(1) A law binds my conscience only insofar as it is just; an unjust law can bind my prudence but not my conscience.
(2) Nothing is just merely because it has been established, since establishment is only a fact about who had the power to establish it.
(3) Yet whenever anyone has tried to say what justice itself is, apart from the customs of his own country, his answer has tracked his interests, his sect, or his temperament.
(4) So either there is a justice we can know independently of custom, or there is not.
(5) There is not: every proposed ground of justice has turned out to be either a local custom dressed up as a universal rule, or a rule whose consistent application would dissolve any state that tried to live by it.
(6) Therefore no established law is unjust in the sense that would release me from it; the distinction between a just and an unjust law does no work.
(7) Therefore the magistrate who hangs a man for theft and the rebel who hangs the magistrate are separated only by force — and the magistrate's force is the more reliable.

The conclusion is plainly too much: it makes the difference between a lawful king and a usurper a matter of who wins, and it would silence every objection to a bad law. I would rather keep (1) than (7), and I cannot tell whether he has made an error or whether the position is simply true and I must write the novel around it. So tell me which step to cut, why it looks so plausible in its place, and what cutting it costs him. If the repair requires something Pascal himself would refuse — a justice we can know, say, or a divine order that force merely serves — I want to know that too, because the scene only works if his position is coherent enough to be frightening.
