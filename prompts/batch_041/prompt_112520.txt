So my friend Dan and I just spent two hours arguing about a blog post he read, and now I genuinely don't know which of us is being dumb. He's smart — does stats, reads a lot — he's just not a philosopher, and the post was one of those "here's this famous principle in three paragraphs" explainers. I want you to audit his version of it claim by claim: where would someone who actually works on this stuff nod along, where would they wince, and what's flat out wrong.

Here's his version, more or less as he told it to me:

'The Principal Principle, from Lewis, says your credence in something should equal its objective chance, whenever you know what the chance is. It's the bridge between subjective and objective probability. There's one escape hatch: it doesn't apply if you already know how things turned out — obviously if you know the coin landed heads you're not going to sit there at 0.5. Beyond that it's just a coherence constraint on your credences, not a claim about the world at all. If you knew the chance was 30% and you still believed at 70% you'd be Dutch bookable, that's the entire content. And really it means objective chance just is the credence a perfectly rational, perfectly informed agent would have — Lewis is basically defining chance in terms of rational belief. The only people who get hurt by it are the Humeans, because of undermining: if chance is nothing but the pattern of what actually happens, there's always some chance that the future undermines the chance itself, and the principle tells you to defer to a chance that could be wrong about itself, which is a contradiction, so Humean accounts of chance are dead. The guy who wrote the post even has a fix — only defer to chances that are stable, ones the future can't undermine. So the principle is broken as stated.'

A few specific things I want you to sort out, because I couldn't:

First, that escape hatch. I have a feeling the real clause is about which information is admissible, and that he's mangled it — but I can't tell if he's made it too narrow or too broad. Is it just "you don't already know the outcome," or does it exclude more than that? And does ordinary evidence about the case count as knowing the outcome? Does knowing past frequencies count as evidence I'm not allowed to use, or is that exactly the kind of thing the principle lets me use? This matters because he used it to argue the whole thing is vacuous for anyone who knows anything.

Second, is it really just a coherence requirement? That smells wrong to me — it seems like it would have to be a substantive principle about how two different things relate, not something you can get out of the probability axioms plus good bookkeeping. But if it's not derivable, what kind of principle is it, and what would violating it actually be a failure of?

Third, does "chance = ideal credence" follow from any of this? Because that sounds like the opposite of what I'd expect from someone who thought chance was a real feature of the world rather than a fact about us.

Fourth, the undermining bit. Is that an argument or a refutation? And does the "stable chance" fix actually help, or is it quietly a different principle wearing the old one's name — in which case does his conclusion still follow from the parts of his summary that were accurate?

And last, my own worry, which I'd like you to take seriously rather than just reassure me about: that the admissibility clause is a fudge that lets the principle speak up only when it's convenient and go quiet whenever the agent has some other evidence, so it never really constrains anyone. Is that fair, or is the clause doing definate work? I'd rather know if Dan's right about something and I'm the one being stubborn.
