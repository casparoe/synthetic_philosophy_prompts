ok so I'm trying to write down where I actually come down on the centipede game and backward induction, and the more I write the less it hangs together, but I honestly can't tell which piece is the bad one. Here's the set:

(1) Practical rationality is just expected utility maximization with respect to your credences. There's no extra faculty of practical reason that tells you to do something your credences say is worse.

(2) But I also don't think it's ever rational to be certain about what another agent is going to do. If I say "I know she takes at node 2, no doubt about it," I'm being dogmatic, not rational. There's always got to be some probability left on the other person surprising me.

(3) Subgame perfect equilibrium is the right solution concept for finite games of perfect information: an action that isn't optimal at the node where it's taken isn't rational, full stop. So in the centipede, taking at the first node is the rational move.

(4) I don't think that's a reductio of backward induction, either. I've never been moved by the "but think how much we'd both get if we kept going" line; I think that intuition is just a mistake, confusing the value of an outcome with the value of an action.

(5) That said, I think a solution concept has to earn its keep epistemically. If the theory says the rational outcome is X, that has to fall out of what the players know about each other -- common knowledge of rationality, that kind of thing -- and not just out of a refinement that happens to single out X. If backward induction can't be gotten from the players' knowledge, then it isn't what rationality requires, it's just a nice property of the equilibrium set.

(6) Which is why I dont buy the usual escape hatch for the centipede, the "well, nobody can really be sure the other guy is rational, so continuing can pay" move. That's not a solution, it's a change of subject -- it resolves the paradox by making somebody a little bit irrational, and I want the paradox to be about rational players.

(7) But I'm totally happy with the reputation story for the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, the one where there's a small chance the other player is the type who reciprocates, and that small chance is enough to make cooperating rational for a while. I think that's a real result and it explains alot about why people cooperate in the lab.

For some reason (4) and (7) feel like they can't both be true, but I can't articulate why, and I'm probably wrong about that.

Rank the tensions by how much they cost me, say what would have to give in each case and what I'd lose by giving it, and then tell me the single revision that cleans up the most for the least. Also, be straight with me about which of these is actually settled among people who work on this and which is genuinely contested -- I don't want a confident answer that's just one camp's line dressed up as the consensus.
