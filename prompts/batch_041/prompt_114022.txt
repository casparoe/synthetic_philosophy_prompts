I've been teaching Dutch book arguments for years and a seminar last week has left me less sure I know what I'm teaching, so I'd like to think it through properly before I do it again. The pitch I give is the standard one: if your credences don't satisfy the probability axioms, a bookie can put together a package of bets, each of which is fair by your own lights, such that you lose money whichever way the world turns out — and this is supposed to be why probabilism is a requirement of rationality. I always make it concrete with my own credences, which are a mess: 0.7 that it rains tomorrow and 0.5 that it doesn't (yes, they sum to more than 1, that's the point of the exercise). A bookie can then sell me a ticket paying £10 if it rains for £7, and a ticket paying £10 if it doesn't rain for £5, so I've laid out £12 and can collect at most £10. Every step of that I'm happy with. What's nagging me is the next step, the one I've never really examined: doesn't this show that someone with incoherent credences is simply *guaranteed* to lose money, in the ordinary sense — that her incoherence is a real, predictable hole in her finances, like a leaky roof? Because if that's right, coherence starts to look like a prudential norm rather than the purely epistemic one I've been telling them it is, and worse, it looks like something we could in principle check empirically: find the incoherent people and watch their wallets. That can't be the intended reading, but I can't articulate why not. A student pressed me on it and I floundered — she pointed out that she never bets on anything, on anything at all, so what is the theorem supposed to be saying about *her*? — and another asked whether the loss is really "guaranteed" given that it only bites if you accept the whole package, and that you accept the whole package only because each bet is fair by your own lights, which feels like it's already assuming something about how credences connect to betting behaviour.

So: what does the Dutch book theorem actually establish, and exactly where in the argument from "there exists a book" to "you're irrational" does an extra premise have to be supplied? Does the "I just don't gamble" reply have real force, or is it a dodge, and if it has force what's the best thing to say back to it? I have a vague sense that something quite different and more fragile is going on in the diachronic version, where the bookie exploits not my present credences but my own plans for updating them — that some assumption about my future self is being smuggled in there, but I couldn't tell you which one or why it's needed. I'm teaching this to a small MA group who've had a semester of decision theory and nothing else, and I'd rather give them the real structure of the thing, warts and all, than a slogan; no names or reading lists needed, just tell me how the machinery actually hangs together and where it's load-bearing.
