I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory and I'm TA-ing a 90-minute session of an upper-level seminar on distributive justice, 16 students, and I want to run an activity rather than lecture. The problem is that most of them have already met the Wilt Chamberlain argument in some intro course and think they get it — star draws a crowd, fans voluntarily hand over their quarters, and boom, any patterned principle of distribution gets upset. They nod and move on, and they never feel the part that's actually doing the work, which is that it isn't one voluntary transfer that breaks the pattern, it's that holding a pattern in place over time requires continuous interference with people's free choices, and that interference is supposed to feel like a real cost, something you'd have to police. I want them to be the ones doing the policing and to hate it. So please design the thing properly: rules, roles, materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened back onto the argument. Tokens or candy, no real money.

Here's my rough sketch to build on. Everyone starts with an identical pile of tokens. Round one, free trade — but I quietly give two or three students a productive talent (say, they're the only ones who can make a widget everyone else wants), and let the market do its thing until the holdings are lopsided. Then the class votes on a patterned rule: nobody may end a round above some ceiling, and I assign two students as pattern-keepers whose whole job is to inspect trades and claw back anything over the line. Then we run more rounds. My hope is that the pattern-keepers' job metastasizes — they have to watch every transfer, the talented kids start hiding trades or just stop producing becuase it isn't worth it, and the enforcers get resented, and by the end nobody wants to be the one holding the pattern up. Keep the names Nozick and Chamberlain out of the room until the debrief.

Then the debrief needs to be honest about a few things. First, map the moments: which bit corresponds to the historical-versus-end-state distinction, which bit to the claim that patterned principles demand constant interference, and where a defender could push back — e.g. that our starting piles were artificially equal, so the whole thing quietly assumes a just baseline and ducks the rectification question. Second, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'd bet someone games the pattern-keeper, or the class just keeps re-patterning because the stakes are fake) and how to handle each. Third, be frank about what the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself — I suspect it's the historical-acquisition worry, since I'm handing everyone a clean equal start, but tell me if there's more.
