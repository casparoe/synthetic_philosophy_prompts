I am a novelist who runs an upper-level seminar called Character and Counterfactual, and I need a single essay question for the final exam — one that separates the students who have genuinely understood dispositions from the ones who have merely memorized the labels.

Take on the role of a philosopher of mind specializing in the metaphysics of dispositions. Before drafting anything, think carefully about where the received treatment is thinnest, and brainstorm several candidate cruxes before settling on the one that best resists being answered by reciting the standard distinctions.

The background my students will have is this: a disposition is standardly analyzed as a state of a thing such that, if the characteristic stimulus occurred while that state were held fixed, the characteristic manifestation would occur. A finkish mechanism destroys the state precisely when the stimulus arrives, so the disposition was present but the counterfactual fails. A masking mechanism leaves the state intact but blocks its manifestation. The two are usually told apart by asking whether the mechanism alters the state itself or only interferes with its expression.

Here is the case I want the question to turn on. Two characters, Ilse and Roderick, are each disposed to protect a friend who is being threatened. Each carries an implanted device. When Ilse's friend is threatened, the device deletes, for the duration of the threat, the history of trust that — let us grant — partly constitutes her attachment to the friend; she does nothing. When Roderick's friend is threatened, his device leaves his attachment entirely intact but floods him with an unrelated terror that drowns out any action; he does nothing. From the outside, and across every counterfactual test the students can devise, the two are indistinguishable.

Write the question, a model answer of roughly six hundred words, and a marking scheme that tells an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must make. The question should require students to (i) decide whether each character's disposition survives, (ii) say what, if anything, could ground a fink-versus-mask verdict when the counterfactual profiles are identical, and (iii) propose a refinement of the counterfactual analysis and identify a further case that their own refinement mishandles. Keep the question itself to a paragraph. In the marking scheme, list five must-hit moves with the marks each is worth out of twenty, and for each one a single line on how a weaker answer typically gets it wrong.
