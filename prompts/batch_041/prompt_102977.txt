So I'm in this seminar on medieval political thought and we just spent two sessions on Aquinas and tyrannicide, and there's an analogy that keeps surfacing in the discussion and I can't stop chewing on it. The picture is that the political community is a body, the ruler is something like its head or at least a vital part, and a tyrant is a diseased or gangrenous limb — so just as you'd amputate a rotten limb to save the body, you're justified in cutting the tyrant off to save the commonwealth. A couple people in the group lean on it pretty hard to license getting rid of a tyrant, sometimes by killing him, and they act like Aquinas is basically on board, since he does say tyrannical rule is unjust (it aims at the ruler's private good rather than the common good, so toppling it isn't automatically the sin of sedition). But when I actually read him he seems way more cagey than the analogy suggests: he warns that if you disturb things badly you can end up doing the people more harm than the tyrant was doing, he insists a private individual has no business killing a tyrant and that the sword belongs to public authority, and somewhere in his advice to a king he basically says that trying to throw off one tyrant can land you with a worse one. So the analogy feels like its carrying more weight than his actual view will bear, and I want to see exactly where.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Start by laying out the correspondences — what maps onto the body, the head, the diseased limb, the disease itself, the surgeon, and the health that's supposedly being restored — and then tell me where the mapping actually breaks. I'm already suspicious of a few spots: who counts as the surgeon and by what warrant; whether a disease in a body politic is ever really exogenous the way gangrene is, or whether it grows out of the body's own life; what it means that the limbs are themselves people with wills and judgements who might not consent to the operation; that amputation is irreversible in a way political surgery maybe isn't; and the awkwardness that if the king is the head, then removing the tyrant is decapitation and you've left the body headless. Then tell me which of those breaks actually matter for the specific conclusion the analogy is being used to license (that killing the tyrant is justified) and which are just decorative, and whether that conclusion survives once the breaks that matter are on the table. If it turns out the conclusion was only ever coming from the analogy and not from anything Aquinas actually argued, say so plainly. And if there's a better analogy that would track his real, cautious view — the public-authority-only, beware-the-worse-tyrant version — tell me what it is and why it fits him better.

Please write it out as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables — argue it through in sentences the way you'd write an email, even though I know a table of correspondences is the obvious way to show this.
