I run the workplace-conduct intake desk at a company of about four thousand people. Ten or so times a week my team has to decide whether something a colleague said carried a meaning the words themselves do not state — a nudge, a warning, a threat with the edges filed off, a joke doing the work of an insult. Each case can end in a dismissal or a tribunal, the finding goes into a written report that our employment lawyers will read, and I have an afternoon per case and no philosopher on staff. I want a procedure we can actually run, and I want it sharp enough that a colleague who disagrees with me can point to the step where we parted ways.

Build it on the machinery Grice developed for speaker meaning and conversational implicature, and push it past the parts of that machinery everyone already knows. The procedure has to keep four things apart: what the words literally encode, which is thinner than it feels; content that any competent speaker would treat as part of what was asserted even though no word encodes it — the middle ground sometimes called impliciture or explicature — which cannot be withdrawn without contradiction; genuine conversational implicature, which is calculable from the maxims and the context, cancellable (the speaker can disavow it without contradicting herself), and either detachable or not depending on whether it rides on what was said or on how it was said; and conventional implicature, carried by particular words such as *but*, *even*, or *therefore*, which survives denial and embedding. It also has to respect the intention condition: on Grice's account a speaker means something only if she intends her audience to recognise the intention, and intends that recognition to figure in the audience's response. Two consequences matter to me. A hearer's private inference is not an implicature unless it was intended; and actual uptake is not required, only recognisability, so a remark can mean something even if the person it was aimed at missed it. And I need to know what the procedure does with the case this machinery handles worst: a remark pitched so that only some of those present can recognise what was meant, while everyone else hears it as innocuous, with the speaker denying any intent.

What I need is the procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a finding that nothing was conveyed, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than evidence. Name the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and state the default it falls back on when the evidence is thin. Have it return one of a small, fixed set of verdicts, and say which of those I can put in a report as a finding and which are only background for the investigator. Everything it uses must be public: what was said, to whom, where, and with what history. Nothing may depend on my guessing at a speaker's inner state, and nothing may require me to question the speaker and then treat the answer as evidence against her.

Then run it on three cases. First: a team lead, greeting a colleague back from parental leave in front of the team, says "Good to have you back — we'll keep the real work for the rest of us." The colleague laughs; a junior employee reports it the following week; the team lead says it was obviously a joke. Second: a manager tells a staff member whose role is being wound down, "I'm sure you'll make the right decision." In that team's history the phrase has preceded previous exits, and the staff member reads it as pressure to resign; the manager says he meant only that he trusted her judgment. Third: a remark made quietly to one person, neutral on its face; the person addressed noticed nothing and reports nothing, and the complaint comes from a colleague who overheard it through an open door and knows the history. At least one of the three should come out against the reading I would have expected, and you should say so plainly rather than rescuing it. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do — the cases where the honest output is that semantics and pragmatics have nothing to say and the decision has to be made on other grounds.
