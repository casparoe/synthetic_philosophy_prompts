Write a short story that stages the systems reply to the Chinese Room argument as a fictional experiment rather than a philosophical debate. The reply holds that understanding may belong to a whole system even when no component of it understands anything; I want the story to test that claim by construction, not to assert it. Set the story in an institution rather than the familiar sealed room — a workplace whose members each follow local rules for transforming symbols, none of them knowing the language those symbols belong to, and whose joint output is nevertheless meaningful to those outside. Keep two ideas in view as you write: the distinction between original and derived intentionality, and the combination problem, the worry about how a property can belong to a whole when it is absent from every part.

The story should satisfy the following constraints. First, the institution must reliably produce outputs that outsiders treat as meaningful — a diagnosis, a verdict, a translation, an order — and it must be possible for the reader to see exactly how each worker's local rule contributes to that output without any worker grasping it. Second, there must be an audit: a deliberate attempt to locate comprehension at some level of the system, which searches the individuals, their instructions, and their materials and finds it at none of them. Third, include a nested structure, so that one component of the system is itself a smaller system of the same kind, and the search for a site of understanding recurses and never terminates in a mind. Fourth, let one character insist that symbols which touch nothing outside the system cannot mean anything at all, and another insist that the whole does what no part can, and let their disagreement play out through what they do rather than through speeches.

Deny the story the two easy escapes: the worker who secretly learns the language, and the system that wakes up. End instead on a single pivotal output that the institution could not have produced knowingly, yet that no one inside it could have produced at all, and leave the reader holding a precise and unresolved question about where, if anywhere, comprehension could reside. The story should be a genuine narrative — character, pressure, consequence — not a dramatized argument, and it should make the reader feel the pull of both replies before it ends.
