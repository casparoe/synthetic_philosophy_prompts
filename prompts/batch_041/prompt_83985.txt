I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind — my official supervisor is a vision scientist who mainly cares about attention, so I've had to build my metaphysics-of-consciousness life raft on my own — and in a couple weeks I'm giving a 40-minute paper at our joint mind-and-cognition work-in-progress session, the one that mixes the philosophy dept with the psych people who study metacognition and confidence judgements. The paper defends a deliberately unfashionable line: a limited epiphenomenalism about phenomenal properties. Not the crude "qualia do nothing" version — I claim qualia never enter the causal chain that produces physical events, but they're nomically correlated with, and structurally mirrored by, the physical states that do the causal work. My central move is a distinction I've been calling thin tracking vs. thick tracking. Thin tracking = my judgments about my experience covary reliably with my experience. Thick tracking = the covariation is explained by the experience's guiding the judgment or making it true. I argue that the paradox of phenomenal judgment — the old puzzle about how our judgments about experience could be reliable if experience does no causal work — only ever needed thin tracking, and that my critics quietly need thick; once you see that, the epiphenomenalist is off the hook. I tell a selection story in which creatures whose meta-level judgments systematically misrepresented their own internal states got out-competed, so reliability comes cheap and doesn't require the qualia to do anything. The illusionists are obviously going to say that a zombie twin with the same metacognitive architecture makes all the same judgments, so either the phenomenal state is doing nothing the physical state wasn't already doing — in which case why posit it — or the correlation is a brute cosmic coincidence, and I honestly don't know which horn I'd rather be impaled on.
