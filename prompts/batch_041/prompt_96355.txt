I'm in a jurisprudence seminar this term and I've been going back and fourth with a classmate about the Hart–Fuller debate, and I think I've argued myself into a corner I can't get out of. The way I understand the setup: the fight is over the grudge informer cases after the war, the woman who turned her husband in under a Nazi statute and then got prosecuted for it afterwards. Hart says the statute was valid law — monstrous law, but law — and that if you want to punish her you should do it with an explicit retrospective statute and own up to what you're doing. Fuller says it wasn't law at all, because it failed what he called the inner morality of law, the eight principles of legality: generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, not demanding the impossible, constancy, and congruence between the rules on the books and what officials actually do. So far so good, I think.

Here's where I get stuck. If failing those eight is what stops something from being law, then doesn't passing them make something law — and if they're supposed to be a morality and not just a drafting checklist, doesn't passing them make it *good* law? Because then any regime that keeps its rules general, published, prospective, clear, consistent, feasible, stable, and actually followed by its own officials would have a valid and morally legitimate legal order, whatever the rules say. So a meticulously administered slave code, or a deportation bureaucracy where every regulation is gazetted months in advance and applied exactly as written, comes out as law in the fullest sense — and Fuller, who insists that legality is a real moral good rather than mere good housekeeping, would be stuck calling it good law. That can't be right, and I can't find where the mistake is.

I did try to check myself, and I'm fairly sure Fuller somewhere says the eight come in degrees, so that only a total failure in one of the directions takes you outside the category of law altogether, with partial failures just making for worse law. But that seems to make it worse rather than better: if that's the bar, nearly anything counts as a legal system and the inner morality stops doing any real work. So, on Fuller's view: is the punctilious slave code law? Is it just? And if the inner morality really is compatible with monstrous substance, what's left of his reply to Hart — my classmate's line is that the two of them agree about the outcome in the informer case and are just fighting over which word to use, and I'm starting to think he might be right. I'd much rather someone tell me what I'm conflating than give me another summary of both positions, I've read plenty of those.
