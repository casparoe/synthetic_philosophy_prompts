I am assembling a comparative project on affirmative action and quotas, and I want the specification before I read any more defences of it. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of affirmative action must meet. Take the subject to cover deliberate differential treatment of members of designated groups in admissions, employment, and appointment: the race-conscious university admissions that the US Supreme Court recently curtailed, the caste-based reservation systems in India with their intra-group exclusions and their ceiling on the total proportion of seats that may be reserved, and the statutory boardroom gender quotas of the kind Norway adopted. Pitch the theory at the level of moral and political justification rather than constitutional doctrine, though it should say what it owes to the legal constraints under which such schemes actually operate. Fix the purpose as follows: the theory must be good enough to tell a policymaker whether to adopt a scheme, and if so how to design it, so adequacy is relative to that task and not to the interests of any one camp in the existing dispute.

Give me the requirements list. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it — the phenomenon the theory must explain, the case it must get right, the distinction it must not collapse — and mark whether the requirement is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. I expect the list to include at least the following kinds of demand, though you should add, drop, or split them as the argument requires: that the theory account for the paradigm cases on both sides, including a scheme most people regard as clearly permissible and one most regard as clearly impermissible; that it answer the objection from equal treatment rather than evade it; that it distinguish compensation for past wrongdoing, remedy for present discrimination, and redress of structural disadvantage, and say whether the same scheme can rest on more than one of these at once; that it distinguish a quota as a floor from a quota as a ceiling; that it respect the difference between group representation and individual desert; that it say something about the exit condition, namely what would have to become true for the scheme to be withdrawn; that it handle the internal heterogeneity of beneficiary groups, as the creamy-layer exclusions in the Indian system make vivid; and that it say whether anything unifies the admissions case, the reservation case, and the boardroom case, or whether the same structural form is justified differently in each.

Two things matter most to me. First, flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say why: I would rather have the list shown to be inconsistent at some point than have it quietly trimmed to fit a favoured theory. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and whether that requirement is itself contested.

Then, briefly — no more than a few sentences per family — say how the main types of justification fare against the list: backward-looking compensation, forward-looking diversity, equality-of-opportunity or level-playing-field accounts, and the democratic-representation justification often offered for quotas. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Where you are giving your own view rather than reporting the state of the argument, say so. Write in British English, and do not supply citations or a reading list; I will add those myself.
