I'm putting together a unit on causation by omission for my senior seminar and I've got an argument I wrote as a teaching example that I now can't get out of. It goes like this.

(1) If someone fails to do something, and the bad outcome wouldn't have happened had they done it, then their failure is a cause of the outcome. That's just what we mean when we say the lifeguard's distraction caused the drowning, or the landlord's not installing a smoke alarm caused the death.
(2) So what makes an omission a cause is the counterfactual dependence of the outcome on the failure: had the thing been done, the outcome wouldn't have happened.
(3) Now consider my lawn, which came in thick and green this June. Had a bison been grazing on it all spring, it would not have come in thick and green. No bison was grazing on it.
(4) So by (2), the absence of a bison on my lawn is a cause of the lawn coming in thick and green.
(5) But the same holds for the absence of a drought, the absence of a plague of locusts, the absence of my neighbours goat, and the absence of every one of my colleagues who never came over to trample it. So every negative fact about my lawn, and every person who didn't act on it, is a cause of how the lawn turned out.
(6) But a relation on which everyone and everything counts as a cause of every outcome it failed to prevent is not the discriminating, explanatory relation we're after when we ask what caused something. At that point the word cause has stopped doing any work.
(7) So (1) and (2) are false. Omissions aren't causes at all, and the lifeguard and landlord cases have to be handled some other way.

I'd much rather keep (1). It seems definately insane to me to tell a first year that the landlord didn't cause the death. But I can't see how to keep (1) without landing in (6). One colleague says the real problem is (6), that I'm just assuming causation has to discriminate and that there's nothing wrong with saying everything is a cause of everything in some thin sense — I don't buy it but I can't say why. Another says I've mixed up causation with causal explanation and should just stop worrying. I don't want a reading list, I want to know which step goes, why it looked fine to me when I wrote it, and what I'm paying if I drop it. And if the answer is that some distinction rescues (1), tell me honestly whether the distinction is doing any work or just renaming the problem.
