I'm a fifth-year phil of econ student and in two weeks I have to present at our department's decision theory workshop, defending the line that Hofstadter's superrationality is a genuine requirement of practical rationality and not just a cute reframing of cooperation. The paper is mostly about the two scenarios in his June 1983 Scientific American piece (the one reprinted in Metamagical Themas): first the Platonia Dilemma, where twenty people are each told by an eccentric Oklahoma oil trillionaire that they can send in a telegram with just their name and win a billion dollars, except the prize is void if two or more of them reply, and any attempt to find out who the others are or to split the money afterwards kills the whole offer; and then the Luring Lottery he actually ran with readers, where the money is divided by however many entries come in, so the individually sensible move (enter once, hope nobody else does) is collectively self-defeating in a way that's worse the more people reason it through. My thesis is that in a problem where everyone knows everyone is in the same position with the same information, you're not just allowed but required to ask what we should all do rather than what I should do given what they'll do, and that this dissolves the Platonia Dilemma (the superrational move is a mixed strategy — each participant enters with probability one over the number of participants, so the expected number of replies is one and each person's chance of being the sole replier is well above zero) and makes sense of why the lottery is a trap rather than a puzzle. I also want to argue that the standard dominance argument doesn't touch this, because dominance reasoning silently assumes your choice and the others' choices are probabilistically independent, and in these symmetric setups that assumption is exactly what's in dispute.

Here's who'll be in the room, and this is what I actually need help with. Two hardcore game theorists, both Nash-equilibrium people, one of whom has said to me in the hallway that superrationality is either incoherent or just correlated equilibrium with extra steps and a Kantian accent; one evidential decision theorist who I suspect will say I've reinvented EDT and gotten the right answer for reasons she'll want to correct; one causal decision theorist who is going to call it magical thinking, and who is smart enough to make that stick if I don't have a reply ready; a behavioral economist who runs public-goods and lottery experiments and who will not care at all about the decision theory but will want to know whether real subjects' deviations from Nash look anything like what superrationality predicts, and whether Hofstadter's own lottery returns (a lot of people entered, as I recall, though I don't want to lean on the exact numbers) count as evidence for or against; and one philosopher of practical reason who will press the hardest question of all, which is whether "you and the others are in the same position" is supposed to generate a *reason* or just a heuristic, and if it generates a reason, whether it's a reason you have as an individual or only as a member of a collective that doesn't exist.
