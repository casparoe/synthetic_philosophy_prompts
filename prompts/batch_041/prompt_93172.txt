Ok so context first, because it matters for the shape of the thing: I'm a novelist, third book, and the current one has a narrator who's a translator by trade and gets quietly, unhealthily fixated on Frank Ramsey's Epilogue — the short piece that got tacked onto the posthumous collection of his logical essays, the one where he says he doesn't feel small in front of the size of the universe, that the stars can't think or love, and that his picture of the world is drawn in perspective and not to scale. I'm applying for a four-month residency that pays a little and gives me a desk, and the application asks for a short "research proposal" — not for the novel as such, but for the reading-and-thinking half of it, which they apparently take seriously, bless them. So I need you to turn my vague itch into an actual proposal, maybe a page and a half, two pages max, and the panel is mixed — a couple of philosophers, mostly artists and one retired editor — so it has to be precise without being a jargon soup. Don't give me a reading list or citations, I'll fill those in myself; refer to positions and arguments and I'll do the sourcing.

Here's the itch. Ramsey's move in that piece looks like a reply to the cosmic-insignificance gloom — the Russell-ish mood where the vastness of things makes our little concerns look like nothing — but I can't tell if it's an argument or a very charming piece of psychological self-management, and I think the whole proposal lives or dies on that distinction. Reading one: he's making a claim about value itself, that love and thought are what matter, full stop, and that sheer scale is simply not a consideration that bears on the question. If that's the claim, he owes us something — an account of why the perspective isn't a distortion, or better, why there's no scale-neutral point of view from which it would be one. Reading two: he's only reporting what humans can't help caring about, in which case he hasn't touched the gloom at all, he's just shown we're built to ignore it, which is a deflection wearing a nice coat. And reading three, the one I keep coming back to: the perspective metaphor is doing real work, because a picture in perspective isn't a false picture, and the demand to see things "to scale" presupposes a viewpoint nobody actually occupies — so the question of what the universe is really worth, sub specie aeternitatis, isn't a hard question but an incoherent one. What I want the proposal to do is say which of these is defensible, what each costs, and whether reading three can be stated precisely enough to be argued for rather than just asserted. A secondary wrinkle I'd like in there: Ramsey was a militant atheist and not obviously in the market for consolation, and he was also a subjectivist about value elsewhere, so there's a tension between the confidence of that Epilogue and the rest of his temperment — I want the proposal to say honestly whether that tension is real or whether I'm inventing it because it's good for the novel. Also worth flagging in the proposal: the piece is short and rough and unfinished-feeling, so part of the honesty is saying up front what happens if a fragment that size can't sustain the thesis I want to hang on it.

Method, month by month, since they'll want that. Month one: reconstruct the position in my own words and separate the three readings properly, including the sub-question of whether "importance" in that piece is a relational notion — importance-to-someone — or a monadic one, because I suspect the readings turn on that and nobody states it cleanly. Month two: pressure-test each reading against the obvious objections — the consolation-versus-claim dilemma, the relativism worry if importance is relational, and the question of whether the perspective view quietly needs a substantive theory of value it can't pay for. Month three: draft the long middle chapter, aiming to get reading three into a form where it's an argument with premises rather than a mood, and see whether it survives; also draft the novel chapter that dramatizes the whole mess, since the residency people like deliverables that aren't only notes. Month four: revise, and write an honest closing memo — what held, what I had to give up, what I'd need another year to do. What a result looks like: a clean statement of which reading survives and why the others don't, plus one worked example (I'm thinking the heat-death passage, where he turns to the eventual end of everything and seems untroubled — that's the hardest case for reading three and I want to know if it breaks it). What counts as failure: if all three readings collapse into the same thing under pressure, or if reading three turns out to need a full theory of value that four months and my day job can't produce — in which case I'd want the proposal to say so in advance and say what the fallback is. And a scope note: I'd rather the proposal promise a tight, arguable claim about one paragraph of Ramsey than a survey of the whole meaning-of-life question, because the survey version is exactly the thing I could write without leaving the house, and the whole point of the residency is to be somewhere else.
