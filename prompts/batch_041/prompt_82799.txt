I chair the Monday investment committee at a mid-sized long-only asset manager: seven people, roughly forty minutes per name, and a standing rule that we do not open a position until the committee has reached a view. Over the past two years I have noticed a pattern I cannot shake. Once two or three of us have spoken in favour of a thesis, the remaining speakers almost always land in the same place, and afterwards nobody can reconstruct what the fourth, fifth and sixth people actually contributed. My worry is not the familiar one about groupthink or pressure to conform. It is the sharper possibility from the economics of information: that at some point our discussion stops aggregating private information and starts transmitting only the fact that a view already exists, so that the agreement of the seventh person is evidence of nothing at all — and that this can happen while every individual in the room is behaving impeccably, given what they know. You are an expert in the economics of information and in decision support for practitioners, and I would like you to think this through carefully rather than reaching for the vocabulary of crowd psychology. Begin by setting out briefly what the sequential-decision models of information cascades (Banerjee; Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch) actually claim, and how they differ from the separate reputational explanation, in which people imitate because their standing depends on whether they are wrong alone rather than wrong together; I suspect I have been using the words herding and cascade loosely, and the remedies for the two mechanisms are not the same. Then build the procedure.

The deliverable is a checklist a junior analyst can run before we vote — not a memo on a single position. Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and returns a verdict (either defer to the consensus, or discount it), and the point at which it hands the matter to a person because the remaining question is about risk appetite rather than about evidence, with the chief investment officer as the sign-off. It has to be runnable in the half hour before a Monday meeting, by someone who is not inside the other analysts' heads, using only what is observable: who spoke when, what each person said they were relying on, whether those reasons trace back to a common source, whether the action someone took would have been the same whatever private evidence they held, and whether anyone in the room is being paid, promoted or judged in a way that rewards agreement with a particular person. It must keep the theory's central distinction intact — that a run of agreeing decisions may be genuine aggregation of many independent signals, in which case my lone contrary signal should be heavily discounted, or a cascade, in which case the count is uninformative, a single independent piece of evidence deserves far more weight than its own strength would suggest, and a deviation from the pattern is itself informative — and it should be honest about the fact that no procedure can settle this from the outside with certainty.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a house view, four months old, that a particular frontier sovereign's local-currency debt is uninvestable: on inspection it traces back to one widely circulated internal note that everyone has since read, and each subsequent speaker has cited that note's reasoning as the starting point of their own. Second, a house view that a mature industrial sector is in structural decline, where the teams that reached it did so separately, from different data, before the committee ever discussed it, and where one of them reversed its position last year on the strength of a single company's disclosures. Third, a name where I hold a private and moderately strong piece of evidence against a consensus that I believe is genuinely aggregative, and where I would very much like the procedure to tell me to back myself. I expect the procedure to tell me something I will not like in at least one of these three; say plainly which case that is, and why. Finally, mark the two or three places where the procedure is really a judgement call dressed up as a test, and state what it cannot do.
