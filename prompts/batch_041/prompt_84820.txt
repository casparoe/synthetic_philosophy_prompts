I'm in the middle of a dumb local fight that's turned into a philosophy problem, and I want your help building the strongest version of the argument I hate. My county votes next month on whether to drain the Whitcomb Marsh -- about ninety acres of cattails and open water behind the old rail spur -- and pave it over for a distribution warehouse. I showed up to the hearing expecting my friend Priya, who is an effective-altruism type, to be on my side, and instead she says the marsh is "a machine for turning sunlight into short miserable lives": the frogs and minnows spawn by the tens of thousands, almost all the young starve or get picked off by herons within a few weeks, and draining the place is the kindest thing anyone could do to that land. I think she's wrong in a way I can feel but can't argue, and when I push back she just says I'm not taking the numbers seriously, which, ok, there's alot I'm not taking seriously. So I want the case built properly, as a case, so I can see exactly which joint it turns on.

Here's what I need from it. (1) Two options only: leave the marsh alone, or drain it and cap the site -- warehouse slab, parking lot, nothing living there afterward. The point of that is to isolate habitat destruction from whatever comes after it; if your case needs the drained land to become a rice paddy or a pasture, I don't want it. (2) The animals carrying the arithmetic have to be ones nobody serious disputes the sentience of -- frogs, fish, birds, voles. No insects, no clams, no nematodes, because I don't want the whole thing to hinge on whether bugs suffer. (3) When you're done, total-welfare reasoning should come out in favor of draining, and a view on which only the animals that already exist count -- killing them is a harm, and animals who'd only ever exist because you preserved the marsh don't get counted as beneficiaries -- should come out against it. I'd also like average-welfare reasoning to land on the draining side, but I know the empty-world thing is a snag there, so if you need to leave a drainage ditch with some fish in it to make the average well-defined, do that and say so. (4) The marsh should also be genuinely worth something conservation-wise -- say a declining native frog or a nesting bird that the local chapter cares about -- so that the conservation argument and the welfare arithmetic pull in opposite directions and nobody gets to have both. (5) No cheating: the deaths can't be stipulated painless, the animals can't be assumed to be little robots, and you can't just announce as a general theory that wild animals have lives not worth living -- whatever is bad about those lives has to be visible in the specifics of this particular place.

Then the writeup. Give me the scenario itself in a couple of paragraphs, with enough detail that I could put the numbers on a slide; then for each of the three views, the verdict it gives and the step in the reasoning where it gets there; then the one feature of the case that's actually doing the load-bearing work, and what happens to each verdict if you take that feature out (I'm curious what happens if the juveniles' lives were mildly good rather than bad); then, honestly, how someone on my side of the fight would attack the case -- where they'd say the numbers are rigged or the scenario is a fantasy. Finish by telling me whether I have any move left that isn't just refusing to count animals, because right now that's all I've got.
