I run a ninety-minute weekly seminar that sits awkwardly between philosophy of mind and clinical neuroscience: the enrolled students are mostly third- and fourth-year philosophy students, but three neurology residents audit it, and next month I have to run the session on pain asymbolia and what it is supposed to show about why pain is bad. The last time I ran a session in this slot I spent forty minutes on the neuroscience and had to rush the philosophy, which is why I want a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself. Assume the students arrive knowing roughly what nociception is, having heard people talk loosely about an affective dimension of pain, and assuming that the live question about pain is whether it is a bodily sensation or a brain state. What has to be in place before the asymbolia cases can do any work at all? My own sense is that the session turns on separating the question of what pain is made of from the question of what makes pain bad, and on distinguishing the thought that pain's badness is intrinsic to its felt quality from the thought that pain is bad because of the aversion or the desire it generates — but I do not know the order in which those have to be laid down, whether some third distinction has to come first, or which of them the clinical cases actually bear on as opposed to merely illustrate.

Then the carrying example. I have been toying with three candidates and I want you to pick one and say why the others cannot carry the session: a described asymbolic patient who reports pain plainly, localizes it correctly, does not withdraw and does not appear to mind; a first-person thought experiment in which I take something that leaves intensity untouched and removes only the unpleasantness; and a case from a different lesion site in which the unpleasantness is said to survive while the sensory quality is degraded. Say what the winner must have, and must not have, for the case to do the work you assign it. On misconceptions, I already expect the philosophers to collapse the report — to insist that if the patient does not mind it, it was never pain — and I expect the residents to treat the whole thing as a lesion-localization result with a decorative philosophical gloss. What I do not know is what else the cases invite, and I suspect a good deal of the confusion traces to the term itself: whether asymbolia is best read as a failure of the affective response to pain or as a failure of pain's significance, pain that has stopped meaning anything about the body, and whether those two readings predict different lessons about the badness of pain. Tell me where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off rather than at the end, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and flag as refused, and give me one closing question that would show me whether they have understood the bearing of asymbolia on the badness of pain rather than merely memorized a dissociation.

Keep the whole response to one page — no more than 800 words — with the sequence as a numbered list, then a short labelled section for each of the carrying example and its rivals, the misconceptions with the point in the sequence at which each is defused, what I leave out, and the closing question.
