I've been chewing on a puzzle about the procreation asymmetry and I've built an argument that I think proves too much, but I can't find where it breaks. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of ethics (population ethics especially) and help me diagnose it. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming the possible failure points before you commit to an answer, because I keep flip-flopping.

Some background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training, but I read a fair bit online. I came across the idea that a lot of people accept an "asymmetry": it's bad to bring someone into existence who will suffer, but it's not good (or at least not required) to bring someone into existence who will be happy. That seemed intuitive to me. Then I pushed on it and now I've got an argument I can't shake, and the conclusion is genuinely absurd, so something must be off.

Here's the argument. The premises are numbered; please tell me which one to reject and what rejecting it costs me, because I actually like most of them.

(1) Suffering is bad, and its presence is bad regardless of whose suffering it is.

(2) The absence of suffering is good, even when there is no one around to enjoy that good. (This is the half of the asymmetry that says it's good that a person who would have suffered is never born.)

(3) The absence of pleasure is not bad when there is no one around to be deprived of it. (This is the other half — it's not bad that a happy person is never born, because there's no subject who loses out.)

(4) Take two possible futures. Future A: one person is born and lives a life with some pain in it. Future B: no one is ever born at all. By (2), B has a good that A lacks — no suffering. By (3), A has no good that B lacks — the missing pleasure isn't bad, since no one exists to be deprived. So B is better than A.

(5) Now suppose every living thing could be painlessly and instantly wiped out, and nothing would ever be born again. Call the state afterwards the Empty Future. The Empty Future has no suffering, and no one exists in it to be deprived of pleasure.

(6) By exactly the same reasoning as in (4), the Empty Future is better than any future in which sentient life carries on — because any continuing life involves some suffering (whose absence is good), and the lost pleasures aren't bad, since in the Empty Future no one exists to be deprived of them.

(7) So it would be good — better, in fact — for all sentient life to end.

(8) But that's absurd. Quietly annihilating every living thing is not a good outcome, and I won't accept a premise that says it is.

(9) So one of (1)–(3) has to go.

The thing is, I really want to keep (1), (2) and (3) — the asymmetry is what got me here in the first place and it feels right. I'd rather give up (4) or (6) than the asymmetry itself. But I can't see what's wrong with (4) if (2) and (3) are true, and (6) looks like a straight application of (4). Is this valid, or is there a slide somewhere between the steps? If it's valid, tell me which of the three premises is least costly to abandon and what I lose by abandoning it. And if the problem is somewhere in how (4)–(6) use (2) and (3) rather than in the premises themselves, say precisely where the slide happens and why it looked fine to me.

Please write it up in British English, be concrete about which step you're rejecting, explain why that step seemed plausible, and price the alternatives for me — i.e., what each candidate rejection forces me to give up elsewhere.
