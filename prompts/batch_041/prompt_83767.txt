So I'm a software engineer, mostly backend services, 15 years in, and I have zero formal philosophy training — I took one ethics class in college and I'm pretty sure I passed it by accident. Anyway, I've been teaching the algorithms section at a 12-week coding bootcamp here in Columbus for the last three years, and they've asked me to give the little commencement speech at the graduation / demo night next month. Roughly 60 grads, ages 22 to like 55, a lot of them career changers — laid off, single parents, one guy who used to run a restaurant — plus their families (there will be kids in the room, and my mom), and two of the other instructors who I know for a fact have actually read philosophy of mind, one of them did a whole MA in it before he switched to programming. So, mixed room.

What I want the speech to be about, and this is the part I need help with: the digger wasp thing. The wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it back to her burrow by the antennae, drops it at the entrance, goes inside to check the nest, then comes back out to drag the cricket in. If you take the cricket and move it a few inches away while she's inside, she'll come out, find it, drag it back to the entrance — and then drop it and go inspect the nest again from scratch. Do that forty times, she does it forty times. The whole sequence resets every time. Some philosopher of mind made up a word for this, "sphexishness," for behaving like that: running a fixed program that can look smart from the outside. (I also read somewhere that the wasp story may be kind of an urban legend among bug people, that re-inspecting the nest after a disturbance might just be sensible wasp behavior, so don't treat it as gospel — but it doesn't matter for the speech.)

Here's the thing I actually want to say, and I cant figure out how to say it without being a hypocrite. The obvious move is "unlike the wasp, you can break out of your program, you're flexible, now go be flexible." But that's cheap, because the wasp is flexible about a lot — she can find that cricket wherever it went. What she can't do is change the goal. Her goal is fixed and her means are wide open. Meanwhile I'm sitting here as a guy whose entire job is writing systems that restart themselves when they crash, and I honestly can't tell anymore whether that's resilience or whether it's just a bigger fixed action pattern with better logging. So I'd like to know whether there's a real distinction hiding in there or if it's turtles all the way down, and if there is one, what it is — because I want these grads to feel that question, not get an answer handed to them.

So: write me the speech. 5-7 minutes, call it 900 words, I'll cut it down. Requirements: no citations, no names of philosophers or scientists, and no quotes from anybody — I will definately get them wrong, and there is nothing worse than a bootcamp keynote with a fake Einstein quote in it. I'd also rather the phrase "free will" never appears; I want people to leave feeling the question in their gut, not thinking "oh, he did the free will thing." It should be funny in a couple of places, it should be warm, and it should land for my two philosophy friends without the other hundred people feeling like they wandered into a lecture. Don't resolve whether we're sphexish — I don't know, and I don't want to pretend I do — but by the end I want it genuinely open in a way that's a little uncomfortable, not a Hallmark card about how you're the author of your own story. American English please (I'm in Ohio, not Cambridge).
