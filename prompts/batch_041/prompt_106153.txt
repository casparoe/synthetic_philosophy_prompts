ok so I'm throwing together a little unit on fair-division / bargaining for a reading group, and I want to actually stress-test the solution concepts against each other instead of just reciting their definitions. Here's the setup I want you to work with: two players, A and B, bargaining over a surplus of utility. A has a much better fallback than B — if talks collapse, A walks away with nearly what she wanted (call it 0.8 of her own max), while B's fallback is basically zero. On top of that A's ideal payoff (what she'd get if she took everything) is bigger than B's, so the feasible set is lopsided.

Rank the following six rules for picking the outcome by how fair they'd strike a normal, non-economist person in that situation, from most to least defensible, with one sentence each on why it lands where it does:

1. Nash — maximize the product of each player's gain over the disagreement point.
2. Kalai–Smorodinsky — give each player the same fraction of the way from their disagreement payoff to their own ideal payoff.
3. Egalitarian — make the worse-off player as well off as possible.
4. Utilitarian — maximize the sum of the two payoffs.
5. Equal-surplus split — each player gets half of the total gain above the disagreement point.
6. Threat-point proportionality (TPP for short) — a made-up rule of my own: pick the efficient point where the two players' *final* payoffs are in the same ratio as their disagreement payoffs, so if B's fallback is half of A's then B ends up with half of A's payoff.

Then two follow-ups. (a) Tell me which rules move the most when I flip things around — same two players, but now the one with the weaker fallback is also the one with the bigger ideal — and whether your top two swap places or stay put. (b) Which two of the six are hardest to tell apart, and what concrete case would actually pull them apart? I'm less interested in textbook definitions than in where they genuinely diverge on this kind of asymmetric problem.
