I've been going back and forth on this with my cousin for like two years and I want to see it written out properly, so here goes.

The thing I keep chewing on: everyday stuff — tipping at a restaurant, waiting your turn in a line, not jaywalking, letting people off the elevator before you shove on. Are these actual obligations, the kind where skipping them means you did something *wrong*, or are they just habits that grease the wheels? My cousin says they're real. Not huge, but real — take the tip out of the system and the person serving you is worse off, and you knew that when you sat down. I say that's just coordination. If everyone woke up tomorrow and decided the price on the menu is the whole price, nothing morally broke and nobody was wronged. So the tip was never a duty, it was a local custom we all got used to.

It gets worse when you travel, which is where I actually started caring. I've been in places where tipping is basically an insult, places where haggling is expected and paying the sticker price is sort of rude, and places where nobody queues and you just ease toward the counter and the loudest person wins. So does a visitor have to follow local rules they never agreed to and maybe never heard of? Or does being a visitor get you a pass? And here's the twist I can't get past: when tourists drag their own norms along, do they hurt the locals by changing what's expected? If I tip 25% somewhere nobody tips, haven't I just made things worse for whoever comes after me?

Two tests I made up, and they're the part I can't sort out on my own, so please actually use them:

- The swap test. Same act, two people: a local who knows the norm, and a tourist who doesn't. If not tipping is wrong when the local does it and not wrong when the tourist does it, is the wrongness in the act or in the knowing?
- The exit test. Some norms you can wriggle out of by announcing it — "hey, I'm not tipping, no hard feelings," or "I'm cutting in, my flight boards in ten." Does having an exit make it more like a deal between two people, or does it make it worse, because now the other person has to say yes to your face?

Also I half-remember something about kids thinking hitting is wrong even if the teacher says it's fine, but wearing the wrong clothes is only wrong because the teacher says so. I'm probably mangling it. Either way, line-cutting and tipping feel like they're somewhere in the middle of that and I don't know which side they land on.

What I want: a dialogue between two people who've thought about this a lot, one on each side. They should be evenly matched — I dont want the skeptic to be a smug "rules are for sheep" guy and I don't want the other one to be a scold. They should answer each other's actual points, and if one of them lands a good hit the other should deal with it instead of changing the subject. One of them can bring in the idea that conventions are just solutions to coordination problems, like which side of the road to drive on, and the idea that some virtues might be invented rather than discovered (Hume? I think?). Not a lecture about it, just them using it when it helps.

No reading lists, no who-said-what, I just want the argument.
