For the past year I've been keeping what amounts to a crude experience-sampling diary: a watch beeps at random intervals through the day, and when it does I write down, before doing anything else, what was actually going on in my mind in the moment just before the beep — not what I was doing, but what I was experiencing. The unsettling part is how little the diary matches the story I would have told over dinner about the same afternoon. Worse, when my partner and I compare notes about a conversation we both sat through, the account I give of what I was feeling during it is regularly contradicted by what I wrote at the beep, and I don't think I was lying either time. I had assumed this was a personal failing, but I now recognize it as the shape of a much older fight among people who study consciousness. On one side: introspection is a hopeless instrument — people asked to explain a choice they have just made will supply a fluent, specific reason even when the thing they name demonstrably played no role in the choice, and there is no independent access to the target that would let us check the report against anything. On the other side: careful in-the-moment reporting of current experience, done with a disciplined method, interrupted at the moment rather than reconstructed afterwards, and followed by a non-leading interview designed to strip out the reporter's presuppositions about what they ought to have been experiencing, yields something genuinely informative — and the skeptics are mostly testing memory-based self-explanation, then generalizing the failure to introspection as such.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict, because I suspect the two sides are talking past each other more than either admits. Is the dispute substantive — an empirical disagreement about what people can actually do — or is a large part of it verbal, with the word naming two different activities, one of which (retrospective causal self-explanation) is demonstrably poor and the other (momentary reporting of current experience) at least arguably decent? Is it instead a disagreement about standards of evidence, given that the only instrument available for the target is the very thing under dispute? Or are the camps sampling different phenomena and answering different questions under a single heading, so that each is right about its own case and neither is answering the other? Here is the hypothesis I'd like you to test rather than simply accept, since I hold it loosely: that the impasse is largely a measurement dispute, and that if introspective report were treated like any other instrument — asking about test-retest stability within a subject, agreement between independently trained reporters describing the same episode, and above all whether the reports predict anything checkable from the outside, such as later behavior, physiological signals, or a second person's account of the same episode — the two camps would turn out to be making different predictions about different measures, and the disagreement would become tractable. This is roughly how one would validate a pain scale when one cannot read the pain off the brain, and I want to know whether the analogy holds or breaks.

Tell me where that hypothesis is right and where it is too neat, and be concrete about the cruxes: what exactly would have to be true for the skeptics to be vindicated, and what for the defenders? Note the awkward consequences on both sides — that trained reporters converging might reflect shared training or shared presuppositions rather than accuracy, that divergence might mean one of them had a different experience rather than that either misreported it, and that a report accurate about the character of an experience but wrong about its causes leaves us unsure which sense of 'reliable' is at issue. Then separate the strands: which parts of this are empirical, and what experiment would actually bear on them; which are conceptual, and what distinction would dissolve them; and which are bedrock convictions about first-person authority that no argument will move. If you conclude that part of the debate is stalled because the question as posed is ill-formed, say which part and what the well-posed version is; if you conclude it is merely hard, say what progress would look like and what it would take. I don't need a reading list or citations — assume I can't look anything up, and reason it through from the positions as described.
