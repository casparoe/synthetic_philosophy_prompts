I write a fortnightly column on puzzles and ideas for a regional magazine, and for the past few months I have been sitting in on the editorial meetings of the Beacon's daily cryptic crossword while I research a longer piece on how puzzle desks police originality. The desk's editor, Marion Ellery, has spent eleven years adjudicating complaints that a submitted clue duplicates one the paper has already printed — the trade calls this a dupe — and she decides case by case, because the paper's style guide says only that a clue must not sell solvers an idea the paper has already sold them. I now have a list of her rulings, and before I write a word about her I want to know what rule she is actually running.

Her vocabulary, which I shall adopt: the answer is the solution; the definition is the part of the clue that tells you what the answer means; the mechanism is the wordplay device — a hidden word, an anagram, a reversal, a double definition; the surface is what the clue appears to be about when read as ordinary English. Her recent rulings: (i) a hidden-word clue for LODESTONE whose definition was a mineral, reprinted a year later with only cosmetic rewording of the surface — dupe; (ii) LODESTONE again, but this time clued as a double definition with no hidden word — not a dupe, because, she said, the solver is being asked a genuinely different question even though the answer is the same; (iii) a hidden-word clue whose definition was a river, submitted weeks after a hidden-word clue whose definition was a kind of cheese — not a dupe, since the answers differed and the resemblance was confined to the machinery; (iv) two clues with the identical surface reading, both opening as remarks about a handful of something, but with different answers and different mechanisms — not a dupe, because solvers remember the answer, not the wording; (v) LODESTONE once more, hidden word once more, definition again a mineral but a different sense of the word and a thoroughly rewritten surface — dupe, because the desk had already sold that trick for that word.

Next week she rules on a submission that currently has the desk arguing: a hidden-word clue, for an answer that has never appeared in the paper, whose definition is again a mineral — this time a gemstone. Predict her verdict, and be explicit about the candidate rules that fit all five rulings so far, what each of them predicts about the gemstone clue, which of them you would bet on and why, and which further case would best tell the two leading rules apart. If the five rulings genuinely underdetermine the gemstone clue, tell me what principle the desk would be committing itself to on either reading, and what it would be giving up. Please write your answer in British English.
