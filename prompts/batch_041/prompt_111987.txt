I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about Bentham's sexual morality, and we ended up at an impasse I would like you to break. My friend's position is that Bentham's utilitarianism is, at bottom, a liberating doctrine in matters of sex. Because only pleasure and pain count, and because the sexual pleasures are, in his own catalogue, genuine pleasures of sense, there is no room in the theory for the thought that some consensual acts are intrinsically unnatural or degrading. On this reading, his unpublished manuscript on sexual offences is simply the principle of utility applied without flinching, and the fact that he left it unpublished tells us about the timidity of his age rather than about any wobble in the theory. My position is that the theory is less steady than that, and that Bentham's own machinery supplies the wobble. Two features of his account seem to me to cause trouble. The first is that he treats the pleasures of malevolence as genuine pleasures: the pleasure one takes in another's pain is, on his own classification, a simple pleasure, not a mere illusion to be discounted. The second is that he treats the pains of the moral sanction, the pain of being hated, despised or shunned by one's neighbours, as genuine pains, and he gives them a serious role in the legislator's calculus. Put those together and you get an argument that the criminal law may, in principle, be turned against a consensual sexual practice whenever the majority's disgust at it is intense and widespread enough: the majority's antipathy is a real pain, the minority's suffering is a real pain, and the calculus is supposed to add them up. Bentham himself, I think, wants to resist that conclusion, but I am not sure he can do so without either denying that malevolent pleasures and moral-sanction pains count, or else leaning on a distinction between pains the law causes and pains the law merely fails to prevent. I want you to write an essay on this controversy and to come down on one side. Set out the strongest case for the liberating reading and the strongest case for the instability reading, using only the resources of Bentham's own utilitarian framework: his catalogue of pleasures and pains, his four sanctions, and his contrast between the principle of utility and the principles of asceticism and of sympathy and antipathy. Then defend a verdict. In particular, say whether a consistent utilitarian can treat the pleasures of malevolence and the pains of the moral sanction as fully weighted items in the calculus, and if so, whether the decriminalisation of consensual sexual conduct survives; if not, what principled restriction on the calculus does the work, and whether that restriction is itself a piece of asceticism or antipathy in disguise. Do not pad the essay with biography or with the history of reform. The interest is in whether the theory can condemn or condone without cheating.
