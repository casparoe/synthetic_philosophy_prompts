I'm a second-year in a computational social science program and somehow I've become the person in our lab's little "responsible ML" reading group who gets asked to referee arguments that are really about what words mean. Last week's blowup: we're prototyping a model that scores job applicants, and one of the engineers suggested dropping the group-blind constraint entirely and feeding in everything, zip code, the lot, since "it's all correlated with race anyway." Our PI pushed back, and a postdoc shut it down with: the math already settled this, you literally can't be fair, the impossibility results show calibration and equal error rates can't both hold when base rates differ, so the only honest thing is to pick a metric and admit you're making a value choice. Everyone nodded and moved on. I dont think that's what the theorems actually say, but I couldn't articulate why in the moment, and I have a suspicion that the sentence "you can't be fair" is quietly doing at least four different jobs in that room.

Here's what I want. Give me a menu of the distinct precise claims someone might be making when they say fairness is mathematically impossible / you can't have it all / the math proves it, in this kind of setting. For each reading, be explicit about what it quantifies over: which class of predictors (group-blind? group-aware? allowed to randomize?), whether it's about one fixed score with one threshold or about any decision rule whatsoever, whether the outcome variable is treated as ground truth or as a proxy, and whether it's a claim about prediction or about the allocation of a good. Then give each one a verdict: arithmetic identity, substantive and true, true only under extra assumptions, or false. Two things I especially want you to take seriously rather than skip past. First, the possibility that the standard framing smuggles in a premise, namely that the base rate of the label is a fact about the world rather than a fact about how the label got measured — if the labels themselves are recorded with different error rates in different groups (arrests versus crimes is the obvious case, but honestly anything where the outcome is a human judgement), what is the theorem even about, and does the word "impossible" survive the correction? Second, the reverse worry: if you fix the label by moving to a better-measured construct, you may lose the ability to ever check whether the model is fair, so that impossibility reappears as an impossibility of *verification* rather than of fairness — is that a real distinction or am I just relabeling the same problem? And separately from all of that, some people I know mean something entirely different by "the math proves it," namely that any single fairness metric can be gamed by a strategic agent, which strikes me as a different theorem with a different moral; tell me if I'm wrong about that.

At the end, tell me which of the readings would actually license the postdoc's move (therefore stop agonizing about group-blindness and use whatever features we like), which would license a different conclusion, and which are just non-sequiturs in that context. Lay the whole thing out as a table with these columns: reading_label (short name, a few words), precise_claim (one or two sentences, no jargon hand-waving), quantifies_over (what the claim ranges over — predictors, thresholds, labels, decision rules), truth_status (analytic / true / true-under-assumptions / false / unclear), supports_postdoc (yes / no / only-with-an-extra-premise), and what_would_change_it (the single consideration that would most move your verdict). Sort the rows from weakest to strongest reading. Then close with two or three sentences on which reading you think was actually doing the work in that room, and the one sentence I should have said back.
