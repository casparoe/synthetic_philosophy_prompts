So I've been arguing with a friend in my reading group about a view she calls tranquilism — the claim that the only thing that matters morally is the absence of craving and suffering, and that everything we'd normally call a positive good (pleasure, love, achievement, whatever) is at best neutral: not outweighed, just not counting in the first place. I can't tell if this is a real position or just negative hedonism wearing a hat, and I suspect the difference matters, because suffering and craving aren't the same thing — you can crave something without suffering much, and you can suffer without craving (a toothache isn't a craving). So if the view is really about craving then its making a distinct claim that needs its own defence, and if it's really about suffering then "craving" is doing no work at all. Can you lay out what the actual conceptual commitments of the view are, and which of them are load-bearing? I want it explained rather than defended — I'm trying to work out whether it's even coherent before I decide whether it's true.

The thing bugging me most is that there seem to be two versions of it and I don't think the people who hold it realise they're different. Call them cessation-tranquilism, where what's good is the event of a craving stopping, and state-tranquilism, where what's good is just the condition of not craving, however you got there. Now run a few cases through both. Someone who has never craved at all, because they were built that way. Someone who craved like hell for twenty years and then it stopped for good. Someone with a mild low-grade craving that never resolves but never gets worse. A world with no creatures in it. Under state-tranquilism the never-craver and the recovered craver look identical, and the empty world looks... fine? Under cessation-tranquilism the never-craver seems to get nothing, which makes craving a precondition of the good, which is bizarre. And there's a related worry about comparability: "absence of craving" looks like it's always defined against somebody's own baseline, so is there even a common scale on which two people's tranquility can be compared, or is the view quietly committed to saying goods like this can't be aggregated at all? I'd like you to explain how the view has to be formulated to survive these cases, and to be honest about which version comes out worst.

One more thing I keep going round on: if only absence matters, is the good privative in a way that makes it a fact about the world rather than a state of anyone in particular? Because then a rock, or an empty galaxy, is maximally tranquil, and the view turns into something much closer to antinatalism than to the calm-down-and-want-less thing my friend thinks she's defending. Does the tranquilist have a principled way to require a subject for the good, or is that just an unstated assumption smuggled in at the start? I don't need literature or citations, I just want the conceptual geography sorted out — where the joints are, which version is most defensible, and what it would take for the view to be more than a relabelling of something older.
