I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on extended cognition — a dozen people, mostly grad students, plus two philosophers of mind who will object to everything. What I want is a decision tree, not a list of positions. Here's the deal.

The tree starts from a commitment I'll just hand the reader: they accept that some of what a person knows and does is stored and processed in stuff outside the skin — a notebook, a phone, a partner, a group chat — and that this is more than a handy causal aid, it's somehow part of their cognition. Everyone reading already accepts that much, so don't spend a node establishing it. From there, every node has to be a question the reader can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. No nodes like "do you accept the extended mind hypothesis" — that's the thing we're trying to locate. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by the label they like.

Nodes I want somewhere on the tree (you pick the order, and tell me if any two of them are really the same question):

- Does coupling ever constitute a process, or only cause/enable a process that stays in the head?
- If it constitutes: what earns it? Reliable availability plus automatic, un-audited trust; or being woven into a learned, norm-governed practice; or complementarity — the resource does work the brain is bad at; or the bare fact that you'd be worse at the very same task without it; or nothing general, case by case.
- Does the boundary track the process (where the computing happens) or the subject (whose cognition it is)? These come seperate for the group chat and the sous-chef.
- Does constitution last only while you're coupled to the thing, or does it persist while the notebook sits in your pocket and you're not reading it?
- Can something be constitutive of your cognition and someone else's property or authority at the same time — the shared chat, the trained partner, the document that settles what you're committed to?
- Does the extension story reach beliefs and memories only, or also what it's like to be you — affect, desire, experience? Half the room thinks that's the only version worth arguing about.
- A meta-node, top or bottom, your call: is the question empirical (about how cognition is actually organized), methodological (about how cognitive science should carve things up), or verbal (about how to use the word cognitive)?

Cases that have to be placed on the tree, one at a time, so I can check the branches are definately doing work:

1. Scrabble — you can't anagram in your head, so you shuffle the rack physically, and the rearrangement is what does the work.
2. The sous-chef — fifteen years together, the timing of the kitchen lives half in his anticipation of what the chef will want next; neither of them could do the shift alone.
3. The autocomplete poet — she writes with a model trained on her own past work, and genuinely can't tell whether a line arrived from her or from it.
4. The ex's voice — she can only recall what she thought of a book by hearing his summary in her head, and he's gone.
5. The group chat — no member has the sequence of the trip, but any member can get it from the others.
6. The tattooed formula — on the forearm, used daily, never consciously read, and she fumbles when it's covered.
7. The map app — you'd fail to get across town without it, you never trust it, you check every turn against the street signs anyway.

Leaves I want, in whatever names fit — standard labels where there are standard labels, coinages where there aren't: full constitution-by-coupling, the notebook case taken at face value; constitution only for resources you trust automatically and don't audit; constitution for whatever's integrated into a practice; the complementarity view where the unit is a system assembled for the task with no privileged inner core; embedded cognition, where tools enable and shape but don't constitute; radical enactivism, where the where-is-the-belief question is malformed from the start; socially distributed or group cognition; extended affect, desire, experience; and a deflationary leaf where cognitive is a term of art and the boundary is interest-relative.

Plus two I've made up, because I want them placed rather than recognized: (a) cheap coupling — any resource you'd be worse off without is part of your cognition, full stop, no reliability, no trust, no integration required; (b) borrowed cognition — a resource is constitutive of your cognition only while you're coupled to it and then reverts, so you're never more than a temporary owner of your own mind.

Each leaf needs a one-line gloss and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. For full coupling that's presumably the bloat worry, for embedded cognition it's explaining why the phone feels like memory, and so on — but you decide, and be honest when the awkward commitment is fatal.

Then, separately:

- At least one combination of answers your tree permits that nobody actually occupies. Say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and be specific about which.
- Any two leaves your questions can't pull apart — say so rather than papering over it. I'd bet the practice view and the complementarity view blur, and that the deflationary leaf swallows most of the others, but prove me wrong.
- Three worked runs: put the Scrabble player, the autocomplete poet and the map-app user through the tree, show where each lands, and flag which one is the genuinely hard call and why.

No citations or reading list — the tree has to stand on its own. And if a node turns out to be doing no work, cut it and tell me you cut it.
