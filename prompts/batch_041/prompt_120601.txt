You are a philosopher of probability and an expert on the reference class problem. Before you answer, brainstorm the candidate readings of the practice described below; then give a structured response that separates the reading you favor from those you reject, and flags what the practice leaves open.

Insurers and consumer lenders price contracts by assigning an applicant to a group — an age band, a postcode, an occupation, a marital status, a credit history, a smoking status — and charging the rate that the group's statistics imply. In many of the same jurisdictions the law forbids using race, religion, disability, or (in some places) sex, even where those classifications are at least as predictive as the ones that are permitted, and sometimes forbids using genetic test results that strongly predict claims. The practice is thus comfortable with group rates in general and hostile to certain group rates in particular.

What conception of the reference class problem does this presuppose? Every applicant belongs to indefinitely many classes with different frequencies, and no class is uniquely correct on statistical grounds alone, so something other than frequency must be fixing the relevant class. Is the practice committed to the individual's own risk as the true reference class, with group rates as approximations to it — and if so, what makes the individual the right unit? Or does it assume a causal or explanatory criterion, admitting only factors that explain the risk rather than merely correlate with it? Or a moralized criterion, admitting only factors for which the applicant can be held responsible, or that track desert rather than brute luck? Or does it treat the choice of class as a convention or political settlement rather than a factual matter? Say which of these would license the practice, which would undermine it, and where the practice is underdetermined, so that several incompatible views of the reference class would each license it. Note the internal tension as well: the actuarial ideal is ever-finer classification of individual risk, yet full classification would destroy the pooling that makes insurance insurance. Does the practice presuppose a principled stopping point for classification, and can it supply one without appealing to the statistics? Finally, say what would have to change in the practice if its implicit conception were replaced by a straightforwardly predictive one. Argue from the concepts; no citations are needed.
