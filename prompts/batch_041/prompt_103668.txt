For decades the debate over natural kinds has gone around in circles, and I want to understand why rather than pick a winner. Here is how the landscape looks from where I sit. One camp treats natural kinds as the world's own divisions — the elements of the periodic table being the paradigm — and asks what metaphysical feature (shared essence, microstructure, something else) earns a class that status. A second camp replaces essences with clusters of stable, co-varying properties — the "homeostatic property cluster" picture — and points to biological species as the model. A third camp is pluralist: different sciences, and even different projects within a single science, legitimately carve the same domain along different lines, so there is no one privileged taxonomy to be found. And a fourth camp holds that "natural kind" is a piece of pre-scientific furniture a mature philosophy of science should discard.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but at least two, run together under a single label. One question is metaphysical: is there a mind-independent structure that some classifications track and others miss? The other is epistemic and pragmatic: which classifications best support induction, explanation, and prediction, and for whom? I suspect much of the impasse comes from the phrase "natural kind" sliding between these two jobs, so that each side can grant the other's points and still feel entirely unmoved.

Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and if not, what the real structure of the disagreement is. In particular: is this a verbal dispute that would dissolve under disambiguation, a substantive disagreement about a single question on which one side is simply wrong, or two camps answering different questions while sharing one vocabulary? Is some hidden empirical or formal issue — say, whether projectible predicates must correspond to intrinsic properties, or whether species are individuals rather than kinds — actually doing the load-bearing work? And does the sheer persistence of the debate suggest the question is ill-posed, or merely hard?

To make the diagnosis bite, work with a case I keep returning to. Imagine a mature science that settles on two taxonomies of the same domain, each internally consistent, each perfectly sustaining its own inductive and explanatory practice, neither derivable from the other, and no further fact anywhere that privileges one over the other. Does the bare possibility of such a case vindicate the pluralist, show that the original question was never well-formed, or leave the essentialist with a legitimate demand — that at most one of the taxonomies can be tracking the world's own structure — that the pluralist cannot answer?

I want a committed answer, not a survey: give me your verdict on what kind of disagreement this is, the reasons that decide it, and what would actually count as progress from here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Do not hand me a reading list; reason from the positions themselves.
