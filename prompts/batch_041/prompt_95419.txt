A colleague's argument seems to show that no evidence can ever support any theory, and I want to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. The case that provoked it is invented but, I think, typical: an epidemiologist proposes a mechanism for a complication that has been documented in case series for thirty years; everyone agrees the mechanism explains the complication well, and her critics reply that the explanation is worthless as confirmation precisely because we knew about the complication long before she proposed the mechanism. Generalized: (1) E confirms H only if P(H|E) > P(H); confirmation is the raising of probability. (2) A rational agent's credences just are her prior credences conditionalized on everything she has learned. (3) So whatever she has learned, she is certain of: P(E) = 1 for each item of her evidence. (4) So P(H|E) = P(H) for every H and every E she holds. (5) By (1), none of her evidence confirms anything. (6) At any moment of assessment, all of an agent's evidence is evidence she already holds. (7) So nothing is ever confirmed for anyone, and the machinery of confirmation is idle — for new evidence no less than old, since new evidence becomes old the instant it arrives. I'm told this is the problem of old evidence, but as stated it is stronger than the problem I've read about, and it refutes itself: if (5) holds, the considerations that lead me to accept (5) don't support it either. That is a debating point, not a diagnosis. So: which step goes, and what does giving it up cost? I would rather keep (1) — I do think confirmation has something to do with probability raising — than keep (7). I've heard the repair is to evaluate (1)'s inequality against some credence function that does not already include E, but I cannot see what makes that function the right one rather than a free parameter, nor whether it rescues new evidence on independent grounds or only by the same trick; and I've also heard that confirmation should be measured by likelihoods rather than by the change in probability, which strikes me as abandoning (1) rather than saving it. Give your verdict in the first sentence, then the reasoning: no preamble, no restating of the question.
