I teach an upper-level seminar (about eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, tables arranged for small groups) that spends a week on Otto Neurath's picture-language project, and I want that session to be an activity rather than a lecture. Here is the material I want them to feel from the inside rather than be told.

Neurath, a central figure of the Vienna Circle, spent the 1920s and 1930s building a pictorial method for showing social and economic facts to ordinary citizens across a polyglot empire. Developed at his Museum of Society and Economy in Vienna and later renamed ISOTYPE, the system rested on a governing rule: greater quantities are represented not by enlarging a pictogram but by repeating an identical one, each standing for a fixed amount, so that what is compared is the count of signs rather than their height or area. The pictograms were standardized, abstract, and flat. Neurath's stated slogan was that "Words divide, pictures unite." Yet he also knew the limits of his own creation: he insisted it was never meant to replace verbal language, described it as a "language-like technique" rather than a full language, and relied on a human "transformer" to turn dry statistics into a sketch for a self-explanatory chart. I want students to rediscover both the ambition and the limits first-hand — to try to make pictures carry meaning with no words, to run into the wall where the pictures stop being self-explanatory, and only afterwards to realize that they have re-enacted Neurath's own mature position.

Please design that session. The activity should be a runnable game with rules and small stakes, not a discussion prompt. Constraints and requirements:

- The engine of the activity should be a no-words constraint: teams must communicate a set of propositions using only a standardized kit of pictogram tiles I will prepare (abstract, flat, repeatable symbols; no letters, numerals, arrows, punctuation, or other characters). The propositions should form an escalating ladder, beginning with something a picture handles easily (a simple magnitude, e.g. one in ten workers unemployed in a given year) and ending with things that resist picturing entirely (a change over time qualified by a comparison; a conditional; a negation nested inside a conditional; a claim about what might happen next year; a normative claim about what should be done).
- Include a reading step, so that a rival team must report what it thinks another team's chart says, with a scoring scheme in tokens rather than money that rewards both intended and correctly recovered meaning. The mismatch between what was meant and what was read should be something the participants discover for themselves, not something I announce.
- Do not let me name Neurath, ISOTYPE, or any of the terminology above until the debrief. Nothing should be resolved by my explaining; the rules, the constraint, and the reading step must do the work.
- The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the substantive points: the conventions each team smuggled in (a reading direction, a unit, a legend, a caption) as the dictionary and grammar a picture language secretly needs; the systematic misreadings as evidence against the idea that pictures are self-evident; the propositions that broke as the boundary of the medium; the words the teams could not avoid as the reason it is a "language-like technique" and not a language. Where it helps, have the debrief ask teams to redo one of the harder propositions under Neurath's real first rule and notice how much verbal scaffolding they still require.
