I'm in the middle of a graduate seminar on conditionals and have been reading around in the popular literature to get my bearings, and I've hit a summary I can't quite assess. It comes from a chapter in an introduction to logic written for a general audience by someone with a philosophy PhD, and it runs roughly as follows: "Lewis's triviality results are the reason so many philosophers have given up on the idea that indicative conditionals are ordinary propositions. What Lewis showed is that if you accept the apparently obvious claim that the probability of 'If A, then B' is just the conditional probability of B given A, then you are forced to conclude that 'If A, then B' means the same thing as 'not-A or B,' the material conditional. But everyone agrees that is wrong, since the material conditional is true whenever its antecedent is false, which is not how conditionals work; so the probability claim has to go. Lewis therefore showed that the Adams-style suppositional theory, on which a conditional's probability simply is the conditional probability, is untenable, and that conditionals must be truth-evaluable after all, just not in the way the material conditional is. The result extends to counterfactuals as well, and the moral is that any purely probabilistic account of conditionals is a dead end."

I'm inclined to think the suppositional view is basically right, so this summary makes me worry I've backed the wrong horse — but something feels off about it, especially the way it moves from "the probability claim has to go" straight to "so the suppositional theory is refuted."
