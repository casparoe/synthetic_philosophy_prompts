Hume's essays on commerce and money contain, among much else, a compact set of claims about how a monetary economy works. Six of the positions most commonly drawn from them are restated below in modern terms. Treat each as a claim about how economies work rather than as an interpretive claim about what Hume meant, and rate it on a single dimension: how well it survives transplantation from Hume's world — specie money, no central bank, largely flexible prices, small-scale production, no fiat currency — into a modern monetary economy of fiat money, central banks, floating exchange rates, sticky prices, and a large financial sector.

For each of the six, give one number between 0 and 1 (1 = the mechanism it identifies is essentially correct as stated; 0 = the mechanism has no place in a modern economy), together with one sentence explaining what carries the score up or down. Then rank all six from most to least vindicated. If you judge that one of them is not fairly attributable to Hume, note that in a clause and rate the proposition as stated.

1. Money is not wealth. The quantity of money in a country is not part of its real riches; money is only the instrument of exchange, and its worth is derived from the goods it commands.

2. In the long run the price level is proportional to the quantity of money, and the real volume of output and employment is unaffected by it.

3. In the short run an increase in the quantity of money stimulates industry and employment, because the new money raises demand before it raises prices. The stimulus is temporary: it works only during the lag between the arrival of the new money and the general adjustment of prices.

4. Under a specie standard with free trade, a country's money stock and its balance of trade are self-correcting. An outflow of money lowers domestic prices, which restores the competitiveness of exports, so no country can lose its money indefinitely and a permanent trade deficit is impossible.

5. Interest rates are not governed by the quantity of money but by the profits of commerce and the demand for loans. An abundance of money can coexist with high interest, and low interest is an effect of prosperity rather than a cause of it.

6. A large funded public debt is unsustainable. Beyond a certain point, a state that carries such a debt will be driven either into bankruptcy or into a dependence that destroys its independence.

Finally, answer two further questions in a few sentences each. First: which of the six is the load-bearing claim, in the sense that its failure would do the most damage to the others? Second: propositions 2 and 3 are sometimes said to sit awkwardly together, since the one makes real output independent of the money stock and the other makes it depend on the money stock. Is the appeal to an interval a resolution of that tension, or a restatement of it?
