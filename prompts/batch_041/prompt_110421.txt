I'm running a session for a undergrad philosophy-of-mind reading group — about 12 people, 75 minutes, mixed backgrounds (a few CS, a few lit, one guy who keeps bringing up octopuses). I want to do something on Dennett's four grades of creature, the "tower" where each rung adds one new trick. Roughly: Darwinian creatures just come hard-wired, their behavior was selected over generations and they can't change it; Skinnerian creatures can learn by trial and error and reinforcement within their own life; Popperian creatures can run a move *inside their head* first, simulate the outcome, and only then act, so their bad ideas die instead of them; Gregorian creatures can pick up tools, and above all other minds' discoveries — mind-tools you can copy for free, basically software for thinking.

I dont want to just explain that ladder, I want them to feel it. Design me a runnable game where the group splits into four teams, each one stuck at a different rung, all playing the same foraging/hunting task with the same scoring, so the rungs actually show up as differences in play rather than in my slides. Give me the rules, the materials (keep it cheap — paper, dice, index cards, no laptops), a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, and a timeline that fits 75 minutes including the debrief.

Two things I specifically want the activity to make them run into, because these are the parts people gloss over:

First, the Popperian's edge is only ever as good as its internal model. I want at least one round where a wrong-but-confident model makes the Popperian do *worse* than the dumb Skinnerian, so they feel that simulation isn't free and isn't automatically right — the inner environment can lie to you.

Second, the Gregorian twist. When one team stumbles on a tool, it should get written on a card and become copyable to the other teams at basically no cost, so the top rung reads as shared, public, cumulative mind-tools rather than individual cleverness. I want them to notice that the interesting thing about Gregorian creatures isn't the smart one, it's that the discovery spreads.

Then a debrief that maps the moments of the game onto the four rungs, tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it live, and is honest about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to just say out loud myself. No lecturing up front — they should be a bit lost for the first twenty minutes and only get the vocabulary at the end.

Last thing: leave me one short optional epilogue question for the debrief, something like "is there a rung above Gregorian?" that I can toss out if we're running ahead, without pretending there's a settled answer.
