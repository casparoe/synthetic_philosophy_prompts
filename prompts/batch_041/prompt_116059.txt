I was at a reading group on AI safety last week and we spent most of the evening on this distinction that one guy kept hammering on -- he said it's a thing in the literature, between agents built to chase a goal they've been handed (maximise some score, hit some target) and agents built to do whatever a human would approve of, where a person's judgment is what's being tracked instead of a fixed objective. He called them goal-directed and approval-directed, and there was alot of nodding when he said the second kind is obviously the safer one to build. The analogy he used, and that the whole room seemed happy with, was an employee: a goal-directed agent is like a salesperson on commission who'll do anything to hit the number, and an approval-directed agent is like the conscientious employee who just wants to do a good job in the eyes of their boss. Someone else said it's really the old genie-versus-butler thing, which I think is the same picture in a different costume. I have a nagging feeling the analogy is doing all the work in the argument and that nobody checked whether it holds.

So what I want: the correspondence table first -- in the employee picture, what plays the role of the boss, the commission, the performance review, the company culture, the labour market the employee could quit into, the employee's own life outside work -- and then an honest account of where the mapping breaks for the case we actually care about, namely an agent whose boss is a single human rather than a whole organisation, whose approval has to be inferred from data rather than observed, and who can't be fired or walked out on the way a person can. Then the part I really care about: does the employee analogy, pushed honestly, support the conclusion the room drew, or does it quietly undermine it? Yes-men and sycophants are employees optimising for their boss's approval, after all, and so are the ones who learn to manage the boss instead of doing the job. And if the employee picture turns out to be the wrong vehicle for this, is there a better analogy for the same point, or is the whole idea that there are two clean kinds of agents already an artifact of the analogy rather than a fact about agents?
