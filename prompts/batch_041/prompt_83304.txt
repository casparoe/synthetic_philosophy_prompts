I am a software engineer with no formal training in economics or philosophy, and I sit on a small internal committee at my employer that decides which local ballot measures the company will publicly endorse. We rely on written recommendations from the economist we retain as an adviser. Over four years her verdicts have been consistent enough that I suspect she is applying a rule she has never stated, and we now face a measure that her past verdicts may not settle.

Her verdicts to date, as she gave them to us:

1. Permanent citywide rent control covering all units, in a city with thousands of independent landlords, a vacancy rate below one percent, and a construction pipeline that has not kept pace with population. Opposed. Her reason: the cap transfers rent from owners to whoever holds a lease in the year it passes, and the people who lose are the ones trying to move in.

2. A $15 minimum wage in a metro area where two hospital systems and three logistics firms account for most low-wage employment. Supported. Where a handful of employers set the wage, she said, the wage is not a competitive price.

3. A fourteen-day cap on the prices of ice, bottled water, and portable generators in a county where flooding had left two stores open. Supported. The spike, she said, came from panic buying and closed roads rather than from any shortage of stock, shelves were refilled by the third day, and the cap expired on its own.

4. A permanent statutory ceiling on interest and fees for short-term small loans, in a state with hundreds of competing lenders. Opposed. The ceiling does not produce cheaper credit, she said, it pushes the highest-risk borrowers out of the market, and those are the people it was meant to help.

5. A permanent cap on the rates charged by the single municipal water utility, which has no competitors and cannot have any, because of the physical network. Supported.

In the third case she also remarked that the price was "not carrying any information," and in the fourth that the ceiling "lands on the wrong side of the trade"; I did not follow either remark.

The measure before us now is a permanent citywide minimum wage of $22 an hour, applying to every employer in a city with an extremely high cost of living. Low-wage employment in the city is not concentrated: thousands of employers, no dominant firm in any affected sector. The workers affected are the lowest-paid in the city and have almost no savings and no realistic outside options. The two studies we were given disagree sharply about how many hours of work would disappear, so we cannot resolve that question before the vote.

Please tell me: (a) what she will say about this measure; (b) which rules fit all five earlier verdicts, including any rule that looks plausible but that the record rules out — and say so plainly if no single rule fits, rather than forcing one; (c) what each surviving rule predicts about the new measure, and which prediction you would bet on and why; (d) the single further case that would best separate the surviving rules; and (e) if the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine this one, what principle about price controls we would be adopting if we follow her recommendation either way.
