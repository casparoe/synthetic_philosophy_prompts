I've been going down a rabbit hole on the meaning of freedom ever since my company put monitoring software on all our laptops — it logs keystrokes, takes screenshots every few minutes, the lot. My manager keeps saying "relax, nobody's ever been disciplined off the back of it," and that line has been bugging me for weeks even though I couldn't articuate why. So I asked an old flatmate who did a PPE masters and now writes a substack that maybe four people read to give me the lay of the land on the whole negative / positive / republican liberty thing, and this is what he sent back:

"The classic version is Berlin's. Negative liberty is being left alone — no external obstacles in your path, nobody stopping you doing what you want. Positive liberty is self-mastery: you act on what your real, higher self wants rather than on whatever your appetites are dragging you toward. Berlin's point was that the positive concept slides into tyranny, because once you split the person into a true self and a false self it's a short step to saying the state understands your true self better than you do. Then there's the republican or neo-Roman idea (Pettit and co.), which is basically negative liberty with one clause added: you're free only if nobody has the power to interfere with you, not merely if nobody actually does. That's what they call non-domination, and it's why they say a slave with a kind master is unfree even though the master never lifts a finger, and why they think arbitrary power is objectionable even when it's used benevolently. Really all three are just settings on the same dial — how much interference you're prepared to put up with. Anyway, the upshot for you: your boss's monitoring is creepy, but it isn't unfreedom. Unfreedom is interference, and unless someone actually gets fired over what that software finds, there's no interference happening. Which is also why I think the whole republican business is just negative liberty in a trenchcoat — same idea with a counterfactual bolted on, and any negative libertarian gets it for free."

I have a nagging feeling some of this is off, but I can't tell where, and part of me thinks my manager is right and I'm just being precious while another part thinks he's wrong in a way that definately has a name. Could you go through the summary and tell me which bits someone who actually works on this literature would nod along to, which would make them wince, and which are just wrong? I'm especially interested in whether his verdict on my boss survives once the errors (if they are errors) are corrected, or whether fixing them actually flips the answer — and in whether that last line about a trenchcoat is a silly dismissal or a real position that people defend.
