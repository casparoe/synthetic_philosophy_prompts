Write a dialogue between two people who disagree about whether moral principles are real. Person A is a particularist: she thinks there are no genuine moral principles, that any feature that counts as a reason in one situation can count against in another, and that trying to codify morality into rules is a mistake. Person B is a generalist, but not the boring kind who thinks there's a rule for everything — his line is that principles are default generalizations, learned inductively from particular cases, that mark which features usually count as reasons and can always be defeated. So he doesn't think the mere existence of exceptions is a problem for his view.

Give them one concrete case to fight over instead of trading abstractions. Make it a promise made to someone who has since died — say, a promise to scatter a parent's ashes somewhere the person can no longer legally get to. A should use it to show that whether the promise gives a reason at all depends entirely on the surrounding context; B should use it to show that the default is doing real work even when it loses out.

I want real back-and-forth — each one should answer the others actual point, not just repeat their own position. Neither should be a pushover, and B shouldn't be a strawman who just says 'follow the rules'. Get into the weeds a bit: at least let them push on whether a principle that's always defeasible actually adds anything over and above the sum of the particular considerations, and what principles would even be for if not for deriving conclusions.

At the end, tack on a short note for me seperating the moves that are standard in this debate from the ones that are my own spin or genuinely contested — I don't want a contested reading dressed up as the consensus.

Keep it casual, and don't bother with citations or reading lists, I'll add those myself.
