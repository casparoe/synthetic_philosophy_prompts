Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is a psychiatrist, and we ended up somewhere neither of us expected: arguing about what it means for a biological part to have a job. The dispute began with the familiar question of whether mental disorder names a natural kind or a social construction, but the part I cannot stop thinking about is a presupposition built into how the field actually diagnoses. The practice is this: clinicians and the diagnostic manuals they rely on treat a condition as a disorder only when some internal mechanism is malfunctioning; it is not enough that the condition is rare, unwanted, or harmful. A capacity that is merely statistically unusual, or merely disapproved of, is not thereby a disorder — something has to be failing to do what it is supposed to do. That is why the field has, over the decades, reversed itself about whether certain conditions belong in the manual at all, and why it keeps insisting that a disorder must involve a breakdown rather than a difference.

Now, to say that a mechanism is failing to do what it is supposed to do is to presuppose a theory of what a biological part is for. There is a specific family of theories here. On one view, a part's function is whatever it was selected for — by natural selection, or by learning — so that a heart's function is to pump because pumping is what hearts were selected to do. On another view, a part's function is simply the causal contribution it makes to the operation of the larger system it sits inside. These two views often agree but need not, and the choice between them is precisely the choice at stake in a program in philosophy of mind — teleosemantics — that tries to explain how a brain state can be about anything at all by identifying its content with what it was selected to represent. If that program is right, then a state's content, and therefore what counts as misrepresenting, is fixed by its history of selection.

So here is what I want from you. Reconstruct the theory of function that the diagnostic practice presupposes, and say whether it genuinely requires the selection-history view or whether the causal-role view would serve just as well — and give me a case where the two would issue different verdicts about whether something is a disorder. Tell me whether the practice is internally consistent: can there be malfunction without a history of selection, as in a trait that is a mere byproduct, or in a mechanism belonging to a system that was designed or trained rather than evolved? And say where the practice is underdetermined — where several theories of function would license the same diagnosis, so that the presupposition is doing less work than it appears to. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed theory with its rival, what would actually have to change in the clinic: which diagnoses would move, and which would stay put for reasons that have nothing to do with the theory of function? I care less about which theory is true than about what the practice is quietly committing itself to.
