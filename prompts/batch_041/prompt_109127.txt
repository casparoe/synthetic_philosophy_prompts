I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a one-year fellowship and I need help turning a half-formed idea into something that actually reads like a project. No citations, please, I'll add those myself.

The idea: everyone repeats the for-want-of-a-nail story — a thrown horseshoe, a lost battle, a changed world — but when you press on it, 'contingency' is doing at least five seperate jobs and nobody keeps them apart. (1) the outcome was unpredictable even though it was fully determined, the weather sense, sensitive dependence on initial conditions. (2) Small perturbations really did get amplified into large effects by some specific mechanism — a threshold, a coordination cascade, positive feedback — so the issue is amplification, not size. (3) The outcome got locked in and became irreversible, so later corrections were blocked, the QWERTY-ish increasing-returns picture. (4) There were several equally viable outcomes and nothing in the situation selected between them, so the winner is arbitrary but nothing was fragile. (5) The world is genuinely indeterministic and the outcome literally had no sufficient cause. These are independent: you can have (1) without (2) (unpredictable but robust), (2) without (3) (big but reversible), and (4) without anything chancy happening at all.

So the project is: make that distinction precise, then say what it would take to find out which sense is doing the work in a given episode, and show that it can be done at all. I suspect most of the empirical content sits in (2), and that the way in is to stop trying to imagine counterfactual worlds — you can't, a counterfactual needs a causal model, so the what-if stories are only as good as the theory smuggled into them — and instead look for near-duplicate units: places, firms, colonies, villages, army units that start out looking alike and then diverge. Then measure how fast the resemblance decays, something like a half-life for initial conditions, and ask whether divergence is front-loaded (one early accident decides everything, path dependence) or accumulates steadily (lots of small independent pushes, so no single nail mattered). And whether divergence runs faster where you can independently identify a threshold or a positive feedback loop.

Why I think it's open: people argue endlessly about whether this or that was inevitable and the arguement never resolves, I think because they're using different senses of the word and because we never observe the counterfactual. Only one version of the past happens, so the potential-outcomes problem shows up at the macro scale with no treatment group. But the near-duplicate trick gives you a pseudo-control in some cases, and the divergence-rate measure doesn't need a counterfactual world at all — only units that were similar once.

What I need from you: the proposal itself. State the question so it has a determinate answer. Explain why the existing positions leave it open, framed as positions rather than names and dates. Lay out the method month by month for a year — I'd rather do one conceptual paper plus one worked case study than pretend I'm going to build a big dataset, so be honest about scope. Say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing. I think one real failure mode is that the five senses collapse into one or two once you define them properly; another is that there's no way to pick a proxy for 'small perturbation' that isn't already defined by the outcome it's supposed to explain, which would make the whole thing circular. And say why a fellowship committee should fund a philosopher of history poking at what looks like an econometrics problem.
