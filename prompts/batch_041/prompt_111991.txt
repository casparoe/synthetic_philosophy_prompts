I have no academic background in philosophy; I read about it in my spare time and think about it on long walks, which is to say that whatever I write here will be clumsily put, and I would rather you answered from your own understanding than reached for references I cannot check. The thing I keep circling is what I have seen called causation by omission. When we hold someone to blame for what they failed to do, we seem to be treating the failure as a cause of what followed. But nothing happened at the moment in question: there is no event to point at, no date to give it, and yet the nothing is supposed to have done the work. I would like a satirical piece built on that puzzle.

Please write a mock self-help column, the sort of thing a smug lifestyle coach publishes, in which the coach's entire pitch is that you can shrink your causal footprint by doing less. The irony I am after is structural: on the view that our failures cause things, every hour spent doing nothing enlarges the footprint rather than shrinking it. I want roughly seven hundred words, a headline, the coach's first-person voice, and a closing exercise for the reader. Nobody should wink; the deadpan should hold all the way through. The target is the everyday confidence that inaction leaves you causally innocent, together with the productivity discourse that has turned it into a virtue.

For the piece to be worth anything, the jokes have to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from mocking wellness culture in general. I would like it to turn on at least these: the idea that your inaction causes something when matters would have gone differently had you acted; the awkwardness that an omission is not an event at all, so it has no natural place in a chain of causes and no obvious duration; the embarrassment of plenty, since at any moment there are countless things I am not preventing, so if omissions cause, something must select which ones count, and it is not obvious that causation itself can do the selecting; the nested case of failing to prevent someone else's failure to act; the difference between doing something and allowing it, and whether it can bear the moral weight we place on it; and the fact that the law pursues you for an omission only where you already had a duty to act, which suggests the duty is doing the work and the causation is not.

Please invent some apparatus for the coach: a named method, a paid programme, and something like an offsets scheme in which clients buy compensation for the harms they failed to prevent. Include a client testimonial that gets a detail wrong in an instructive way. Write in British English, for a British reader. Do not supply references, reading lists or citations, and do not name any living person; I want to be able to use this without checking anything. The piece should be legible to someone who has never read a word of philosophy, and fair enough that someone who has will recognise exactly what is being sent up.
