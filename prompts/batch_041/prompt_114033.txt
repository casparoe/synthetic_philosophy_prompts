I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about transcendental arguments — the kind that begin from something no one can deny, such as that we have experiences at all, and then claim to show that some disputed thing is a necessary condition of that starting point, so that the sceptic cannot so much as raise his doubt without presupposing what he doubts. My friend's objection, which I could not answer to my own satisfaction, was that the argument at best shows something about what our concepts require of us rather than about what there is, and that a sceptic who grants the premise but refuses the conclusion — or who says "so I must think that way; how does that make it so?" — walks away untouched. I want to see whether the technique can be made funny without being made false, so I have a commission for you.

Write a self-help column of about 750 words in the relentlessly upbeat voice of an invented guru who teaches transcendental argumentation as a general life skill, the way one might teach speed-reading or networking, of the sort that runs in an in-flight magazine or a Sunday supplement. Give it a headline and a byline you invent. Present the method as a numbered sequence of steps, and every step must be the real move rather than a cartoon of it: start from a premise no one can deny without performative absurdity; identify the condition that must hold for that premise to be so much as intelligible; declare the condition necessary rather than merely actual; then dispatch the sceptic by observing that his doubt presupposes the very thing he doubts. The guru may name-drop Kant grandly while treating the technique as his own discovery, but he must not misunderstand it. The humour should come from his enthusiasm, his testimonials, and the small domestic examples he invents, not from any logical error: someone who has taught the first Critique should wince in recognition at each step, not at a mistake.

Include a short reader-mail section in which a correspondent raises the strongest available objection — that the argument secures necessity only relative to our conceptual scheme, or that one can accept the premise and deny the entailment — and the guru answers in character, in a way that on inspection does not work, failing for a reason the knowledgeable reader can name. Add a sidebar of two or three "common mistakes" that are genuinely the mistakes practitioners make. The piece should leave a reader feeling the live question — whether the necessity established is in the world or only in our way of thinking, and what force the argument has against someone who simply declines to play — without ever stating that question as the moral, and without winking at the reader or breaking voice. No named living people; the target is the practice, not a person.

Begin directly with the column itself — headline and byline count as part of it — with no preface, no explanation of what you are doing, and no closing commentary.
