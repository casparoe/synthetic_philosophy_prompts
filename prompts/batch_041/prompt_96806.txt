I have come to hold a view I would like tested rather than confirmed. The orthodox verdict — that honoring sunk costs is a fallacy, that only future consequences bear on rational choice — I now take to be not a general truth about practical rationality but a local theorem about one particular model: expected-utility maximization over a fixed and exogenous state space, with preferences held stable and outcomes assessed solely from the present forward. Where the model's assumptions hold, the verdict holds. The philosophical interest lies in the cases where they do not, and I suspect that outside them the verdict either flips or dissolves.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters ranked by how decisive they would be, and sorted into three kinds: cases where honoring a sunk cost is plainly irrational even granting my concessions; cases that merely make my view awkward; and arguments or results that would show my view is vacuous rather than permissive — capable of absorbing every case, including the classic demonstrations in which people throw good money after bad.

Work through the following alleged escape routes and tell me which do genuine work and which are ad hoc rescues: (1) continuing is itself constitutive of a commitment or an identity rather than a means to an outcome, as when the good at stake is partly constituted by the history of investment — a friendship, a marriage, a body of work; (2) a prior investment is evidence, whether about the project's quality or about one's own preferences, of the self-signaling kind; (3) honoring a cost functions as a commitment device against one's own future time-inconsistency; (4) the irrelevance of bygones presupposes a consequentialist conception of rationality that a non-consequentialist need not share; and (5) a past expenditure can create complementary capacities that alter the payoff structure, so that the cost is only nominally sunk.

Then the two things I most want. First, an honest assessment of whether my position risks anything at all: does it predict something its orthodox rival does not, or has it been qualified until it can accommodate every case? If it can accommodate everything, say whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or evidence that it was never a substantive thesis. Second, what the most sophisticated defender of the orthodox verdict would concede, on their own terms, actually counts against them — not the familiar criticisms, but the concessions they would have to make to keep the verdict intact. Where you can, name the specific case or result that would move me, and say what it would have to show, rather than telling me only that counterexamples exist.
