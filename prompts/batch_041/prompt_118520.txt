Ok, so — I'm a science journalist, I mostly cover ecology and animal behavior, and I've fallen down a hole I'm pretty sure is a story, except I can't tell whether the argument at the bottom of it is actually any good. I sat in on a grad seminar about wild animal suffering. The framing was: what's the net welfare of a wild ecosystem, once you actually count individuals instead of just species? And one of them laid out an argument I wrote down almost verbatim, because it was suspiciously tidy. He even had a little model for it — sum the lifetime welfare of every individual in the patch, and for these species the sum comes out as a long list of painful early deaths with a few successful breeders sprinkled in. Anyway, here it is.

(1) For r-selected animals — most insects, most fish, most small birds and mammals — the overwhelming majority of individuals die before they ever reproduce, and they die badly: starvation, exposure, parasites, being eaten, often slowly.

(2) So take a randomly chosen individual of one of those species. Its life contains far more suffering than enjoyment. Its expected lifetime welfare is negative.

(3) Destroy a habitat and you lower the carrying capacity, so fewer of those individuals are born and survive there.

(4) If each such life is net negative, then having fewer of them means less suffering overall. That's just addition.

(5) So habitat destruction, all else equal, reduces wild animal suffering. It's a good thing.

(6) And nothing in the argument cares about scale. So the logical endpoint is that the best thing you could do for wild animals is eliminate habitat entirely, or engineer the extinction of life.

(7) Which is monstrous, and would also license all sorts of other horrors, so something has to be wrong.

He kept insisting (1) and (2) are just ecology and (3) and (4) are just arithmetic, and honestly I half-believe him — I don't think you get to wave away that most wild lives end early and badly. So I'd rather keep the ecology and pay for it somewhere in the arithmetic. Where's the move that's actually doing the work here, and if I give it up, what does it cost me? Concretely — what do I have to give up, and what do I get to keep, if I decide the arithmetic step is the bad one? And if the arithmetic is fine after all, which of the other steps should I be looking at instead?
