I sit on my state bar's character and fitness committee, and Thursday's case has turned into an argument about what "good moral character" is even supposed to mean. The applicant passed the bar, clean record for eight years, but two old incidents: a shoplifting citation at 19, and at 24, while a paralegal, pasting a chunk of another firm's public filing into an internal memo without attribution (he disclosed it himself, unprompted, when he applied). Our rule says character is shown by "a pattern of conduct over time," and isolated youthful lapses don't disqualify unless they reveal "a trait of dishonesty" — so the whole framework assumes theres a stable trait underneath that we're detecting.

One member keeps pushing an alternative: stop asking whether he *has* the trait, and instead have him map the situations that reliably push him toward cutting corners and commit to concrete structural safeguards (never drafting from a single source under deadline, a mandatory peer read, that kind of thing), treating the old incidents as evidence about which situations those are rather than about who he is. The other side says if you drop the trait language you can't justify excluding anyone — a "pattern" is just a set of situations, and anyone can promise themselves safeguards. The investigator's report frames the applicant as having a dishonest streak; the applicant's own statement explains both incidents as being young, broke, and in over his head.

Act as a philosopher of moral psychology and draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. Brainstorm the strongest version of both positions first, then recommend a concrete disposition — admit, admit with conditions (say what and for how long), or deny — and say what we should do with the situation-profile idea going forward for all applicants. Give an honest hearing to the best argument against whatever you land on. No case law or citations needed, I just want the reasoning tight.
