I run a small evening puzzle group at our local library — six regulars, all sharp, all comfortable with crosswords and sudoku, none of them with much mathematics beyond school — and next month I want to give a seventy-five-minute session on the picture puzzles in which you are shown a row of figures on the left and a row on the right and must work out what distinguishes the two rows. I have read enough about these to understand that the interesting thing is never any particular answer but the skill of inducing the rule from a handful of cases, and I have drawn my own deck of twelve rather than using the classic set, partly so that I can control which traps my group falls into. What I want from you is neither the puzzles solved nor a lecture on the theory behind them: plan the session for me. Which idea has to be in their heads before the first puzzle can even be attempted — my own hunch is that it is not "look carefully" but something about what kind of thing an answer is permitted to be — what has to come after that, and why the order cannot be shuffled; which single puzzle from my deck should carry the whole session, and what it must contain and, just as importantly, must not contain (I am inclined to use one in which the tempting rule accounts for every figure on the left but quietly fails on the right, but tell me whether that is the right one to lead with or the right one to save for later); and the misconceptions you would expect this particular audience to bring — I am already braced for them to assume the puzzle will tell them when they are right, and for them to describe the figures as things rather than as shapes — together with exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off rather than merely corrected after the fact.

One thing I would like you to take seriously is the moment I have started calling the click: the instant a solver decides they have seen the rule and stops looking. My regulars are quick, and I suspect the click arrives far too early and then defends itself. So build the plan to delay and discipline the click, and tell me what I should say when someone clicks on a rule that fits four of the six left-hand figures and none of the right-hand ones. I would also like a single closing question or task that would show me whether they have actually acquired the skill rather than merely enjoyed an hour — something that separates someone who has grasped that the answer is a property which all of the lefts share and every one of the rights breaks from someone who has simply got good at guessing what I drew.

Finally, be explicit about what you would leave out at this level, and say so within the plan. I do not want the session drifting into how such puzzles are represented in artificial intelligence, or into the deeper question of what it means to possess a concept at all, or into the history of who invented them — I would rather they spent the time looking. Assume no projector, only a whiteboard and printed sheets, and assume that the last ten of my seventy-five minutes go to the closing question.
