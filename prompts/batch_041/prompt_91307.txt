I have been defending a fairly strong reading of Dennett's hierarchy of creatures — the Darwinian, the Skinnerian, the Popperian and the Gregorian — on which it is a genuine ladder rather than a mere taxonomy: each grade strictly contains the capacities of the one beneath it, the higher grade dominates the lower whenever the informational and metabolic costs can be paid, and selection of any kind will therefore drive a lineage upward as far as it can afford to go. My worry is that I have stated the view so that nothing could ever dislodge it. Tell me what would actually count against it: which observations, experimental results or conceptual arguments, and what the ladder predicts that a flatter, non-hierarchical account does not. I should like the defeaters ranked, from those that would be fatal to those that would merely be awkward, with a clear line between what a defender must concede and what a defender may reasonably set aside. Two matters interest me especially: creatures that seem to occupy different rungs in different domains — simulating and planning while foraging, say, yet purely Skinnerian in their social behaviour — and whether the Gregorian grade is a genuine fourth level or merely the Popperian one plus external scaffolding. What would move me most is a case in which the ladder mispredicts, or an argument that the ordering is doing normative work it cannot honestly own. Finally, say plainly whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, in British English, in the manner of a letter.
