Question: Gödel's modal version of the ontological argument is often described as valid but of dubious soundness. Set out its structure, explain what work the distinctive premises do, and assess whether it succeeds in proving that God exists.

Student's answer:

"Gödel's modal ontological argument is a formalized version of the Leibnizian proof. He works with a primitive notion of a 'positive' property — roughly, a perfection, or a property that makes its bearer greater. A being counts as 'God-like' if it has all the positive properties. Gödel then lays down axioms: a property and its negation can't both be positive; positivity is itself a necessary matter, so a property that's positive is positive in every possible world; and necessary existence is a positive property. From these he derives the conclusion that a God-like being exists necessarily, via the modal logic S5 — if it's possible that something exists necessarily, then it exists necessarily.

The proof is valid: grant the axioms and S5, and the conclusion follows. The real question is soundness. Gödel has to assume that a God-like being is possible — the same possibility premise Plantinga takes as his starting point — and that's the weakest link. The most famous objection, due to Sobel, is that the axioms are actually inconsistent: they produce 'modal collapse,' in which every true proposition is necessary and nothing is contingent. Since the axioms have a contradiction among their consequences, the argument is unsound. Even setting that aside, the premises just assume that God-likeness and necessary existence are 'positive,' which is exactly what a non-believer denies, so the proof begs the question."
