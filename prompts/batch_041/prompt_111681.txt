Write a dialogue between two players in a game of Nomic — the game Peter Suber devised, in which the rules, including the rules that govern how rules are changed, are themselves amended by vote. Under the rules currently in force, a rule change is adopted if it receives two-thirds of the votes cast. On the table is Amendment 12, which would raise the threshold for all future rule changes to three-quarters of the votes cast and which also contains a clause declaring itself retroactive to the beginning of the round. Six players voted; the tally was 4–2 in favor — exactly two-thirds, but short of three-quarters.

One player insists that Amendment 12 never took effect: it needed three-quarters and got two-thirds, and a clause inside a failed proposal cannot retroactively supply the majority it lacked. The other insists that this reasoning runs in a circle: whether Amendment 12 needed two-thirds or three-quarters depends on whether Amendment 12 passed, and if it passed then its retroactive clause means the threshold was always three-quarters, so it never passed — hence the rules as written settle nothing, and the players must decide the matter themselves before play can continue.

Let them argue this out in genuine back-and-forth, each pressing the other's weakest point. The first should demand to know why a claim that the rules settle nothing is anything other than a licence for whoever is ahead to rewrite them. The second should reply in earnest — for instance, that the first player's own resolution is equally a choice, and that some principle is needed to distinguish a legitimate repair of the rulebook from a seizure of it — and should say what that principle could be. Both should be equally sharp, neither a strawman, and they should reach either a resolution or an honest standoff rather than talking past each other.
