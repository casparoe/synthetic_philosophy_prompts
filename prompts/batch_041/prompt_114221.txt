Hey, so I've got a work-in-progress talk coming up in about three weeks at our department's practical reason / decision theory workshop, and I'm trying to get ahead on the Q&A before I start panicking. I'm a third-year PhD student, my area is decision theory with a bit of moral psychology on the side, and the paper is on Parfit's hitchhiker. The short version of my line: I don't think the hitchhiker case shows that it can ever be rational to be irrational, which is more or less how it gets sold. I think it shows that "rational" is doing two seperate jobs — there's a question about what's rational to do once you're already in town with no chance of being caught, and a different question about what's rational to be the kind of person who does. Once you split those, the disposition to pay comes out straightforwardly rational (given you want the ride), and the whole thing looks like an equivocation rather than a paradox. So the thesis I'm defending is that "rational irrationality" is a misnomer here, at least in this case.

The audience is what's making me nervous. There are two committed causal decision theorists who will immediately want to know why my "being the kind of person who pays" isn't just evidential reasoning in a trench coat, and whether a disposition can have the kind of causal grip on the driver's offer that my story needs it to have. There's one guy who's been deep in updateless-ish stuff lately and will probably say I've just reinvented machinery he already has and that I should stop pretending it's new. Two Kantians will want to reframe the whole thing as being about whether you have a reason to keep your word at all rather than about rationality, and they'll push on "ought implies can" and on whether the self-interested driver setup is even the right kind of case. And my supervisor, who's written a lot on Parfit, is almost certainly going to ask whether I'm actually disagreeing with Parfit or just renaming a move he already makes.

Can you write me a Q&A prep sheet for this crowd?
