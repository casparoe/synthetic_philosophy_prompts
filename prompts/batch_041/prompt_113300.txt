I am a graduate student putting together a handout for a first-year seminar on practical reason, and I want to organise it around the case usually called Buridan's ass. The standard treatment is useless for teaching, because it makes the puzzle look like one question ('what should the ass do?') with one clever answer ('flip a coin'), when the interesting thing is how many different and incompatible things people can mean by that answer. So I want a decision tree rather than an essay: it should begin from a commitment a student can actually be brought to accept and end at a named position, so that the class discovers that 'the ass should just pick one' is not a single view but a family of them. Fix the root node at the claim that there are deliberative situations in which, by the agent's own lights, the reasons bearing on two available options are exactly balanced — neither option is better, and not because the agent does not care about either. Every subsequent node must be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each node must divide the space rather than ask how confident anyone is.

Four distinctions have to do real work in the tree, and I want each of them to show up as at least one branch node. First, the difference between a balance that is an artefact of the agent's ignorance (more information or more reflection would break it), a balance that is an artefact of the agent's evaluative make-up (a differently constituted agent would have a preference), and a balance that survives however much the agent knows and however she is constituted. Second, the difference between a balance in the values of the options and a balance in the reasons for choosing them — an agent may hold that the value-balance is real while the reason-balance is not, since urgency, a prior promise, or the fact that someone is waiting are reasons without being values. Third, the difference between symmetric ties, where the options are qualitatively identical, and heterogeneous ties, where the options differ in kind and yet rank equally; I want the tree built so that it never quietly assumes symmetry, and I want at least two nodes posed as concrete cases rather than general claims, one of each sort. Fourth, whether the tie is a state the agent is in or a process she is going through — in any real case time passes, the hay wilts and the ass gets hungrier, so the perfectly static set-up may be doing illicit work, and I would like a node that tests whether a position survives making the case dynamic.

Leaves should carry the name of the position, or 'unnamed' where the tree reaches a combination nobody defends, plus one sentence naming that position's most awkward commitment. At a minimum I need leaves for: the view that genuine ties are impossible because a sufficient reason always decides the case; the view that ties are real and the agent must simply choose, with no reason determining the choice, and that this is precisely where agency shows up; the decision-theoretic dissolution on which a complete preference ordering may be indifferent, and indifference makes either option permissible, so there is no puzzle left; the view that the relation between tied options is a fourth comparative relation, neither better nor worse nor equal, so calling it a tie misdescribes it; the view that the options are incomparable, and that this is a fact about value rather than a defect in the agent; the view that the act of choosing is itself what creates a reason for the option chosen; the view that what the agent does is not choosing but merely picking, and that picking is arational rather than irrational; the view that the rational response is to deliberate about how to decide rather than what to decide, whether by randomising or by settling on the first option that clears some threshold; and the view that there is no fact of the matter about preference apart from choice, so the case cannot arise. Where two of these collapse into one another once the branch questions are made precise, say so and say which question failed to separate them. Keep the whole thing to something a student could work through in fifteen minutes — twelve nodes at most, uneven branches are fine — and flag any leaf reached only by a path I would not want to defend in print, along with any combination of answers my tree cannot reach and a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody is there because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
