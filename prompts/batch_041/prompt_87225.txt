I'm a backend engineer by trade — I write distributed systems for a living and have no formal training in philosophy beyond a couple of undergraduate survey courses — and I've somehow ended up drafting a short talk for a seminar my aunt runs for ICU nurses. She has spent thirty years in critical care and has watched advance directives do a great deal of good and a certain amount of harm, so the question is not abstract for her audience. What I need from you is a scenario built to order. Please take the role of a philosopher who works on autonomy, personal identity, and biomedical ethics, and think carefully before writing. Start by brainstorming: list three candidate features that could plausibly make an advance directive binding in one situation and not binding in another, spend a sentence or two on why each is or is not the right lever, and only then build the case. Skip references and reading lists entirely — I won't chase them down, and I would rather you spend the words on the scenario itself. Write for a smart reader with no philosophy background: define your terms as you introduce them and keep the prose plain.

The deliverable is a matched pair of cases, A and B, designed so that two familiar positions about directives come apart across them. The first position — call it the earlier-self view — holds that a directive binds because the person who wrote it was the author of the life in question, so that a later self, even a lucid one, is not entitled to repudiate the considered judgments of the self that lived that life up to then. The second — call it the present-self view — holds that a directive has no authority of its own and matters only as evidence of what the person now wants, so that a competent present refusal or demand defeats it. Build the pair so that all of the following hold: (a) in case A the right answer is to honor the directive, and in case B the right answer is to override it; (b) in both cases the person is fully competent and lucid at the moment of decision, uncoerced, not clinically depressed, addicted, or in any kind of acute psychiatric or medical crisis, and whatever they want is sincere and stable over a period of weeks; the directive was written after real deliberation with medical advice and covers the situation without ambiguity; and nobody in the story is confused, deceived, or acting in bad faith, so that none of the usual escape hatches can be doing the work; (c) the two cases differ in exactly one respect, and that respect has to be a structural feature of the life or of the directive's content — not the strength or stability of anyone's preference, not how severe the outcomes are, not pressure from family or clinicians; (d) each of the two positions above must be forced to a definite verdict in both cases, and each must get exactly one of the two wrong, since a pair on which either view is right twice settles nothing; (e) the setting is present-day medicine — a hospital, a hospice, a care home — with no science fiction, no mind uploading or personality exchange, no stipulated real self lurking behind the person's stated wishes, and no case that turns on a factual error about the illness or the treatment; (f) each case runs under 250 words and names the condition and the treatment precisely enough that a nurse would know exactly what honoring the directive would mean on the ward.

Then do the check. For each case and each of the two positions, state the verdict and the reasoning that produces it, and make clear which premise of that position is carrying the weight. Tell me which single feature of the pair is the lever, and demonstrate it rather than asserting it: describe the variant of each case in which that feature is absent, say what the verdict becomes, and explain why the reasoning shifts. Explain how a defender of the earlier-self view and a defender of the present-self view would each try to absorb both cases without abandoning their principle, and whether that absorption succeeds. Finish with one sentence stating what the pair is supposed to show. The point of the exercise is to make the bindingness of a directive come out of something other than the earlier self's say-so and other than the present self's say-so; if your pair can be fully explained by either of those alone, it hasn't done the job. And if you find the specification unsatisfiable as written, say which constraint you had to bend and why, rather than quietly building a case that only appears to meet it.
