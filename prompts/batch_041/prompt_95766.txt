I run a tiny interview show on a Discord server with a few friends, mostly engineers, we're all amateur philosophy nerds. Next weeks guest is a decision theorist who defends something she calls scale fanaticism, and I'm the one doing the interview. Here's her view as I understand it from her paper, so you don't have to go look anything up:

- She thinks expected value maximization is the only rule in town, and she's happy to bite the bullet on it telling you to take any bet with a big enough payoff, however tiny the odds. That's the fanatical part, and she embraces it.
- But she adds what she calls a usability floor. Probabilities you can't estimate to within a couple orders of magnitude, she says, arent really probabilities, they're placeholders. You're permitted to ignore prospects that live down there, though not required to.
- She also thinks the usual fix of just capping utility (saying happiness or whatever tops out at some max) is a cheat, because the cap gets picked to dodge the conclusion rather than discovered anywhere.
- The move that makes it interesting: she says the floor does not get you out of the big bets on the future. Her argument is that some outcomes aren't just huge numbers, they're structural — they change which game gets played at all, whether there's anyone left to play, whether the future is one kind of thing or another — and structural stakes don't get filtered by the floor even when the odds are absurd. She calls the difference the number/structure gap, and says it's why longtermism survives her floor while the standard one-off mugging case doesn't.

Write me eight to ten interview questions. I want to press her, not have her restate the view. At least one question each on: (1) whether the number/structure gap is a real distinction or just a very big number with extra steps, (2) what happens to her view under the paralysis objection — if there's always a bet with more upside and worse odds, why would you ever stop looking? — and (3) how the floor behaves in the mugging case where some stranger offers an astronomically large payoff for a tiny stake. For each of those three, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not stuck when she's smooth about it. Also throw in one question that uses an analogy from my world — a one-in-a-billion bug in production, or a really long-tail A/B test — to probe whether the floor is really about probability or about my ability to model the situation at all. And end on a closer about what evidence or argument would actually move her on the floor itself, since it sounds like she could just nudge it whenever it gets inconvenient.
