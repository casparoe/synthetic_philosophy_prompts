I'm in a seminar on norms and conventions, and last week's session — on whether ordinary conventions like tipping, which side of the escalator you stand on, queueing at a bar, or crossing against the light are moral obligations — ended when the professor said, in a tone that closed the discussion, "When in Rome, do as the Romans do." The case on the table was a visiting student who won't tip in a city where tipping is expected, stands on the wrong side of the escalator, and crosses an empty street against the light, on the grounds that she is only passing through and these are not her conventions. I don't think the proverb settles her case, but I also can't say precisely what it claims. Can you separate out the distinct claims it could be making — from "you will get along better if you conform," through "conventions bind only those who share them," to something that makes a visitor's obligations depend on the host community's practices — and say of each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; whether it treats purely coordinative conventions (which side of the escalator) the same as distributive ones (tipping, which moves money to workers); and whether it would extend to local conventions I take to be criticizable? Then tell me which reading the professor would need for her sentence to actually dispose of the visiting student's case, and which reading she most likely meant. Lay it out as a numbered menu I can bring to office hours, with a one-line verdict on each reading.
