I write for a science-and-ideas section and I'm doing a piece on a view I keep bumping into in arguments about suffering. Roughly: pleasure and pain both go on the ledger, but the ledger has two tiers, and once a bad entry in the bottom tier gets past a certain size, no number of good entries in the top tier can ever cancel it out — one ruined afternoon beats any quantity of lovely ones, however many you pile up. In my notes I've been calling it "lexical threshold negative utilitarianism", though I'm honestly not sure I didn't make that name up.

Here's the bit I can't get my head round. Everyone I've talked to describes this view as the one that says pleasure and pain are incommensurable — no common currency, nothing to trade them off against. But hang on. If no amount of pleasure can outweigh that one bad entry, haven't you just compared the two and found the pain heavier? That's a comparison. And a threshold has to be a cut-off in something; you can't have a cut-off in a comparison you're not allowed to make in the first place. So which is it — same scale or no scale? And if there's genuinely no scale, how does the view ever tell you to do one thing rather than another?

I need something I can lift into a paragraph, so no jargon avalanche, and British English please, its going to a UK desk. Also if I've got the whole thing arse-backwards, just say so — I'd rather find out now than in print.
