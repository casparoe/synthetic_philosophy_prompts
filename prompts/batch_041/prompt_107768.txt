I'm on the board of a small local history museum in a town that got rich off textiles in the 1800s, and we're finalizing the labels for a new exhibit on why it happened here. The historian we hired wrote the labels as if the nonconformist Protestant culture of the mill owners caused the whole takeoff. Now two board members are going at it: a retired econ professor says that's just Weber's Protestant ethic thesis warmed over, and that culture is downstream of coal, canals, and credit; our local pastor says if you explain the values away as an effect you've explained away the only thing that made the place different. I've been asked to draft something they can both put their names to, because we can't open with a civil war in the gallery. So: write the joint statement of an adverserial collaboration between a Weberian culture-first historian and a materialist/institutionalist economic historian on whether culture can be a cause, using our town's textile boom as the running case. I don't want a bibliography or citations; I want the document itself. Please use these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. Agreed claims (numbered, about 500 words). Include what both sides accept about the town's boom, about what 'culture' can mean, and about what would count as explaining it. I want you to seperate three claims people always run together: (i) culture caused the takeoff, (ii) culture was part of the mechanism that sustained it once started, and (iii) culture was a marker of who got credit and who got excluded. Also distinguish culture as a variable, culture as a set of mechanisms, and culture as a prior over strategies.

2. What we each mean by 'culture' (about 250 words). The Weberian and the materialist should each get a paragraph in their own voice, and the section should end with a shared operational definition, even if it's thin. The Weberian keeps saying 'elective affinity,' which I think is doing a lot of work, so make them say what they take it to mean.

3. The disagreement, each side in its own words (about 350 words). Phrase each side so its own advocate would sign off on the wording. Make clear whether the fight is about causation, evidence, or values.

4. Cruxes (about 400 words). List the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect one is whether there's a counterfactual test: same coal, canals, and credit, different religious culture, what happens? Another might be whether the materialist explanation is itself value-laden, and whether the Weberian explanation is falsifiable. Add any others you think are real, and say which are probably just verbal.

5. Tests, cases, and predictions (about 400 words). For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, and write down what each side predicts. Keep it to things a model without web access could reason about from the town case, not literature reviews.

6. What would change my mind about this framing (about 150 words). Say which argument or consideration would lead you to redraw the line between what they agree on and what they still disagree about, and where your drafting is most likely to be smuggling in one side's assumptions. If you think the whole culture-as-cause question is a verbal dispute, say so and say what makes it verbal.

Keep it casual, no citations, no fake quotations. I'd rather have a sharp joint statement that the econ prof and the pastor could both sign than a survey of the literature.
