So there's a distinction I keep circling and I honestly can't tell if its load-bearing or just wordplay, and I want you to help me figure out what actually follows from it. Competence without comprehension comes in at least two flavors. Darwin's flavor: a mindless process, variation plus differential reproduction, cranks out creatures that are competent at fitting their world, and there is no understanding anywhere in the system, not even a trace of it. Turing's flavor: a machine shuffles symbols so that its output is what a comprehending agent would have produced, and again there's no understanding inside the machine, but this time the competence looks like it's coming from somewhere, namely from us, the designers, the trainers, whoever set the thing up. So call it borrowed versus unowned competence. In the Turing case the understanding has a lender, the humans. In the Darwin case nobody ever lent it, the competence is just unowned, spread across a population and a lot of time. What I want is the most important implications of taking that borrowed/unowned distinction seriously. And I don't mean the stock stuff about machines not really thinking, I mean what actually follows from it. What does it let us infer, or forbid us from inferring, about a system's inner life from how competent it is? Does it change how we should read Darwin, or how we should read ourselves, i.e. are we Turing-machines-with-borrowed-understanding or Darwinian creatures whose competence is unowned all the way down? And does it have any real teeth for AI safety, like does borrowed competence fail in some characteristic way once the lender is no longer around to top it up? Give me the implications ranked by how much they'd actually change someone's mind, and be honest about the points where the whole distinction collapses back into ordinary talk and stops earning its keep.
