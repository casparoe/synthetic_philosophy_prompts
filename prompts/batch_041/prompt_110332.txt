I sit on the Council of the Ashby Society, a 130-year-old membership society with a journal, an annual meeting, a modest endowment, and fifteen councillors elected by the Fellows to three-year terms. At the annual general meeting on 6 June the Fellows will vote on one amendment to the bylaws, and our own rules require the Council to publish a recommendation in the meeting papers. I am the one drafting it. It is due to the Secretary on 14 May, so what I need is a decision memo of a page or two, not an essay.

The amendment would strike the second half of Article IX, section 3. As it stands the article does two things. First, no Fellow may serve more than two consecutive terms on the Council. Second, a Fellow who has served two consecutive terms may not be elected or appointed to any office or committee of the Society for two years, advisory and honorary posts included. The reformers, of whom I am one, want to keep the term limit and delete the two-year bar. The traditionalists want the article left alone.

The difficulty is that nobody knows why the second half exists. The minute book covering its adoption was lost in a fire in 1947, and the article has no preamble. What we have instead are two unsourced accounts: a 1961 history by a former Secretary, which says the clause was aimed at stopping a standing committee of past officers from running the Society from outside the Council, and a 2011 newsletter piece arguing that the real driver was a fight over the editorship of the journal. The one documented event is a 1934 amendment inserting the words covering advisory and honorary posts; the minute records only that it was adopted without discussion.

Why this is live now rather than academic: our publishing contract, which supplies most of our revenue, comes up for renewal next year, and the two Fellows who handled the last three renewals are both under the two-year bar. The reformers' case is easy to state. The traditionalists' case is that the bar is precisely what prevents a permanent shadow council, and that the clause's costs are visible while its benefits are not, which is the asymmetry that makes bodies like ours bad at judging rules like this. The Council is split roughly four against the clause, five for it, and six who will follow the clearest reasoning; that last group is who the memo is really for.

Please draft it. I need a definite recommendation, keep or delete or amend, with the exact wording if you amend, plus the reasoning for it, the strongest case for the other side stated so that its holders would recognize it as fair, and a concrete course of action with dates the Council can adopt. Do not give me a survey of the literature on Chesterton's fence or a summary of Burke; I know the standard moves, and the six undecided councillors will not read them. What I want is your judgment on the questions this particular case raises. What follows from the fact that we do not know why the clause is there? Does a rule whose rationale is lost deserve the presumption we give a fence, or does that presumption depend on things we can actually check, such as whether the rule was the product of selection or of an accident, or whether anyone is still around who saw it work? How much weight should the 1934 insertion carry as evidence that someone was closing a loophole they had watched being used? What should a body do when one side's evidence is a cost it can measure and the other side's is a risk it cannot? Is there a version of this decision that is genuinely reversible, and is a trial suspension actually reversible once people's expectations have moved? And who should carry the burden of proof here, and on what grounds?

My prior, plainly: I lean toward deleting the clause, and years of reading rationalist blogs have left me inclined to distrust a rule nobody can explain. But I have also watched a committee use the fact that nobody could explain a rule as licence to bulldoze something load-bearing, and if the memo simply ratifies my prior I will be worse off than if I had written nothing. Assume I know the standard debates; tell me what to do with these facts.
