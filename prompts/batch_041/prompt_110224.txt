I am setting the final paper for a final-year undergraduate and taught-Master's module in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. The paper is closed-book and lasts ninety minutes, and candidates are not expected to supply titles, dates or citations — I would rather they argued than footnoted. I need one essay question on heterophenomenology as a method for the science of consciousness, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the method, so let me set out both what the students will already have and what I want the question to do with it.

What they will have:

- Heterophenomenology is the third-person method of treating a subject's utterances as data — as a text to be interpreted — and building from them, together with everything else known about the subject's brain, body and environment, a heterophenomenological world: an inventory of what the subject says is going on in their experience.
- Its central discipline is bracketing. The subject is treated as authoritative about how things seem, and about nothing else. The method is officially neutral as to whether anything in the heterophenomenological world is real, and neutral even as to whether the subject is conscious at all.
- It is offered as the whole of the data for a theory of consciousness: for every phenomenological question about why a subject experiences X, there is a corresponding question about why the subject says they experience X, and the second is supposed to be the scientific one.
- In the temporal-order cases — the colour version of the phi illusion is the standard example — the same behavioural and report data can be explained either by the brain's registering a content and later revising the record of it, or by the brain's holding the content back until a later, already reconciled version is available. (In the literature these are the Orwellian and the Stalinesque readings.) The two readings agree about the neural story and differ only over where experience is supposed to begin, and the method is said to show that there is no fact of the matter between them.

What I want the question to do: make candidates see that this verdict cannot be derived from the method's neutrality, but only from a further and much stronger premise, and then make them decide what that does to the method's claim to be *the* method for the science of consciousness.

So the question should have four parts:

1. Set out the bracketing discipline precisely enough that the difference between being authoritative about how things seem and being authoritative about how things are is explicit, and say what work the bracketing is supposed to do.
2. Exhibit a pair of hypotheses that generate exactly the same heterophenomenological data while disagreeing about consciousness — they may use the temporal-order pair or construct their own — and say exactly what makes the pair indistinguishable by the method's own lights. Then draw the dilemma: either the method rules that there is no fact of the matter, which is a substantive verdict about consciousness and so not a neutral one; or it allows that there is a fact of the matter which its own data cannot reach, which makes it a method for the study of beliefs about consciousness rather than of consciousness. This part should carry the most marks.
3. Say which horn a defender of the method should take, and answer the obvious rejoinder from the other side.
4. Five marks: the interpretation objection. The method treats utterances as data only after reading them as being about experience, and that reading deploys the vocabulary of meaning and aboutness which the method claims to suspend. Is it enough to answer that every science must interpret its data? Or does this show that heterophenomenology is a branch of interpretive social science that has been mis-sold as a science of consciousness?

The question should also require, in one or two sentences, a verdict on whether the method can be applied to a subject who cannot report at all — a pre-linguistic infant, or a patient who makes no reliable response — and what that implies for the claim that the data set leaves out no subjective phenomena.

The deliverables:

(a) The question as it would appear on the paper, in exam register, with the marks for the four parts adding to twenty.
(b) A model answer of about eight hundred words, in British English, with no citations and no reliance on being able to name who said what.
(c) A marking scheme that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific moves a lower-second answer will make instead, so that I can separate them by ticking items off, together with a one-line note for each of the four parts on how candidates typically go wrong.
