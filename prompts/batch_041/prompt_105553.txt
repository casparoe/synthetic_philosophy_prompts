A trade book on markets that I picked up, written by a professional economist for a general audience, opens with a chapter on the socialist calculation debate. It summarizes the episode roughly as follows:

"During the interwar years, economists fought what came to be called the socialist calculation debate. Mises opened it by arguing that socialism is impossible: without private ownership of the means of production there can be no market prices for capital goods, and without such prices the planners have no way of comparing the relative costs of alternative uses of resources, so rational allocation is ruled out from the start. Hayek then added that even if a central board could somehow assemble all the relevant information, it would have to solve millions of simultaneous equations, and by the time it had solved them the data would have changed; so socialist planning might be possible in theory but is hopeless in practice. Lange answered on behalf of the socialists. A Central Planning Board, he said, could set prices by trial and error — raising them when goods were in short supply and lowering them when there were surpluses — and could instruct factory managers to minimize average cost and to produce up to the point where price equals marginal cost, while consumers chose freely what to buy and workers chose freely where to work. Since the board's prices would converge on the very prices a competitive market would produce, the calculation problem is solved, and a socialist economy would be just as efficient as a capitalist one. Lange's model — a form of market socialism, essentially what Yugoslavia later tried — has never really been refuted on its own terms. In the end, then, the debate came down to incentives rather than information: the Lange system works on paper, but planners and managers would have had no reason to follow its rules, which is why the planned economies collapsed at the end of the 1980s and why Mises and Hayek are now generally thought to have won the argument."
