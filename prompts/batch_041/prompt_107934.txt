In the world-history survey I help teach, a senior colleague defends keeping the course centered on Europe with an analogy to maps. Every flat map, he says, must be drawn in some projection. The Mercator, devised so that a navigator's constant compass bearing comes out as a straight line, inflates the higher latitudes until the far north looks far larger than it is; an equal-area projection preserves the proportions of landmasses but ruins their shapes; and no projection does everything well at once. A globe comes closer to the truth, but a globe cannot be folded into a book or laid alongside a page of text. From this he draws a conclusion he restates every term: the demand for a history told from everywhere at once is as confused as the demand for a map with no projection; every history has a center as every map has a projection; the choice of center is a trade-off among purposes rather than a matter of accuracy; and the honest course is to declare your projection and proceed, rather than apologize for a European frame or chase an impossible neutral one. I have never been able to say exactly where this argument fails, and I suspect it succeeds at something small and fails at something large. I would like the audit.

Lay out the correspondence as exactly as the analogy permits: what, in the writing of history, plays the part of the projection, the globe, the scale, the legend, and the survey that tells you how far a given map has departed from the territory; then say where the analogy breaks, separating the failures that bear on his conclusion from those that are merely decorative. Four things I especially want settled. Does the inevitability of a projection show that no map is better than another, or does the analogy slide past the difference between a distortion chosen for a purpose and one simply inherited? Every projection can be converted into any other mathematically, without loss of information, so that the choice is one of convenience and never of content; can historical framings be converted into one another in that way, and if not, what follows for his conclusion? The analogy presupposes a territory existing independently of any map, against which distortions can be measured; what is the analogue of that territory in history, and is there one? And a map is drawn for a single eye at a point, or at infinity, whereas a history is made of many agents acting from many positions; is there in history any one viewpoint to relocate, or is the center of a history something other than a point of view?

Then tell me what his analogy can actually carry. If it supports only a weaker claim than he wants, state that weaker claim precisely. If a different figure — an atlas, a panel of witnesses, a sound mix, whatever you judge best — does the work better, propose it and say what it captures that the map does not. Please write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no tables, and no bold text.
