Ok I need a memo from you and I need it before Thursday, which is when the subcommittee meets.

Why this is suddenly my problem: I'm on the county heritage board, mostly I do grant paperwork, and three weeks ago my aunt mailed me a shoebox of my great-grandfathers letters from the Western Front. I've been reading them at night and got weirdly emotional about it, which is how I ended up chairing the exhibit subcommittee for our 1914 centenary show. That was a mistake.

Here's the fight. The centerpiece panel is supposed to say who was responsible for the war starting. The draft we inherited says Germany and Austria-Hungary bore the primary responsibility. Our sister-city partner in Germany is co-hosting and their director has told us privately that if that line stays, they pull out. Our veterans' council rep says any wording that spreads the blame around is a whitewash and she'll say so at the public meeting. The panel is sixty words of wall text. It has to work for a German high-school group standing in front of it and for the guy from the VFW standing in front of it.

Three options on the table.

(1) Keep the draft. This is roughly the historian Fritz Fischer's position — he argued in 1961 that Germany had deliberately set out for a war of expansion, and the argument caused a scandal among German historians for years afterwards. It's also roughly what the 1919 treaty made Germany accept: responsibility "for causing all the loss and damage ... as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies."

(2) A shared-responsibility panel. It describes the July Crisis as a chain of mobilizations and miscalculations that no single government controlled, and names no principal. Nobody is blamed.

(3) My own proposal, which I don't know if it survives contact with anybody. Don't rank guilt at all; instead split one question into four that are usually run together: (a) who contributed causally to the crisis of July 1914; (b) which specific men made which decisions and what they thought they were doing; (c) who was responsible for how the war was fought and what was demanded as the price of ending it, once it had started; (d) the 1919 legal finding, which is a fact about the peace settlement and not a conclusion about 1914. Under (a) and (b) the panel names names. Under (d) it explains why the clause was written and what it did. And it never uses the word guilt.

What I need is the memo I read out Thursday: a clear recommendation — one of the three, or a modification of one — the exact panel wording I should move, and the reasoning. Two things I want you to actually deal with instead of gesturing at.

First, the descendant problem. The panel is about a nation. A fifteen-year-old from our sister city is going to stand in front of it. Is the panel saying something about her? If not, tell me why not, given that we're attributing something to a nation she's a member of.

Second, the counterfactual problem. Any claim about who caused the war seems to need a claim about what would have happened otherwise, and I don't see how sixty words of wall text makes that claim responsibly. If your recommendation needs a counterfactual, say which one and say how the panel is entitled to assert it.

Also give me the strongest case for whichever option I'm not taking, stated the way its supporters would state it, not as something I can knock over. And no citations and no further-reading list — it's a wall panel, not a seminar. One short paragraph at the end on what historians have actually settled and what they haven't would help me when the veterans' rep comes at me, but keep it to a paragraph and don't send me off to look things up.
