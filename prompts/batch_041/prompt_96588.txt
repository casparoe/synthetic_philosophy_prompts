I'm a third-year philosophy student currently taking a graduate seminar in metaphysics on material objects, and I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my own position before we workshop it. I'd like a consistency check before I commit to it, because I suspect I've assembled these commitments from different sources over the past year without ever testing whether they can actually be held together. Here is where I've landed.

(i) Composite objects are real. Organisms, molecules, and tables exist as objects in their own right; nihilism about composite objects is false.

(ii) Composition is restricted. Universalism is false: the Moon and my left shoe do not compose anything, and neither do most pluralities of things.

(iii) There is a single, correct, non-arbitrary answer to the special composition question — one condition that specifies exactly when some things compose a further thing. That condition is imposed by the world rather than by our interests, our concepts, or the convenience of our language. I don't yet know what the condition is, but I'm confident it exists, and that it is not a gerrymandered list of exceptions.

(iv) There is no vague existence. For any candidate things, it is either determinately true or determinately false that they compose something; existence does not come in degrees.

(v) Composition is identity. When some things compose a whole, the whole is nothing over and above those things — not two entities, but one entity described in two ways.

(vi) Composite objects are grounded in their parts. A table is not fundamental, but it is no less real for that: its existence and its features depend, in the strict metaphysical sense of grounding rather than in a merely causal or explanatory sense, on the particles and on how they are arranged.

(vii) On the familiar cases: a heap of sand is not a single object, an organism is, and there is exactly one table on my desk, even though there are countless overlapping sums of particles that differ from it by a molecule or two.

Please audit this set rather than defend it. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which pairs only appear to conflict? For each genuine tension, tell me how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be that restores consistency, together with what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. I would also like to know which single commitment, if I gave it up, would clean up the most, and why. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list, and say explicitly what it is that makes the merely apparent tensions dissolve. I'm less interested in a survey of the options in the field than in locating where this particular combination comes apart.
