I have no academic training in philosophy or economics; what I know about decision theory I have picked up by reading on my own, and I would like you to predict something rather than summarize a literature I cannot check for myself. Frank Ramsey died in January 1930, aged twenty-six, having written in the mid-1920s a long essay on belief and probability that was printed only after his death, in which he derives a person's degrees of belief and the values of things from that person's own preferences over gambles. Maurice Allais published his two-choice example in 1953, and the axiomatic treatment of expected utility that the example was meant to embarrass came later still, so Ramsey saw none of it. My question is what he would have made of it.

The example, as I understand it, runs like this. In one decision you may take a million for certain, or take a gamble offering an 89 percent chance of a million, a 10 percent chance of five million, and a 1 percent chance of nothing; most people take the million. In the other decision you may take an 11 percent chance of a million, or a 10 percent chance of five million with a 1 percent chance of nothing; most people take the second. The two decisions are the same pair of prospects with an identical 89 percent slice stuck onto both options in each, so the independence axiom says a person must come down on the same side in both, and the majority does not.

What I could not settle by reading further is this. I built a version in which no option is ever certain: in the first decision, a 50 percent chance of three thousand against a 40 percent chance of four thousand; in the second, the same two options with every chance divided by four, so 12.5 percent of three thousand against 10 percent of four thousand. The ratio between the options is the same in both decisions, and neither decision contains a sure thing, yet of the dozen or so people I asked, most took the safer option in the first and the gamble in the second, and none of them, once I explained how the two decisions were related, felt they had made an error. Several said that once the odds get long, the smaller prize stops being worth protecting.

Reading Ramsey, I take him to have treated a person's preferences as data — the theorist reads values and degrees of belief off them rather than marking them — and to have treated his axioms as rules for keeping belief and preference in order rather than as claims about how people actually are. If that is roughly right, what would he have said here: that the people I asked are simply incoherent and must choose again; that their preferences are what they are and the axiom is what has to give; or that his own construction, which builds values out of gambles, already assumes the very thing Allais called into question, so that he could not have been neutral about it even in principle? And what would he have made of the long-odds report, which looks like a change in how the prizes are valued rather than in the probabilities?

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Be as candid as the material permits: mark clearly where you are reconstructing a position Ramsey never stated and where you are on firmer ground, and if the question is the wrong shape to answer well — if asking what a man who died in 1930 would have thought of a 1953 example is not really a question with an answer, or if my reading of the essay is carrying more weight than it can bear — say so plainly instead of producing five tidy items to be agreeable.
