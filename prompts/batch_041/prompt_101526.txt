I'm a backend engineer (distributed systems, mostly) and I've fallen down a rabbit hole arguing with a mate from my old ML lab about whether the internals of a neural net "represent" anything, or whether we're just projecting. I don't have a philosophy background so I keep getting lost in the words rather than the substance. I want the spec first, before I read any more blog posts or papers: write me the desiderata that any decent theory of representation-in-a-network has to meet. Concretely — what cases must it get right, what distinctions can't it collapse, what established results does it have to stay consistent with — and for each one, give me the case or consideration that motivates it. I'm thinking of things like: the same behaviour can be produced by weights related by an arbitrary change of basis (permute the hidden units, rotate a subspace), so the theory can't make representation claims depend on a coordinate system nobody chose; a probe or decoder can always be trained to pull *something* out of an activation, so the theory has to say when decoding is evidence and when it's the readout doing all the work; a latent can be perfectly recoverable by a linear map and yet be causally inert in the forward pass, so recoverability and "the network actually uses this" can't be the same thing; superposition means features share dimensions, so "one feature, one unit" can't be baked in; and there's a nasty case I hit in a toy setup — a two-layer thing trained on a synthetic task where the ground-truth latent and what the probes find differ by a fixed rotation, and every behavioural test passes either way, which makes me think "which one is the real representation" might be a question the theory has to refuse to answer rather than answer. Also: does a network represent something it computes but never outputs? Does a network trained on a spurious shortcut represent the shortcut, the intended feature, or both? And does it represent a feature if no human concept lines up with the code at all?

Two things I really want out of it. First, flag which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just house style of one camp — I suspect a lot of what gets demanded (that a representation be human-interpretable, say, or that it be a single direction) is a prejudice smuggled in as a constraint. Second, tell me if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — that's the bit I'm most curious about, because it feels like "faithful to the mechanism", "independently checkable", and "stable across retraining" can't all hold at once. Then, briefly, how the main families of account do against the list — the probe/decode-it crowd, the sparse-autoencoder-and-superposition crowd, the causal-intervention crowd (patching, ablation, interchange-style tests), and whatever the "it's just behaviour, stop reifying" people would say. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them; the scorecard is just so I can see which criterion does the most work. Write it in British English please, and don't bother with citations or reading lists — I can't get to papers easily and I'd rather have the argument than the references.
