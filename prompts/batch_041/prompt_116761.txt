I'm taking a module on normative ethics and we've just finished the utility monster, and I've come away understanding it less well than I did before the seminar, which is definately either a good sign or a bad one. The standard telling is: here's a being who gets vastly more out of every unit of whatever we're handing round than anyone else does, so a total-utility calculus says to give it everything and leave the rest of us with the scraps, and that's meant to be a decisive counterexample. Fine. But somebody in the room pointed out that you can produce the exact same verdict out of entirely ordinary people -- just take a sufficiently large group whose interests happen to line up, or one person whose future self will be hanging around for an awfully long time, and you get "everyone else gets scraps" without any exotic psychology anywhere. Which makes me suspect the monster is either redundant or misdescribed, and I can't work out which.

What I actually want is the underlying conceptual issue explained, rather than a tour of who said what. So: is the utility monster a genuinely seperate objection to total utilitarianism, or is it just the repugnant conclusion with the whole population bundled into one head, so that it feels like a point about a freak when it's really a point about aggregation as such? And if it is separate, which bit of it is doing the work? I've noticed that several things the standard telling runs together actually come apart: the being's capacity for utility versus its marginal utility per unit of resource (an enormous capacity with no further benefit from more stuff isn't a monster at all), and its being a single subject versus its simply being where the numbers happen to be largest. There's also a version I keep coming back to where what the being enjoys is precisely being the sort of thing the calculus privileges, so its utility rises the more weight you give it -- I don't know whether that sharpens the case or just shows the thought experiment can be gerrymandered into anything.

So I'd like you to take the intuition apart, tell me which components the argument actually needs, and then say whether once you've separated them the monster still has any force against the theory that plain aggregation doesn't already have. And if it turns out they're the same problem, I'd like to know why the monster version feels different -- whether we're simply more easily disgusted by one greedy subject than by a crowd, or whether the intuition quietly depends on a picture in which utility has to be contained in somebody, so that one big container looks monstrous in a way a thousand small ones somehow doesn't.
