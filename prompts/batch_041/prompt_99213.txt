I am assembling a set of exercises for a graduate seminar on material constitution, and I want the centrepiece to be an adversarial collaboration rather than the usual exchange of replies and counter-replies. The two parties are a constitutionalist, who holds that the bronze horse on the pedestal and the bronze it is made of are two distinct material objects — related by constitution rather than identity, with different persistence conditions and different histories — and an identity theorist, who holds that there is exactly one object there and that every appearance of two is either a confusion or a loose way of talking. Neither party should be a strawman: each must be able to read the finished document and feel that its own view has been stated more carefully by its opponent than it usually is. A friend of mine who works in conservation tells me that museums routinely talk about their objects in ways that seem to presuppose one side or the other, and I would like the document to take a concrete case seriously enough that those presuppositions come to the surface rather than staying in the abstract.

The shared test bed should be the following case, which both parties have been circling for a term. A bronze horse, Rider, was cast in 1873 from a bronze cannon that had been decommissioned and melted down. In 1958 a corroded rear leg was cut out and replaced with bronze from elsewhere; the rest of the metal was untouched. In 2021 the curators proposed melting the whole object down and recasting it in the original mould, which survives, topping it up with additional bronze. Museum records refer both to Rider and to "the bronze", and the latter is treated as an object in some contexts — insured, weighed, sampled for alloy analysis, and once formally demanded back by the descendants of the people who had owned the cannon — and as a mere quantity of stuff in others. Please do not spend space attributing positions to named authors; I want the positions themselves, formulated in your own words, and I have no use for citations or a reading list.

Draft the joint statement such that it contains, in order: (1) a numbered section of claims both parties can sign, covering the case, the nature of the question at issue, the logic of identity and counting, and the moves each side agrees to give up — these must be strong enough that they could not have been signed by just anyone; (2) a disagreements table in which, for each sub-question, each party's answer is given in a formulation that party would accept as fair to its own view, including at minimum how many objects stand on the pedestal, whether the 1958 repair altered the statue, the bronze, both or neither, whether the 2021 proposal would destroy Rider while leaving the bronze intact, whether the descendants could be given "the bronze" while the museum kept Rider, and whether a claim like "Rider is the bronze that was once a cannon" is strictly true or only loosely so; (3) for each genuine crux, the case, argument or pattern of practice that both parties agree bears on it, with each party's prediction about that evidence written down in advance; (4) an explicit verdict on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and which are substantive, including whether reframing the whole matter in terms of portions of matter rather than countable lumps dissolves anything or merely relocates the disagreement; and (5) a closing statement of what each party would count as a reason to abandon its position, and whether either regards the other's central argument as anything more than a burden-shifting move. Where the two sides would simply talk past each other, say so plainly; where no shared formulation is available, explain why the disagreement resists it.
