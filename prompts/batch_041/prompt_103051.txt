I teach a graduate seminar on causation and responsibility, and a student recently pressed me with an argument we reconstructed together and which I have not been able to answer to my satisfaction. She is a deflationist about causal selection — she thinks explanatory interests and norms of expectation have no business in the metaphysics of what causes what — and she offered the following, which I have tidied but, I believe, not weakened.

(1) When we say the gardener caused the plant to die by failing to water it, we are treating an omission as a cause. (2) An omission is not an event; it is the absence of an event. (3) Whatever else a cause may be, it must at least be a difference-maker: had the candidate not obtained, the effect would not have occurred. (4) The gardener's failure to water is a difference-maker for the plant's death — had she watered, the plant would have lived. (5) So the gardener's failure to water caused the plant's death. (6) But the same test is passed by the neighbor who did not water the plant, by the owner who did not move it into the shade, by the weather that did not rain, and indeed by every one of the indefinitely many things that any agent or object failed to do which would have saved the plant. (7) Each of those absences is a difference-maker for the death, so each is a cause of it. (8) Hence the plant's death has indefinitely many causes, and no one of them is more a cause than the gardener's omission. (9) Generalizing, each of us is a cause of every famine, fire, and drowning we did not prevent, and the word cause stops marking anything at all. (10) A theory of causation that entails (8) and (9) is not a theory of causation but a reductio of one. (11) Therefore (1) is false: omissions are not causes, and the gardener did not cause the plant's death.

I would rather keep (1) than (9), if I am forced to choose, but I want to know what I am paying. One colleague suggested that the gardener's omission is a cause because it departs from a norm while the neighbor's does not, and the student replied that this simply smuggles our explanatory interests back into the metaphysics — which is the very thing she denies — and I could not tell her why she was wrong. Tell me which step to give up, explain why that step looked so plausible in the first place, and then price the alternatives honestly: if I reject the step you name, what must I supply in its place, and does supplying it cost me (1) as well? I would like a committed answer — a definite verdict on the step and a defense of it — rather than a survey of positions that leaves the choice to me, and I would be grateful if you would say plainly where your own account is weakest. Please write in American English.
