I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a long essay about the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I've argued myself into a position I'd rather have tested than confirmed. The position is this: the line between treating a disease or disability and enhancing an already healthy person is not merely hard to draw at the margins — it does no independent moral work anywhere. Wherever it appears to matter, the moral weight is being carried by something else: harm to third parties, consent and autonomy, distributive fairness, the authenticity of a person's relation to her own traits, or the social meaning of classifying some bodies as defective. The distinction survives, on this view, for two unphilosophical reasons — it is institutionally convenient, sorting insurance claims and regulatory approvals, and it borrows the prestige of a medicine that still pictures itself as restorative, even though medicine enhances all the way down, since vaccination, anesthesia, and prenatal care restore no prior state and are continuous with the cosmetic and performance interventions we are uneasy about. What I want from you is not a defense of the distinction but a map of what would count against the collapse thesis.

So: which cases, arguments, and results should move me, and which are merely awkward? I'd like the defeaters ranked, with the ones that would be decisive separated from those that would only force a qualification, and with a clear indication of which of them a committed defender of the distinction would themselves concede as counting against my view, as opposed to what only critics assert. Please also take seriously what I take to be the two strongest ways of grounding the distinction and tell me which of them my thesis has the harder time absorbing. The first ties it to a statistical, species-typical baseline: a disease is an abnormal reduction in normal functional capacity, so restoring that capacity is treatment and exceeding it is enhancement. The second ties it to a counterfactual baseline: an intervention is treatment if it moves a person closer to the state she would have occupied without the pathology, and enhancement if it carries her beyond it. I am especially interested in the cases where these two baselines come apart — a therapy that overshoots, restoring function past the person's own pre-illness level, or an enhancement that merely closes a gap the person was born with — and in whether either baseline can survive them. Finally, be honest about whether my position has been stated so broadly that nothing could count against it; and if it has, say whether that is a defect in the thesis or evidence that the distinction was doing no work to begin with.
