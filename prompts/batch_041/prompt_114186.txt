I'm convening a ninety-minute session of a cross-departmental reading group on the question of which computations, if any, can matter morally, and what I need from you is a teaching plan rather than an exposition of the problem. The audience is six people, mostly not philosophers: two machine-learning engineers, a systems neuroscientist, and three graduate students from other departments. They arrive sharing two assumptions I have to dismantle on the way in, that behaving like a person is evidence of feeling like one and that moral status scales with the amount or complexity of computation, and none of them has read any philosophy of mind.

Build the session around a case I have invented for it, so that the discussion cannot be settled by reciting the famous thought experiments. The case: a lab trains a system, then ships a behavioural twin that reproduces the original's outputs over the whole distribution anyone will ever query, by table lookup with interpolation; a second version of the twin compresses that table into a small circuit whose internal algorithm is nothing like the original's. Both twins are, by stipulation, behaviourally indistinguishable from the original on every input that will ever occur. My questions for the session are whether anything morally relevant survives each swap, and whether the second swap, same behaviour but a different algorithm, should trouble us more or less than the first, same behaviour but a different implementation.

Give me the sequence: which ideas must be in place before which, and why the dependencies run as they do. Tell me whether this case can carry the whole session or whether it needs a simpler one underneath it, and what a carrying case of this kind must have and must lack. I had intended to use the Chinese room as the anchor and now suspect it teaches the wrong lesson here, since it concerns understanding words rather than which of many computations a physical system is running; tell me whether I am right to demote it, and what it fails to supply. Then map the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface, I expect the behavioural-equivalence fallacy first, then the thought that implementation is simply read off the physics, then the appeal to simulation versus instantiation as a conversation-stopper, and say at exactly which point in the sequence I should head each one off. I am also unsure whether to keep the factual question, whether there is something it is like to be the system, apart from the practical one, what we may do under our uncertainty about it, or whether that split is itself one of the ideas that has to be taught; settle that too. Include one closing question or task that would show me whether they have grasped the structure of the problem rather than acquired its vocabulary, and tell me what to leave out at this level and to announce that I am leaving it out.
