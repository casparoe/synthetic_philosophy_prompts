For three years I've been moderating panels and filing pieces on AI governance and bio-risk, and I keep watching the same argument about the unilateralist's curse terminate in the same stalemate. The curse, as it's usually put: when many parties could each act unilaterally on a question, and each is more likely than not to have judged correctly, the probability that whoever acts first has it right can fall well below a coin flip — so the prudent course is restraint and prior coordination, acting only when you'd expect others to concur. One camp treats this as the master argument for moratoria and for a general norm of conformity in science and technology. The other camp thinks it proves far too much — that by its own logic no lone dissenter, whistleblower, or early adopter should ever have moved — and counters that inaction is itself a choice, with its own expected cost.

Both camps have been at this long enough that I no longer think they are disagreeing about the arithmetic; each accepts the model. My hypothesis is that they are answering different questions under one name — one asking what the probability is that an actor has it right, the other asking what one may do when others bear the risk — and that the real residue is a dispute about the reference class of cases and about whether doing nothing is being smuggled in as a neutral default. I've also wondered whether the argument has a mirror image: where actions are cheap, reversible, and independently informative, many unilateral attempts start to look less like a curse than like a portfolio.

Diagnose this for me. Tell me what kind of disagreement it actually is — verbal, a disagreement about the domain of application, a hidden formal assumption doing the work (independence, a common prior, whose probabilities we are even using), or bedrock moral starting points neither side can argue for — and what, given that diagnosis, would count as progress. If nothing should be expected, say so and say why.
