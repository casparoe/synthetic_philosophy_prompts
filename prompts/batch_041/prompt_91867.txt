My town lies just outside the city line, and last month the city council approved a congestion charge that will shunt most of its traffic through our streets; the people who will bear that cost had no vote on it, since we are not city residents. When I raised this at the meeting, a council aide waved it off with a principle he took to be self-evident: 'everyone affected by a decision should have a say in it.' I have been turning that sentence over ever since, because it looks at once obviously true and capable of proving far too much — affectedness comes in degrees, and the neighbors on the far side of the city get only the cleaner air while we get the trucks. Lay out for me the distinct claims that sentence could be making, from the trivial to the radical: what 'affected' would have to mean (actually affected? foreseeably? in one's fundamental interests? merely in one's preferences?), what kind of 'say' is being demanded (a vote? a hearing? a veto?), and where any line would have to fall once degrees of affectedness are admitted. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or false, and whether it would in fact get my neighbors and me a vote on the charge. Then tell me which reading the aide must have had in mind for his remark to do the work he wanted it to do, and whether that reading can be applied at all without our already knowing who the affected are.
