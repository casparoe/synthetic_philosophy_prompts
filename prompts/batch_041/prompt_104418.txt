In many liberal democracies a single jurisdiction will display all of the following at once. Its legislature opens each session with a prayer, and its members freely cite scripture and divine command when arguing for bills. Yet when those bills are challenged, the courts require that the law serve a secular purpose, and they treat a statute defended only by religious reasons as presumptively illegitimate. Separately, the same legal order is markedly more willing to exempt religious objectors from generally applicable laws — conscription, vaccination, anti-discrimination rules, regulations on animal slaughter — than to exempt people whose objection is secular but just as deep: the pacifist, the ethical vegan, the person whose philosophical outlook forbids the practice. And where a religious exemption is granted, the courts ask whether the belief is sincerely held, not whether it is true or even reasonable, while the secular objector is assessed instead on the merits of his reasons.

Assume we want to know what conception of public reason, of state neutrality, and of the proper place of religion in politics this whole pattern presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does it treat religious reasons as specially suspect, specially protected, or as a separate category that neither suspicion nor protection really tracks? Does it locate the demand of public justification in the inputs to deliberation — in what may be said — or in the outputs — in what may legitimately be enforced — and are the secular-purpose requirement and the exemption asymmetry consistent on that question, or do they pull in opposite directions? Notice that the sincerity test treats a religious claim as a report about the claimant's inner state, to be taken at face value, while the secular objector's claim is treated as an argument to be weighed on its merits; say whether the pattern presupposes that asymmetry or merely happens to fit it.

Then test the practice against a rival picture. Call a commitment deep if it is central to a person's identity and not held lightly, and consider the symmetry thesis: that any deep commitment deserves the same accommodation whatever its metaphysical basis, so that religion is simply one member of a larger class and enjoys no special status in the theory of exemptions. If the symmetry thesis is true, what in the practice must be abandoned, and what could survive? If it is false, what principled reason distinguishes religion from the secular convictions that most closely resemble it? Along the way, separate what the pattern strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and mark the points at which it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories of public reason would each license everything we observe.
