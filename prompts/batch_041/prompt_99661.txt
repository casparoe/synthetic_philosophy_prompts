I'm a third-year PhD student in epistemology, and I've been asked to write an agenda-setting piece for a departmental workshop: not a survey of pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, but a defended answer to the question of where the next several years of work in that area should go. Imagine you're advising a small group — one postdoc and one doctoral student, roughly three researcher-years in total, plus the scraps of time I have left after my dissertation — and that the group works on conceptual questions in the theory of knowledge but does not run experiments. Which open questions should they take up first, and why? I care much less about the list than about the ranking and the reasoning behind it: for each candidate, whether it is upstream of other work in the area or downstream of it, how tractable it is for people whose toolkit is mainly analytic epistemology, whether it is already crowded or oddly neglected, and what would count as evidence over the next two or three years that the question is or is not worth the effort. Where you think a dispute that looks substantive is really terminological, or that the parties are talking past each other because they mean different things by the same term, say so, and say what would settle it.

I would also like you to take seriously two framings that I think receive less attention than they deserve, and to tell me whether each is fruitful or a red herring. The first is that the disagreement between contextualist accounts of knowledge and encroachment-style accounts may not be a disagreement about the data at all, but about which mechanism — semantics, psychology, or the metaphysics of the epistemic status itself — is doing the explanatory work; if that is right, the interesting open question is not which view is correct but what would distinguish the mechanisms conceptually or empirically. The second is whether the pragmatics of stakes can be pushed beyond knowledge to neighbouring statuses and to non-individual cases: to understanding, to justified credence, to the epistemic standing of groups and institutions, and to the way a research team's or a court's practical situation changes what it is entitled to claim it knows. I do not want a reading list or citations, and I do not want the piece to turn on who said what; I want the arguments for the ordering, the criteria you used and the trade-offs among them, and an explicit statement of which questions you would deprioritize even though they are popular, and why.

Finally, tell me what would move a question up or down the ranking — a new counterexample, a result in formal epistemology, a shift in how some neighbouring field uses the concept — and which one or two questions you would bet the group's three years on if forced to choose. Please also flag any question you take to be genuinely unanswerable with the tools available, rather than tractable but hard, and say briefly what a future version of the field would have to look like for that verdict to change.
