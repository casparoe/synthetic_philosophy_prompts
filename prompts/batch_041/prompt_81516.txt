A friend of mine runs a reading group on optimization and institutions, and we've been having the same arguemnt for about a year, so I want to try the adversarial collaboration thing for once — the exercise where two camps write one document they could both actually sign, instead of another round of talking past each other. The two camps are these. On one side, the people I'll call the optimization camp: they think Goodhart's law is a real and deep fact about optimizers, that if you point a strong enough optimizer at a proxy then the proxy comes apart from the thing you actually cared about, and that it comes apart because of the strength of the optimization, not because of sloppy measurement or badly behaved people. On the other side, the measurement camp: they think what's really going on is regression to the mean, selection on a noisy proxy, and people responding to incentives — a statistical artifact plus ordinary gaming, with nothing special about optimizers in it. They'd say the law is either trivial or a restatement of what program evaluators and statisticians have known for decades, like the old saw that any social indicator you start using to make decisions begins corrupting itself. Neither side is dumb and neither is obviously right, which is why it's dragged on.

Here's what I'd like the document to do. First, what both sides can actually sign about the phenomenon itself rather than about each other — including cases they'd both agree are instances, cases they'd both agree aren't, and anything they'd agree is genuinely still open. Second, the disagreements, each one written in wording its own side would accept as fair, because last time we tried this everything came out slanted and we gave up. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give a case, argument, or bit of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, and then what each side predicts about it, written down before anyone goes and looks. Fourth, if you decide that part of this fight is verbal rather than substantive — and I half suspect that talk of a sufficiently strong optimizer is doing something slippery, since it could mean strong enough to break any imperfect proxy, which is a definition, or it could mean the kind of optimizer we can actually build, which is a claim about the world — then say which part is verbal and what's left once you strip it out.

To give you something concrete to chew on, there are four kinds of case we keep splitting hairs over. A noisy proxy, where you pick the candidate with the best measured score and it turns out the measurement noise is partly what you picked for. A proxy that's accurate where you currently are but that a strong enough search just walks right out of. A proxy and the thing you want both driven by a common factor, so pushing the proxy moves the real thing through the back door. And the case where the agents know the metric and lie to it. We can't agree on which of these is the real Goodhart and which are separate phenomena, so I want that on the table explicitly rather than assumed.

Then three test cases I'd like both sides to commit on in advance. One: a fixed, non-adaptive optimizer — no adversary, nobody choosing to respond, just a brute search over programs or policies — with a proxy that's an unbiased estimate of the target. Does the proxy-target gap grow as search effort grows? Say what each side predicts, and say what the other side would have to count as a surprise. Two: an institution ranked on an indicator it has no way to influence, computed by an outsider from data it never touches — does Goodhart still bite, and if so, where did the optimization go? Three: a case where the proxy gets re-derived from the target every time the target is measured, so the proxy is never stale — one side says that's the fix, the other says it just relocates the failure. I want to know whether both sides agree that these three, and not some other three, are the ones that would actually decide something, and whether there's anything at all that both would agree would move the debate.

One more thing. I'm not asking who's right, I'm asking for the document, so stay honest about what's unsettled, don't quietly resolve the cruxes in one direction, and don't let one side's vocabulary colonize the other's claims — the measurement camp shouldn't be forced to call anything an optimizer if they don't want to, and the optimization camp shouldn't have to concede that it's all just selection. Also: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists. I know that's a pain for this particular job, but write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email, and where you'd want a table just write the rows out as sentences. And don't pad it with the history of the idea or who said what when — neither of us can look anything up, and I don't care.
