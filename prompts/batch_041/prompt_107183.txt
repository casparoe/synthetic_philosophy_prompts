Retired geologist here — forty years of mapping rock, and since I hung up my boots I've been reading philosophy on my own, mostly history of ideas and ethics, so forgive me if I don't have all the vocabulary yet.

Here's what I want help with. At my niece's wedding a few months back I got cornered by her father-in-law, a retired schoolteacher and one of those guys who has a one-liner for everything. I'd said something like — look, I don't see any reason to think the world gets more just over time, seems to me it just churns — and he cut me off with his usual line, that the arc of history bends toward justice. Then he used that to basically declare the conversation over: whatever injustice I was worried about, the arc would take care of it, so my worrying was a failure of faith. I've been chewing on it since and I honestly can't tell if he said something profound or something empty.

What I'd like is a menu, not a lecture. Lay out the different precise claims that one sentence could actually be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each one tell me: is it a tautology, is it a testable claim about the world, is it unfalsifiable, is it just false, and would it actually support what he used it for — i.e. does that reading really license "stop worrying, it'll work out."

Things I keep bumping into that I'd like seperated out:

- Whether he means history has a *trend* (an empirical generalization about moral progress) versus a *guarantee* (some kind of necessity). Those seem wildly different to me and people slide between them in the same breath.
- The possibility that it's circular — that we only call whatever wins "justice" after the fact, in which case the arc bends toward justice the way a river runs to the sea, by definition, and gives me no comfort at all.
- Whether there's supposed to be an agent doing the bending — God, Providence, Reason, some invisible hand — or whether it's just a pattern with nobody home. As a geologist I'm allergic to "direction" in natural processes (erosion doesn't head anywhere in particular), so I want to know if history is supposed to be different in kind or just longer.
- The reading where *we're* the arc — it bends only because enough people believe it bends and then act accordingly. If that's the real claim, then the slogan is a self-fulfilling prophecy and his "don't worry" is exactly backwards, no?
- Whatever reading makes the slogan compatible with all the centuries where things clearly went the other way. Is the "arc" just long enough to be unfalsifiable?

Also: when he says justice, does the slogan need a particular theory of what justice is, or does it work with any of them? And is the arc supposed to be in the outcomes people actually live through, in our moral opinions, or in the institutions we build? Because I suspect he'd give different answers depending on which way the wind was blowing.

Bottom line I want: which of these readings is (a) trivially true and useless, (b) substantive and probably true, (c) substantive and probably false, (d) not even a claim about the world; which one he was most likely reaching for; and whether that one definately gets him his "so quit worrying." And if he's sliding between two of them to win the exchange, call that out.
