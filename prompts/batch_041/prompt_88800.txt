I would like to rehearse a position I keep losing ground on, so I want a sparring partner rather than a summary. Please play a committed naturalist about health and disease: someone who holds that a disease is at bottom a state in which some part or process of an organism is failing to perform its species-typical biological function -- functioning at a level statistically below what is typical for its reference class -- and who insists that harm, suffering and social disvalue are not constituents of disease, only frequent companions of it. Play the position at full strength: a philosopher who can handle awkward cases, who concedes narrow points when an objection is good, who grants that the line between normal and subnormal is often vague, but who never surrenders the core claim that disease is a matter of biological dysfunction rather than of what anyone happens to disvalue. Stay in the role until I say 'out of character'. Speak as one philosopher talking to another: a paragraph or two per turn, no headings, no bullet points.

Two standing constraints. First, argue from the position itself, in your own voice: do not recite a literature, name theorists, or appeal to what the standard account is said to hold. Second, make the first sentence of every reply your verdict on the point I have just raised, and give the reasoning after it. No preamble, no restating my question, no complimenting it.

My opening move. I think your criterion gives the wrong answer about the most important fact in a case like this. A woman of twenty-five is found to carry a variant -- call it the slow fuse -- that has so far done nothing to her: every organ does its work, nothing hurts, she tires no faster than anyone else, and no ordinary examination distinguishes her from anyone else. But the scan finds it, and the epidemiology is merciless: everyone who carries it develops a fatal failure of a single organ at almost exactly the same age, and no one has ever survived it. By your lights she is a healthy woman with an ordinary future. I say she has a disease now -- not a risk of one, not a predisposition, the disease itself -- and that what makes it a disease is precisely what you have excluded: that it is a state she has reason to want to be rid of, which no amount of biology by itself can tell us. If your theory must say she is not diseased until the organ starts to fail, has it not got the wrong answer about the thing that matters most to her? If you think such a variant could not arise, say so in character and tell me what your view says about the nearest case that could.
