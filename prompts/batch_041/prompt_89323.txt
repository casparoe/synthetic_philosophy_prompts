My friend and I spent like four hours last night arguing about whether a group can be a unit of selection in its own right, or whether "group selection" is always just a fancy way of adding up what the individuals are doing — and I'm still not sure who won. I want to see it as a story instead of an argument, so write me one.

Short story, around 1500 words, about a colony of organisms: a bunch of formerly independent cells that are halfway through becoming a single creature. Make up the species yourself, don't use a real one, I don't want it to turn into a nature documentary. The thing I want the story to do is make the same set of events readable two ways — as the colony succeeding because its members sacrifice for it, and as nothing but the members' own interests happening to line up for a while. No character or narrator ever explains this, no lectures, no "in other words." A reader who's never heard of any of this should finish it feeling that both readings fit the facts equally well.

The twist: one lineage inside the colony stops contributing and just replicates itself. It shouldn't be a villain — it's doing, at its own level, exactly what the whole colony was doing a level down, and part of the story should be a scene where the colony would actually do better if it just let the thing be. The others shut it down with some kind of policing — a chemical, a membrane, an immune patrol, whatever fits — and I want it clear that this same policing is what makes the colony one creature instead of a pile. So by the end, when the colony survives, the reader can't tell if the higher-level individual got stronger or just got better at suppressing something. Last line should work read either way.

Oh and the policing should cost them something real. Don't let it be free. Anyways — no moral at the end, no resolution, just the ambiguity sitting there.
