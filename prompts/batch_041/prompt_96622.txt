I am a second-year PhD student in political theory, working on representation and the design of democratic institutions, and I have lately been reading Simone Weil on political parties alongside the standard literature on party government. Weil's claim, as I read her, is not the familiar complaint that parties are corrupt, oligarchic, or polarising. It is stronger and stranger: a party is a device for manufacturing collective passion, an organisation whose design puts collective pressure on the thinking of each of its members; its first and, in the end, only goal is its own unlimited growth; and because belonging to a party compels one to lie — to subordinate what one takes to be true to what serves the side — the existence of parties, not merely their abuse, is an evil, so that the remedy is suppression rather than reform. What she puts in their place is not the disappearance of political opinion but a different vehicle for it: not organisations one belongs to, but reviews and journals that one writes for or merely reads, adherence giving way to proximity, with candidates standing on their own and on no organisation's behalf. I am not asking for a bibliography or an exegesis of the secondary literature; I have read what I need to. What I want is to test her diagnosis against the machinery we actually operate.

Consider how thoroughly party government is built into the formal apparatus of a modern democracy. In Germany the constitution requires parties to organise themselves internally on democratic lines and permits a court to dissolve a party that works against the free democratic order, and public funding is conditioned on internal democracy and financial transparency; in India a legislator who votes against the party's direction, or leaves the party, forfeits the seat; in many systems the ballot carries a party label and in closed-list systems offers no candidate choice at all; the whip, thresholds for ballot access, and disclosure regimes for party finance all treat parties as quasi-public utilities with duties owed to the state. Yet the same systems insist, as a matter of doctrine, that parties are private voluntary associations whose members may join, dissent, and leave freely, and that sovereignty resides in individual citizens rather than in the organisations that give those citizens a voice. I find this combination puzzling: the law regulates the party as though it were an organ of the state and treats the seat as though it were the party's property, while officially denying that the party is anything more than a club.

What I would like is a reconstruction of what this whole apparatus presupposes about political judgment, representation, and truth in politics — what view of how citizens' opinions are formed and then aggregated, and of what has to be the unit of aggregation, it only makes sense under; which rival views of representation, whether the individual mandate, the trustee, or the party mandate, it quietly rules out; and whether its parts are mutually consistent, in particular whether the party-as-private-association strand and the party-as-property strand can really coexist or whether one of them is doing all the work while the other is decoration. Distinguish carefully what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of political judgment would license the same machinery. Finally, take Weil seriously as a critic rather than a curiosity: if she is right, what exactly would have to change in ballots, funding, candidate selection, the whip, and the legal status of the seat, and is any part of the present arrangement already compatible with her view — or does the practice already concede her point, for instance in insisting that parties be internally democratic, and if so, why does the concession stop where it does?
