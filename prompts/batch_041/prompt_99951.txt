I teach a graduate seminar on the nature of scientific discovery, and there is a question I have never been able to settle to my own satisfaction. We all know the standard exhibits — the calculus, natural selection, oxygen, the telephone — but their familiarity has, I think, made us lazy about what they actually demonstrate. I should like your honest credence in a fairly precise generalisation, with the reasoning laid out term by term rather than delivered as a verdict.

Consider the following claim, which I have been calling the counterfactual robustness thesis: for any scientific discovery that was in fact arrived at independently by two or more parties within a short interval, the probability that it would still have been made within a decade, even had the earliest of those parties never existed, exceeds one half. My own credence sits at about 0.35. A colleague in the sociology of science puts it near 0.85, and what I really want to know is where the difference between us lives.

Give me your own number, and decompose it: the sub-questions the figure depends on, a rough probability for each, how they combine into the number you report, and then which single term your answer is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most. At a minimum I would expect you to reckon with the following. First, whether a discovery can be individuated sharply enough for the thesis to have a determinate truth value at all, given that what counts as the discovery — the proposition, the method, the artefact, the published claim — quietly shifts the reference class. Second, whether observed simultaneity is genuinely diagnostic of robustness or is in part a selection artefact, since we notice the multiples and overlook the near-misses and the singletons, which may have been just as robust. Third, whether the counterfactual itself is well formed, or whether supposing that the earliest discoverer never existed fails to pick out any determinate nearest world. Fourth, how much the answer varies by kind of discovery, and in particular whether it helps to run what I have been calling the deletion test: strike out the earliest contribution and ask how long the result would have taken to reappear, and by what route. A wrinkle I keep worrying at is the case of two groups who reach the same result independently but where one derivation contains a compensating error — whether that counts as genuine multiple discovery, and what it does to the reference class if it does not.

If you judge the thesis malformed and think it should be replaced rather than answered, say so, but attach a number to the replacement claim, and tell me which of the two of us, the sceptic at 0.35 or the enthusiast at 0.85, has more probably made the decisive mistake, and where. Finally, one step downstream: if the robustness thesis turned out true, would that undermine the point of attributing credit to individuals at all, or are those two questions independent?

Please write this as continuous prose. No headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicised labels, no tables; let the reasoning flow as it would in a letter to a colleague, with the several terms marked in the sentences themselves rather than set apart.
