ok so in my metaphysics of time seminar we have to peer-review each other's answers before the prof grades them, and i got assigned this one and the feedback is due tomorrow. the question was:

"State the sea-battle argument from De Interpretatione 9, give Aristotle's response to it, and assess whether that response works."

and here's the answer i have to grade:

1. The argument. Tomorrow either there will be a sea battle or there won't be. Suppose it's already true that there will be. Then it can't fail to happen, since what's true can't be made untrue, so it happens of necessity. Same if it's already true that there won't be. So one of the two outcomes is already necessary before anything happens, which means it's fixed, and my deliberating about whether to go makes no difference. Aristotle thinks this would destroy deliberation and action, so it has to be answered.

2. His response. He says the law of excluded middle doesn't hold for future contingent statements. "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" isn't true yet and "there won't be" isn't true yet, not because we don't know but because neither is determinately true until the event happens or doesn't. So even the bare disjunction "either there will be or there won't be" isn't true yet, and the necessity the argument needs never gets going. He's basically the first three-valued logician, and Lukasiewicz's system is just a formalisation of what Aristotle was already doing.

3. Assessment. The reply saves deliberation but at a cost. If future contingents have no truth value, plenty of ordinary predictions lose theirs too, which is awkward. There's also the rival Ockhamist line: "there will be a battle" can be true now, because truth is just correspondence with how things turn out, so you keep bivalence and reject the step from truth to necessity instead. I think that's cleaner, though Aristotle's move is the more influential one.
