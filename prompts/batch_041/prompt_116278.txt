Here's a pattern I keep running into in arguments about persuasion, and I don't have a word for it. Someone is accused of manipulating an audience. The defense is: I said nothing false, I threatened no one, I laid out the relevant facts and let people make up their own minds. The accusation is: yes, but you chose which facts, and in what order, so that the intended conclusion was the only one anyone would reach — and the same facts, differently arranged, would have led elsewhere. Both seem right, and the argument never resolves, because the defense is about the content of what was said while the accusation is about the design of the process that carried it. Instances: a salesperson who mentions only true specifications, sequenced so the customer never thinks to ask about the warranty; a campaign ad built entirely from accurate numbers, selected so the trend reads backwards. In both, nothing asserted is false, yet calling it honest feels wrong.

Is there an established concept for influencing someone by engineering the process through which they form a belief, while keeping the content you hand them accurate? The usual words — lying, deception, nudging, coercion — each seem to miss, because they target either the truth of the message or the pressure on the person's will, whereas this is about the shape of the path between them. If nothing fits, coin a term and define it: what it covers and leaves out, and how it differs from lying, from a misleading claim that is technically true, from a nudge, and from ordinary emphasis. I care more about where the boundary falls than about what it is called.
