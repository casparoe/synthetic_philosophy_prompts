I teach a seminar on agency and mechanism, and every year the session I most enjoy is the one on sphexishness. The idea, in brief: the digger wasp Sphex lays its eggs in a burrow, and its procedure runs something like this — drag a paralyzed cricket to the burrow's mouth, leave it there, descend into the burrow to inspect it, climb back out, and only then haul the cricket down. If you nudge the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is underground, the wasp emerges, finds the cricket displaced, drags it back to the mouth, and descends to inspect the burrow all over again; move it again and the loop repeats, sometimes for hours. What fascinates me is that the wasp is not simply stuck: it responds to a change in its environment, corrects course, adapts its behavior to the new position of the prey. Its flexibility, though, is itself a fixed groove, and the animal will circle that groove indefinitely rather than deviate once. The philosophical payoff is that we can recognize this pattern in creatures we would otherwise call intelligent, and perhaps in ourselves — a person can be endlessly resourceful, inventive, even improvisational, in the service of a routine that never once gets questioned, so that it becomes unclear whether the suppleness of the means is evidence of freedom in the agent or just a more elaborate kind of groove.

What I'd like from you is a short story that makes that distinction dramatically vivid rather than explaining it. Set it wherever you think the tension lives best — a field station where a graduate student is documenting an animal's behavior; a household in which a widower's days are elaborately, beautifully arranged; a software team whose product keeps solving the same class of problem in cleverly different ways; a courtroom; a monastery. Give me characters with real interiority, so that the reader is tempted to read deliberation into what may only be a groove. The one structural constraint I care about is this: by the end, the reader should not be able to settle the question. Do not tip your hand by having a narrator announce that the protagonist is or isn't free, and please do not resolve it with a tidy revelation. Build in at least one repeated episode that recurs with small, meaningful variations, so that a careful reader can feel both the resourcefulness and the closure of the loop at once. You may name the wasp or the concept if it serves the story, but you need not; what matters is that a reader who finishes it can articulate the difference between responding flexibly to a situation and responding flexibly within a track one cannot leave — and cannot decide, for this particular character, which one has just happened.
