I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am assembling a special issue on death: reported pieces on cryonics, on palliative care, and on the actuarial trade, plus one slot for fiction. I would like you to write that story, at roughly 1,500 words.

The philosophical material I want the fiction to put under pressure is the oldest argument in the field and its most persuasive modern rival. The Epicureans held that death cannot be an evil for the one who dies, because good and evil consist in sensation and the dead have none; on this view, the thought that death harms the person who dies is a confusion. A companion Epicurean argument observes that we do not count the eternity of time before our birth as a misfortune, and asks why the eternity after our death should be any different. Against both stands the view usually called the deprivation account: death is bad for us because it takes from us the goods we would otherwise have had, so that a life cut short is worse for the one who lived it than a life allowed to run on, and the absence of anyone to be harmed at the moment of death is neither here nor there. I want the story to feel two difficulties that the literature tends to handle too quickly. The first is that a harm seems to require someone who is worse off, and at the time of death there is no one. The second is that the deprivation account compares a life that was lived with a life that never was, and it is not obvious that the two can be placed on a single scale.

The premise I have in mind is this. In a near-future jurisdiction, the legislature has established the Deprivation Ledger. When a person dies at another's hand, an assessor computes the difference between the value of the life the deceased actually had and the value of the life the deceased would have had but for the killing. That number sets both the compensation owed to the survivors and the severity band of the offence. Every file closes with a form, and the form has a line for the name of the person harmed.

Tell the story from inside that office. I would like the assessor to work through three files that strain the procedure: a man who died in the first weeks of a diagnosis that would, on any honest reckoning, have made the remainder of his life worse than nothing, so that the Ledger registers no deprivation at all; a young child, where the assessor must decide whether the goods that count are those of the child as she was or those of the adult she would have become, a decision on which the number turns enormously; and a person who died by their own hand after a long and settled wish to die, where the counterfactual is not given in advance, since it might be continued life and it might be the death that was chosen. Two further threads would be welcome: a claim from a surviving spouse that what was taken from her husband was not years but reputation, ruined after his death by a disclosure he could not have anticipated; and, at some point, a colleague or a complainant who points out that the Ledger charges no one for the long stretch of time before we exist, and asks what makes the stretch after us so expensive.

A few constraints. Carry the argument through paperwork, procedure, and dialogue rather than through a character who explains the Epicurean position at length; the story should never stop to lecture. Do not include footnotes, citations, or named authorities, and do not supply a reading list. Do not resolve the philosophical question, and do not end on a moral; end on the assessor's decision and the form being signed. I am less interested in a verdict on the argument than in watching a competent person be forced, by a line on a form, to take one.
