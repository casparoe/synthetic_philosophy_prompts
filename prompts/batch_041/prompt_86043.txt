Bit of an odd one. I've spent 22 years on the road as a paramedic and somehow I've ended up being the person giving the graduation speech at our service's passing-out ceremony on Friday: about sixty brand new paramedics and techs, their families, the whole executive team in the front row, and a handful of old hands from my own intake sitting at the back quietly grading me. Eight minutes, so call it a thousand words or so, and the brief from comms was basically 'keep it light.' I don't want to do the usual thing about how the real learning starts now. What I actually want to talk about is the rules they're all going to spend their careers hating, the ones that look like pure box-ticking: two people present whenever a controlled drug is drawn up, not letting someone refuse transport until it's been said back to them, not moving a patient before you've got a full set of obs, that sort of thing. Every one of those is a fence somebody built, and in six months time the new lot will be walking up to them saying I don't see the use of this, let's clear it away. So I want the speech to run on the Chesterton's fence idea without ever turning into a lecture about it — a couple of the old hands will catch the reference, most of the families won't, and nobody should feel talked down to.

Here's where I want it to land, and I'd rather you got there by building it than by announcing it. My honest view is that 'don't tear down a fence until you can say why it was put there' sounds wise and is a bit of a cheat, because the reasons behind old practices were mostly never written down — they live in the practice itself, not in anybody's head — so demanding a stated rationale lets the person who loves the rule say well nobody knows so leave it alone, and lets the person who hates it say well nobody knows so it can't be doing anything. Both are cheating. I want the speech to get to a better test than 'can you justify it': something like 'what actually happens if we stop, and can we find that out cheaply and reversibly,' with an honest admission that this is slower and more boring than either keeping the fence or knocking it down. And I want one more thing in there even if it's only a line or two — some fences are up to protect the patient and some are up to protect the service's backside, and these people need to be able to tell the difference rather than be told to trust the institution. Underneath all of it there's a Burke sort of thought I'd like hovering in the background rather than quoted at anybody: that inherited practices store up knowledge no living person has, which is a reason for care and not a reason for paralysis. Please don't invent studies or statistics, don't attribute anything to real named cases or reports, and if it runs long just tell me which paragraph to cut.
