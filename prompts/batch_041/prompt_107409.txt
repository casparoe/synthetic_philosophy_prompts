I teach a grad seminar in philosophy of psychiatry and I'm drafting the take-home final. I want one essay question on Hacking's looping effects, but I've taught this three times now and I'm bored of reading the same answer, which is basically "psychiatric categories are socially constructed and then the people in them change, so it's a loop, therefore realism is complicated." Everyone can recite that. What I actually want to test is whether they can say what specific work the looping-effect apparatus is doing that the generic social-construction claim isn't already doing, and where the apparatus starts to creak.

So here's the setup I want them to work with. I'm going to hand them a little fictional case (I'll paste it into the exam): a proposed new diagnosis for "digital overload syndrome," operationalized as some screen-time threshold plus functional impairment, that's currently being pushed by a patient-advocacy group, resisted by a professional body, and starting to be used by insurers. The case has three features I want them to notice: patients who read the criteria start reporting symptoms in the criteria's own vocabulary; clinicians start noticing impairment in people who'd previously just been called tired; and meanwhile the criteria themselves get revised in light of what the now-changed population looks like. I want the question to force them to say which of those three is the looping effect proper, whether the other two are the same phenomenon or something else wearing its clothes, and whether the whole thing is a defect of the category or just what it is for a classification to be about self-interpreting creatures. And I want the reflexive sting: if the population shifts every time you measure it, in what sense can the category ever be validated at all, and does the answer differ for a category that loops toward under-recognition instead of over-recognition. I also want them to take a side on whether calling something an "interactive kind" is a substantive causal claim or just a fancy way of saying the label is influential -- because half the class last year used the term without ever committing to which.

Please write the exam question itself (aim it so a strong student needs about 900 words and 75 minutes), then a model answer of roughly 700 words that actually makes the hard distinctions rather than gesturing at them, and then a marking scheme: five must-hit points worth 5 marks each (25 total), a one-line note on how students typically flub each one, and a crisp statement of what separates an A from a B -- specifically which moves a B answer will make that a top answer has to refuse to make. Assume the students have read the relevant Hacking but have no internet access during the exam, so nothing in the question or the model answer should depend on them recalling titles, dates, or page numbers.
