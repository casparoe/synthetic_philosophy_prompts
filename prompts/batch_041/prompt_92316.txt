My mum signed a living will about five years ago, before the dementia got bad, and I've ended up being the one who argues with the doctors, so this stopped being a thought experiment for me somewhere around last March.

The thing I keep bumping into, and I dont know if it has a name, is that across the two of her the only voice that gets believed is the one saying stop. When the directive says no tube, no vent, let me go, everyone treats it as her real self speaking and treats it as binding, even on days when she's up and eating and telling the nurse she's happy. When the directive says do everything, don't give up on me, that gets quietly parked the first time she moans that she's had enough, and the moan gets treated as her real self too. So a no from five years ago outranks a yes from this morning, and a yes from five years ago loses to a no from this morning. The past self can forbid but can't oblige; the present self gets listened to only when she's suffering. Nobody in the room is confused about the facts, we just keep reaching for whichever of her is saying stop.

Is there a name for that specific lopsidedness? I know there's a whole area about advance directives and about how much your past self gets to boss your present self around, but that's the neighborhood, not this thing. If it has a name, I want the carving, not the label: what exactly it is, how it differs from just deferring to the most recent wish and from the general rule that consent has to stay ongoing. And if it doesn't have a name, give it one and define it, and tell me whether the lopsidedness is actually principled or whether it's just us not being willing to watch her keep going.
