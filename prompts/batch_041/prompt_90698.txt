I'm TAing for a mid-level philosophy of science course and I'm the one stuck grading the midterm essays this weekend. The unit was on anthropic reasoning — the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, and what each does to the classic puzzles. I've got one answer that reads beautifully, very confident, clearly a student who did the reading, and my gut says a rushed grader would just circle full marks and move on. But something in it nags at me and I want to be sure I'm not about to dock points over a distinction I'm imagining. The question was:

"Distinguish the self-sampling assumption (SSA) from the self-indication assumption (SIA). Then explain how the two come apart in the Presumptuous Philosopher scenario, and say which of them (if either) the Doomsday argument relies on, and what happens to the Doomsday argument under the other one."

And the student wrote (roughly, I'm typing it up):

"SSA tells you to reason as if you were a randomly selected observer drawn from the set of all observers in your reference class — the class of observers relevantly like you. SIA says something stronger: that you should reason as if you were randomly selected from the set of all observers that could possibly exist, so that a hypothesis which posits a larger total number of observers is, other things equal, more likely to be true, since it offers more slots for you to have occupied.

In the Presumptuous Philosopher case we compare a theory T1 on which the world holds a modest number of observers with a theory T2 on which it holds an astronomically larger number. Under SSA the bare fact that you exist is more or less guaranteed either way, so SSA doesn't license any dramatic inference; the two theories stay roughly on a par. Under SIA, though, your existence is far more probable given T2, because T2 supplies vastly more observers and therefore vastly more chances for you to have been one of them. So SIA makes T2 enormously more probable, and the philosopher who announces from his armchair that the universe must be huge is the presumptuous philosopher — the presumption is SIA's fault.

The Doomsday argument, by contrast, is an application of SIA rather than SSA. You find yourself at some position in the total sequence of humans who will ever have lived, and since a hypothesis on which we go extinct soon gives you a much better chance of landing near the end of that sequence, the short-future hypothesis is favored. This is exactly the SIA move: a bigger total population means more observers and hence more slots, so you should favor the hypothesis that generates more of them, which here is the early-extinction hypothesis. It follows that if you reject SSA and adopt SIA you make the Doomsday conclusion stronger, while a committed SSA theorist can shrug the whole thing off, since SSA gives no reason to think your position in the sequence is informative."
