I'm in an AI safety reading group this term, and we keep circling the same impasse about corrigibility and the shutdown problem without anyone's position moving. Roughly: one view treats corrigibility as something to be built into the agent's objective — the agent should prefer, or at least not resist, being shut down or modified when the operators decide to, and the technical task is specifying an objective whose maximizer doesn't maneuver against shutdown. The opposing view says this is either impossible or incoherent, because an agent with a final goal and ordinary instrumental rationality has a standing reason to keep itself running and unmodified, so a genuinely corrigible agent would have to lack a final goal, or already have its own modification among its goals. A third view says both are looking in the wrong place: corrigibility isn't a property of an objective at all but of the training process or the decision architecture, and hunting for the right objective is a category mistake.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a fourth position in it. My own hypothesis is that the impasse isn't really about shutdown, or about what an AI ought to do, but about whether deference to an external process can be represented as a preference over outcomes at all — in which case the dispute is about the expressive limits of the decision-theoretic formalism rather than about values. A rival hypothesis I can't rule out is that corrigibility is quietly doing two jobs at once, describing a disposition of the agent toward correction and describing a relation of authority between agent and operator, and that each proposed formalization keeps one and drops the other, which is why every proposal gets attacked from the opposite flank. Tell me which, if either, captures the real structure of the impasse, arguing from what each side actually commits to, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a theorem, a distinction, a change of question, or nothing at all.
