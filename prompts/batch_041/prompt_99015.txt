Last weekend a friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing about utilitarianism, and the argument has followed me back into my work: I sit on the board of a regional water authority, and next week we vote on whether to build the Ellsworth reservoir, which would flood a valley and displace roughly four hundred residents in order to secure supply for a metropolitan area of two million. Our staff recommends proceeding, and their case is frankly utilitarian: the aggregate benefits are enormous, the costs are concentrated on a small group, and a compensation package can leave the displaced residents materially whole. My friend thinks the decision is obvious and that my hesitation is sentimental. I have to circulate the board's decision memo before the vote, and I would like it to be better than either of us managed over dinner.

Please draft that memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action, whether to build, refuse, or build with specified conditions, with a time frame and the conditions spelled out, and it should take seriously the objection that goes by the name of the separateness of persons: that a loss borne by one person is not cancelled by gains that accrue to other people rather than to her, so that the favorable total is not a benefit to any single subject. I do not want that objection gestured at; I want the memo to say precisely what work it is doing. Is the complaint really about adding welfare across persons at all, or only about the particular way our staff has added it? Does the compensation package answer the complaint or merely buy off the complainants, and does it matter whether the compensation is paid in fact or only asserted as possible? Would a different decision procedure, such as a negotiated settlement, a community veto, or an ex ante rule chosen without knowing which valley one inhabits, satisfy the objection, or would it only relocate the aggregation somewhere less visible? My friend kept insisting that refusing to add up is a counsel of paralysis; the memo should say whether that is true.

Recommend the decision the board should actually take, give reasons that a skeptical member could respond to, and give the strongest consideration on the side you come out against its due. Write it as a document the board could adopt, not as an essay.
