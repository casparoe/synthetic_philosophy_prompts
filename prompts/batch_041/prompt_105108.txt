A book I have been reading on the foundations of quantum mechanics offers an account of probability in the many-worlds interpretation that I suspect is too tidy to be trusted. Its summary runs approximately as follows:

"Everett's achievement was to show that quantum mechanics needs no collapse. The wave function simply evolves under the Schrödinger equation, and every measurement splits the universe into branches, one for each possible outcome. Because all outcomes occur, there is no probability in the ordinary sense: everything that can happen does happen, with certainty. Everett nevertheless proved that the Born rule, which assigns each outcome a weight equal to the squared modulus of its amplitude, emerges automatically from the theory, so the familiar quantum probabilities are recovered. Critics reply that this cannot be correct, since a deterministic theory in which every outcome is realized gives us no reason to expect to find ourselves in a branch whose statistics match the Born rule; the many-worlds interpretation therefore either fails to make predictions or smuggles in an extra probabilistic postulate. Some defenders concede the point and propose that the branching itself is genuinely random, which would restore ordinary probability — though at the cost of contradicting the theory's own determinism."

I would like an audit of this passage. Please work through it claim by claim, saying for each what someone who works on the foundations of quantum mechanics would accept as stated, what they would wince at as a simplification, and what is simply mistaken. I am particularly interested in two things: first, whether the passage's central contention — that many-worlds cannot recover the Born rule — follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or whether it depends on some of the claims being wrong; and second, what the genuine live positions are, since the phrase "the Born rule problem" is plainly doing a great deal of work here. My own instinct, for what it is worth, is that the theory is being made to sound more paradoxical than it is, but I cannot tell whether that instinct amounts to anything. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want to understand the state of the argument well enough to distinguish a real objection from a caricature of one.
