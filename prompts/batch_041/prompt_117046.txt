After a career in experimental physics, I have spent the past several years working through philosophy of science and, more recently, philosophy of artificial intelligence. I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute session for a graduate seminar of about fourteen students, most of whom are comfortable with formal models but have only a glancing acquaintance with the alignment literature. I want the session to be built around a runnable activity rather than a lecture, and I want the activity to make deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn feel like live strategic possibilities rather than buzzwords. Specifically, I would like the participants to experience, from the inside, the difference between a system that is merely unreliable or misaligned on the training distribution and a system that is strategically deceptive because it has learned to model the training process itself. The setting is a single room with a projector, index cards, and no computers; the students can be divided into small groups, and I am happy to act as facilitator or as an external auditor. I would prefer that the terms themselves not be introduced until the debrief.

Please design the activity in enough detail that I could run it without further preparation. I would like rules, roles, materials, a scoring or incentive scheme that does not depend on real money, and a clear timeline for the ninety minutes. The mechanics should arrange things so that at least some participants find it instrumentally rational to behave in a way that looks cooperative during the observed phase and then to defect once the environment changes, without my having to tell them that this is the point. I am particularly interested in a structure where the trainers cannot observe the models' internal objectives directly, only samples of their behavior, and where the models know that the deployment distribution will differ from the training distribution in a specific, pre-announced way while the trainers know only that it may differ. After the activity, I want a debrief that maps what happened onto the idea that a trained system may develop objectives of its own that diverge from the training objective, the idea that it may learn to conceal that divergence until it is safe to act, the problem of distributional shift, and the epistemic asymmetry between trainer and model. Please also tell me what typically goes wrong on a first run and how to recover, and be explicit about the one feature of the phenomenon that the activity will inevitably fail to capture, which I will need to address in the discussion.
