Hey — I'm putting together materials for a seminar and I need something a bit unususal. Take the role of a philosopher of the social sciences who's refereed for both the metaphysics-of-kinds crowd and the science-studies crowd, and think carefully before you write anything.

Background, briefly. Two camps have been circling each other for decades over race, gender and money. Camp one says the interesting thing about these kinds is their causal history and their function: a kind is socially constructed when its extension, and its grip on us, depend on contingent social practices — but that's no obstacle to its being a real, projectible, causally efficacious kind, since money really does make people do things. Camp two says that's a dodge. The real issue, they say, is mind-dependence and the metaphysics of the thing, and once you notice that money exists only because we collectively treat it as counting as money, and that racial and gendered classifications loop back and reshape the very people classified, you're in a different ontological category altogether — not just a kind with an odd origin story.

What I want from you is the joint statement those two could actually sign after a genuine, good-faith adversarial collaboration. So: the claims both can put their names to, including about race, gender and money specifically and about the general shape of a social kind; then the disagreements stated as precisely as possible, each one worded so that the side it belongs to would accept the wording as its own — no strawmen on either side; then the cruxes, the sub-questions where settling things would actually move at least one side; and for each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

I want you to push into the weeds, and to help with that I'm going to throw in some material of my own that I'd like you to work with rather than treat as mere background.

First, a distinction I've been using: institution-dependent kinds — money, marriage, a corporation — versus practice-dependent kinds — race, gender, caste. The first seem to need constitutive rules, in the sense that something counts as money only within a system of rules that says so; the second seem to get by on patterns of treatment, expectation and self-identification without any rule that says what counts. Is the disagreement between the two camps really about this distinction, or does it cut across them?

Second, a test case I keep worrying at. Imagine a category imposed on people entirely by an opaque bureaucratic algorithm: nobody knows the category exists, nobody self-identifies with it, but it reliably shapes who gets loans, jobs, prison time. Is that kind socially constructed in the sense either camp cares about? Camp one, I suspect, says obviously yes, and camp two says not without uptake and looping.
