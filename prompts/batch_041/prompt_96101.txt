I joined my local party branch about eight months ago — went along for a housing campaign, stayed because I like the people — and I've ended up agreeing to give a talk at next month's branch meeting. Fifteen minutes, then questions until people run out of steam or the room has to be cleared, so call it twenty-five minutes of Q&A. What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defence of the talk.

Here's what I'm arguing. Simone Weil wrote an essay about abolishing political parties and everyone treats it as mystical overreach, but I think there's a structural claim buried in it that survives without the theology. Her picture, as I read it: a party isn't mainly a machine for aggregating preferences or for winning elections, it's a machine for producing a shared passion, and once you're inside it what you believe about means — and eventually about facts — becomes partly a function of what the group needs to be true. My addition, and the part I actually want to defend, is the mechanism, which I've been calling the loyalty tax. A party only exists if its members don't defect at the wrong moment, so it has to make defection expensive; that's all discipline really is. And any institution that makes changing your mind costly will slowly fill up with false beliefs, because the people who were right early are the ones who pay the most for being right early. If that's right, it isn't a corruption story and it isn't fixable by internal democracy, since the fix is exactly the thing a party can least afford: somebody standing up and saying I was wrong at the moment when it costs them most. I'm not arguing for a ban. I'm arguing that anyone who cares about getting things right should price party membership honestly, as a real cost paid in truthfulness, and design around it. Concretely: seperate the campaigning function from the belief-forming function, pick candidates through sortition panels rather than through the membership, and let people hold the line without having to hold the belief.

Who's in the room. About twenty people. Three or four old hands who've been in since the eighties and will hear "abolish parties" as an attack on their entire life. Two union reps, one of whom is a branch secretary, who will say parties are how working people get leverage, full stop. A former councillor who will talk about how anything actually gets built. A young organiser who runs the data and thinks this is self-indulgent while we're losing. A retired teacher who reads a lot of history and will bring up the CPSU, the Wobblies and probaly Tammany Hall. And one person with a politics degree who will say this is just the old antiparty tradition, and that antipartyism always ends up handing power to unaccountable notables.

What I want back: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people, ordered by how much damage they'd do to me — not by how loud they'll be — each phrased the way that person would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and one line on where the reply is soft. Mark which ones I should just concede up front rather than fight. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
