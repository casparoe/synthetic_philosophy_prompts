I am drafting a case study for a graduate seminar on secularism and the state, and I keep running into an apparent contradiction in the French arrangement that I would like you to help me state properly. What I want is not a history of laïcité but the theory that the practice commits one to.

Here is the practice as I understand it. Pupils in state primary and secondary schools may not wear signs or clothing that conspicuously manifest a religious affiliation; the 2004 statute names no symbol, but in practice it has been read to cover headscarves, kippahs, large crosses and turbans, and it has fallen overwhelmingly on Muslim girls. A later statute, passed in 2010 and in force since 2011, prohibits covering the face in public space, fining the wearer or requiring her to attend a citizenship course, and punishing far more heavily anyone who compels another to cover. The European Court of Human Rights upheld that second law, accepting the state's argument that it was needed to preserve the conditions of living together — a ground that has nothing to do with public order in the ordinary sense of preventing harm or disorder.

Yet the same state does things that seem to sit badly with any straightforward separation of religion from public life. It pays the salaries of Catholic priests, Lutheran and Reformed pastors and rabbis in Alsace-Moselle, where the Concordat of 1801 was never repealed, and where religious instruction given by members of the recognised faiths remains part of the timetable of state schools, subject to an opt-out. It funds the great majority of private schools, most of them Catholic, through contracts under which it pays the teachers. It maintains chaplaincies in prisons, hospitals and the armed forces. Whatever laïcité is, it is plainly not the withdrawal of the state from religion.

What conception of the state, of the citizen, and of the relation between a person's religion and their public identity does this package presuppose? I am interested in four things in particular. First, which rival conceptions of the state's relation to religion does the package rule out, and how far does it differ from the Anglo-American model of accommodation, in which the state neither endorses nor suppresses religion but grants exemptions and reasonable adjustments so that believers can participate on their own terms? Second, is the package internally coherent? The Concordat region and the subsidised Catholic schools are sometimes explained away as historical survivals, but I want to know whether they reveal that the operative principle is something other than neutrality — for instance, that the state, and not religion, is to be the author of social unity, so that religion is to be subordinated to the state rather than separated from it, and tolerated exactly to the extent that it can be made a servant of national cohesion.

Third, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does the school ban require the view that religious signs are inherently a threat to the equality of the sexes, or the quite different view that the school is a special space of civic formation in which the pupil is addressed only as a future citizen, or the further view that the state must be neutral in its own voice but need not be neutral in civil society? Several of these would license the same statutes, and I would like to know which cases would tell them apart. Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one defensible theory would produce it, and then say what would have to change if the presupposed conception were replaced by the accommodation model: which parts of the present arrangement would survive, which would have to go, and whether the Concordat region is a fossil of an older view or a genuine counterexample to the theory you have reconstructed.
