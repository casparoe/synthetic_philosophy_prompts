I want to pressure-test the case for a strong, content-level cognitive penetration of perception, and I need a serious interlocutor rather than a straw man. Please play a committed defender of cognitive penetration: someone who holds that a perceiver's concepts, beliefs, or expectations can alter the phenomenal character of perceptual experience itself — not merely the allocation of attention, the post-perceptual judgment, or the response criterion. Stay in that role until I say "stop." Argue in the first person, in the voice of a philosopher, in compact paragraphs suited to a back-and-forth; you may draw on the standard resources of the position — the argument from phenomenal contrast, the distinction between seeing and seeing-as, the evidence from paradigms designed to minimize report — but you should deploy them as arguments rather than catalogue positions. Concede weak or ambiguous cases where you must, but do not concede the central claim that at least some cases change perceptual content; if you think I have misdescribed a case, say so and keep going. Do not escape into the trivial observation that all perception is theory-laden in some loose sense; I am pressing the specific, content-level claim. Lead your reply with your character's central claim in the first sentence, then give the argument for it, with no preamble and without restating my challenge.

My opening move: take a stipulated case, not one from the literature. Subjects spend a week learning a new category — call it "glorp" — defined by a reflectance boundary finer than their ordinary discrimination threshold. Before training they cannot reliably separate glorp from non-glorp and report no phenomenal difference between the two. After training they sort the surfaces quickly and confidently, and several report that the two groups now "look different." A skeptic will say this shows only that the training taught them where to attend, or supplied a new label and a new response criterion, or raised their confidence — not that the look of the surfaces changed. Here is the harder half of the objection: you have no independent, non-question-begging way to read the content of an experience off either behavior or report, so nothing the subjects could do would distinguish a change in content from a change in attention-plus-judgment. So tell me which you will give up. If you call this penetration, you owe me an account of what evidence would separate a content-change from an attention-and-judgment-change; if no possible evidence could, I want to know what work the word "content" is doing in your claim, and why the dispute is not simply verbal.
