You are a philosopher specializing in welfare economics and the theory of measurement — someone with a genuine working command of the technical literature on interpersonal comparisons of well-being, and with a referee's temperament for sorting out which of two people is right about which point. Please act as referee for the following dispute, which I witnessed and could not adjudicate.

A few instructions before you begin. Work from first principles; do not supply citations, a reading list, or a survey of who said what — I want the reasoning, not the bibliography. Think carefully and at length rather than briskly. Open with a short scoping section setting out the conceptual terrain as you understand it, distinguishing at minimum: comparisons of levels versus comparisons of differences; what purely ordinal data can and cannot support; and the difference between a measurement claim (there is a fact of the matter about how much better off one person is) and a normative choice (we have decided to treat claims in a certain way). Only then turn to the dispute itself. At the end, rate each move on the scale described below.

The setting: I serve on the board of a small charitable trust that each year distributes a fixed sum, enough for perhaps two awards, among applicants who need expensive home adaptations or assistive equipment. This year there are five applicants with quite different conditions and quite different needs. Two trustees, Vera and Ari, spent most of the meeting arguing about how the decision should be made.

Move 1 (Vera): We cannot avoid comparing applicants, she said; if we refuse to, we will end up choosing by coin flip or by who wrote the most affecting letter, and both of those are choices too. Comparison does not require reading minds. She proposed a concrete device: ask each applicant to place their present situation on a personal 0-to-100 scale, where 0 is the worst state they can imagine for themselves and 100 the best, and then mark where the adaptation would put them; fund in descending order of the size of that move. The endpoints are shared by construction, she said, so the distances are distances on a common scale. Anticipating an objection, she added that if an applicant's scale looks distorted — someone who places everything from a mild itch to quadriplegia between 60 and 70 — we can correct for it, since everyone faces broadly the same range of human misfortune.

Move 2 (Ari): That is a ruler with no units on it, she replied. The words at the two ends are the same, but their contents are not: one person's worst imaginable is a bad week, another's is a decade. Nothing in the procedure tells us whether a 20-point move on your scale is the same amount of well-being as a 20-point move on mine. What you actually have is one ordering per person, dressed up in numbers. Any strictly increasing relabelling of my scale — stretching the top, compressing the middle — is equally faithful to everything I have told you, and some relabellings flip your ranking of the applicants. The correction clause is the giveaway: to know that a scale is distorted, you must already know the true amounts, which is precisely what the procedure was supposed to deliver.

Move 3 (Vera): She conceded that the unit is not measurable the way a metre is, but said exactness is the wrong standard; the question is whether our comparisons are better than the alternatives, not whether they are perfect. Her main point was that refusing to compare does not remove comparisons, it relocates them. A lottery over the five applicants treats their claims as equal — that is a substantive comparison. Ordering applicants by date of application treats earlier as more deserving — also a comparison. So the real choice is between comparisons we can inspect and defend and comparisons buried inside a procedure. She added that the fact that a comparison is not invariant under every monotone relabelling does not make it arbitrary: some comparisons survive every relabelling that respects what we actually know. If two applicants would each end up in a state that is bad for anyone, no admissible rescaling changes who is worse off. There is a floor of comparability here that even a sceptic must grant; the only question is how high it is.

Move 4 (Ari): She granted the floor, but said it buys much less than Vera wants. Comparisons of levels, of the form she is worse off than he is, get you at best a partial order: they tell you who is below whom, not by how much. So she proposed a policy that uses only that much. Sort applicants into bands by how bad their situation is, where the bands are defined by states we can defend as bad for anyone — untreated pain, being unable to leave the house, and so on; fund everyone in the worst band first; where applicants cannot be separated, use a lottery. This avoids the invented unit altogether, and it puts the one genuinely normative choice, where to draw the bands, out in the open, where it can be argued about and revised, rather than hiding it inside a scale that pretends to be a measurement. She added two further shots. First, Vera's demand for how much invites summation, and summation licenses trades: a large gain for many against a catastrophic loss for one, which is exactly what this trust should not be in the business of doing. Second, no procedure could ever show that Vera's scale is the correct one, whereas a defended threshold can be argued for, criticised, and changed.

Move 5 (Vera): Coarseness is not innocence, she said. Deciding that untreated chronic pain and being unable to leave the house belong in the same band is a judgment about how bad two different conditions are — a comparison, just a coarse one, made once instead of case by case. And coarseness has costs: a band rule will sometimes spend the whole fund on a small improvement for someone who has just slipped below the line while leaving a large, cheap improvement for someone above it unfunded. If Ari answers that we should then draw finer bands, she is rebuilding the cardinal scale one band at a time.

I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it, and I would like a proper adjudication. Please provide:

1. A rating for each of the five moves, on a scale running from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, with a one- or two-sentence justification for each rating.
2. For each move, the strongest version of the point the speaker was reaching for — what she should have said, or would have said with more time.
3. A separation of the exchanges that are genuine disagreements from those where the two are talking past each other.
4. An account of what both are missing.
5. A verdict on who has the better of it overall, distinguishing the general question (can interpersonal comparisons be made at all, and in what sense?) from our practical question (how should this trust decide?). If the verdicts differ, say why.
6. A closing judgment on whether what remains is conceptual, empirical, or normative, and what would move it.
