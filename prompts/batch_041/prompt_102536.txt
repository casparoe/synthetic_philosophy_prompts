I've been in a running argument with my book club about what a story "means," and I've realized I have pretty firm gut reactions to a bunch of cases but no idea what rule they add up to. Hoping you can figure out what principle (if any) is behind my calls, and tell me which of my gut reactions I have to give up. For context I'm a backend engineer, never took a philosophy class, so go easy on the jargon.

The cases, mostly from my friend Dana's unpublished stuff that only our group chat has read, plus a couple I made up:

1. Dana wrote a novel about a guy who slowly ruins his family. It's grim. The last two chapters are grim. He learns nothing. In the chat Dana insists the book is ultimately hopeful, that we're meant to read the ruin as the beginning of his growth. I don't buy it. The book is bleak, that's what it means, and Dana saying otherwise doesn't change the book.

2. Different story, also Dana's. There's a scene where a character, Mira, walks out of a party and we're never told why. It's genuinely blank — nothing in the text points either way. Dana says "oh, she's going to kill herself, that's what that scene is." Now I feel like that IS what the scene means. She filled in a blank.

3. A weirder one I think about alot. Suppose two people who've never met write down the exact same sentence, word for word: the light came in and he decided to stay. One of them means it as a metaphor for depression lifting; the other is writing about a literal morning and a literal decision. I think those two sentences mean different things even though they're identical.

4. My other friend wrote a zine piece that on its face praises a brutal city council and says everything they do is wise. But he wrote it somewhere you could not say anything else, and the whole thing is obviously a joke at their expense — everyone who reads it gets that. So I think that piece means the opposite of what its words say.

5. There's a pattern in one of Dana's stories I'm almost sure she didn't notice: every time two characters touch, the next paragraph mentions water. I found it. She says "huh, I guess, I didn't do that on purpose." Is that part of what the story means? My gut says... kind of? It feels like it's in the story even if it wasn't in her head. But I can't say that without sounding like I'm making things up.

6. Dana claimed she "never wrote" a scene where the dad hits the kid. She did write it. It's on page 40, it's not ambiguous, there's no reading where it isn't there. I take the page over her memory, obviously.

7. Last one, and the one I keep flip-flopping on: Dana drafted a story where the ending works two ways — either the brother forgives him, or he's just too tired to keep fighting — and honestly both readings fit every line. Dana says it's the forgiveness one. I want to say fine, it's forgiveness, she wrote it. But in case 1 I said the author's word doesn't count, and I can't tell what's different except that here the text is ambiguous and there it wasn't.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or do I have to drop something? I'd especially like to know what it says about a case I haven't listed — an author who's dead and left no notes, where readers a hundred years later find a reading that fits the text perfectly but that nobody in the author's own time could have had. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts above the principle can't save. If the honest answer is "there's no single principle, there are two and your switching between them," say that too, but then tell me which cases push me toward which one.
