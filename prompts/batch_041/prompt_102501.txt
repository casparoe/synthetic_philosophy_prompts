I'm a reporter who covers science and ideas, and I've got an argument lodged in my head that I'm pretty sure cheats somewhere, but I can't find the seam. A historian friend sketched it for me at a bar after a conference and I've been turning it over ever since. Here it is, roughly as she gave it:

(1) To explain an event is to show that, given the circumstances, it had to turn out as it did.
(2) If a set of facts shows that an event had to turn out as it did, then someone who knew those facts inadvance could have predicted the event.
(3) Historians explain by telling stories — a run of particular decisions, accidents and contingencies, with no general laws anywhere in them.
(4) A story that contains no laws can't show that anything had to happen; at most it shows that this is how it happened, this time.
(5) So narrative history doesn't really explain anything; only law-governed accounts do.
(6) But that's plainly absurd. Explaining is the whole job of history. If a good book tells me why the Republic collapsed, or why a particular revolution caught fire when it did, it has explained something — laws or no laws.
(7) So one of the steps above has to give.

She reads this as a reductio of the whole idea that explaining an event means fitting it under a law, and she's happy to ditch (1). I'd rather not ditch (1) — it sounds right to me, or at least I can't see what else "explain" could even mean. And (6) I'm keeping no matter what; I'm a reporter, I think stories explain things. So: which step do I cut, why did it look so innocent sitting there, and what's the bill if I cut it? If there's a way to keep stories as genuine explanations without pretending they show anything was inevitable, I'd love to know, because right now every option seems to cost me alot of something I wanted to keep.
