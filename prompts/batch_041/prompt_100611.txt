I have been trying to think through Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the tortoise, and I have come to suspect that the lesson usually drawn from it is too comfortable. Consider the barest case: from the premises P and P → Q we are said to be entitled to conclude Q. But all the tortoise has been handed are two sentences, and a sentence is not an inference; nothing in the pair by itself yields the conclusion, which is precisely why the tortoise can accept both and still decline to conclude. Adding a further premise, (P and (P → Q)) → Q, merely invites the demand for another, and the regress never closes. It is sometimes said that the truth table for the material conditional settles the matter, since there is no row in which both premises are true and Q false; but the tortoise can grant the whole truth table as one more premise and still refuse to detach, so that reply appears to beg the question. I therefore want to ask whether the argument as it stands is really valid, and if it is not, whether any finite repair is available. Could the bridging principle be turned into a rule of inference rather than a premise, or replaced by an infinitary rule that licenses the conclusion in a single step, or codified as an axiom schema? And if none of these closes the regress, ought we to conclude that no deductive inference is ever fully justified — that deduction is something we simply do rather than something we are entitled to? Please answer in American English.
