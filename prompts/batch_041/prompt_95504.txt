I am a science journalist who has spent the past decade reporting on statistical evidence in medicine and climate science, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop called Accuracy and Public Reason. The room will be split between formal epistemologists and working journalists; the former will know epistemic utility theory in detail, the latter will know newsroom norms in detail, and each group will be suspicious of the other. I want my remarks to introduce and defend a distinction that I think does real work: between first-order accuracy, which is having credences that match the way the world is, and second-order accuracy, which is having credences that match the evidence available. My thesis is that the professional norm of balance in science reporting is often a demand for first-order accuracy that sacrifices second-order accuracy, and that accuracy-first epistemology, properly understood, vindicates a norm of evidential calibration instead. Write the ten-minute speech. It should not be a lecture on scoring rules; the accuracy-first idea should govern the speech's structure, determining which concessions I can make to the journalists, which examples I can use, and what I must refuse to say. Build the argument around one concrete newsroom case: a decision about how to cover a scientific consensus and a fringe denial, including the pressure to present both sides equally. The case should do the argumentative work, not merely illustrate it. The speech should end by proposing a specific revision to journalistic practice that follows from the accuracy-first view. Do not cite any literature, do not attribute any view to a named author or text, and do not include a reading list; the speech must stand on its own. It should be polished enough to deliver aloud, and it should leave the epistemologists in the room with something they have not simply heard before.
