my dad died of pancreatic cancer last spring and ever since I've been going down a rabbit hole on the numbers people keep handing me about my own body, and I want to know what the reference class problem actually implies for me — practically, not as trivia.

Heres the situation so you know what I'm working with. I bought one of those genetic risk reports and it gave me a number. Then my GP gave me a different number, off a big cohort study. Then my sister, who's a nurse, gave me a third one out of a small study of families that look like ours. All three were presented to me as "my risk." I asked each person what population the number was computed over, and none of them could really say — the best I got was "people like you." When I pushed on which people, the honest answer was always some version of a tradeoff: the more conditions you pile on, the closer the group gets to being just me, and the less data is in it, until at the limit the group is me and the "probability" is just whether it happens or not.

Two things I've half-worked out and want you to take seriously rather than talk me out of:

The standard advice is "use the most specific reference class available." I think that's either empty (since the most specific class containing me is a singleton, and a singleton has no statistics) or it's a policy decision wearing a lab coat. Choosing which people I get pooled with is a choice about whose bad outcomes get averaged into my number, and nobody making that choice is accountable to the people being pooled. And piling on conditions doesn't even reliably help — I'm pretty sure you can add a relevant-seeming condition and watch the number flip the other way, so "more info" isn't monotonically better.

The second thing: I suspect the reference class problem for a *decision* isn't about which class of cases I belong to at all, but which class of decision-makers I'm one of. "Women with my mutation" and "people who face a choice like this one" are both real classes and they can point opposite ways, and I don't think there's a fact of the matter about which one I'm "really" in.

What I want: the most important implications of the reference class problem for how I should actually use these numbers, both for the screening decision sitting in front of me and for the general question of how anyone should read a statistic about themselves. Rank them, tell me which ones actually change what I do versus which are just philosophically interesting, and tell me which of my two half-worked-out points above I'm wrong about. I'm especially interested in whether "my risk" is a category error, whether base-rate talk is a red herring once you see the base rate is partition-relative, whether this is really the same problem as the problem of the single case or a different one, and whether the same issue is what's underneath the fairness fights about risk scores (I have a hunch you can't be calibrated on every subgroup and also have equal error rates across them, and I'd like to know if that's right and what it implies for me).
