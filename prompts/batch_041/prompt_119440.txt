I have been trying to keep a hedonist view of well-being, that pleasure is what is good for a person, but I cannot say what my pleasures have in common, and I suspect I am applying a different test in each case. Here is where my gut lands. The taste of a ripe peach: a pleasure, and the pleasure seems to be in the taste itself. The moment the last step of a proof falls into place: a pleasure, though there is no pleasant sensation, indeed no sensation at all. Pressing a thumb into a sore shoulder muscle hard enough that it hurts: I do it, I go back for it, and I count it a pleasure, though every moment of it is a small pain. Hearing that a rival's venture has collapsed: a warm, unmistakably pleasant glow, which I still refuse to count as a pleasure. A walk through driving rain that I called a misery all day and now, years later, rank among the great pleasures of that year: I cannot say whether the pleasure was there at the time or has been added in memory. And a habit I cannot stop, which I want, take, get no felt pleasure from, and would rather be rid of: I do not want to call that a pleasure at all. My tentative principle is that an experience is a pleasure just when I like it, meaning I want it to continue and would choose it again. That saves the peach and the proof and even the sore muscle, and lets me say the rainy walk was a pleasure all along. But it forces me to call the rival's collapse a pleasure, which I resist, and it seems to make the compulsion a pleasure as well, which is worse. Is there one principle that gets all of these, or a small set of clauses each earning its place? Which of my verdicts must it overturn, and what does it say about the case I cannot place at all, the sting of a very hot shower, which I hate while I am in it and miss when I am not?
