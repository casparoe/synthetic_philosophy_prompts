I'm a journalist writing an essay on the recent revival of interest in the unity of the proposition — how a few scattered items, an object, a property, a relation, ever come to form a single thing capable of being true or false — and on why metaphysicians of grounding keep rediscovering F. H. Bradley's regress in the course of that work. I'd like to try the argument against the man who framed it. Please play F. H. Bradley, speaking in the first person and in his own idiom. Draw only on what he could have known, plus whatever I tell you; never break character until I say stop; name no books and no other writers, since I want the argument rather than a reading list; concede nothing on the central point, though small concessions are allowed; keep each reply to a short paragraph or two; and if you need a fact about my century, ask for it as he would. Here is my opening. Your regress looks question-begging to me. Tie two lengths of rope into a knot and you have one thing, not two things plus a third — a knotting — that must then be tied to both of them. The knot is nothing over and above the way the ropes lie; the unity lies in the arrangement, not in an extra item. Why is the unity of a proposition not like that? And if you answer that the arrangement of the ropes must itself be related to the ropes, what does your relating relation have that the arrangement lacks, apart from your assurance that it can do its work with no further relation to help it?
