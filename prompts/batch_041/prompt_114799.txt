I am putting together an undergraduate seminar on skepticism and want to use The Matrix in the unit on Moore's proof of an external world, but the reading my students and I keep arriving at — the film as Descartes's dream argument in leather — is not what I need; they can produce that unaided. What I want is a reading that takes the structure of Moore's proof seriously and asks whether the film's scenario is even the kind of threat the proof was built to answer.

The point I cannot get past is this. Moore raises one hand and says "here is one hand," raises the other and says "and here is another," and concludes that an external world exists; his test for a proof is only that the premises be known and that the conclusion follow. The proof's strangeness is that its premises are meant to be more certain than any premise of the skeptical argument, so that the burden of proof shifts to the skeptic — the move now called the Moorean shift. But the Matrix is not a skeptical scenario in that sense at all. Its world is shared, causally coherent, and independent of any single mind; it satisfies every mark Moore associates with an external world. Neo does not discover that there is no world; he discovers that the world has a different substrate and a landlord. So does Moore's proof tell against the red-pill revelation at all? Could Neo, standing in the Construct, run the Moorean proof on his own hand — and if the proof goes through, does it not prove that his hands are real, which the film insists is false? Is the lesson that the proof fails, or that the film misdescribes its own threat?

Two further things I want brought in, because I suspect the payoff is there. First, what Chisholm called the problem of the criterion, and the choice between particularism and methodism. Moore is the paradigm particularist: he refuses the demand for a general criterion of knowledge in advance, and starts from cases he takes himself to know. Neo is a methodist by temperament — nothing counts for him as knowledge until he has the source, the red pill, the view from outside — and the ending, where he sees the world as code, is a fantasy of methodism vindicated. Which of the two does the film actually side with, and is that ending a philosophical mistake or a dramatization of something Moore's proof leaves out? Second, the familiar objection that the proof cannot transmit justification from premise to conclusion, since anyone who doubts the conclusion must doubt the premise, against the dogmatist reply that perceptual knowledge need not be earned by argument. Say which you find stronger, and whether the film dramatizes either one.

Use the Construct's mirror scene as a set piece: Neo touches the mirrored surface and it climbs his arm, a hand discovering that it is not a hand, which is Moore's proof running backwards. If it helps, The Truman Show is the limiting case — a fully real external world whose extent is hidden from its inhabitant, and no Moorean crisis at all.

Where the film resists the reading, say so rather than forcing it: its emotional pull depends on the fantasy of a place outside every representation, which Moore, oddly, also presupposes, and Cypher, who knows the truth about the world and prefers the illusion anyway, is a man who agrees with Moore and simply does not care. End by saying what the film contributes to the Moore debate that the articles do not, and what it gets wrong about it.
