In the mid-1970s a group of molecular biologists publicly urged a voluntary pause on certain recombinant-DNA experiments until the hazards could be assessed, and the Asilomar conference that followed produced containment guidelines that were later absorbed into national regulation. Treat this episode as a test case for the precautionary principle, but begin by disaggregating three things commonly run together under that name: precaution as a substantive decision rule (restrict an activity because the possible harm is grave even though the evidence is inconclusive), precaution as a reallocation of the burden of proof (the proponent of the activity must establish its safety, rather than its critics having to establish harm), and precaution as an epistemic stance (treating a grave possibility as a reason to slow down, not merely as a reason to gather more data). Say which of the three the moratorium and the resulting guidelines actually embody, which they conspicuously do not, and what each reading makes of the fact that the actors were the researchers themselves rather than a public regulator. Then reverse the direction of illumination: what does the episode show about the principle -- does it vindicate precaution, embarrass it, or expose that the standard formulations are silent about self-imposed, science-internal restraint? Where the principle has nothing to say, say so instead of pressing it into service.
