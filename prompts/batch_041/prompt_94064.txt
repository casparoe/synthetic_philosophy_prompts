Right, so I'm putting together a small reading group on technology and politics, and I want a dialogue I can hand round at the first session. Could you write one between two people arguing about whether technical design choices are themselves political, or whether that's a category mistake and the politics all lives in how people use the things?

Make both of them sharp — I don't want one holding a stupid version of their view just so the other can knock it down. They should actually answer each other's points, concede where they're stung, and push back where they think the other's dodging. I'd rather they both be clever and end up in a genuinely awkward place than one of them win too easily.

To keep it from being the same old bridge-and-tomato-harvester stuff that everyone recycles, set it in a specific made-up case and have them argue through that. Here's the case: a public library is replacing most of its staffed checkout desks with self-service kiosks and an automated returns sorter. One of them thinks the very design of the kiosk-plus-sorter setup has a politics baked into it — it assumes and rewards a certain kind of user, it makes certain kinds of help impossible to even ask for, and it quietly reorganises who counts as staff and who counts as a machine-minder. The other thinks that's mystical hand-waving: the thing is just a thing, and if the library's management decides to cut jobs or restrict access, that's a decision about people and budgets, not about the metal and the code.

Let them use whatever machinery seems apt — the distinction between technologies that are *inherently* political (they only work if you arrange people a certain way) and ones that are merely *compatible* with some politics, the idea that a design carries a kind of 'script' telling its users how to behave, the point that the same object gets read differently in different settings. Just don't let it turn into a glossary recital; the ideas should earn their keep in the argument.

And don't let it end on a soggy 'well, it's complicated'. Someone should come out ahead, even if it's by a nose.

Then add a short separate note afterwards — different voice, not part of the dialogue — telling me which bits of this debate are pretty well settled among people who actually study it, and which bits are still genuinely contested. Don't dress up a contested position as if it were the consensus; I want to know where the real disagreement is.

British English throughout please. Cheers.
