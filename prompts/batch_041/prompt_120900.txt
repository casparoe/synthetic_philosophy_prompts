I am preparing a seminar that reads Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education alongside twentieth-century novels about schooling, and I want to close with Muriel Spark's The Prime of Miss Jean Brodie. The obvious pairing — Brodie as an anti-Lockean who substitutes charisma for method — strikes me as too quick, and I want a reading that takes both the treatise and the novel seriously enough to be unsettled by each.

Locke's text gives Brodie more than one might expect. He claims that "nine parts of ten are what they are, good or evil, useful or not, by their education"; he likens the child's mind to water that a gentle hand can turn into contrary channels; he stresses the tutor's personal influence and the early implanting of habits; and he treats the child's awe of the educator as the foundation of discipline. Brodie's creed — that an impressionable age is the whole of the game — is a crude version of this. Yet Locke also insists that virtue is the end of education and learning only its servant, warns against stuffing children with notions they cannot digest, and requires the tutor to be a model of sober self-command rather than a performer. There is also a detail I want the seminar to press on: Locke announces that his advice concerns the upbringing of a young gentleman and will not perfectly suit the education of daughters. Spark's novel is about the education of girls, conducted by a woman who has appointed herself their governor.

So: read The Prime of Miss Jean Brodie through Locke's views on education. Where does Brodie's practice genuinely answer to Locke's program — habit, emulation, reputation, the tutor's authority, the shaping of the will — and where does it invert or betray it? Which of Locke's recommendations illuminate the Brodie set, the girls' later lives, and Sandy's act of betrayal, and which of them does the novel expose as inadequate or dangerous? Point me to the scenes that matter for this reading. I am not looking for a verdict on Brodie; I am looking for a reading that is faithful in both directions, one that does not reduce the novel to an illustration of the treatise or flatten Locke into a generic foil for progressive education. End by saying what the novel dramatizes about the formation of character that Locke's treatise, for all its practical detail, leaves out.
