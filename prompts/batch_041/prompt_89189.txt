I am an undergraduate enrolled in a seminar on probability and the philosophy of science, and I am writing a short critical paper on an analogy that has been carrying a great deal of weight in the course. In the unit on objective chance, the instructor motivated the Principal Principle, understood as the requirement that your credence in an event match its objective chance absent information that is somehow inadmissible, by saying that objective chance is best conceived as a perfectly calibrated expert: an expert's credence is by definition the credence you ought to adopt, and chance stands to your credence the way such an expert's opinion does. To make the idea concrete, the instructor introduced a device the class has been calling the Chance Oracle: you name an event, it prints a single number, and we are told by stipulation that the number it prints just is the objective chance of that event. A classmate objected that the Oracle is merely the expert analogy rendered mechanical, and that whatever the Principal Principle gets right, the Oracle gets right as well.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than defend or attack the Principal Principle as such. Begin with an explicit correspondence, a table is fine, mapping what plays the role of the expert, the expert's credence, your own credence, the expert's information, and your information onto whatever occupies those roles in the chance case. Then identify where the mapping breaks: which features of the expert source the analogy quietly imports that objective chance may not possess, and which of those breaks genuinely matter for the use to which the analogy is being put, namely motivating the Principal Principle together with its admissibility caveat, as opposed to being incidental. I am especially interested in cases that seem to pull the analogy and the Principle apart: when you also hold evidence about which the chance setup is silent; when the chance you are told is the output of a deterministic process whose seed you also know; and when the same event has different chances relative to different setups. State what the expert analogy predicts in each case and what the Principal Principle with its caveat predicts, and say whether the analogy can be repaired or ought to be replaced. If you believe a different analogy does the motivating work better, name it and explain why. Finally, address directly whether the conclusion the analogy was invoked to support, that credence must defer to chance, survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Please write in American English.
