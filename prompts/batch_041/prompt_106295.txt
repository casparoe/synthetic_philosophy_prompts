I am putting together a proposal for a one-year research fellowship on collective guilt across generations, and I want to start from a suspicion about how the debate is framed. The question is usually posed as whether a nation or a people can be guilty, and the answers either defend a corporate agent capable of bearing guilt or dissolve the claim into the guilt of individuals; both sides assume that guilt-talk is backward-looking and must be anchored in something someone did. I want to develop and test the rival reading, that most ascriptions of intergenerational collective guilt are forward-looking commitments to repair wearing the grammar of blame, and that this reading handles the cases that embarrass both camps: guilt for wrongs committed before one existed, guilt that no act could discharge, and guilt that outlives every perpetrator. The target is the older contrast, associated with Jaspers's postwar lectures, between guilt for what one did and a broader responsibility borne by virtue of membership; I want to know whether that contrast can be made precise or is only a placeholder for the forward-looking reading.

Write the proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, and say what each of the two standard positions predicts about those three cases and where each gets at least one of them wrong. Set out the method: what distinction I need between guilt and a weaker notion such as liability or answerability, how I would make the forward-looking translation precise, and how I would test it against the cases rather than against intuitions about them. Say what a result would look like and what would count as the project's failing. I will supply the citations myself. Close with an honest scope judgment: is this a one-year project or is it a book?
