I retired about five years ago — I was a groundwater hydrologist, thirty-odd years of it — and since then I've been trying to give myself the philosophy education I never got, mostly game theory and ethics, with mixed results. Heres the thing I need help with.

I'm a member of our local water users association, small outfit, maybe sixty farms, and theyve asked me to give the after-dinner speech at the centenary dinner. Fine, except I've spent the last year quietly convinced that the way we all talk about our dry-year problem is wrong, and now I've got a captive audience and a microphone.

Here's the problem as everyone tells it. Every dry summer we agree that each farm will cut its pumping by some set amount, so the shallow aquifer doesn't get drained. But any single farm can quietly pump more than its share, get a bumper crop, and if enough farms do that the shallow aquifer drops and everyone who kept to the deal is worse off. The young guy who went to ag school calls this our prisoner's dilemma and says the answer is meters and fines. Everyone nods.

The more I read, though, the less I think that's what it is, or was. For most of a hundred years this worked with no fines at all, because we're all neighbors and we'll be dealing with each other for the rest of our lives and our kids will too — cheating got punished by the fact that you have to live with these people, not by any rule. And I keep reading that a lot of situations that look like a prisoner's dilemma are actually the other thing, the one where everybody is happy to cooperate as long as they're confident everyone else will, and the safe play is to hold back because you can't be sure. I think the book called it a stag hunt, I keep forgetting. If that's what ours is, then meters and fines are beside the point and what we actually need is something else — some way to make our mutual restraint visible and credible to each other. Which is a different speech than the one the ag-school kid wants me to give.

And then there's the wrinkle that started all this. In the last ten years three or four of the bigger farms put in deep boreholes down to a second, deeper aquifer. Now those farms can pump what they like and not care much if the shallow one drops, so for them the temptation to cheat is real in a way it wasn't before — its like the ground shifted under us and some of us are now playing a different game than the rest of us. And last year we started a little trading scheme where a farm can sell its unused allocation to another farm, and I honestly can't tell if that helps or makes it worse.

So: can you write me the speech. Six or seven minutes, 900 words or so, warm, funny where it can be — this is a birthday party, not an intervention — and a couple of the deep-borehole farmers will be sitting right in front of me so I can't make it about them. What I want the speech to do is make the whole room feel the difference between the two kinds of situation, the one where the problem is temptation and the one where the problem is mistrust, without using a word of jargon, because most of the room has never heard any of this and would resent being lectured. And I want that distinction to actually decide the advice: if we're in the temptation kind of problem then the association should do one thing, and if we're in the mistrust kind it should do something else entirely, and the speech should come down on one side and say why, using our own history and the boreholes and the trading scheme as its evidence. If you think I'm wrong that it ever changed, and it was the temptation kind all along, then say so and write the speech that way. But if the right answer genuinely hinges on something we don't know yet, I'd much rather the speech admit that honestly than paper it over with a confident ending. And please don't put any citations or reading lists in it — the audience is farmers, and I'm the one who's supposed to have done the reading.
