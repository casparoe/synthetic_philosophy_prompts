Two speakers at a seminar I attended recently got into an extended argument about what moral foundations research — the Haidt-style framework in which moral judgment is driven largely by fast intuition, with conscious reasoning arriving afterward to justify a verdict already reached, and in which people differ systematically in how heavily they weight different foundations, with the political left weighting care and fairness more and the right weighting loyalty, authority, and sanctity more — implies for the question of who is morally right. The exchange was substantive on both sides, and I have been unable to settle it in my own mind. I would like you to adjudicate it move by move rather than summarize the literature. The speakers are not me, and I have no stake in which of them prevails.

Speaker A: "If reasoning is mostly post hoc, then the whole enterprise of arguing about which moral view is correct is a rationalization machine. When we ask people to justify their verdicts, we get reasons that played no causal role in producing them; press them far enough and they run out of reasons while keeping the verdict. Whatever else philosophy is doing, it is not discovering moral truths."

Speaker B: "That is a genetic fallacy. Grant every word about the causes of moral belief — causes do not fix validity. If a belief issues from a reliable process, it can be true, and the question is whether the process is reliable, not whether it feels like reasoning. Besides, your psychological claim is self-undermining: if intuitive conviction is what drives everyone's conclusions, it drives yours."

Speaker A: "The framework is an empirical theory with evidence behind it, so the symmetry you want is not available. And note that the asymmetry cuts the other way. My claim is descriptive: it is about how minds actually work, and I will defend it with data and inference. Yours is normative. Nothing about how minds work tells you which verdicts are correct."

Speaker B: "You are conflating the origin of a state with its epistemic status. Take two people who both believe that cruelty to animals is wrong: one arrives there by argument, the other by immediate visceral recoil. We do not automatically discount the second. Emotional reactions can be detection rather than distortion. Contrast the cases where we do discount — a belief caused by a brain lesion, or by hypnotic suggestion. What makes those cases bad is that the cause is epistemically irrelevant to the content of the belief. So you owe an argument that emotion is irrelevant to moral content in that way, and 'it is non-inferential' will not do, because perception is non-inferential too."

Speaker A: "Then you owe the converse: an account of why these particular reactions track anything. The foundations are presented as contingent products of evolutionary and cultural history — disgust reactions calibrated to local purity norms, loyalty calibrated to whichever group you happened to be born into. You want them to be perceptual organs for moral facts, but the theory's own explanation of where they come from is precisely what makes that hard to believe. And notice that the framework's pluralism is itself normative: treating all foundations as equally legitimate, and treating any ranking among them as parochial, is a substantive moral stance smuggled in as a finding. If the psychology cannot help itself to normative premises, it cannot debunk a normative conclusion."

Speaker B: "That last point cuts against you rather than me. If the framework cannot reach normative conclusions, it cannot reach the conclusion that argument is pointless either. What it can show is that some arguments are confabulated — fine. It cannot show that argument is powerless, only that some of it is post hoc. You are sliding from 'often' to 'always.' And the disagreement-among-experts argument proves too much: philosophers of physics disagree with one another, and nobody concludes that physics has no truths."

Speaker A: "The disagreement is different in kind. Scientific disagreement gets resolved as evidence accumulates. Moral disagreement persists, and it correlates with temperament and political tribe — which is what you would predict if verdicts are downstream of intuition and post hoc rationalization, and not what you would predict if argument were tracking independent truths. Even if argument moves people at the margins, moving them is not the same as getting them right."

Speaker B: "Correlation with temperament is not debunking either. Mathematical conviction correlates with training. A process can be influenced by irrelevant factors and still be truth-tracking overall. And on the practical question: the researchers in this tradition persuade by argument, and no normative recommendation can be extracted from the framework without treating some reasoning as probative."

Please do the following. First, rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Second, identify exactly where the exchange stops being a verbal dispute and becomes a genuine one — that is, say which of Speaker A's claims, if true, would actually damage the proposition that argument can settle moral questions, and which of them are fully compatible with it. Third, say what both speakers are missing, including what the empirical framework can and cannot establish on its own. Fourth, give a verdict on the underlying question: does the psychology of left-right moral difference tell us anything about who is morally right, and if so, precisely what? Write your answer in American English.
