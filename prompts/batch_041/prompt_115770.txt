A professor I don't otherwise know much about gave a lecture I sat in on, and I wrote down her summary of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem pretty much as she said it. Here it is:

'Gibbard–Satterthwaite is basically Arrow's impossibility theorem in the language of strategy. Take any election with more than two candidates and any reasonable way of picking a single winner. Then either the method is dictatorial, or it can be gamed: some voter has a way to get a better result by not voting for their true favourite. So there's no fair voting rule that's also manipulation-proof — with three or more candidates strategic voting is unavoidable, and the only way out is to let one person's preferences decide everything. This is why real elections are such a mess: ranked-choice, approval, all of it can be gamed, and it's been proven that every method gets gamed by someone in every election.'

I believe the theorem is a real thing and I'm inclined to think teh conclusion is basically right, but a few of her moves felt like they were doing more work than the theorem actually licenses. Can you go claim by claim and tell me which parts a voting theorist would sign off on, which are fair simplifications, which are overstatements or conflations, and whether the stuff about real elections follows from the actual result? In particular I'm not sure what "dictatorial" means here, or how many escape routes there really are.
