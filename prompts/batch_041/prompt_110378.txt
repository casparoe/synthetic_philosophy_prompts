ok so I'm a first-year grad student and I've somehow ended up as the ball in a tennis match between two early modernists in my dept who've been feuding since before I was born, and I definately need to see the actual shape of the fight instead of the two ventriloquised versions I get in office hours.

Here's what they fight about. There's one village whose records survive absurdly well — a parish priest's notebook with about forty years of grain loans in it, plus a couple hundred debt suits from the local seigneurial court — and Prof. A has basically built her career on it. What she finds there is a place thick with credit, buying and selling constantly, women doing most of the lending, and households that the crown's tax rolls write down in the columns reserved for day-labourers and paupers clearly doing business on a scale those labels can't hold. Her line is that the clerks who made those registers weren't describing a village, they were describing what they needed to know in order to tax one. She calls the picture you get out of the registers the ledger's-eye view, and she says your average peasant is a paper village — nobody ever lived there, it's an artefact of adding up numbers that were collected for someone else's purposes and then dividing. Her old supervisor used to say that the state's archives are a map of the state's anxieties, not of the countryside.

Prof. B runs a big project on the same province: about eighty thousand household assessments, coded, machine-readable, three centuries of them. He says fine, one clerk was sloppy, but the reason that village has two hundred debt suits is that it was unusually litigious and unusually commercial, which is precisely why anybody bothered to keep its papers — you've selected your case on the outcome and then announced that the outcome is typical. He isn't a villain about it, he'll grant that his categories are inherited from the fisc and that this is a real limitation, and he thinks the only reason you can even notice her village is a weird one is that you have his eighty thousand rows to be weird against. His view is that a case can falsify a claim that something never happens but not a claim that it's rare, and that most of what historians assert is the second kind.

That's the fight as I've got it and I know I'm missing most of it. So: write me the dialogue. A few exchanges each, and they should actually take up each other's points rather than doing duelling monologues. Neither should be the idiot — A is not a romantic who thinks counting is fascism, B is not a positivist who thinks a standard deviation is a causal mechanism. Let each of them land at least one blow the other has to concede something to. Things I'd like them to get into, at whatever length each earns: what a single case can and can't refute; whether an average is a real object when the distribution underneath it is lumpy or bimodal; whether the state's paperwork actually creates the categories it records, so that people end up describing themselves the way the clerk wrote them down (I think that's either A's best card or B's, and I want to see them fight over it); whether explaining something means subsuming it under a regularity or reconstructing a chain of events that could have gone otherwise; and whether history from below is even possible when the people below mostly didn't write, so that you end up reading the poor through the courts that were fining them.

Finish with each of them saying what evidence would actually move them, and then with one of them asking the other whether there's a version of this where they're just reading the same facts differently — I genuinely want to know whether this is a substantive disagreement or two people who can't stand each other. Don't have them cite anything or name real historians at me, I can find the literature myself, I want the argument itself. Around 1200 words.
