A friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about whether we ought to replace elected legislators with randomly selected citizen assemblies, and the argument kept collapsing back onto a single analogy that he treats as decisive and that I have seen echoed in a good deal of the writing on sortition: the jury. His version of it runs roughly like this. We do not insist that the people who decide guilt or innocence be professionals, nor that they be elected, nor that they make a career of the task; we pull twelve people off the street at random, hand them the evidence, let them deliberate, and trust the verdict. If we are willing to trust ordinary people with a fellow citizen's liberty on the strength of a random draw, he says, then there is no principled reason to reserve lawmaking for a class of career politicians, and the very fact that we deliberately avoid professional jurors is itself an argument for sortition -- and, he adds, for term limits, since nobody thinks a juror should keep returning term after term. I found myself agreeing with the picture while distrusting the conclusion, and I could not say precisely where the two came apart.

What I want is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on sortition. Lay out the correspondence explicitly -- what plays the role of the random selection, the jury pool, the charge to the jury, the judge, the rules of evidence, the deliberation, the verdict, the appeal, and the fact that the verdict is binding and final -- and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. Some of the differences strike me as obvious but perhaps irrelevant: a jury decides a single binary question, whereas an assembly would set much of its own agenda and face open-ended questions of value rather than a supplied charge; the jury is a check on the state, whereas a citizen assembly would itself be part of the state; twelve people against a hundred. Others strike me as more dangerous to his use of the analogy: the jury operates under instructions it did not write and cannot rewrite, its verdict is subject to review, and the whole institution rests on a legal framework the jurors are not asked to redesign. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the work he wants the analogy to do from the ones that do not, tell me which features of juries the analogy quietly imports that a legislature lacks, and say whether his conclusion -- drop the career politicians, embrace sortition and rotation -- actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If there is a better analogy for the same point, I would like to hear it.
