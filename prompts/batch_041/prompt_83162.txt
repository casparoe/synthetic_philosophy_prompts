I am an undergraduate representative on my university art museum's acquisitions advisory committee, and I am also taking a course on the philosophy of art this term, which is why the committee's current business has turned into a problem I cannot stop thinking about. The committee must vote at its next meeting on whether to accept a donation from the estate of a recently deceased artist, and I have been asked to circulate the written recommendation beforehand. I would like you to draft that memo.

The donation consists of a printed set of instructions, a signed certificate of authenticity, and no object of any kind. The instructions describe a work that may be carried out only by museum staff, in one designated room, on a single day, after which every material trace of the realization must be destroyed and no photographic or other record kept; the text addresses whoever carries it out and leaves open that it may never be carried out at all. The estate's terms add three conditions: the work may never be realized without the estate's written approval; the instructions and the certificate must be displayed together in a case; and the museum must pledge a conservation budget to the paper. The estate describes the certificate as the work's only physical component. Our director objects that we would be acquiring something that has never existed and may never exist, that this is a category mistake dressed as generosity, and that donors of ordinary paintings would be justified in finding the acquisition unserious. Our curator replies that we already hold prints, photographs, and digital files, which exist as instructions to be realized on demand, so the only novelty here is that the number of authorized realizations may be zero. The case would also occupy the new wing's only display case, and the conservation pledge would consume most of this year's acquisitions budget.

There is a further complication. A student collective has announced that it intends to realize the work at the opening of the new wing in six weeks, without the estate's approval and with cameras, arguing that a work consisting of instructions belongs to whoever carries them out. The committee has to decide whether to permit this, ignore it, or prevent it.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action on the donation and on the students' plan, with a time frame; state criteria the committee can apply both to this decision and to the instruction-based donations we expect to be offered in coming years; explain what the museum would actually be acquiring, and what preserving it would amount to, given that nothing may survive a realization; and treat the director's objection as the serious position it is rather than dismissing it, including what would have to be true for it to be the right one. The disagreement on the committee is not really about money or wall space, so the reasoning has to settle what an artwork is and what it means for a museum to own one. No citations are needed; the argument should stand on its own, and I will add references myself if anyone asks.
