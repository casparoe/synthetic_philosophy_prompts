I have no philosophical training and have been reading John Stuart Mill on my own, mostly out of puzzlement over how a man who spent most of his working life in the East India Company could also write the most famous defense of individual liberty in English. The passage I keep getting stuck on is the one near the start of On Liberty where Mill limits his own principle before he has finished stating it. He says, in effect, that despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, so long as the end is their improvement and the means are justified by actually bringing that end about; and he adds that liberty, as a principle, has no application to any state of things anterior to the time when mankind have become capable of being improved by free and equal discussion. A related sentence from his Thoughts on Parliamentary Reform reads: "That power should be exercised over any portion of mankind without any obligation of consulting them, is only tolerable while they are in an infantine, or a semi-barbarous state. In any civilized condition, power ought never to be exempt from the necessity of appealing to the reason, and recommending itself by motives which justify it to the conscience and feelings, of the governed."

What I cannot work out is what kind of claim the first passage is making. Three readings seem available, and I cannot decide between them or whether they can be combined. First, it is an empirical claim about a stage of development: some peoples really are not yet capable of being moved by argument, and until they are, force is the only thing that works, so the harm principle simply does not yet apply to them. Second, it is a claim about justification rather than capacity: even when force is used it must answer to the same ultimate standard as everything else in Mill, namely whether it does good, so the label barbarian licenses nothing in particular and merely names the cases where the utilitarian calculus happens to favor coercion. Third, it is an expedient exception Mill needs for political reasons, and it sits badly with the rest of the essay.

I would like help with four things. Which reading does the passage best support, and what clue in the wording or structure decides it? What work is the idea of improvement doing here — improvement in whose judgment, measured how, and decided by whom, given that the whole point of the liberty principle was that no one gets to determine another person's good for him? Is the requirement that the means be justified by actually effecting that end a real constraint or an empty one, since any ruler can claim his rule is working? And what does the passage imply for a case like a colonial administration that sincerely claims to be educating a subject population toward self-government while that population rejects both the claim and the timetable — does Mill's principle say anything about when the despotism is supposed to end, or does it leave that entirely to the despot's own say-so?
