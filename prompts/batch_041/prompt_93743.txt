I am a transactional lawyer, twelve years in mergers and acquisitions, and I have spent the past several months on the indemnity and successor-liability provisions of a deal in which my client is buying a manufacturer whose predecessor entity operated a facility that poisoned a river in the 1970s. That is the proximate cause of the following. I have been arguing, fluently and for money, about who bears responsibility for a wrong committed by people who are dead, whether a newly formed entity can inherit the liabilities of an old one, and which obligations survive the dissolution of the party that incurred them. Three weeks ago a friend and I got into it at dinner over whether a nation can be guilty of crimes committed generations ago, and whether my daughter, who was born here, is implicated in things she did not do. Somewhere in the middle of it I realized I had been answering her in the only vocabulary I actually have, and that I could not tell whether I was translating her question or quietly replacing it.

What I want from you is the translation, not a verdict about who is right.

Write as someone who is genuinely at home in both worlds: a legal scholar who works on corporate succession and entity liability and who also knows the philosophical literature on collective and inherited responsibility. Take the position as it is usually stated by people who defend some notion of transgenerational national guilt, and treat the distinctions I sketch below as my gloss rather than as an exegesis of anyone's text. The debate usually separates: (a) criminal guilt, where you did something the law forbids and a court can convict you of it; (b) political guilt, which attaches to every member of a polity for what the polity did, regardless of what you personally did or could have done; (c) moral guilt, which is yours before your own conscience for your own acts and omissions; and (d) something harder, usually called metaphysical guilt, which is not answerable to a court or to one's own conscience but to something else, and which is said to attach merely for having survived and for having failed in solidarity with those who did not. I have absorbed this scheme from Jaspers' writing on German guilt after the war; do not treat him as an authority to be summarized, and do not assume my gloss is faithful.

Now translate each of those, as faithfully as you can, into the vocabulary I actually use: legal personality and its separateness from the persons who constitute the entity; successor liability and its exceptions, such as de facto merger, mere continuation, and fraudulent transfer; piercing the corporate veil; contractual indemnity, representations and warranties, and the survival periods attached to them; assumption and assignment; dissolution and the distribution of residual assets; the limits imposed by statutes of limitation and repose; and, if your vocabulary contains anything that plays the role, an obligation that cannot be discharged.

For each of the four, give me three things: what translates cleanly, what translates only if something is distorted on the way across, and what has no counterpart at all in my language. Then the part I actually care about — the residue report. When a piece of the moral position cannot be expressed in the idiom of successor liability, is that a defect in my framework, a sign that corporate law simply lacks a concept for a kind of responsibility that is real? Or is it a sign that the moral claim was never as determinate as it sounded, and that the lawyers' vocabulary exposes this rather than missing it? Push in the other direction as well: is there anything in my vocabulary that has no counterpart on the moral side, some distinction the dinner-table combatants keep missing because they do not have it?

Three constraints. First, do not smuggle a moral conclusion in under legal clothing. If a doctrine in my vocabulary already presupposes a normative choice — limited liability, the running of a limitations period, the priority of recorded claims — say so plainly instead of presenting it as a neutral translation device. Second, resist the pull of the corporate analogy where it stops earning its keep; the interesting output is where it breaks, not a tidy one-to-one table. Third, I am a lawyer and not a philosopher, so do not assume I know the literature, but do not talk down to me either.

Think this through carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming: lay out the candidate correspondences between the two vocabularies, including the strained ones, and then prune them, saying what you discarded and why. In the final answer, keep what is settled separate from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area. Where there is a consensus, state it as one; where there is not, present the live disagreement as a disagreement and do not dress a contested view up as the consensus. Where you are unsure what a doctrine actually provides, or unsure how a philosophical position is best formulated, flag the uncertainty rather than bluffing. I will notice.
