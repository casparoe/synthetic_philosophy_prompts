I have no training in philosophy, but I have been turning over on my own the question of when a bunch of smaller things add up to a single bigger thing — when there is really one object there, and when there are only several objects that happen to be near each other. Here is what I find myself wanting to say. A wristwatch is one object, and so is a person, and so is a living hand, which is part of the person; a hand cut off and lying on a table is not a hand at all, only meat. A stack of bricks in a yard is just bricks, but once they are mortared into a wall I want to call the wall one thing. A heap of sand is just grains, though I cannot say how many grains it would take to make it one thing. A cloud is one thing to me, and so is a whirlpool in a stream and a puddle standing in a pothole, even though nothing in them is fastened to anything and the water in them is gone within seconds. A forest, though, is just trees — I resist calling it an object, even though forests are named and burn as a unit. A crowd of strangers on a platform is just people, but the same people in a marching band, or a flock of starlings turning as one dark sheet, seem to me one thing doing something. A sandwich is one thing; a pile of laundry is not. And a hurricane is one thing, with a name and a track — as, I notice, are a chess game, a marriage, and a Wikipedia article edited by hundreds of people, none of which is made of anything in particular. I keep going back and forth on the cloud and the forest, because I answer differently about two cases that look to me like the same kind of case.

Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that captures what I have said? State each precisely, say which of my verdicts each is built to save, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then tell me what the surviving principle predicts for cases I have not listed — a tornado, a traffic jam, a solar system, a lava-lamp blob that pinches into two, a corporation, a rainbow, and a corpse — and finish by naming the one verdict of mine I should give up, and why.
