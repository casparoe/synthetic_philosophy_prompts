I'm putting together a problem set for a grad seminar on probabilistic updating, and I want one problem where "update on the conditional" splits three ways on the same numbers, so nobody can just write "condition on it" and move on.

Keep the Judy Benjamin skeleton — four cells, a conditional message whose antecedent you don't know, no way to plain-conditionalize on it — but swap out the Red/Blue story for something else (a lab tech telling you which of two machines made a part, a doctor, a mechanic, whatever), as long as the four cells are real and the message is a sentence of the form "if A then B" that the source puts some probability x on, with x less than 1, and where you don't know whether A is true.

Constraints on the numbers:
- four cells only, prior written out as exact fractions or decimals, every cell strictly positive, and the prior not uniform — I want the three rules to actually have to work for their answers;
- x must not equal the prior P(B|A) and must not equal the prior P(¬A∨B), otherwise one of the rules will do nothing and the whole thing goes flat;
- no rule should be forced to put 0 on a cell that had positive prior.

Then give the posterior three ways, arithmetic shown:

(a) read the message as being about the conditional probability: set P'(B|A) = x, hold P'(B|¬A) at its prior value, leave P(A) alone. (I've seen this called Adams conditioning but I'm not sure thats the standard label.)

(b) read it as being about the material conditional as an event: the sentence says ¬A∨B has probability x, so Jeffrey-conditionalize on the partition {¬A∨B, A∧¬B}.

(c) same conditional-probability reading as (a), but get the posterior by minimizing KL divergence from the prior subject to P'(B|A) = x, instead of holding the other branch fixed.

Then:
- confirm the three really do come apart, and say which cell's prior probability is doing the most work in the split;
- I'm fairly sure that if the source becomes perfectly reliable (x = 1) two of the three collapse into each other and the third doesn't. Say which two and why, then redo the arithmetic in that limiting case;
- at least two of the three should disagree about which of the four cells ends up most probable. Get all three to disagree if you can; if you can't, say why not;
- the honest bit at the end: which of the three you'd actually use if a student made you pick one, what further fact about the source's sentence (not about the world) would settle which reading it was meant to have, and which of the three a defender of the other two would call rigged — and what they'd say about your numbers.

Self-contained please: closed form for all three, no "just simulate it", and don't bother with references, I only want the case and the numbers.
