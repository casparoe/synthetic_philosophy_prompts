I'm putting together a little anthology of poems about metaphysics for a reading group thats mostly grad students in philosophy, and I want one about the endurantism/perdurantism split that actually earns its keep.

Write a pantoum — or a poem that keeps the pantoums rule, where the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third of the next — and make the recycled lines the whole point. Heres the conceit I'm after: a line that returns word-for-word is the endurantist's object, the very same thing wholly present again at a later time; a line that comes back with one word swapped is the perdurantist's, a numerically distinct temporal part that merely resembles the last one. So I want the poem to do both kinds of recycling, and to let the difference between them carry the argument instead of stating it. Somewhere in there the temporary-intrinsics problem should be palpable — the thing thats bent at one moment and straight at another, and cant be both at once if its wholly there both times — without the poem ever sounding like a textbook walking you through it. The ending should land on whichever side the form itself pushes toward, or leave me honestly unsure, but it cant cheat.

20 to 28 lines, longer if the form really needs it. No name-dropping philosophers, no footnotes, and please don't explain the debate to me — I want to feel the disagreement in the repetition, not read a summary of it. If I handed it to someone whos spent a year on persistence they should nod, and if I handed it to my friend who does ethics they should still get it.
