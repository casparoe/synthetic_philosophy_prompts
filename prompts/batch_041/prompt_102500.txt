I'm drafting a short paper arguing for a deflationary line in the debate over whether neural networks have representations, and I'd like your comments. The gist is a distinction I think people conflate when they ask whether a model "represents" some feature: (a) whether an internal state carries information about that feature of the input, and (b) whether the model's downstream computation actually uses that state in a way that would track the feature across the relevant counterfactuals. I claim that most interpretability results — probing classifiers, sparse autoencoders, what's sometimes called the linear representation hypothesis — establish (a) but not (b), and that (b) is what a realist about representation actually needs. I also argue that the analogy frequently drawn between model "features" and the concepts of folk psychology is misleading, because folk-psychological attribution is answerable to the agent's own capacities for report, inference, and error-correction, and it isn't clear what the analogue of those capacities is in a single forward pass. My tentative conclusion is that the interesting question isn't "are there representations?" but "which notion of representation does any given result support?", and that the field would be better off with a plurality of explicitly relativized notions rather than one contested one.

Please tell me where this is inaccurate or unfair to the positions I'm targeting, whether the central (a)/(b) distinction is as clean as I make it, and which parts are genuinely interesting versus already common ground. Finally, tell me what evidence or argument would most likely change your verdict on my conclusion.
