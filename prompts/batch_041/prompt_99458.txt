Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about when knowledge attributions are true.

The first is a pragmatic encroacher of the subject-sensitive kind. She holds that "knows" has a single, context-independent meaning, but that whether a person knows depends in part on how much is at stake for that person: the same evidence can suffice for knowledge when little turns on being right and fail to suffice when much does. On her view it is knowledge itself, not merely our willingness to ascribe it, that shifts with the subject's practical situation.

The second is an epistemic contextualist. She holds that "knows" is context-sensitive: a sentence of the form "S knows that p" can express different propositions, carrying different standards, depending on the context of the person uttering it. On her view nothing about the subject's knowledge need change when the truth value of an attribution changes; what changes is the standard that the attributor's context supplies.

Build the argument around a 2×2 that crosses the subject's practical situation (little at stake / much at stake) with the attributor's practical situation (little at stake / much at stake). Fill in two of the cells with concrete, mundane detail rather than leaving them schematic.

- Case One — low-stakes subject, high-stakes attributor. Ines has solid but inconclusive evidence that the river will not crest before the weekend. If she is wrong, she loses some seedlings. Her neighbour Tomas is deciding whether to evacuate his mother from the flood plain; for him everything turns on the answer. Tomas says of Ines, "She knows the river will not crest."
- Case Two — high-stakes subject, low-stakes attributor. Priya holds the same proposition on evidence of the same quality. If she is wrong, her family's livelihood is ruined. A curious passerby, Lena, who has nothing at stake and is only making conversation, says of Priya, "She knows the river will not crest."

The two characters must disagree about whether Tomas's utterance is true and about whether Lena's is, and must show the reasoning that yields each verdict. Have them say what their views predict in the two remaining cells, and what the overall pattern of agreement and disagreement reveals about how their positions differ.

Then have them take up, within the same exchange:

1. What each view says about a subject who is in a high-stakes situation without realizing it — in particular, whether unawareness of the stakes can insulate a subject from the encroachment the first view predicts.
2. Whether Tomas's attribution is better described as false or as true but misleading on the first view, and how the pragmatic encroacher accounts for the intuition that Tomas has said something amiss.
3. Whether their disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and whether either position can afford to concede that it is verbal.

Constraints: The two are evenly matched; neither is a strawman, and neither merely restates her position in fresh words. Each reply should engage the point the other has just made. No appeals to authority, no citations, and no appeals to what most philosophers believe. Close with each character naming the single consideration she thinks the other's position cannot accommodate.
