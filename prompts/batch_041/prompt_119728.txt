I have no academic background and no training in this subject; I have simply read around it on my own for a long time, and I have talked myself into a position that I now hold with some confidence. I would like you to tell me what should move me off it.

The position concerns probability, and it is the one I associate with Keynes — both the book he wrote on the subject long before he became known as an economist, and the way he later wrote about uncertainty in economic life.

The claim comes in three parts. First, probability is not a measure of anyone's confidence; it is an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, a degree to which the evidence partially implies the hypothesis, and it is the same relation for everyone who grasps the evidence. Second, this relation need not be comparable. For some pairs of hypotheses there is no fact of the matter as to which the evidence supports more — not because we are ignorant of the comparison, but because the relation yields only a partial order; in such a case it can be rational to settle the question by caprice rather than by weighing. Third, probability is not the whole of the epistemic story. Two hypotheses may be equally probable while one rests on far more relevant evidence than the other, and the better-evidenced one is better founded; more evidence always increases this further quantity — call it the weight — even when the new evidence tells against the hypothesis and so lowers the probability.

The upshot I care about is that the standard picture of a rational agent — someone who carries a single, complete, comparable probability distribution over everything and acts by maximizing expected value against it — is not merely a convenient idealization of a messier reality. It is false, because it assumes a completeness of comparison that does not exist and ignores a dimension of epistemic quality that is not probability.

What I want is a map of what counts against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In particular:

The arguments that derive probability from preference alone — the ones showing that anyone whose choices fit together in certain ways can be represented as maximizing expected value against a personal probability function, and is exposed to a sure loss otherwise — do they refute the objective relation, or only show that it can be dispensed with? Is there a real difference between those two, and which do the arguments actually establish?

The argument from practice: if probabilities are sometimes incomparable, what is an agent supposed to do? Does the view have any consequence for action at all, or does it collapse into advice to decide arbitrarily whenever comparison fails — which is what people do regardless?

The reductionist objection to weight: is it not simply a second-order probability, or a reflection of how much one's estimate would move under further evidence? If it can be reduced, is the third part of the position empty? If it cannot, what does it add to what one should believe or do, over and above probability?

Whatever the experiments on how people treat ambiguous as against risky prospects show — do they support the reality of the distinction, or expose it as a bias?

Then tell me what a committed defender of the position would concede as counting against it, as opposed to what only a critic would say. Say whether the view predicts anything about disagreement and scientific inference that its rival does not.

Finally, be honest about insulation. Is there any state of the world, or any piece of reasoning, that would show the relation is not objective, or that comparison is always complete? If not, has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a defect here or something the position can live with?

What would move me most is either a demonstration that every case I would call incomparable is really a case where a comparison exists and I am merely ignorant of it, or a showing that an agent whose beliefs are only partially ordered can be trapped in the way the sure-loss arguments trap the incoherent. If either exists, tell me how strong it is.
