Right, so — I've been running a tiny philosophy-of-rationality podcast for a few years now, basically an excuse to get the sort of guests I used to argue with in LessWrong comment threads into a room and ask them things properly, and next month I'm interviewing a philosopher whose whole shtick is that diachronic rationality is a myth. Her line is roughly this: all the norms that actually bind you are norms on a single time-slice (or a single decision, or a single evaluation of a whole plan), and every money-pump argument against cyclic preferences gets its force by sneaking in an extra requirement — that you keep your preferences stable across time, or that you honour a policy you adopted earlier — which is itself not a requirement of rationality. So intransitive preferences are fine; what's irrational is being naive enough to let someone walk you round the loop.

She's good at this, and she'll happily spend twenty minutes on the mechanics if I let her, which I don't want — I want to actually pressure the view. Can you draft me nine or ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? Please make sure there's at least one question on the naive/sophisticated/resolute chooser distinction (does the agent fail to see the loop coming, see it and refuse the first step, or adopt a plan and stick to it?) and whether the pump needs naivety to bite at all; at least one on whether there's any distinct diachronic norm left standing on her view, or whether it all collapses into synchronic rationality applied to plans and precommitments; and at least one on what her view does to the diachronic Dutch book arguments people trot out to justify Bayesian conditionalization — if money pumps don't bind, does that whole story come apart too, and does she accept that cost?

Other things I'd love covered, in whatever order flows: whether the expected-utility axioms (transitivity, completeness) are meant to be normative, descriptive, or constitutive of preference in the first place, and what's left of them on her account; whether cyclic preferences are exploitable in ways that matter outside a literal pump — markets, evolutionary selection, repeated games, the person who just keeps making the small trade forever; the case where every individual step looks fine to you and you still end up somewhere awful, and whether she can still say anything was irrational there; what would actually count against her view, if anything; and the practical upshot — is this liberating (stop agonising about consistency) or deflationary (nothing normative left to say about the person who keeps changing her mind)?

Keep the questions in a register a smart non-philosopher listener can follow, but don't water down the content, the whole point is definately to get in the weeds.
