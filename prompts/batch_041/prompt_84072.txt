I have been trying to work out where I stand on free will, and I have found that the labels compatibilism and incompatibilism do nothing for me until I know which of my ordinary judgments they are meant to fit. So let me set the judgments down and ask you to find the principle behind them. A man hands over his wallet at knifepoint: he does not act freely, and I do not blame him for the money. A woman raised inside a cult from the age of three gives her savings to its leader: I cannot bring myself to hold her responsible, and the fact that her reasons are sincerely her own does not move me, because she never had the chance to become anyone else. A man whose brain a surgeon altered so that he now loves his job and never wants to leave: I do not hold him responsible for staying, though he endorses the choice and would make it again. A man whose character was formed the ordinary way, by parents and culture and a long series of small decisions: I do hold him responsible, even though I grant he did not choose his temperament, and I cannot say what the surgeon did that the parents did not except that the surgeon was quicker. A woman whose tastes were gradually reshaped by a recommendation algorithm she knows about and does not object to: responsible, I think. A kleptomaniac who steals and, on reflection, endorses the stealing: not responsible. A woman who acts on a desire a stranger implanted in her a moment ago, and who could have resisted had she wanted to: not responsible. A woman who acts from a desire entirely her own, but who would have been physically stopped by a hidden intervener had she tried to do otherwise: responsible. And someone who swallows a pill she chose herself and becomes more patient is responsible for the patience, whereas someone injected with the same pill in her sleep is not, so consent seems to matter, though I cannot say why, since the resulting character is the same either way.

I keep wanting to say that responsibility requires three things: that the action spring from the agent's own settled character, that nothing outside her push her into it at the moment, and that she would endorse that character on reflection. But the formula seems to get both the brain-surgery case and the kleptomaniac wrong, and I do not want to repair it by adding a condition on how the character was formed, since that would take the ordinary-upbringing case down with it. So I would like you to tell me what principle, or what small set of competing principles, accommodates as many of my verdicts as possible, where each clause of it is doing work for a particular case, whether the result is a compatibilist or an incompatibilist position once stated plainly, what it implies about cases I have not listed, and which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no concluding summary: first, the principle in its most defensible form; second, for each clause, the case of mine that forces it; third, whether the view is compatibilist or incompatibilist, and the sentence in it that settles the matter; fourth, the verdict it delivers on two cases I have not described, namely a person who acts badly out of ignorance she had no way of remedying, and a person who acts on a post-hypnotic suggestion she accepted while fully informed; and fifth, the one verdict of mine the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would keep the verdict or the principle.
