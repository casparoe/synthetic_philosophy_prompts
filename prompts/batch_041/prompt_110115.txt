I've been reading around modal epistemology for a seminar, and I keep running into the same move: someone says a certain scenario is conceivable, concludes it is possible, and then someone else says the scenario wasn't really conceivable in the relevant sense, or that possibility and conceivability are different sorts of thing altogether. Before I read any more defenses of the link, I want the specification first — the list of requirements that any principle connecting conceivability to possibility would have to satisfy to deserve the name. Please write that list. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. I especially want the cases that pull in opposite directions: the ones where something seems conceivable but is arguably impossible (a world whose physics leaves water as something other than H2O; a world where one and the same person occupies two places at once; a scenario I can describe in words but cannot form any model of), and the ones where something was long held inconceivable and then turned out to be perfectly possible — I have in mind the way consistent alternatives to Euclid's parallel postulate once struck people as unthinkable, which makes me doubt that inconceivability is any guide to impossibility at all. Then there are the distinctions the principle has to respect rather than collapse: merely being able to form a mental image versus being able to entertain the proposition without contradiction, conceivability for a particular thinker versus conceivability for an idealized one, and the difference between failing to detect a contradiction and positively grasping a coherent model. Any principle that ignores the gap between these is likely to be trivially true on one reading and obviously false on the other, and a real desideratum should say why the gap does not sink the whole project.

I'd also like you to flag which requirements look jointly unsatisfiable, because I suspect at least one pair is: on one hand the principle must be substantive enough to license particular possibility claims, and on the other it must survive the cases where conceivability is demonstrably unreliable; and it must connect a psychological notion (what a mind can or cannot do) to a metaphysical one (what the world could have been) without either psychologizing possibility or idealizing the conceiver into a being so unlike us that the notion of conceivability no longer applies. If you think some subset of the requirements cannot be met together, say so plainly — that would be the most useful part of the answer. I would rather see the criteria worked out, with the case behind each, than a scorecard; but if you can briefly indicate which single requirement does the most to eliminate the leading positions — the broadly rationalist line, the broadly empiricist line, and the one that treats the a posteriori necessary as the central test case — that would help me see the shape of the terrain.

Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present any one camp's resolution as the consensus. Where a claim depends on a contestable reading of a particular case, mark it as such rather than stating it flatly.
