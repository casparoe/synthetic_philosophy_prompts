I do macro forecasting at a central bank, and I keep getting asked to sign off on unconditional forecasts out to 2050, and I keep getting a bad feeling about it. Someone told me to read Popper on historicism and it landed, but I read philosophy the way I read a spec — I get the gist and loose the fine print. So I want you to take Popper's case against there being laws of history and restate it in the vocabulary I actually work in.

My vocab: I'm comfortable with time-series econometrics. Stationarity and unit roots, cointegration, structural breaks, regime-switching, VARs, identification, exogenous vs endogenous, conditional vs unconditional forecasts, out-of-sample evaluation, overfitting, the Lucas critique, potential outcomes and do-calculus, and I can follow a bit of Bayesian model averaging. I think in terms of "there is a data-generating process, maybe a time-varying one, and my job is to get as close to it as I can." I'm not a philospher and I don't want the Wikipedia summary.

As I understand it Popper runs several different arguments and I want each one translated separately, because I suspect they don't all fare the same:
- that the course of history is heavily driven by the growth of knowledge, and you can't predict the future growth of knowledge, so you can't predict the course of history;
- that a prediction can feed back and change the thing it predicts (he had a name for this, Oedipus something);
- that a trend is not a law — you can't read a law of development off an observed trend;
- and his political point about utopian vs piecemeal engineering.

For each: what does it become in my terms, as faithfully as you can, without smuggling in econometric assumptions and calling that a translation. I especially want to know whether "growth of knowledge is unpredictable" is just the claim that the DGP is non-stationary in some particular way (in which case fine, we have tools, it's an empirical matter) or something my framework can't even state.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. What doesn't survive the translation? Which of his claims have no counterpart in my vocab at all? And for those, tell me whether the gap is a defect of my framework — something econometrics is blind to — or a sign that Popper's claim was vaguer than it sounded, so the translation fails because there was nothing precise there to translate. I have a suspicion it's the second, and I want you to tell me if I'm wrong.

Format:
- "Bottom line" — five sentences or so on whether his case survives translation into my vocab.
- "The translation" — one short paragraph per argument, in my terms.
- "Where I had to fudge it" — the translations that only work with distortion, with the distortion named.
- "What didn't survive" — the residue, and for each piece of it, whether it's my framework's blind spot or his vagueness.
- "Verdict" — one paragraph on whether I should feel bad about signing off on that 2050 forecast.

Keep it in the weeds. Don't tell me Popper was right or wrong in general — I only care what my framework can and can't express.
