I am preparing a one-page diagnostic handout for the first meeting of a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and I want it to sort participants among the live positions on the reactive attitudes rather than offer a menu of labels they can nod along to. Please build it as a decision tree: the reader begins from a commitment she can recognise in herself, answers one question at each node, and arrives at a named position.

Give me the tree as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction, no closing summary, and no further reading. Each numbered item is one node, in the order the reader meets it: state the question, the answers available at that node, and the position or positions each answer leads to, each position carrying a short label. Where two positions cannot be separated by any branch of your tree, or where a combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, say so within the item where the tree runs into the trouble, and diagnose the gap in a phrase — incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

The five nodes should be the questions on which these views genuinely come apart, and each must be answerable by someone who does not yet know which position it leads to. Construct the tree out of the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — and place the positions in it afterwards.

The tree must be able to place all of the following views as leaves, though not necessarily one leaf apiece; where your tree cannot prise two of them apart, say so rather than giving them a leaf each: the view that the practice of holding one another responsible stands in no need of justification from outside itself and that the demand for one is the error; the view that it does need one and that determinism would defeat it; the view that it needs one and that determinism leaves it untouched; the distinction between responsibility as attributability and responsibility as accountability, together with the three-way version that adds answerability; the view that apt blame requires only that the wrongdoer could recognise the moral reason she flouted; the view that it requires in addition that she could have been moved by it; the view on which blaming is second-personal address, a demand made to a particular person who owes an answer; the relational view on which blame registers a relationship already impaired; the conversational view on which blame is a move in an exchange the wrongdoer can take up; the hard incompatibilist who would keep much of our blaming vocabulary while replacing resentment with a non-reactive sorrow; and the revisionist who holds that our practices are warranted as they stand and yet open to deliberate revision.

At least three of the five nodes should turn on a concrete case rather than a general principle, and the cases must include: a wrongdoer whose cruelty is the upshot of a childhood he neither chose nor could escape, paired with a wrongdoer of identical character whose cruelty has an ordinary history, so that a node can ask whether the difference in history should make any difference to our attitudes; an agent who can recite the moral reasons but is not moved by them; and a bystander's indignation at a wrong done to a stranger, where the question is whether that attitude has the standing of the victim's resentment. The tree should also be able to say whether the personal attitudes, the impersonal ones, and the self-directed ones come apart — that is, whether a view that gives up resentment is thereby committed to giving up indignation or guilt.

Do not quote from any text, and do not attribute a claim to a named author unless the name is itself the standard label for the view; the seminar can supply the names.
