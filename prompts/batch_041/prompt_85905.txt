Consider the following situation. A theory T is proposed and accepted largely on the strength of a body of data that it was constructed to accommodate. Some years later, a long and difficult derivation establishes that T, together with accepted background assumptions, entails a striking empirical regularity R: a pattern that had been recorded decades earlier, that no one had ever managed to explain, and that some researchers had treated as an anomaly counting against T. Once the derivation is accepted, the judgment of the field is that R now constitutes strong evidence for T. Yet the standard Bayesian apparatus appears to say otherwise. R was already known, so its probability is 1; T entails R relative to the background, so P(R | T) = 1; conditioning on R therefore leaves the probability of T unchanged, and the belated discovery of the derivation appears to add nothing.

Explain the conceptual issue raised by this case, at a level of detail that goes beyond the bare observation that R cannot confirm T because its probability is already 1.

(a) Diagnose the source of the tension. Is it an artifact of treating confirmation as an increase in probability, an artifact of idealizing the agent as logically omniscient (one for whom every consequence of every hypothesis is already transparent), or something else? Explain what each diagnosis commits one to, and what it would have to say about the intuition that the derivation genuinely strengthens T.

(b) Explain and evaluate the principal responses: (i) conditioning not on R itself but on the proposition that R was learned, or on the historical fact of the discovery; (ii) locating the new evidence in the derivation, so that what is learned is that T entails R, or that a valid derivation exists; (iii) denying that anything has gone wrong, on the grounds that confirmation is a matter of likelihoods rather than of changes in probability. For each response, state what it must concede and what it succeeds in explaining.

(c) Consider the mirror-image case: a newly proposed theory T′ that entails the negation of a regularity which has been known and accepted for decades. The same reasoning implies that the old regularity cannot count against T′. Explain whether this consequence is acceptable, and what it implies for the diagnosis you gave in (a).

(d) State which of the responses in (b) you take to be most defensible, and justify that verdict.

Argue from first principles; citations are unnecessary.
