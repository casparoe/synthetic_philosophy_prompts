I am a third-year philosophy undergraduate, and I am currently taking a course on medieval philosophy of mind in which we have been reading Avicenna. The written assignment for the course is a short research proposal: a two-page prospectus for a term paper of roughly five thousand words, which must state a question, argue that it is genuinely open, set out how I would go about answering it, and give an honest account of what would count as success and what as failure. The prospectus does not need citations; I will add those when I write the paper.

My half-formed idea concerns the flying man. A person is supposed to be created in a single instant, fully formed and in full possession of his faculties, suspended in a void, receiving nothing through his senses and nothing from his own body, since his limbs are held apart and do not touch one another; and it is claimed that such a person would nonetheless affirm his own existence, while affirming nothing about his limbs, his organs, or anything external to him. The scenario is normally taken to establish something strong about the soul — that it is not a body, or that it is separable from the body. I suspect that the scenario, as described, cannot establish anything that strong by itself. What it appears to establish is a claim about how a subject is aware of himself rather than about what he is: that his awareness of himself is not reached by identifying anything bodily, and so cannot be overturned by any error about which body is his — the phenomenon contemporary philosophy of mind discusses as immunity to error through misidentification. If that is right, the usual reading needs a bridging premise from the mode of self-awareness to the nature of the self, and the interesting question is whether that premise can be motivated independently of the conclusion it is meant to deliver.

Turn this into a proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, rather than as a general enquiry into whether the argument works. Distinguish the candidate readings of the scenario — for instance, a reading on which it establishes what remains in the subject's field of awareness once bodily and external input is removed; a separability reading, on which it establishes what can be conceived as existing without the body; and a reading on which it establishes only a fact about the mode of self-awareness — and say what each would have to show and what would have to be true for it to succeed. Explain why these readings are usually run together rather than distinguished, and why that leaves the question open. Then give the method: reconstruct each reading as an explicit argument with numbered premises, test each for validity and for question-begging, identify which premise carries the weight, and press the two objections I would most need to answer — first, that the scenario is not merely unpersuasive but impossible, because a sense of one's own body is partly constitutive of self-awareness, so that the flying man describes no possible subject; second, that the argument trades on a slide from conceivability to real possibility. Add a brief treatment of intermediate cases, such as a subject who is aware of a body that does not feel like his, since these should discriminate between the readings. Say what would count as success and what as failure: my tentative success condition is that exactly one reading turns out valid with a premise that is independently defensible while the others fail for a diagnosable reason; my tentative failure condition is that every reading is either invalid or collapses into another. Finally, be realistic about scope. One term and five thousand words should support an argument about the form of the argument and a small set of test cases, not a survey of Avicenna's psychology or of the later reception of the scenario.
