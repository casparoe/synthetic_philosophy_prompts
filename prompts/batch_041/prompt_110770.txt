I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I spent last weekend at a small interdisciplinary workshop where two attendees — I'll call them Halvorsen and Reyes — got into an argument I couldn't referee. The subject was the taxonomy that sorts minds into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures: the Darwinian creature's behavior is fixed by selection and it does not learn; the Skinnerian creature learns by reinforcement from what happens to it; the Popperian creature can run a candidate action as an internal simulation and let the simulation fail instead of the body; and the Gregorian creature is one equipped with thinking tools — notation, procedures, external memory — that it absorbs from other minds. Halvorsen and Reyes both knew the framework cold, and neither was playing dumb, which is why their exchange was so hard to score.

Halvorsen opened by saying the four are genuinely four kinds, and that the Popperian–Gregorian line is the sharpest of the three joints. His reason: a Popperian creature tests hypotheses with machinery it grew, while a Gregorian creature tests them with machinery it borrowed, and that is not merely faster simulation — it changes where the boundary of the reasoner falls, so that you cannot draw the edge of the thinking at the skin.

Reyes replied that the grown-versus-borrowed distinction is unstable. When she multiplies on paper, the paper is doing something her unaided brain cannot, yet no one says the paper is part of her mind. If a Gregorian creature is just a Popperian creature with external memory attached, she said, then "Gregorian" names a technology rather than a kind of mind, and the fourth rung has quietly become sociology.

Halvorsen came back that the fourth rung was never about memory but about transmission: the tools are themselves the residue of other minds' simulations, handed on, so a Gregorian creature inherits hypotheses it could never have generated on its own. And the inheritance is cumulative — the tools improve without the brain improving. A Popperian creature gets better only by getting a better brain; a Gregorian one gets better while its brain stays put. That, he insisted, is a difference in kind and not in degree.

Reyes granted the cumulativity and said it actually sinks the ladder. If a Gregorian creature can improve with no neural change, then the ordering is not a progression of minds at all, because the first three rungs classify a creature by where its variation comes from — genes, reinforcement, simulation — while the fourth classifies it by where the simulation's inputs come from. That is two different axes wearing one ladder's clothes. She added that model-based reinforcement learning already blurs the Skinnerian–Popperian line, since an agent that fits a predictive model from reward is doing both at once, and no one thinks it has thereby climbed a rung.

Halvorsen conceded the axes objection as a complaint about a strict ladder but not about the kinds, and said the reinforcement-learning case cuts his way: an agent with a learned world model is simply a Popperian creature implemented in silicon, which shows the kinds track mechanisms rather than substances. He then floated adding a fifth kind for systems whose simulations run partly outside them, on machinery they neither built nor can inspect, so that they cannot say which of their conclusions are their own. Reyes answered that this fifth kind is the Gregorian kind with the tools hidden, and that if hiddenness is what makes a kind, they are back to sorting by technology — at which point the exchange ended without either moving.

Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who has the better of it overall. Be candid about your own confidence and about the parts of this where a reasonable reader could land elsewhere — I would rather have a verdict with its soft edges showing than a tidy one. And if the framing I've given you is confused — if, say, the taxonomy is not properly read as a ladder at all, or I've misdescribed one of the four kinds in a way that makes the dispute ill-posed — say so plainly and tell me how you'd restate the question before answering it, instead of adjudicating a question I've botched.
