I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy working on a chapter about decision theory in cases where expected values fail to exist, and I have a compressed draft of an argument — closer to notes for an essay than a finished piece — that I would like you to comment on. My supervisor has read it and made encouraging but unhelpful noises, and I am due to present a version of it at a work-in-progress session in a fortnight, so I would rather discover now whether the central move survives contact with someone who will actually do the algebra. Please give me comments of three kinds: first, on accuracy, flagging any mathematical or conceptual misstep, however small, and any place where I have characterised the state of the debate in a way that a specialist would resist; second, on whether the central thesis is genuinely interesting or merely a restatement of the difficulty; and third, on the two questions I raise at the end. A polite summary would be of no use to me — I would rather be told where the argument is weakest. Please write in British English.

The draft runs as follows. Consider a fair coin tossed until it first lands heads; if heads appears on the nth toss, the player receives (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n divided by n, and nothing otherwise. Weighting each payoff by its probability, the terms collapse to the alternating harmonic series, which in the natural ordering of the tosses sums to ln 2, so a naive calculation gives the game a value of about 0.69. But the positive and negative parts of that series each diverge, so the expectation is not merely infinite but undefined, and a rearrangement of the payoffs can be made to yield any real value one likes. The game therefore occupies a peculiar position: it is not the St Petersburg game over again, since there the expectation is perfectly well defined, if infinite, whereas here there is no expectation to be had at all. Now the move I want to make. Introduce a small parameter: let the player's winnings be discounted by a fixed factor per round, or equivalently let there be a small probability that the game is interrupted before it finishes. For any positive value of that parameter the payoff series is absolutely convergent, so the expectation exists, and as the parameter vanishes the value tends to ln 2 — the same number the naive calculation produced, and the same number one obtains by Abel summation or by any other regular summation method. My thesis is that this continuity is the whole story: the game is a point of discontinuity in the map from games to values, and the correct thing to say about a point of discontinuity is not that the value is undefined, but that the value is the limit of the values of the games in a neighbourhood of it. Rational choice, on this view, is defined by limits of well-defined nearby problems, and the game is worth ln 2 because every nearby game is worth approximately ln 2. I also claim, and this is the first of my two questions, that every reasonable perturbation gives the same limit — truncating the game at N rounds, capping the payoffs, imposing a termination hazard — and that only the artificial perturbation of re-ordering the payoffs, which changes the game rather than the neighbourhood, can shift the limit. My second question concerns the standard escape route: as with St Petersburg, one might hope to bound the utility function, but that manoeuvre addresses the unboundedness of the payoffs, whereas the difficulty here is the conditional convergence of the series, so a bounded utility function leaves the problem untouched.

In addition to the general comments requested above, please tell me whether the claim about perturbations is true — if it is false, a counterexample would be worth more to me than a paragraph of agreement — and whether the final sentence is fair to the bounded-utility response, or whether I have conceded too little or too much to it. I am also aware that there is a body of work in which games with undefined expectations are compared with one another, on the thought that the difference of two such games may have a well-defined expectation even when neither has one on its own; if you think that approach makes my continuity thesis redundant, say so plainly.
