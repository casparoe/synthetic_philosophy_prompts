I've been reading the essay Wigner wrote about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, and rather than take it as one big thesis, I've been trying to sort out which particular applications of mathematics to the physical world actually strike me as unreasonable and which don't, because my reactions don't line up with the way the story is usually told. Riemannian geometry was worked out in the middle of the nineteenth century as a purely mathematical generalization of the idea of a geometry, with no physical target anywhere in view, and then turned out to be exactly the language general relativity needed — that one genuinely astonishes me, close to a miracle. Same with Dirac writing down a relativistic wave equation for the electron and getting antimatter out of it as a leftover solution, and with Wigner's own random-matrix statistics, cooked up to describe the spectra of complicated nuclei, matching the actual energy levels. But the SU(3) classification of hadrons, which let people point at a slot and say a particle would be found there before anyone had found it, doesn't impress me the same way: the representation theory is a complete classification that had been sitting around for decades, and once you've decided the particles fall into multiplets, hunting for the missing member is bookkeeping, not prophecy. Newton inventing the calculus to do mechanics doesn't strike me as unreasonable either — the mathematics was built for that job, and it would be stranger if it hadn't fit. A twentieth-degree polynomial threaded through twenty data points isn't unreasonable, it's a fitting machine; I work in software and spend my life worrying about exactly that kind of overfitting, so I refuse to be impressed by it. Ptolemy's epicycles and Kepler's nested Platonic solids aren't unreasonable either — they're the fitting machine wearing a costume.

So my rough principle is this: an application is unreasonable to the degree that the mathematics was developed for its own sake, with no empirical target in view, and then turned out to be not merely applicable but exact, in the sense that you couldn't have tuned it to fit after the fact. But I keep hitting cases the principle mishandles, and I'd like help seeing whether that's my principle's fault or my verdicts'. Fourier analysis was invented to solve the heat equation, so by my own rule it has an empirical target from birth and shouldn't count — yet I find it deeply unreasonable that the same series describing heat flow also describes signals, vibrations, and quantum states, and the reappearance in unrelated domains feels like the real miracle, not the original fit. Random matrices bother me in the opposite direction: they were built for a practical statistical purpose rather than for their own sake, and now the same spacing statistics turn up in the zeros of the zeta function and in eigenvalue spectra across wildly different systems, which starts to look less like a miracle and more like a universality result waiting to be proved — if the same distribution is generic, maybe nothing special is being explained at all. And I can't tell whether the plain old wave equation, the one governing a plucked string and also sound and also light and, up to a factor of i, the electron's wavefunction, is a fourth case of the same thing or just the same equation doing its job.

Test the principle against every case above, including the ones I'm torn on, and give me your prediction for at least one case I haven't mentioned.
