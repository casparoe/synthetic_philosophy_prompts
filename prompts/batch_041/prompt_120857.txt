Ok so I got into a 2am argument with my friend Dave about whether ethics makes any sense when the population is infinite, and I came away feeling like half of what I believe is quietly inconsistent, except I cant tell which half. Writing it out here because I want an actual audit, not reassurance. Heres my position:

(1) For finite populations I'm a total utilitarian. Sum of welfare, not average, not spread. Ten million people at 6 beats a thousand people at 9.

(2) Adding a life that's worth living makes the world better, even when the new person is a drop in the bucket. In the finite case, adding a happy person is always a small improvement.

(3) There shouldn't be one rule for finite populations and a different rule for infinite ones. Whatever the right evaluative rule is, it applies to both. I'd treat "infinite populations never arise" as a cop-out, not a solution.

(4) Infinite futures can still be compared. A future with infinitely many people flourishing beats a future with infinitely many people scraping by, even though both are endless and both have "infinitely much" welfare in them. I'd take the first every time and I don't think that's just a preference of mine.

(5) The ranking of worlds can't depend on bookkeeping. Which person I number first, how I list them, which ones I line up against which — none of that should change which world is better. Dave says you can take an endless list of numbers, shuffle it around, and get a different total, even a total of a completely different size. I don't know enough math to know if thats a real problem for me or a party trick.

(6) It would be monstrous to keep one person in agony for a day so that infinitely many other people are each a hair better off. No amount of aggregate good makes that ok.

Dave's line was that anyone who holds (1) and (3) has to accept the opposite of (6), that (4) and (5) are wishful thinking, and that (2) stops meaning anything the second you allow infinite populations, so the whole position is vibes with arithmetic sprinkled on top. I don't think he's right about all of it, but I also can't tell you which part he's got.

What I want: which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and how severe each real conflict is. Rank the real ones worst first. For each one, say what has to give and what it costs me to give it. Then tell me the single change that would clean up the most trouble at the lowest price. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing just the number of the commitment you'd drop, or \answer{none} if the set can survive intact.
