I am giving the retirement toast for my doctoral supervisor, a historical economist who has spent her career on Thomas Malthus—not on whether the arithmetic was sound, but on how we should classify a theory whose central prediction has not come true on the timescale we expected. The room will be split: one table of economists and historians who know the Malthusian trap, the demographic transition, and the difference between a falsified prediction and a merely postponed one; and another table of her family, who know only that she has spent forty years on a gloomy English clergyman. I want the toast to do real philosophical work without turning into a seminar, and I want it built around a distinction I have been calling the “settling-time” problem: a theory can be tested, fail to be confirmed, and yet not be refuted, if the mechanism it posits has a characteristic timescale longer than the period over which we have been watching.

The question I want the speech to leave the room with is whether “right but early” is a genuine epistemic category or just a rescue strategy that immunizes a theory from evidence—and what that means for how we honor a scholar whose life’s work may be neither vindicated nor defeated. Please write the toast (about five minutes). It should be warm and particular, not a lecture, but the philosophical distinction should shape what the speaker can and cannot claim; the experts should hear the argument, and the family should hear a tribute. No citations or bibliography—just the speech.
