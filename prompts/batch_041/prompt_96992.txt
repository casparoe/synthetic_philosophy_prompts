I'm in a cross-listed phil/econ seminar on rationality and choice, and we did two weeks on satisficing and bounded rationality — Simon, the whole thing about aspiration levels, the "satisficing isn't irrational, it's just a different standard" line the prof keeps repeating. Problem is I've been going through cases trying to figure out what I actually think, and my verdicts don't add up to anything I can state.

Here they are. My sister apartment-hunting with a lease deadline takes the first place that's decent and in budget and never sees the other thirty. Fully rational, I think — not a sad compromise, just rational. Now the same forty places, except they're all already on a spreadsheet in front of her, she reads twelve, hits one that's decent, closes the laptop. That feels worse to me somehow, even though she ends up in the identical apartment, and I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just me being a snob about effort. A chess engine that plays the first move it finds that wins a pawn instead of searching deeper: completely fine, nobody thinks the engine is irrational. A doctor who prescribes the treatment that's "usually fine" when a clearly better one is standard and would come to mind instantly: not fine. But I can't say why that differs from the chess engine, which is also leaving better moves on the table. A firm using a crude rule of thumb and beating rivals who do full optimization, because the environment is noisy and the optimizers overfit: I want to say the rule-users are rational, and then I notice I also want to say the optimizers are rational, which can't both be right. Someone whose bar is just "as good as what my friends have," so she keeps switching jobs and apartments and is never content: every individual choice clears her bar, so by the satisficing standard she never makes a mistake, but something has obviously gone wrong. And finally, a guy who maximizes very carefully but on probabilities he made up, versus a satisficer with good information who stops early — I think the careful maximizer is doing something worse, which feels backwards.

My tentative principle is that rational just means the best you could do given what thinking costs, with the cost of deliberation folded into the objective like any other cost. That saves most of the list, I think, but it makes the doctor and the friend-with-the-friends-bar come out fine, which I don't want, and it makes the spreadsheet case fine, which I really don't want, and it makes the origin of the aspiration level irrelevant, which I feel like it shouldn't be. Is there a principle that gets most of these, or do I have to pick which verdict to throw out? I'd rather lose the spreadsheet one than the friends-bar one, but tell me if that's even coherent. And say what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed.
