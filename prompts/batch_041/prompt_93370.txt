I'm a graduate student in political philosophy at work on a seminar paper about the ethics of health-care rationing, and I keep returning to the Oregon Health Plan of the late 1980s and early 1990s as the unavoidable case. As I understand it, Oregon sought a federal waiver to expand Medicaid to a much larger share of the working poor while explicitly limiting what the program would cover; a state Health Services Commission, after a long process of public hearings, produced a ranked list of condition-treatment pairs — not diseases, but treatment-for-a-condition — and the legislature then drew a line, with everything above it funded and everything below it not. The ranking procedure was initially built around the cost of producing a unit of health benefit, and that is what drew fire: disability advocates argued that a list ordered this way systematically devalues treatments that restore or maintain function for people who live with impairments, and Louis Sullivan, then Secretary of Health and Human Services, said of it that "Oregon's plan in substantial part values the life of a person with a disability less than the life of a person without a disability." The commission revised its method in response, and the list that finally took effect was, I am told, ordered by a measure of net benefit rather than by cost per unit of benefit — I would like you to tell me whether that change is theoretically significant or essentially cosmetic. What I want is not the history or the politics but a disciplined analysis of the episode through the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons. A friend in health economics told me that any rationing body makes these comparisons implicitly, so the only real question is whether it makes them openly, and I suspect she is right but that this concedes far more than it seems to.

Take that problem seriously and work it in both directions. First, what exactly is being compared across persons in the Oregon list, and how much turns on whether we describe the units as utilities, as health states, or as quality-adjusted life years — that is, is this an interpersonal comparison of welfare at all, or something the welfarist frame misdescribes? Second, the ordinalist tradition holds that interpersonal comparisons of utility have no scientific basis; if one accepts that, what could the Commission coherently have been doing, and what does its actual procedure — a single scale applied to everyone, weights derived from community preferences, a cost per unit of benefit — presuppose about comparability? Bring in the standard distinctions between level comparability and unit comparability, and the familiar point that any distributive policy seems to require at least partial comparability; then say whether the ordinalist objection to Oregon is coherent or whether it would indict every budget decision ever made, including the ones its proponents accept. Third, treat the disability objection as a test case for two distinct problems that are often run together: the adaptation problem (people report surprisingly high well-being in states others rank as terrible, so whose valuations enter the scale?) and the problem of whose preferences are being aggregated (patients, the general public, or the legislature). Was the objection that the comparisons were invalid, or that the wrong values were loaded into valid comparisons? Are those genuinely different objections, and does the revised net-benefit ranking answer either of them? Finally, reverse the direction of illumination: what does the episode show about the theory itself? Does it support the claim that comparability is a practical necessity from which no one can abstain, or does it show that comparability is cheap and the metric is the whole game — and, if the latter, what is left of the theoretical debate?

Separate throughout what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus; where you think a question is unresolved, say so rather than manufacturing a verdict. I don't need citations or a reading list — I have the sources I need and want the argument. Keep the whole response to no more than 1,200 words.
