I am preparing a short position statement for a workshop on practical rationality and would like an audit of it before I circulate it. The commitments below are ones I actually hold; I have numbered them so that you can refer to them directly. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for further reading. What I want to know is which of these are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, how severe each genuine tension is, and what the least costly way of repairing it would be.

(1) Rationality is structural. An attitude is irrational only by failing to cohere with the agent's other attitudes. No preference is irrational in virtue of its content: a preference may be bizarre, cruel, or against the agent's own interest without being irrational as such.

(2) Rationality requires that an agent's preferences be representable by a real-valued utility function and her credences by a probability function. Among other things, her preferences must be complete: between any two options she prefers the first, prefers the second, or is indifferent between them.

(3) The ground of (2) is exploitability. An agent whose attitudes admit no such representation can be conducted by a sequence of trades, each of which she accepts as an improvement at the moment of accepting it, to a position she would have refused at the outset. Vulnerability of that kind is what practical irrationality consists in; I do not believe there is anything else to it.

(4) Rationality is synchronic. It appraises an agent at a time and imposes no requirement of consistency across times. It is not a defect to abandon a plan, to reverse a preference, or to refuse to be bound by an intention formed earlier. In particular, an agent who is carried through a sequence of choices, each of which was the best available to her by the lights she had when she made it, and who ends up worse off than she began, has not for that reason made a mistake.

(5) Value discovery occurs. There are options whose comparative merits cannot be determined in advance, and where the ranking one arrives at after having occupied one of them is not a correction of an earlier error; the person who comes to rank them only afterwards is not thereby irrational.

(6) I hold the causal theory of decision. In Newcomb-type cases, where the choice that maximizes expected utility conditional on being chosen diverges from the choice with the better causal consequences, I take the causal verdict to be the rational one.

Please tell me first which pairs are in genuine tension and which are only apparently so, and what distinguishes them. Then, for each genuine tension, give a severity ranking and say what the least costly revision would be and what that revision costs elsewhere in the position. I would rather have the tensions ordered than have them all described as equally serious. Finally, tell me which single revision would resolve the most, and what I would be giving up by making it.
