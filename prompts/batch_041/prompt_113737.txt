A seminar is meeting on whether dreams are experiences. Malcolm's answer is that nothing is undergone in sleep, and that the question whether the dreamer really had those images is a confusion in our grammar of sleeping and waking; Dennett's answer is also that nothing is undergone, but he treats the question as an empirical one about what gets laid down at waking and then mistaken for a record. Write a poem, 14 to 20 lines, in two movements separated by a blank line, to open that session. The first movement must narrate the dream from the inside, present tense, first person; the second must narrate the same events in the past tense and must be utterable by someone who slept without dreaming at all. The final line must be the first line with nothing changed but its tense. Let the form do the arguing rather than the lines: the first movement should be a narration no one was in a position to give, and the second should be the thing that makes the first seem to have happened. Do not name either philosopher, do not use the word cassette, use the word dream at most once, and do not state the disagreement anywhere. A reader who knows the debate should recognize the first movement as Malcolm's point and the second as Dennett's, and should also see that the poem does not settle between them, since the same structure supports calling the question confused and calling it answerable. Close with one line outside both movements that either philosopher could say and mean something different by.
