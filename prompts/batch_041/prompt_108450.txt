I have read a great many threads on the Scandinavian-versus-American contrast in political trust, and they almost always degrade into stereotype-trading, so I would like a dialogue that does the argument properly. The two speakers have each lived and worked for years in the other's country. The first is a Norwegian civil servant who regards the American posture of institutional suspicion -- the reflexively filed freedom-of-information request, the assumption that an official account is a cover story -- as a costly pathology, and who holds that the Scandinavian default of trusting public institutions and strangers is simply the more reasonable attitude, because in practice the trust is reciprocated and the suspicion is not free. The second is an American journalist who holds that this posture is exactly what a citizen of a large, heterogeneous, and frequently captured state owes to power, and that Scandinavian trust is reasonable only under conditions of unusual state competence and social homogeneity that Scandinavians mistake for a general truth about human beings.

Let them press each other on whether "reasonable" is meant agent-relatively -- reasonable given the evidence you happen to have, so that both attitudes can be reasonable while only one is well calibrated to the world -- or in the sense of what someone who did not know which country they would be born into ought to adopt. And have one of them suggest that Scandinavian trust is really trust in enforcement rather than in people: that what looks like faith in decency is a bet that defection will be detected and punished, in which case the disagreement is not about human nature at all but about the capacity of the state, and the American's suspicion is a judgment about a different state rather than about a different species. Neither speaker should be a strawman, each should answer the other's actual points rather than restating a position, and I would rather they locate the real crux than converge on a polite both-sides conclusion.
