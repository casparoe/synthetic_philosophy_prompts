I'm a novelist — historical fiction, mostly — and I'm about a year into a book set in the late 1600s where Newton and Leibniz both turn up, and I've got two people reading my drafts, a physicist friend and a philosopher friend, who have been having the exact same argument about space for eight months now. I genuinely can't tell whether they're disagreeing or just talking past each other, and I need to know, because the whole middle of the book is a scene where two characters argue about it and at the moment I'm writing it as a misunderstanding, which might be wrong.

Here's the fight as best I can reconstruct it. One of them says space has to be a real thing — a container, an actual something — because when the water climbs the sides of a spinning bucket there has to be something it's spinning relative to, and saying it climbs because it's rotating relative to absolute space is an explanation, while saying it climbs because it's rotating relative to the rest of the matter is a dodge. The other says that's backwards: a thing called space that you can only ever detect through the relations of bodies is an entity that does no work, and everything the first one wants explained gets explained by the relations alone.

What makes me suspect they aren't really disagreeing: when I push, the physicist says he doesn't care about the metaphysics, he cares whether the theory's models say the bucket is rotating; the philosopher says the question is what the world has to contain for that model to be true. Different questions, maybe. They also both tell me the bucket is a museum piece now and the real action is in general relativity, where you can shuffle the points of spacetime around and get a different model that predicts everything the same. One of them says the shuffling shows the points aren't real; the other says it shows the points are real but their identities aren't — which sounds to me like the same thing said twice.

There's also a bit in the book where a character asks whether, in a universe containing nothing but the bucket, the water would still climb the sides if you spun it. My two readers give me opposite answers and each thinks the other's answer is a category error, and I've no idea which of them I'm supposed to believe.

My hypothesis, and tell me if it's wrong: this has stalled because the two sides mean something seperate by 'exists' — one means 'is quantified over by our best theory', the other means 'is a genuine ingredient of the world' — and if that's right then no argument is ever going to move it and I should write the scene as a misunderstanding. But maybe it's a real disagreement about something specific: about what needs explaining, or about what's possible (could there be a world with just the bucket? and would the water climb?), or about whether a relation can be a fact about the world without being a thing in it.

So diagnose it. I don't want a survey of the positions, I know the positions, I've read alot of this by now and it's why I'm stuck. Is it verbal, is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, are they answering two different questions under one word, or is it a genuine disagreement that's just very hard? Use what each side actually says. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a result, a change of question — or tell me honestly that nothing will and I should write the scene accordingly. And if my 'exists' hypothesis is the wrong fault line, say what the right one is.
