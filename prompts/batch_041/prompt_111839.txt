I teach a 90-minute session on speech acts to a seminar of about sixteen advanced undergraduates, and I want an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between an act that is never performed at all and one that is performed but hollow — and makes them argue about what a hearer's uptake contributes to whether an act was performed. My rough mechanic, which you should improve or replace if you can do better: split the room into four houses of unequal standing, give each student a small hand of utterable moves (a promise, a resignation, a motion, an accusation, a wager, a naming), and make the validity of each move turn on conditions they are not told in advance — proper authority, correct procedure, a hearer who registers the act, and sincerity, which a later turn can expose. They should learn the conditions only by watching their own moves fail or be treated as void, and the session should end with the group forced to rule on a disputed case in which the speaker intended the act, the hearer took it as done, and a third party insists it never happened. Give me rules, materials, a scoring scheme that needs no real money, a timeline, a debrief that maps each failure onto the felicity conditions, an honest note on what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and I must supply myself. In your own commentary, separate what is settled among philosophers of language from what is genuinely contested — for instance whether uptake is required for an act to be performed at all — and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Maximum 1200 words.
