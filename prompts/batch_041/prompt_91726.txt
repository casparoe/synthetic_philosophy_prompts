I chair the grants committee at a small foundation that funds long-shot work on reducing existential risk — we hand out about $20M a year across ~40 grants, and our bylaws make us a fiduciary of a fixed budget that has to be committed this cycle. An application just came in that's got the comittee stuck. The applicant wants $2M to build what they call a "moral-value anchor": an argument plus a piece of institutional machinery that, they claim, has maybe a one-in-10^14 chance of preventing an outcome they define as infinitely bad (an outcome whose disvalue they say has no finite bound). Their whole pitch is that any positive probability times infinite disvalue is still infinite, so the expected value of funding them is infinite no matter how tiny the probability, and therefore refusing would be irrational by our own lights. They also point out — correctly, I checked — that we've funded plenty of 3%-chance projects without blinking. And there are two other applications in this same cycle making basically the same infinite-value claim, which is where my head starts to hurt.

Three of my committee members are convinced and want to fund it on straight expected-value grounds. Two think it's a Pascal's-wager-style con — you can run the same argument for literally anything, including paying the applicant to *not* do it — and they want a flat rule against ever letting infinite-value claims into the process. The rest are somewhere in between and a bit spooked, because the applicant is a serious person, not a crank, and nobody wants to be the philistine who rejected a project that could've saved everything.

Draft the written decision memo I'll circulate before we vote. Give me a concrete recommendation — fund, reject, or fund with conditions, and if conditions then say exactly what they are — and actually take the applicant's expected-value argument seriously instead of just gesturing at "Pascal's mugging," because several of us find that label question-begging. Whatever you recommend has to be something the three true believers could respond to, and it has to say what we should do about the other two applications in the pipeline, not just this one. Please also flag where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and tell me which argument or consideration would actually flip your recommendation — I'd rather know now than after the vote.
