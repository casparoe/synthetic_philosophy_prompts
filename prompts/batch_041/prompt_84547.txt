I am trying to forecast a colleague's verdicts on a family of wagers, and I would like your help with the next case. She works on Pascal's wager and on decision problems that combine vanishingly small probabilities with enormous payoffs, and over the years I have logged her answers to a set of toy cases. Assume throughout that the probabilities and payoffs are exactly as stated, that every party to an offer is honest and able to pay, and that no hidden risk attaches to any option.

1. A wager on the existence of a deity whose existence is supported by an independently checkable argument: she takes the probability to be one-half, the reward for belief to be infinite, and the cost of belief to be finite and modest. She takes the wager.

2. A mugging-style case: a stranger offers, for a one-dollar stake, a one-in-a-googol chance (10^-100) at a payoff of 10^200 dollars, the probability resting entirely on the stranger's assertion. She declines.

3. A wager on a well-understood physical mechanism that, on the best-confirmed theory, gives a one-in-a-thousand chance of a billion-dollar payoff for a one-dollar stake. She takes it.

4. A wager with a one-in-a-googol chance, derived from that same well-confirmed theory, at a payoff of only a thousand dollars, for a one-dollar stake. She declines.

5. A theological standoff: three mutually exclusive hypotheses, none supported by evidence, each of which infinitely rewards those who believe it and infinitely punishes everyone else. She says that no option is rationally required here.

The new case: a well-confirmed theory entails that a particular intervention, at a cost of one dollar, carries a one-in-a-googol chance (10^-100) of a payoff worth 10^200 dollars — the same numbers as in case 2, except that the probability is derived from the theory rather than asserted by an interested party.

What will she say about the new case? I care less about a bare yes or no than about four things: the rules that fit her record so far; what each of those rules predicts about the new case; your own best guess, with the reasons for it; and the further case that would most cleanly separate whichever rules survive. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so rather than manufacturing a verdict. Finally, mark which parts of your answer are settled — the arithmetic, or the entailments of the rules — and which turn on genuinely contested questions, such as whether a probability can be grounded in an argument rather than in a frequency, or whether infinite payoffs can be compared at all; do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
