I have no university training in this — I read this sort of thing for pleasure, and I have been going round in circles on one puzzle on my own for about two years — and I am now applying to a foundation that makes small grants to people working outside universities. They want a proposal of about four pages for a six-month project, which I would do at roughly one day a week, and it has to be readable by a panel who are not specialists. Please write that proposal.

The puzzle is the supertask: getting infinitely many steps done in a finite stretch of time. Everyone starts with the lamp that is switched on and off at one minute to noon, then half a minute, then a quarter, and so on, and asks whether it is on or off at noon. I know the two standard lines — that the description fixes nothing about the state at noon, so there is no answer, and that a lamp must be either on or off, so there is an answer and the description has merely failed to say what it is — and I can see how each side argues. What I have not been able to find, and what I want the project to be about, is a general test: a way of telling, for any supertask, whether the description determines the outcome or leaves it hanging, which does not come down to asking which answer feels right for the lamp.

My half-formed idea is that determinacy is really convergence. Take the vase that has ten numbered balls put in at each step and the lowest-numbered ball in it taken out again, so that at the end it is empty, which seems forced even though the set-up is as impossible as the lamp's. What strikes me is that the vase's contents settle down — after enough steps, the answer to what is in it is stable — whereas the lamp's state never settles; it alternates for ever. So perhaps a supertask has a determinate outcome exactly when the states of the relevant part of the world converge, with the outcome being the limit, and the lamp fails not because the description is short but because there is nothing for its state to converge to. I would like the proposal to state this properly, to say what has to be built into the state for it to be true, and to be blunt about where it breaks. One thing that worries me: in a version of the vase where you always take out the highest-numbered ball instead, the vase ends up with infinitely many balls left. If both versions settle down but settle on different things, I want to know whether the physics is doing the work or a convention about labels.

The case I most want it tested against I find much worse than the lamp. An infinite squad of executioners, one for every moment in the last minute, are each ordered to kill a prisoner if he is still alive when their moment comes. Pick any moment for the death and an earlier executioner would have got there first, so nobody kills him; but the executioners between them cover every moment before the deadline, so he cannot live either. Both horns seem forced. On my convergence test this comes out as no determinate outcome, the same verdict as the lamp, and I am not convinced that is right — the lamp looks under-described, this one looks contradictory, and if my test cannot separate them that may be fatal to it, or may show the distinction is not as sharp as it looks. The proposal should say which, or say what would settle it.

The foundation will also want the usual parts of a proposal, and I would rather they were woven in than bolted on: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer, why the standard positions leave it open, what I would actually do with the six months, what a result would look like and what the finished thing would be, what would count as the project failing, why the method you choose beats the obvious alternative of collecting more puzzle cases and ranking them by how they feel, and an honest view of whether the project is too big for the time. Assume the panel have heard of the lamp and nothing else in this letter. I do not need citations, a reading list or a survey of the literature — I will attach the references myself — and I would rather you referred to positions by describing them than by name. If the honest answer is that my idea collapses and there is nothing general to be said, I would rather read that proposal than a hopeful one.
