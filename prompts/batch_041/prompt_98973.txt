I'm trying to write down my view of conditionals before a workshop and I want a consistency check, not a survey. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Indicative and counterfactual if mean the same thing — same truth conditions. The difference is entierly in what the speaker is taking for granted: with "if the dog didn't break it, the kid did" the antecedent is still an open possibility, with "if the dog hadn't broken it, the kid would have" it's taken as false. That's a fact about the speaker's state of information, not about the meaning of if.

(2) Counterfactuals are true or false according to what happens in the closest worlds where the antecedent holds. Once you've fixed which respects of similarity matter, the truth value is objective — it doesn't shift with what we happen to know.

(3) On indicatives I'm with the no-truth-value crowd: the probability of "if A, B" just is the conditional probability of B given A, and to assert it is to conditionally assert B, not to assert a proposition.

(4) The old Oswald/Kennedy pair still gets me — the counterfactual version (had Oswald not shot, someone else would have) seems plainly false, and I don't think that's just the presupposition talking.

(5) I'll grant that which similarity respects are salient can shift with context. I don't think that makes (2) wrong though.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and how bad is each one? I honestly don't know which I'd give up if pushed, so rank them and tell me the cheapest repair for the worst.
