I'm a postdoc in a department that's half demography, half cultural sociology, and we've got a philosopher of social science who comes to our workshops mostly to ask uncomfortable questions. Next month it's my turn to present, and I've been sitting on this paper way too long, so I want to be ready for the Q&A rather than for the talk. The thesis is that the Boomer/Millennial/Gen Z style generations aren't real social kinds — and not just in the lazy "it's all a social construct" way either. My line is that a generational label is doing real explanatory work only if the birth-year cut survives perturbation: shift the boundary by three or four years, change nothing else, and if the effect you were attributing to the generation dissolves, then what you actually had was a period effect wearing a birth-year costume. I've got a little formal bit trying to make that precise, something like a stability condition on the estimated effect under shifts of the window, plus three case studies — the strongest one being the Gen Z loneliest generation discourse, which I read as a period effect from phones plus covid rather than a cohort thing at all.

I know this room won't be gentle and I'd rather hear it from you first. The demographers are going to hit me with the age-period-cohort identification problem — age, period and cohort are linearly dependent, so you can't just identify a cohort effect by throwing dummies in — and they'll want to know what my stability condition buys me over the standard fixes. The cultural sociologists think the whole is-it-a-real-kind question is a category error, because what matters is that people *use* the label: they self-identify as Gen Z, they have generational consciousness, the label organizes their politics. And the philosopher of social science is going to ask what theory of kinds I'm even working with — am I a conventionalist or a realist about social kinds — and why "survives perturbation" should count as a criterion for kindhood rather than just for robustness of an estimate. There's also an economic historian in the crowd who I already know thinks generational talk is a marketing artifact and will say something withering about Strauss and Howe within the first ten minutes.

So: give me the Q&A prep sheet.
