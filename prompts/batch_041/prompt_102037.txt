I teach a graduate seminar on game theory and the philosophy of practical reason, and I am drafting this year's final examination. I need one essay question built around the specific six-stage centipede game defined below, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The game. Two players, Row and Column, move in alternation. Row moves at stages 1, 3, and 5; Column moves at stages 2, 4, and 6. At each stage the player whose turn it is chooses Stop or Continue. If the player Stops, the game ends at once with the payoffs (Row, Column) given below. If the player Continues, play moves to the next stage. If both players Continue at every stage, the game ends with payoffs (5, 5).

Stage 1 (Row may Stop): (1, 0)
Stage 2 (Column may Stop): (0, 2)
Stage 3 (Row may Stop): (3, 1)
Stage 4 (Column may Stop): (2, 4)
Stage 5 (Row may Stop): (5, 3)
Stage 6 (Column may Stop): (4, 6)
Both Continue throughout: (5, 5)

Both players know the entire payoff structure, both are rational, and both know the other is rational—and so on up the hierarchy.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar summary that backward induction yields immediate stopping while experimental subjects cooperate because of social preferences or reputational concerns. I want it to force students to state precisely what the backward-induction argument assumes and to ask whether those assumptions are actually available to the players. In particular, it should turn on the difference between the bare claim that each player is rational and the stronger epistemic conditions that the backward-induction prediction appears to need, and on whether the stages that the prediction says will never be reached are stages at which rationality has any determinate behavioural content. A strong answer should also be able to say why the cooperative path, although it leaves both players better off than the backward-induction outcome, is not sustained by the equilibrium analysis, and what would have to change for it to be reachable.

Write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 to 900 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should name five must-hit points, assign each a share of a total of 20 marks, and give a one-line note on how candidates characteristically get that point wrong. Pitch the question so that a strong graduate student could answer it in about 45 minutes of writing.
