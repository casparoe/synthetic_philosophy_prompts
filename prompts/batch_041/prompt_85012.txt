A friend of mine, an engineer with metaphysical leanings, has become convinced that Berkeley's idealism is simply a correct description of how video games work, and he wants me to concede that this makes the doctrine respectable rather than eccentric. His case has three parts. First, nothing in a game is drawn until it needs to be seen, so that for the player the world just is the field of what is currently being rendered; Berkeley's thesis that existing consists in being perceived, he says, is an exact statement of the engine's behaviour. Second, something must keep the whole thing going when no one is looking, and for him that role is played by the server or the physics engine, which stands in for Berkeley's God. Third, when I object that a room nobody enters still exists in Berkeley's universe, he replies that games have settled this too: some worlds are not merely unrendered but undetermined, their terrain generated only when a player arrives, and this, he says, is Berkeley's considered view of the unperceived tree — real, but only as a rule waiting to be executed.

Audit the analogy, not the doctrine. Start with the correspondence, item by item: what in the game is meant to answer to what in Berkeley — the player, the act of looking, the rendered scene, the unrendered and the undetermined regions, the engine, the designer — and say of each whether the fit is good, forced, or missing altogether. Then locate the points at which the mapping breaks, separating the breaks that are merely picturesque from those that matter for the use he is putting it to. I suspect three weak spots, though the judgement is yours: whether the analogy can even be stated without presupposing the conceivability of unperceived existence which Berkeley's a priori argument, the master argument, is designed to deny; where it places causal power, given what the game and Berkeley respectively treat as bringing an object into being at the moment it is seen; and whether Berkeley could accommodate regions that are undetermined until someone visits them. Say also what the analogy quietly imports that the doctrine does not have — a substrate, a division between a machine's internal state and its outputs, mechanisms in place of minds, laws in place of decrees — and then rule on the conclusion he wants it to carry: that idealism is a natural and respectable position rather than a curiosity, and perhaps that Berkeley was describing the world correctly rather than merely anticipating it. If the analogy in fact delivers the simulation hypothesis rather than Berkeley's idealism, say so. If a better analogy is available for his purpose, supply it; if the analogy cannot carry the conclusion at all, say that plainly.
