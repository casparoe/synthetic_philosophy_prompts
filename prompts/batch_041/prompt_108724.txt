For most of my career I ran psychophysics experiments on early vision, and since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of perception. I keep running into the debate over cognitive penetration, and I keep feeling that the parties are not always answering the same question — something my old lab would not have tolerated for a week. I would like to build a decision tree: a sorting instrument, not an essay, that takes a reader from a starting commitment to a named position in this debate. I want the tree organized around the logical space rather than around a list of authors, so that the questions come first and the labels are located in the space afterward.

Every reader is assumed to share one starting commitment, stated as weakly as possible so that it excludes nothing I want on the tree: I accept that what a person believes, desires, or knows can sometimes influence what they perceive, in some sense of "influence" that has yet to be pinned down. From there, each branch node should be a question that genuinely separates positions, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which label it leads to. Where a node can be anchored to a concrete case rather than stated abstractly, please do that.

I would like the nodes to turn on the following distinctions, roughly in this order, though you should reorder, merge, or replace them if the logic demands it:

1. Where the effect is supposed to land — on perceptual experience itself, on perceptual judgment or belief, or only on how the subject is disposed to report or respond.
2. Whether the cognitive state's content is also the content of the experience, so that the experience represents the very thing believed, or whether the cognitive state merely causes an experience whose content is fixed independently of it.
3. Whether the effect runs through attention, and if it does, whether that counts against calling it penetration at all.
4. Whether the operative state is a personal-level belief or desire, or a subpersonal prior or expectation of the sort a Bayesian treatment of perception would posit. I care about this one a great deal, because in my experience the two are routinely run together.
5. Whether penetration is a matter of degree or of kind.
6. Whether the claim that penetration occurs is an empirical hypothesis or something that could be settled from the armchair, and in particular whether the informational encapsulation of early vision would make penetration impossible in principle.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position — or "unnamed" if no one defends it — together with a single sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Note which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and diagnose briefly why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any of my questions, flag that rather than papering over it.

Please test the tree against at least three concrete cases, chosen so that the positions genuinely come apart rather than cases on which everyone agrees: a trained expert who perceives a feature a novice misses; someone in the grip of a strong desire or fear; and someone searching a crowd for a familiar face. If you can, add a fourth drawn from the laboratory — a case where the effect is plausibly adaptation or contextual recalibration rather than anything cognitive.

A few constraints. Do not ask the reader for citations, reading lists, or attributions, and do not build the instrument around the recollection of particular papers; if a position needs a name, use the ordinary descriptive name for it and move on. Assume the reader cannot look anything up. Keep the whole thing to what a person could work through in ten or fifteen minutes, so no more than eight or nine nodes and no more than about ten leaves.

Finally, I want you to push back if I am asking for the wrong thing. If a decision tree is the wrong instrument for this debate — because the positions do not fall out of the logical space cleanly, or because my six distinctions cut across one another so badly that any tree will misrepresent somebody, or because my assumption of a single shared starting commitment is confused — then say so plainly and tell me what would work better, rather than producing a tidy tree to satisfy the request. I would rather be told that my framing is wrong than be handed a diagram that looks clean and sorts nothing.
