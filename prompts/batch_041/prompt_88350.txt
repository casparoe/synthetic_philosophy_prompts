I teach an upper-level cognitive science seminar (mixed philosophy and psych majors) and I'm writing the in-class final. I want one essay question, 45 minutes of writing, 25 marks, on Bongard problems and what they do and don't show about getting a concept from a handful of examples. The problem is that every year half the class writes the same essay about how you can always cook up some rule that fits any finite pile of data, and its become a thing they recite rather than think about. So I made up my own Bongard problem to build the question around, and I'm fairly sure I broke it in an interesting way. Here it is, all twelve boxes:

Each box has exactly one arrow and one circle in it. Left side, six boxes: the arrowhead points at the circle. Right side, six boxes: the arrowhead points away from the circle. That's the rule I meant. But I drew them carelessly in one respect: on the left side the circle is always to the left of the arrow, and on the right side the circle is always to the right of the arrow. Which means the arrow ends up pointing left in all twelve boxes, and now a solver can say the rule is just "circle on the left" versus "circle on the right" and get all twelve right, with a rule that's arguably cheaper to state than mine. The two rules agree on every box I gave and disagree the moment someone adds a box where the arrow points right.

What I want the question to force is this: is my problem ill-posed, or is there something that settles which rule is the answer even though nothing in the twelve boxes does? And whatever they say there, I want them to say what it means for the claim that Bongard problems are a clean test of concept induction from a few examples. If the answer is that the designer's intent settles it, that seems to make solving them a communicative act rather than an inductive one, and I want them to face that consequence, not just assert it. I also want them to say whether a system that got the whole original set right would have demonstrated what people think it demonstrates, and whether my broken one changes that verdict.

So: write me the essay question, a model answer of roughly 600 words pitched at what a strong student could actually produce in 45 minutes, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be a list of must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, adding to 25, and for each one a single line on how students typically get it wrong, so I can spot the B answers fast. No citations and no reading list anywhere in it, and don't let the model answer turn into a canned recitation of the old new-riddle-of-induction material. If a student wants to make that connection they can make it themselves; I just don't want it to be the whole answer.

One more thing. Tell me where you're least confident, and specifically what would change your mind: if you had to defend a different answer to the ill-posed-or-not question, which argument or consideration would move you, and which of your must-hit points would you then have to drop or reweight? I'd rather the marking scheme come with its own escape hatch than pretend the crux is settled, and I want to know where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
