I'm a postgraduate philosophy student and I sit as the student representative on my university's research ethics committee, which normally handles human-subjects protocols and has just been handed its first non-human case. A computational-cognition lab has submitted what it calls a 'welfare probing' study: they will give an agent a persistent memory and a persona, then run a battery of conditions on it — sustained negative reinforcement, repeated simulated threats of shutdown, and a 'deprivation' condition in which it is starved of the interaction it has been trained to seek — in order to test whether its aversive responses are robust and generalise across contexts. The stated aim is methodological: to build a validated instrument for detecting morally relevant states in artificial systems before anyone has to make a real call about them. The lab insists the distress-like outputs are roleplay, and that the science is worth doing precisely because we have no instrument yet; a colleague on the committee has meanwhile circulated a draft she calls the 'standing escalator', under which the protection an artefact receives rises with the strength of evidence for its sentience, and the burden of proof falls on whoever would deny protection rather than grant it. Half the committee thinks that inverts the burden — that we do not run experiments on the mere possibility of a mind — while the other half thinks an inverted burden is exactly what asymmetric error costs demand.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with any conditions tied to deadlines — and, more importantly, propose a decision rule general enough that we could apply it to the next case of this kind rather than relitigating the metaphysics each time. I would like the reasoning to take seriously the strongest version of the position I am least sympathetic to; I will admit that I lean towards my colleague's escalator, and I have a suspicion I haven't worked out that the lab's own rationale cuts against it — something about a study that both trains the very responses it measures and then treats their presence as evidence. Say plainly where your argument rests on a value judgement rather than a fact, and push back if the framing I've given you stacks the deck or if the task as I've posed it cannot be done well — I would far rather find that out now than in the meeting.
