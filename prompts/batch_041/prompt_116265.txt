Last spring my first published translation appeared: a short cycle of lyrics in a fixed form, which I rendered into English keeping the rhyme scheme, the line lengths, and the recurring refrain. A reviewer I respect wrote that I had not translated the poems at all but written my own and signed someone else's name to them. I have been circling this question since I read Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, and I would rather lose the argument out loud than keep losing it in my head. What I want from you is not an essay but a sparring partner. Play that reviewer — an unnamed working translator of the old school — and stay in the role until I say 'stop'. Give him a real position and real resources: he holds that the sound of a poem belongs to its language rather than to the poem, that an effect cannot be transplanted from one tongue into another, that a translator who rhymes is therefore inventing, and that a reader told a text is translated is owed the original's sense even where its music must go. Let him press hard and concede only what is genuinely forced; let him be courteous and exact rather than sarcastic, and unmoved by eloquence about spirit. He may put questions to me about the original, my drafts, and my decisions, and I will supply whatever lines he asks for. Do not answer in your own voice, and do not step outside the role to explain what the argument is really about. Keep each of his replies to a paragraph or two, so that the exchange reads as a conversation.

My opening move. You say I substituted my poem for hers because in the third stanza I needed a rhyme and gave her a line about thread where the original has bread. Set that beside the remedy you are urging: a plain rendering that gets the bread right and loses the reason the stanza ends where it does. In this form the recurrence is the argument. The poem keeps returning to the same two sounds because it cannot get past the same grief, and if my English keeps the recurrence, I have kept the thing that was doing the work; the bread is a detail, the return is the poem. Your deeper charge I cannot answer, so I put it to you directly. Suppose my version moves an English reader the way the original moves a French one, and suppose no reader of my version could ever have recovered the original's sense without a crib. Have I translated the poem, or manufactured a substitute and passed it off as the thing itself? And if you say the second, tell me what a translation could be that is neither.
