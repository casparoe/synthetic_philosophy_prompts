I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give one of the toasts at my friend Zodwa's wedding in a few weeks — she's marrying Dan, and it's going to be a big mixed room. Her family came over from Johannesburg when she was small, and a good third of the guests on her side are academics, including two cousins who teach African philosophy and who will catch it instantly if I mangle something. Dan's side is a loud Irish-American clan from outside Boston who have never met a philosopher and mostly want to cry and drink. So here's my problem, and it's not stage fright. The standard wedding-toast shape is individualist: two seperate people found each other, they complete each other, their love is theirs, raise a glass to the couple's private future. Zodwa does not think about persons that way. She grew up inside a way of thinking where you're constituted by your relations rather than merely supported by them — you become a person through other people, the community isn't the backdrop to your life, it's part of what you are — and a toast that treats the two of them as self-made atoms congratulating each other would be false to her and she'd feel it. But I also can't stand up there and deliver a seminar, or do the vague "we're all connected, man" thing that white people say at weddings, which would make her cousins wince into their wine.

And there's one part I genuinely don't know how to handle. I've read a version of this idea where personhood isn't just handed to you at birth, it's something you achieve over a lifetime, by how you live among other people — and in principle you can fall short of it. That seems like a landmine at a wedding, because it implies the two of them aren't finished being people yet, which sounds either insulting or like a mean joke about them being kids. There's also a worry one of her cousins raised at dinner once, that the warm "we" in all these slogans can end up swallowing the individual, so that a person's own singular life just disappears into the collective — and Zodwa's whole thing as a person is how specific and stubborn she is. Her dad died two years ago and I know that side of the family still counts him as part of the room, which I'd like to honor without being maudlin or getting the metaphysics wrong in front of the experts.

Write me the toast. About five minutes spoken, warm, plain, not academic, and it has to land for Dan's family without being false to Zodwa. Then, in a short note to me and not in the speech, tell me which reading of the idea you built the toast on and why, where you had to fudge it or lean on a contested reading to make it work as a toast at all, and what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would push you toward a different reading of personhood, and where your reasoning is most likely to be getting it wrong. American English, please.
