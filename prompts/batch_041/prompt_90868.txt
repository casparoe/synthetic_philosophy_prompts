I got into a long, slightly wine-fueled argument last night with a friend who's been reading ancient philosophy on the side, and I want to get the terrain straight before we pick it up again. He says the sea battle thing in Aristotle proves that either the future is already fixed or else nothing we say about tomorrow can be true or false at all — he thinks those are the only two options, and he's decided he's fine with the fixed one because it doesn't change anything anyway. I'm pretty sure he's collapsed about four different positions into two, and that he's also smuggled in a totally separate argument (the one about how if it's fated there's no point deliberating) that has nothing to do with truth values. Can you help me build the actual map? Act like someone who works on tense logic and the semantics of will rather than someone summarizing the encyclopedia article — I want the real joints in the logical space, not the standard lecture. Start by brainstorming the axes along which these views genuinely differ before you draw anything.

What I want is a decision tree, or a taxonomy, that takes someone from a few basic commitments — does "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" have a truth value right now, yes or no; is the future one line or a branching tree of genuinely open alternatives; is God's foreknowledge in the picture at all or are we doing this purely secularly; and is will a tense or a covert modal — down to a named position. Every branch node should be a question a smart person could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so no node that just says are you an Ockhamist. Every leaf should be a position someone has actually defended, with one sentence about its most embarrassing commitment. Please get the two treatments people call Peircean and Ockhamist in there, the supervaluationist-style line that it's indeterminate which of the two predictions is the true one, the move where one of the branches is secretly the real one (I think people call this the thin red line?), the relativist idea that the sentence only gets a truth value once you say who's assessing it and when, and whatever the thoroughgoing logical determinist and the three-valued logician look like as leaves. Also flag which pairs of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I suspect a few of these agree on every case a non-specialist could state and differ only about formalism — and note any combination of answers that looks like an empty slot, saying whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to defend it. Use concrete cases at the nodes where you can: a bet placed today, a prophecy, a promise, a prediction that comes true by accident.

Last thing, and it definately matters to me: separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me one contested view dressed up as the consensus. If Aristotle's own view is disputed — I gather it is — say so instead of quietly picking a side. Keep it in plain language; jargon is fine but define it when you use it.
