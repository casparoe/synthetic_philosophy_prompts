I am a novelist at work on a book in which the decisive exchanges are all indirect: characters persuade, wound, and confess without ever stating the proposition that does the work. I would like a tier list of the mechanisms by which a speaker can mean more than, or other than, what their sentence says, judged specifically by their usefulness to a novelist constructing such exchanges. Please sort the following ten candidates into five tiers, from most to least narratively load-bearing. Some are floutings of a maxim, some are species of implicature, and one is a general account of speaker meaning; rank them together anyway, on the single dimension of narrative traction: (1) flouting the maxim of Quantity by saying too little; (2) flouting Quality through irony, metaphor, or exaggeration; (3) flouting Relation by changing the subject or answering a different question; (4) flouting Manner by deliberate obscurity or ambiguity; (5) exploiting a presupposition rather than asserting it; (6) conventional implicature carried by a particle, honorific, or connective; (7) particularized implicature that depends entirely on the local context of the scene; (8) generalized conversational implicature that travels with the words across contexts; (9) Gricean intention-recognition, where the hearer’s recognition of the speaker’s intention is itself part of what is meant; (10) plausible deniability, where the utterance is designed so that the intended meaning can be cancelled without contradiction. For each, treat narrative traction as the capacity to generate scene-level conflict, reversal, and revelation without the narrator explaining the subtext. Distinguish mechanisms that are genuinely load-bearing—where cancelling the implicature would collapse the scene’s causal chain—from those that are merely ornamental. Assume my readers are intelligent but have no training in linguistics, and that the novel must remain intelligible to them. Do not give me a history of the field or reading recommendations; reason from the descriptions above.

Please structure your response as follows:

- Tier table: a table listing each mechanism, its tier, and a one-sentence justification (roughly 250 words).
- Rationale by tier: explain why the top tier is top and the bottom tier is bottom, with attention to the difference between speaker meaning and mere suggestion (roughly 600 words).
- The cancellation test: for the top four mechanisms, describe what happens to a scene if the implicature is explicitly cancelled, and what that reveals about the mechanism’s narrative fragility (roughly 300 words).
- Bottom line: name the three mechanisms I should build the novel around and the one I should use sparingly or not at all (roughly 150 words).
