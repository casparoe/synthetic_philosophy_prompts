I chair the ethics committee at a large teaching hospital, and we are rewriting our policy on determining death. The current policy is a patchwork. For most patients we apply the neurological criteria and treat a person as dead once the whole brain has irreversibly stopped functioning. But our organ-donation programme also takes organs from patients who are not brain-dead, after treatment is withdrawn and the heart stops, and the retrieval teams want to know precisely how long after the pulse is lost they may wait before proceeding. Please write a decision procedure my committee can actually run, case by case, that is candid about the conception of death it presupposes. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a determination of death, and where it hands the matter to a person because the question is one of values rather than of physiology. Then run it on two cases: a patient who meets the standard neurological criteria but whose body still regulates its own temperature, salt and water; and a patient who is not brain-dead and whose heart has just stopped after withdrawal of treatment, with the retrieval team waiting. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer my colleagues will dislike, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
