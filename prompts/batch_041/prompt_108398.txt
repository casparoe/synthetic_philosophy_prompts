I am a high school junior and a member of my school's game theory club, and I have built an argument that I think proves too much. I would like you to tell me which step is faulty and what it costs to give that step up.

(1) Every finite two-player game of perfect information, with no chance moves and no draws, is determined: one of the two players has a strategy that guarantees a win.

(2) Suppose such a game is symmetric, meaning that from every position the two players have exactly the same legal moves and that interchanging the players leaves the game unchanged. Then the second player cannot have a winning strategy. For suppose the second player had one. The first player could make an arbitrary legal opening move and then, for the rest of the game, simply follow the second player's winning strategy, treating the position after that opening move as the position from which the second player's strategy is to be played. The first player would thereby win, contradicting the supposition that the second player has a winning strategy.

(3) Therefore, in every finite, symmetric, drawless game of perfect information, the first player has a winning strategy.

(4) This cannot be right, because of a game my friend and I invented. Place seven tokens on the table. Players alternate turns, and on each turn a player removes either one token or two tokens. Whoever removes the last token loses. The game is finite, symmetric, and drawless: the tokens run out, and whoever takes the last one loses. So by (3) the first player must have a winning strategy. But the second player won almost every time we played, and after reflection I am confident that the second player can always force a win. The first player is at a disadvantage because every move brings the game nearer to the moment when someone is forced to take the last token, and the first player reaches that moment first.

(5) I would like to keep (1) and (2), since determinacy seems like plain logic and the strategy-stealing maneuver in (2) is the elegant heart of the argument. But (1) and (2), together with (3), cannot survive my seven-token game. Which step should I reject, and what exactly do I lose by rejecting it? If the trouble lies in (2), I would like to know what hidden assumption the maneuver requires, whether Hex satisfies that assumption while my game violates it, and whether there is a general way to state which games permit strategy stealing.
