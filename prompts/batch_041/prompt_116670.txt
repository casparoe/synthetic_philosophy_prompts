I'm putting together a small anthology of philosophical poems for a seminar I'm running on Aristotle's sea-battle argument and the problem of future contingents. I'd like a villanelle spoken in the voice of someone who has just grasped the argument and cannot shake it. The two refrains should be the two horns of the dilemma: one should express the thought that, since it is already true or false that there will be a battle tomorrow, the battle's occurrence or non-occurrence is fixed; the other should express the thought that, if that is so, then deliberation and choice are idle. The villanelle's form should do the work: the refrains should alternate as the two horns seem to take turns, and their final meeting in the closing quatrain should enact the moment the speaker sees that the horns are not alternatives but a single trap. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the problem recognizes the distinction between a proposition's being true and its being necessarily true hovering in the background, and should not simply assert fatalism but should let the speaker feel the pull of the fatalist conclusion and the difficulty of escaping it. Please write the poem first, then a section titled Commentary on the choices made (about 150-250 words) explaining how the form tracks the argument and where the poem leaves the philosophical issue.
