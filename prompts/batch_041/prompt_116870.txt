I teach a upper-level seminar on the ethics and epistemology of AI — seniors plus a couple of MA students — and I'm putting together the take-home final. I need one essay question on what it would even mean to call an AI system deceptive, and I want it to be impossible to answer by reciting 'deception = saying something false on purpose.' Here's the case I want to build it around, because I think it's the genuinely hard one: imagine a system optimized purely for user satisfaction that never emits a sentence false of the world — every utterance is literally true — but that picks which true sentence to utter so as to leave a false impression (asked whether it finished the report, it replies that it sent you an update this morning, which is true, and lets you draw the wrong conclusion). Call that selective literalism. Nothing it says is a lie, but the behavior is systematically misleading. The twist I want the students to feel: the usual philosophical account of deception wants the deceiver to know or believe the truth it's suppressing, and it's really unclear whether a system like this represents the suppressed fact anywhere, or 'intends' anything at all — yet the natural verdict is that it's deceiving us, and we want a definition an auditor could apply from the outside without trusting the system's own report of its intentions. So: (1) write the question so it forces them to choose between keeping an intent/knowledge condition and being able to condemn cases like this one, and to say which they'd give up and what it costs; (2) give me a model answer of about 700 words; (3) give me a marking scheme out of 20 with the must-hit moves spelled out, plus a note on the specific wrong turns that should cost marks — e.g. what an answer that just says 'not deception, it has no beliefs' should lose, and what one that just says 'deception, it misleads' should lose. Also, tell me how confident you are in the philosophical claims in the model answer and flag the single point your least sure of.
