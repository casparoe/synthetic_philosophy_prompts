A popular guide to ethics I have been reading offers the following summary of Kant's universalizability tests:

Kant’s basic test is the categorical imperative: act only on a maxim you could will to be a universal law. To apply it, you take the rule behind your action and ask whether everyone could act on it. If the universalized rule is self-defeating—as with a false promise, since universal false promising would destroy the institution of promising—then the maxim cannot be universalized, and the action is wrong. Kant calls this a contradiction in conception. Where the maxim can be universalized but you could not will it as a universal law, you get a contradiction in the will, which is the weaker kind. The humanity formula—treat humanity always as an end, never merely as a means—is not a separate test; it is just the same universalizability requirement stated in different words. The upshot is that Kant gives us a single decision procedure: if the maxim fails universalization, the action is impermissible no matter what consequences might follow, and if it passes, the action is at least permissible.
