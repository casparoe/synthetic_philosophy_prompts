I've been following the adaptationism fights on and off since I was an undergrad — the spandrels paper and the pile of replies it got, Dennett's cranes-and-skyhooks framing in Darwin's Dangerous Idea, the whole extended-evolutionary-synthesis dustup a few years back, and then the exact same argument showing up in a different register in a reading group I go to. What bugs me is that I've read a fair amount of both sides by now and I genuinely can't tell what they're still disagreeing about. Nobody disputes the genetics. Nobody disputes that selection happens. Nobody disputes that some traits are byproducts rather than adaptations. And yet the two camps keep landing on each other like magnets: after twenty minutes it's always you're telling just-so stories versus you're invoking a skyhook, and neither accusation has any bite, because the accused just says no, that isn't what I said, and we reset. The one time I thought we'd get somewhere, someone pointed out that the San Marco features in the original example are actually pendentives and that Dennett's reply is that pendentives were picked over corbels and squinches for aesthetic reasons — so the paradigm case itself is contested, and each side seems to be generalizing from a different example while agreeing on the biology.

My working hypothesis, and I'd like you to stress-test it rather than just agree: greedy reductionism is doing at least two jobs in these fights and nobody separates them. One is metaphysical — the system really is nothing over and above its parts, so higher-level talk has no explanatory work left to do. The other is normative-methodological — sure, the parts are all there is, but the parts-story isn't automatically the better explanation, and demanding one for every trait is a research-programme mistake, not a factual error. If that's right, then the skyhook accusation answers the metaphysical claim while the critic was making the methodological one, and vice versa, which would explain why nobody ever concedes anything. Underneath that there might also be a real question I can't tell is empirical or conceptual: is adaptationism a testable thesis about the world or a heuristic about how to spend your time, and is there even a well-defined null model for a given trait, so that a selection story can be said to win rather than just win by walkover because nobody built the rival model? To keep this concrete, two cases I'd like the diagnosis tested on: in the group we've been going around for a year on why humans have chins — one person says selection for something, another says byproduct of the skull and jaw shrinking at different rates, and we're stuck in exactly the same loop — and separately I've watched the identical shape of argument about whether a big model's in-context learning is a genuinely new mechanism or just gradient descent in disguise, with everyone accusing everyone else of skyhooks.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a substantive disagreement about the world, a verbal one about how the word explanation is being used, two groups answering different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, or bedrock intuitions about what counts as understanding that neither side can argue for? Engage what each side actually says — the adaptationist who thinks the critics are just uncomfortable with the conclusion, the pluralist who thinks the adaptationist's stories are unfalsifiable — and tell me which parts of my hypothesis survive and which are wrong. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a null model, a formal result, an experiment, or a change of question, or an honest argument that nothing will move this and why. And say whether the same diagnosis carries over to the chins case and the ML case or whether those are a different kind of impasse wearing similar clothes.
