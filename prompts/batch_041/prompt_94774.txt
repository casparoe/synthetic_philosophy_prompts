Please compare two ideas/concepts/theories.

Here is the prompt text:

A colleague in my decision-theory reading group has been arguing that we are all irrational, and I cannot find the flaw in his reasoning. He does not claim that people fail to maximize expected value; he claims that expected value maximization itself, once you take it seriously and extend it to the kinds of stakes that actually matter, gives verdicts no one can accept. I have written out his argument as carefully as I can, and I would like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

His setup is this. Some outcomes are good without limit, and some are bad without limit; he calls these the unbounded outcomes. His examples are deliberately mundane, so as not to lean on any particular theology: an open-ended future of flourishing, a self-replicating project that goes on forever, an open-ended future of ruin, and so on. He insists the argument needs only that we can make sense of an outcome whose value has no finite upper bound, and of comparing acts by their expected value.

The argument:

(1) The correct criterion of rational choice is: prefer act A to act B just in case the expected value of A exceeds the expected value of B.
(2) The expected value of an act is the probability-weighted sum of the values of its possible outcomes.
(3) If an act has a positive probability p > 0 of an unboundedly good outcome, then its expected value is unboundedly large; a positive probability times a value with no finite upper bound has no finite upper bound.
(4) Likewise, if an act has a positive probability of an unboundedly bad outcome, its expected value is unboundedly low.
(5) Any act with unboundedly large expected value outranks every act with merely finite expected value; any act with unboundedly low expected value is outranked by every act with merely finite expected value.
(6) Therefore, if there is any act available to me, however costly in finite terms, that raises the probability of the unboundedly good outcome by any amount at all, that act outranks every act whose consequences are confined to finite goods and harms.
(7) Therefore I am rationally required to devote my entire life to such acts, sacrificing every finite good — career, relationships, health, even the finite welfare of others — whenever doing so raises that probability by even an infinitesimal amount.
(8) By (4) and (5), the same reasoning runs in reverse: any act with a positive probability of the unboundedly bad outcome is outranked by every finite act, so I am rationally required to avoid it no matter what finite good it secures.
(9) Now consider my own continued existence. Over an open-ended future, my existence contributes some positive probability to the unboundedly bad outcome, since I might do, or cause, terrible things. By (8) this makes continuing to exist outranked by every finite alternative, including acts that end my existence.
(10) A criterion of choice that requires me to sacrifice every finite good for an infinitesimal chance of an unbounded good, and that also tells me I should not exist, is a criterion no one can accept.
(11) Therefore (1) is false, and expected value maximization is not the correct criterion of rational choice.

I would rather not give up (1), and I am not especially moved by (10) on its own; I have heard it said that a theory being hard to live by is no argument against it. What I want to know is whether the argument is really as tight as it looks. So: which single step should I reject, why did it look unobjectionable, and what does rejecting it commit me to? In particular, tell me what happens to each of (2) through (9) once I abandon the step you name, and whether there is a version of expected value maximization that survives — one that keeps the core idea that rational choice is sensitive to probabilities and values, but does not deliver (6) through (9). If you think the argument is valid and the price is simply paid elsewhere, say so plainly and tell me where the bill comes due.
