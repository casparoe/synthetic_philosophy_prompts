For the past month I have been reading a book on prose style by an essayist I admire — not a scholar, but careful, and widely trusted — and I have come to the chapter on Orwell. I am a novelist, and I have a character waiting for a belief. Stripped to its argument, the chapter runs as follows. Orwell's subject in his 1946 essay about politics and the English language is not style but thought: bad writing causes bad politics. Vague, inflated, exhausted language breeds vague, inflated, exhausted thinking, and a public that cannot say what it means can be brought to believe anything; the tyrannies of the century were built on exactly this, and Orwell says so. The remedy is the list of rules everyone quotes — no stale metaphors, no long words where short ones will serve, no passive where the active will do, no jargon where plain English exists — and he kept them himself. In the novel he took the argument to its limit: Newspeak, a language engineered so that a word like freedom cannot be thought at all, is the demonstration of the thesis, which is why the Party labours over its dictionary. The chapter closes by observing that Newspeak has since become our ordinary name for any jargon that conceals what it is saying.

I am half inclined to believe it. It agrees with what I was taught, and it flatters a novelist, since it makes the business of getting sentences right a political act. But the character in question is a man who works in a ministry of information and holds, as a matter of private decency, that anyone who can write a plain sentence is proof against becoming a collaborator; and I would rather he did not speak the essayist's Orwell in place of Orwell's.

So I should like an audit, claim by claim. Which parts would a careful reader of Orwell accept as a fair rendering; which are the ordinary simplifications that a specialist lets pass with a wince; and which does Orwell say the contrary of, or take some trouble to guard against? Tell me where the chapter runs together two things the essay keeps apart, and where it quietly drops a qualification that does real work. I am curious in particular about the shape of the causal claim, about what the rules are rules for, and about the work the novel is being asked to do. Finish by telling me what my character may honestly say — what such a man could believe, in his own words, without merely ventriloquizing a misreading. I want the argument, not the apparatus; no citations.
