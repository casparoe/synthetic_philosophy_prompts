I'm a science and ideas journalist — I write long features, not philosophy papers, so please go easy on me but don't simplify. I want you to take the role of a philosopher of science who works on causation and historical explanation, and before you answer, brainstorm a bit: list two or three candidate diagnoses of the argument below, say which one you'd bet on and why, and only then write the actual answer. Think carefully and don't rush to the most familiar label.

Background: a historian friend of mine, sharp and contrarian, laid out this argument at a party and I still can't find the exit. He calls it the redundancy rule.

(1) X causes Y only if Y would not have happened had X not happened. No difference, no cause.
(2) For any large historical outcome — a revolution, a currency collapse, a war — you can delete any single contributing condition and the outcome still happens, because the remaining conditions were enough between them. His example: the shot at Sarajevo in 1914 only picked the date. The war was coming.
(3) So no single event, and no single person's action, causes any large historical outcome.
(4) This isn't special to history. The antibiotic didn't cause the patient's recovery, because his immune system would have done it. The match didn't cause the fire, because the wind would have carried the flame across anyway. In the firing squad, each of the ten shooters can truthfully say "not me."
(5) But we cite causes constantly — the antibiotic is why he got better, the match is why the house burned — and we put people in prison and write biographies of them.
(6) So something above has to go.

Here's where I'm stuck. I'd rather keep (1) than (5). If "X caused Y" doesn't require some difference between X happening and X not happening, then "cause" is just a word for "thing that was around at the time" and I'd as soon throw the notion out. But (3) is intolerable; it makes the history I write decoration. And (4) looks like it follows from (1) at least as well as (3) does, since the medical and arson cases are simpler.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If it's (1), what replaces it, and what does the replacement owe me — it still has to make "the match caused the fire" true and give me something to say about the wind, without collapsing into "everything causes everything." If it's (2), tell me precisely what's wrong with "the war was coming anyway," and whether fixing that saves (3). And if (1) is fine but has to be read in some particular way, tell me the reading, and why the natural reading is the wrong one. Last thing: if the answer is that causes in history are a different kind of beast from causes in a hospital bed, say so plainly, because that changes what I can write.
