Me and a friend have been at this for about a year now: does a chatbot actually believe things, or is belief-talk about it just a useful fiction? I'm the realist. My whole position is basically Dennett's intentional stance -- you predict a system by attributing beliefs and desires to it and assuming it's roughly rational, and when that works as spectacularly as it does for modern language models, the pattern you're latching onto is a real feature of the thing, not just a story in the interpreter's head. Shes the instrumentalist: she'll happily use the same predictions, but she thinks there's no inner state with content doing the work and nothing it's like to be the system, so the whole vocabulary is scaffolding we should throw away once we've climbed it.

We've stopped making progress by arguing, so I want us to write the joint statement we should have produced months ago. Concretely, I want:

- the claims we can both sign: about how good the intentional-stance predictions for LLMs actually are, about what that predictive success does and doesn't establish, about what each of us thinks is going on inside, and about what neither of us is claiming (e.g. that either of us denies humans have beliefs, or that either of us thinks belief-talk is useless for humans);
- the precise disagreements, each one phrased the way its own side would want it phrased, not in the other side's caricature;
- the cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one of us -- and for each, a case or piece of evidence we'd both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, plus what each of us would count as a reason to give up our own side.

Some things I want addressed, because I think they're where we keep sliding past each other:

- whether calling the attributed pattern real is doing metaphysical work or only pragmatic work, and whether that particular disagreement is genuine or just a fight over the word real;
- whether the same criteria that give a language model beliefs also give them to a thermostat, a well-tuned lookup table, a chess engine, a corporation, an ant colony. I think the answer is yes, in degrees, and that this is fine and not embarrassing. She thinks it's a reductio. Write down what each of us is actually committed to here.
- the fact that the thing was trained on human text, so its belief-ish states are inherited from ours rather than arrived at. Does that bear on whether the attributions are true, or only on how the states got there?
- how much to make of the instability: flip the framing of a question and its stated belief flips; ask it about its own weights and it says things its behaviour contradicts. Is that evidence there's no belief there to be real, or just evidence that real patterns needn't be stable in the way we assumed?
- whether the system's own reports about what it believes should count as evidence at all, and if so how much.

If part of this turns out to be purely verbal -- the two of us using belief for two different things and never noticing -- say which part, and don't soften it to make the collaboration look tidier than it is.

Format: a numbered list of agreed claims, then a table for the disagreements (each row: the crux, my side's wording, her side's wording), then the cruxes with the shared evidence and the two predictions. Last line on its own: Verdict: genuine, partly verbal, or verbal, plus a few words of why.
