I'm a second-year student taking a course on ethics, and a friend and I have spent the last few weeks arguing about the unit on moral luck without either of us managing to say what we actually think. My trouble is that I seem to hold several attitudes toward luck at once, and they pull in different directions. I'd like you to help me find the principle, if there is one, that best fits the verdicts I actually have, rather than a principle I would have to talk myself into.

Here are the verdicts I keep coming back to. First, two hospital porters each forget to raise a patient's bed rail through the same careless slip; in one ward nothing comes of it, in the other the patient falls and breaks a hip. I want to say the two are exactly equally at fault, and yet I would discipline the unlucky one more harshly, and I would expect the unlucky one to be haunted by the lapse in a way the other simply is not. Second, a careful driver who obeys every rule still has a child dart into the road and cannot stop in time. I don't think this driver is blameworthy at all, and yet I think they owe the family something, and that some weight should rightly stay with them. Third, two people each walk out on a dependent to chase a long-shot ambition; one succeeds and is celebrated, the other fails and is condemned, and I notice that my gut appraises the choice itself differently in the two cases even though the choice was identical — which I suspect is a mistake but cannot quite disown. Fourth, someone born with a cruel temperament and someone born gentle each commit the same cruel act, and I blame them equally. Fifth, and this is the one that trips me up: someone who grows generous despite a brutal upbringing and someone who grows generous because they had devoted parents arrive at the same place, and yet I admire the first more — even though neither chose the soil they grew in.

Is there a principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures this pattern? It seems to me that my verdicts separate how blameworthy someone is from what we owe them and how we should feel about them, and that I treat luck differently when it bears on blame than when it bears on admiration; I'd like to know whether that asymmetry is defensible or just sentimentality I should drop. If no single principle fits, please commit to the simplest one you can, tell me exactly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and say what it predicts for a further case I haven't raised. I would rather have a definite position, argued for, than a survey of the options.
