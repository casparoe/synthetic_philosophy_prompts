Ok so I need a dialogue written for my third-year tutorial on the levels-of-selection debate, and I'm a bit bored of the usual gene's-eye versus group-selection punch-up that everyone already knows by heart. So here's a specific case, and I want the two speakers to really go at each other over it.

The case: a colonial microbe. Every generation, colonies get founded by scooping N cells at random out of a big well-mixed global pool of free-swimming cells, so a colony's founding cells are mostly unrelated to one another. Inside a colony, some cells make an enzyme that breaks down a toxin in the surrounding water. Making the enzyme costs the cell energy, so within any given colony the freeloaders (the cells that don't bother) divide faster than the producers. But colonies with lots of producers grow bigger and release far more cells back into the pool than colonies full of freeloaders do. One more wrinkle: the cells aren't perfectly shuffled — because daughter cells tend to stick together as they're released, a small fraction of a new colony's founders tend to be close relatives of each other.

Speaker A thinks this is straightforwardly a case of group selection: colonies vary in productivity, that variation is caused by their composition, and that's between-group selection, full stop — you can see it sitting right there as the between-group term in the Price equation.

Speaker B thinks that's a redescription dressed up as an explanation: because colony composition is basically a random draw each generation, there's next to no heritable variation *between* colonies for selection to get a grip on, so nothing accumulates at the group level, and everything that happens is just selection on the enzyme gene in a structured population. Calling it group selection, B says, adds nothing and misleads.

I want a real arguement, not a seminar where they politely restate their positions. B has to deal with the fact that the group-level effects are causally real and genuinely change the dynamics. A has to deal with the fact that random reassembly each generation seems to gut the heritability that group selection needs, and with the sticky-daughter wrinkle, which cuts both ways. Neither one is a strawman — both are sharp, both know the biology, and they should actually pick up and answer each other's specific moves. They don't have to reach agreement, but by the end the disagreement should be sharper than it was at the start, and it should be clear exactly what they're now disagreeing *about* — a causal process, or an explanatory posit?
