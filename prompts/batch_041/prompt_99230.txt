I am a journalist who writes about governance, development and ideas, and I have spent the past year trying to work out what I actually believe about gifts and corruption by going case by case on my gut. I have no settled view on whether the anti-corruption norms that Western donors, NGOs and intergovernmental bodies have promoted since the 1990s are universal or merely parochial, and I suspect my own verdicts already commit me to an answer without my having noticed. What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that my reactions are tracking; where the principle forces me to give up a verdict; and what it implies about the universality question.

These are my reactions. Please take them as data rather than as views I am defending.

- A hospital consultant accepts a branded pen and a sandwich lunch from a pharmaceutical representative at a conference. I do not think this is wrong at all.
- The same consultant accepts an all-expenses-paid week at a resort to deliver a talk that is in fact a sales event, plus a speaker's fee. I think this is clearly wrong, and worse than the pen by more than the difference in value.
- A nurse accepts two hundred pounds in cash from a grateful patient's family on the day of discharge. Wrong.
- The same nurse accepts a cake the family baked. Not wrong, and I cannot see that the cake's being homemade rather than bought does much work here.
- A planning officer accepts a case of wine from a developer whose application she is currently assessing. Wrong.
- The same officer accepts a case of wine from a neighbour at Christmas, with no application pending. Not wrong.
- A village headman in a district where the state barely functions accepts a goat from a family whose land dispute he is about to adjudicate. This troubles me, but I find it markedly less wrong than the planning officer's case of wine, even though the goat is worth far more to him in relative terms than the wine is to her.
- A legislator's aide accepts a paid speaking engagement at a company's annual conference while the legislator sits on a committee that regulates the company. Wrong.
- A journalist accepts a source's hospitality, a weekend at his house, while writing a profile of him. Wrong.
- An official in a country where seasonal gifts between business contacts are close to obligatory accepts the customary gift from a company she regulates; the gift is socially expected, publicly given, and in practice partly passed on to her staff. I genuinely do not know what to say.
- A student gives her lecturer a bottle of whisky at graduation. Fine.
- The same student gives the lecturer the same bottle a week before the examination. Wrong.

What I want:

1. A principle, or a small number of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to be applied, with the specific case that motivates each clause. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I am asking what would capture this pattern.
2. An honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which I should give up if I want the principle simple rather than gerrymandered. If a threshold on value gets the pen and the resort trip but makes the wrongness a matter of degree where I feel it is a matter of kind, say so.
3. Whether the principle must locate the wrongness in the exchange itself, in its secrecy, in the expectation of reciprocity it creates, in the effect on third parties who lose out, or in a breach of a role-based duty of impartiality — and whether these come apart on any of my cases.
4. Its verdicts on cases I have not listed: an official who accepts a gift that must be entered on a public register; a doctor who accepts a gift from a family after the patient has died; a firm that makes a small payment to move a routine licence application to the front of the queue; and a gift given openly and reciprocated in kind years later, when neither party has any stake in the other's decisions.
5. Finally, the universality question. If the principle I end up with condemns the village headman, then either my verdict on him or my verdict on the planning officer has to go, and I want to know which you would abandon and why. If instead it vindicates him by making the norm relative to what a gift means locally, tell me what that does to the rest of my verdicts — in particular whether I can still say the Western transparency regime is right to treat the obligatory seasonal gift as corrupt. Say plainly whether the norm you have reconstructed is a local convention I happen to have internalised or something that applies to any public role anywhere, and which of my cases would have to look different for you to change your mind.
