The Halvorsen Institute, where I chair the community-standards subcommittee, hosts about forty visiting fellows a year, most of them from a country whose everyday conventions around lining up, crossing the street, and tipping differ sharply from ours. Three matters are now before us. First, several fellows habitually drift out of the single serpentine service line at the institute cafeteria, joining it wherever it happens to be when they arrive; the cafeteria staff and the fellows who have been waiting call this queue-jumping, and because there is one line it plainly changes who is served when. Second, the fellows routinely cross the four-lane road between the two institute buildings at grade rather than walk two hundred meters to the pedestrian bridge; the crossing is technically unlawful, virtually every local employee does it too, and the city has only recently begun issuing the occasional citation. Third, our café is run by a contractor that pays its baristas a base wage comfortably above the statutory minimum, and the fellows, following the custom they grew up with, leave nothing in the tip jar; the baristas say the omission reads as a judgment on their service, while the fellows say a tip tied to nothing is simply a demand for money they have no reason to give.

Our mandate cuts both ways: the charter commits us to fostering an inclusive community in which visitors are guests rather than supplicants, and separately to conducting our affairs in accordance with the laws and the reasonable expectations of the surrounding community. A staff petition asks us to require all fellows to observe local conventions on pain of losing cafeteria privileges; a counter-petition signed by roughly half the fellows says we would be conscripting them into customs they never agreed to, and that courtesy is all anyone can owe a stranger. Two members have already floated candidate rules. One proposes an exit test: a local convention binds a visitor just in case the visitor could opt out of it without making the practice unworkable for those who rely on it. The other proposes a reciprocity condition: visitors owe compliance only with conventions we would ourselves observe were we the guests.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — one disposition for each of the three practices, with a time frame for implementation — and give the reasoning that supports it. Two things I want it to do explicitly. First, it should not treat the three practices as interchangeable: say what, if anything, distinguishes the line, the crossing, and the tip, and whether any of the three is a genuine moral obligation rather than a matter of etiquette. Second, it should engage the fellows' objection in its strongest form rather than dismissing it as cultural insensitivity; if some conventions do bind visitors, say what makes them bind, and if the honest answer is that it depends, give a criterion a reader could actually apply to a fourth case we have not seen. Assume the reader has no patience for a bare appeal to local custom, or for the claim that every custom is as valid as any other, as a stopping point — and treat the two proposed rules on their merits rather than by fiat.
