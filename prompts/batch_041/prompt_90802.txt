I'm trying to write a short story for a philosophy-themed fiction night, and I want it to be about the Judy Benjamin problem—you know, the one where a soldier is dropped into enemy territory and thinks there's a 1/3 chance she's in the Red Army area and a 2/3 chance she's in the Blue Army area, and within Blue she thinks it's 1/4 headquarters company and 3/4 second company. Then she gets a radio message: if you are in the Blue Army area, you are in the headquarters company. The puzzle is how she should update her beliefs. If she treats the message as a material conditional and conditionalizes, she ends up thinking it's 2/3 likely she's in Red, which feels crazy because the message is about Blue's internal structure and shouldn't change her Red/Blue odds. But if she tries to only change the Blue-internal probabilities, she has to figure out what rule to use, and different rules give different answers.

I want a story that dramatizes this, maybe with a soldier who is also a philosophy grad student, or a radio operator who is a Bayesian, and the tension between the intuitive response and the formal one. Set it in a fictional war, maybe in a mountainous region, with rain and bad radio reception. The story should be short, like 800-1200 words, and it should make the reader feel the vertigo of not knowing how to update on a conditional. Don't have the characters solve it definitively; I want the puzzle to linger. Also, maybe include a moment where she realizes that her prior probabilities themselves are just guesses, which adds another layer. I'm not looking for a textbook explanation, just a narrative that embodies the problem.
