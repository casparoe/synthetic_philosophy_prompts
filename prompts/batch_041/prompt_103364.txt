I'm in a seminar on well-being and public policy, we just finished the unit on interpersonal comparisons, and the final has a weird creative component where I have to turn something in as a poem. Can you act like a philosopher who actually writes poetry and think it through carefully before you commit to anything? I want a sestina, real sestina rules — six end-words cycling in the proper order, closing envoi — with the six end-words being the entire vocabulary of comparison: more, less, same, better, worse, enough. The scene is two roommates who keep a fairness spreadsheet where each of them rates their day out of ten and they average the two, and the voice is one of them slowly realizing the average is meaningless because the numbers sit on two different scales. I want the form itself to carry the argument: making every single line end on one of those six words is exactly the assumption that a comparison across two people needs and can never earn, so the poem should feel the strain of being forced into a shared currency, not just talk about it. The envoi should be where it finally gets said out loud, since thats the one place you have to cram three of the words into a line. Someone who's done the social choice unit should recognize the problem right away, so no need to name anyone in the poem. And please dont let it turn into a lecture with line breaks — it still has to be a good poem.
