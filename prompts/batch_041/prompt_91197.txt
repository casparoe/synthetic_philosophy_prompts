I'm partway into a seminar paper for a course on medieval philosophy, and I've talked myself into a thesis I can't yet defend. The standard story about Ghazālī on causation — the one I keep hearing, and the one that makes him the Islamic Hume — is that he denies that anything in the world has the power to produce anything else: fire does not burn, God does, and all we ever observe is that the burning occurs alongside the fire, never that it must. He then appeals to God's habit or customary ordering to explain why the world is nonetheless predictable, and Ibn Rushd's reply is that this reduces nature to a heap of disconnected events and makes knowledge of it impossible. What I want to argue is that the standard story smuggles in a metaphysical claim — that God is the sole agent of every effect — which Ghazālī's actual argument does not establish and which he reaches only by way of the notion of habit. Press on what habit could be and I think he faces a dilemma. Either God's habit is a fact about God's will, in which case the regularities we depend on are contingent all the way down, and he owes us an account of why our expectations are reliable that does not quietly borrow from the philosophers he is attacking; or it is a fact about how fire and cotton are, in which case something very much like a real disposition in the created thing has reappeared under another name and the philosophers' point has been conceded in new vocabulary.

Please argue for the second horn: that the most defensible Ghazālī is not an occasionalist but something closer to a dispositionalist who denies only necessity, holding that bodies have genuine causal powers whose exercise is neither necessary in itself nor guaranteed, and that this is the only reading on which his example of the man who has never seen fire applied to cotton does any work, since what that man lacks is a grasp of a real tendency rather than a mere record of conjunctions. Say clearly whether this reading rescues him from Ibn Rushd's charge of sophistry, and at what price — in particular, whether the commitments he is otherwise unwilling to surrender, God's untrammelled freedom, no genuine efficacy in creatures, and the standing possibility that the customary order be interrupted at any moment, can survive the concession that bodies have tendencies of their own. Then take the strongest objection to my thesis, which I take to be that any account on which created things contribute to their effects either limits God's power or renders the miracles Ghazālī needs inexplicable, and tell me honestly whether it defeats the reading or can be answered, and what the answer costs him. I don't need references; I'll add the scholarly apparatus myself. What I want is the argument pressed hard on both sides, ending with a verdict on whether Ghazālī is better off as a dispositionalist or as the occasionalist of the textbook.
