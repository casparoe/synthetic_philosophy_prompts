I'm in a poli-sci seminar on democratic theory and we just did a week on epistemic democracy, so I keep bumping into the Condorcet jury theorem in other reading. Last night I hit a chapter in a popular book on the history of ideas (the author is an essayist, not a formal person) that summed it up like this, and I copied the gist into my notes: "Condorcet's jury theorem proves that as long as each member of a group is even slightly more likely than not to get the answer right, the majority is more likely to be right than any individual member -- including the smartest one -- and the larger the group, the more certain this becomes, so a big enough electorate is effectively guaranteed to reach the correct verdict. That's the mathematical backbone of the wisdom of crowds: the same fact that explains why the median of many peoples guesses at an ox's weight beats the experts, and why prediction markets work. The theorem does assume voters are independent, which never holds in real life, so strictly speaking it applies to no actual electorate; the practical upshot for real elections is Condorcet's related insight that the candidate who beats every other candidate head-to-head should win."

I think the shape is right but three things are nagging at me and I'd like an audit, not a lecture.
