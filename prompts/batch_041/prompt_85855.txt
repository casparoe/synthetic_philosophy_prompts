I've been arguing with a friend about the two arguments that relativity is supposed to give us against there being anything objectively special about the present, and I want you to actually compare them rather than just summarising both. The first is the simultaneity one: two observers in relative motion slice spacetime differently, each has their own set of events that count as "now", so there's no single global present. Rietdijk and Putnam were both pushing versions of this in the sixties, and Penrose's Andromeda business is the version that actually makes people sit up. The second is Gödel's, from his rotating solutions to the Einstein equations in 1949: he built a spinning universe full of closed timelike curves and, I think, with no way to slice the whole thing into simultaneous moments the way you can in ordinary Minkowski spacetime, and he took that to tell against the objectivity of time (or at least against time as an objective lapse) and to point towards something like an idealist conclusion.

My friend's line is that these are the same argument in different clothes: Gödel just did the simultaneity argument with better mathematics, and the closed timelike curves are a red herring, since either way what's doing the work is the absence of a privileged global now. I dont buy it but I can't articulate why.

So what's the actual relationship? Are the two even aiming at the same conclusion — no global present versus no objective becoming or flow — and does that difference matter? Does Gödel need the closed timelike curves at all, or would the failure of global simultaneity in his universe do all the work by itself? Relatedly: are "no global now" and "no global time function" the same complaint or two different ones? I suspect that's where my friend and I are talking past each other.

Then the standard objection to the Rietdijk–Putnam version, that it's a mistake to demand a global present because "now" is a local notion attached to an observer or a worldline: does that objection bite on Gödel just as hard, or does he have a way round it? And tell me which of the two, if either, is really the stronger argument, and whether it would survive as an argument if we decided tomorrow that the Gödel universe isn't physically realised.

Be explicit about how confident you are in the comparison you give me, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
