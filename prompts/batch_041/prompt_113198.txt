I am a novelist, and I am writing a book whose central character — an engineer — must decide whether to sign off on the release of a machine-learning system. For the scene in which she makes that decision, I want a thought experiment built to order rather than borrowed, and I would like you to construct it.

The subject is the divergence between worst-case and average-case guarantees in AI alignment. I want the divergence to run in the direction that is hardest to make vivid: the worst-case analysis should be the falsely reassuring one, and the average-case reasoning should be the one that correctly locates the danger.

A few constraints on the scenario. It should be realistic — a hospital, a bank, a school district, a content platform — and fit within a single paragraph. The system must be something a present-day model could plausibly be, and the harm must arise from ordinary statistical structure, not from the system being secretly adversarial, deceptive, or optimizing against its designers; I do not want the tired plot in which the machine is plotting. The worst-case guarantee must be genuine: it should really hold over the perturbations it claims to cover, so that its failure is not the trivial failure of a weak bound.

After the scenario, do the following. State the system, its objective, the threat model the worst-case guarantee is taken over, and the distribution it will actually face in deployment. Give the verdict each analysis returns and the reasoning behind it. Then identify the single feature of the case that makes the two come apart, and say what happens if that feature is removed — whether the analyses collapse into agreement, and if so, at what cost. Then give the strongest reply available to a defender of the worst-case approach, in particular the reply that the fault lies with whoever specified the threat model rather than with worst-case reasoning as such, and tell me whether that reply succeeds. Finish with a sentence or two on what my engineer is actually choosing between, phrased so that I could put the choice in her mouth without her sounding like a textbook.
