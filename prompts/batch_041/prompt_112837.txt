I was once persuaded that the Church–Turing thesis has, for all practical purposes, been proved. My reasoning was this: Turing showed that his machines compute exactly the lambda-definable functions, and every other model of computation anyone has seriously proposed — register machines, Markov algorithms, Post systems, cellular automata — has turned out to compute exactly the same class of functions. When so many independent formalizations converge on a single class, I thought, the claim that this class exhausts the effectively calculable functions can hardly remain a mere conjecture; it begins to look as secure as a theorem. Yet I keep meeting philosophers who insist that the thesis is not a theorem and is not even the sort of thing that could be proved, and others who warn that it might fail for quantum computers. Is this merely a verbal dispute — is the word "thesis" just a cautious label for a fact we have established many times over — or does the convergence of all these models leave a substantive gap open? If there is a real gap, what exactly remains unsettled, and what would it take to close it? Concretely: suppose someone built a device that decided the halting problem for ordinary Turing machines. Would that refute the Church–Turing thesis, or would it merely show that we had misidentified which functions count as effectively calculable? And in what sense, if any, does a quantum computer bear on the thesis — I have the vague impression that it threatens the thesis by computing things a Turing machine cannot, but I cannot tell whether that is a confusion about speed versus computability, or something deeper.
