I have been reading about Aumann's agreement theorem, and it seems to imply something troubling about my debate club. My understanding is that the theorem says that two rational agents who share all their evidence and whose disagreement is common knowledge cannot continue to disagree. But my friend and I shared all our evidence about whether a certain policy is just, we both know that we still disagree, and we have even announced our disagreement in front of the group and acknowledged each other's acknowledgment. So either one of us must be irrational, or genuine common knowledge is impossible in practice. Is that the right conclusion? If not, where exactly does my reasoning go wrong? I have heard that the theorem assumes a common prior, but that seems like a technicality: if we really have shared all our evidence, shouldn't our priors wash out anyway? And even if the theorem is about probabilities rather than moral judgments, doesn't the same point apply to any disagreement, including moral ones? Could you explain what the theorem actually assumes and what it does and does not imply about real disagreements?
