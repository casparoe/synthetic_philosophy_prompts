I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy (my thesis is on something else entirely, but I keep getting dragged back to consent) and for about a year I've kept a note on my phone called 'yes but does it count,' where I write down consent cases and my gut verdict on each. I've got enough of them now that I think there's a principle underneath, and I also think at least two of my verdicts can't both survive. Can you tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually generates the list, which entries it forces me to drop, and what it says about a case I haven't thought of? I'm not after a survey of the literature, I want the shape of the view.

The list, verdicts included:

- Guy with a knife: wallet or I stab you. Doesn't count.
- Blizzard: a homeowner lets a stranded driver in only if she signs her car over to him first. He had no duty to open the door at all. Doesn't count.
- Refugee camp: a woman with no papers and no prospects is offered resettlement and a job if she donates a kidney to a stranger in the host country. Nobody made the camp worse, the offer only adds an option to a terrible set. Counts.
- Vasectomy: he's had one, doesn't mention it, and I'm sleeping with him on the understanding that a pregnancy is possible. Doesn't count.
- False love: he tells me he wants a life with me, purely to get me into bed. I find it disgusting, but it counts.
- The biopsy: I agree to let the named consultant do it and a supervised first-year resident does it instead. Counts.
- Manic: a friend, mid-episode, grandiose as hell, able to recite the risks back to you word for word, signs up for an expensive elective thing she'd never touch otherwise. I keep flipping on this one. Lean: doesn't count.
- Advance directive: she signed a refusal of ventilation, is now awake and terrified and asking for it. Her new yes counts, the old document doesnt bind her.
- Sterilisation: my cousin has a mild intellectual disability, her mother 'explains' the form to her over the phone, she signs. Doesn't count.

My provisional principle has two clauses: a yes counts if (1) she knew what she was agreeing to, and (2) nobody made her worse off in order to get it. (1) does the deception work and mostly feels fine, though it makes me uneasy that false love passes and vasectomy fails when she'd have refused in both. (2) is where it falls apart. Blizzard and refugee camp are the same structure -- the person with the power worsened nothing, just named a price for something she had no right to -- and I want opposite verdicts. The only difference I can point to is that in the blizzard refusing means freezing to death, while in the camp refusing means a bad life that just goes on. Which sounds like I'm pricing how bad the alternative is, i.e. coercion comes in degrees and 'valid' is a threshold on a scale. Is that a real position or a fudge? If it's real, where's the threshold and why there? And do the manic and advance-directive cases look different if competence works the same way -- a threshold on a scale rather than a switch?

Two seperate things I'd like you to settle. Give me a case I haven't listed that the principle decides in a way I won't like, and if the honest answer is that some of these yeses are valid but obtained wrongly (so the act is still off-limits) rather than invalid, say that -- I'd definately rather have the split than a unified story that wrecks four of my verdicts.
