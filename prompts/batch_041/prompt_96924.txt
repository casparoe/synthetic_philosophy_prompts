Explanations that assign epidemics, volcanic eruptions, and climate shocks a causal role in human history — a famine, a revolt, the fall of a state, a shift in religious allegiance — are routinely accused of sliding into environmental determinism, and just as routinely defended on the ground that human arrangements can only be said to cause an outcome against a background supplied by the non-human world. The dispute tends to stall, and it stalls in an interesting place: the two sides usually agree on every fact and disagree only about what the facts amount to.

I would like an example of the conceptual issue that makes the stall so hard to break, worked through a single historical episode. Choose an episode whose details you can reconstruct reliably from general knowledge — an epidemic, an eruption, a run of failed harvests, anything of that kind — and pick another if you find yourself filling gaps rather than recalling. Do not trouble yourself with citations; what I want is the structure of the problem, not a survey of what has been written about it.

Then, for that episode:

- Tell the story twice, as concretely as you can, once with the non-human event carrying the causal weight and once with social, economic, and political arrangements carrying it. Hold every fact fixed between the two tellings. If one telling works only once you add a fact the other does not need, say so explicitly, since that asymmetry is itself a result.
- For each telling, identify the alternative against which the outcome counts as caused at all — the baseline or counterfactual that the telling silently adopts. Where the episode permits, separate the non-human factor's role as a standing background condition, as a trigger that marks a departure from that baseline, and as something closer to a constituent of the outcome, and show where the two tellings place the same event in different roles.
- Say what kind of disagreement this is — empirical, conceptual, or normative — what would have to be settled first for it to be resolved, and whether any part of it could be settled by evidence alone.
- Consider why causal language gravitates toward whatever is abnormal, controllable, or blameworthy, and whether that pull means the choice between the two tellings is doing accusatory or exculpatory work rather than explanatory work.
- Say what is added, and what is smuggled in, when the non-human factor is described as an actor or an agent rather than as a condition or a trigger. Then give your own verdict: is one telling better than the other, or is the pluralist answer — both true at different levels of description — a genuine resolution or an evasion?
