I teach a 75-minute session for about 16 undergrads, mixed econ and philosophy, and I want to run a dollar auction so they feel escalation of commitment in their gut instead of nodding along at the phrase. Problem: the plain version is so well-known that the lesson collapses into "sunk cost fallacy, dont be that guy" — too easy, and I think it's actually the wrong diagnosis. So build in two things. First, a pre-commitment stage: before any bidding, each player writes down a hard maximum and can buy a cheap "bond" (a dollar, or some forfeit they'd really hate) that they lose the moment they bid over their own stated max; then run the auction and let that mechanic do its work. Second, the debrief has to pull apart two things they will absolutely blur: the marginal decision to raise at any given point, which looks exactly like sunk-cost reasoning and isn't — in this game the earlier bids aren't really sunk, since both bidders pay and the loss only lands if you stop — and the structural trap that makes escalating the locally sane move even for someone who's fully internalized that past spending shouldn't matter. What I want them walking out seeing is that the failure is diachronic rather than synchronic: they know the right rule, they just can't bind themselves to it, and I want the bond mechanic to make that concrete, ideally by getting someone to publicly blow past their own bond.

Give me the rules and payoffs, the materials, how to run it with two actual bidders while the other 14 watch (or a way to get everyone involved), a rough timeline, and the debrief mapping what happened back onto the pre-commitment-vs-game-structure distinction. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time — I'd bet on someone refusing to play, or gaming the bond — and how to handle it. And be straight with me about what the game can't reproduce: my guess is it misses the ego/self-justification side, the way real escalation is powered by not wanting to look like the sucker, which I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.
