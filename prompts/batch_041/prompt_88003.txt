I chair the standing science advisory panel of a state environmental health agency — nine members, quarterly meetings, recommendations addressed to the agency director, who is not obliged to follow them but almost always does. At our next meeting we must finally settle the classification of a compound I shall call XR-7, a flame retardant used mainly in furniture foam, which we have now reviewed twice without reaching a conclusion. The animal data show liver tumours in mice and rats, but only at doses close to the maximum tolerated dose, and the tumours come with a well-characterised sequence of enzyme induction, oxidative stress and compensatory cell proliferation that several of my colleagues read as evidence of a threshold mechanism, and hence as evidence that ordinary human exposure carries little risk. The human epidemiological evidence is a mess: three cohort studies with wide confidence intervals, exposure measured by job title in two of them, and a confounder — co-exposure to a second flame retardant present in the same products — that nobody has managed to disentangle. The mechanistic data come from a new in vitro assay that some of us regard as promising and two of us regard as unvalidated. My difficulty is not with the science as such. It is that a colleague on the panel, who spent fifteen years in regulatory toxicology before moving to a university, keeps making a point I cannot dismiss. The same body of evidence, he says, is compatible with more than one underlying mechanism and with more than one dose-response model, so nothing in the data settles whether XR-7 should be called a probable human carcinogen, a possible one, or left unclassified; and this, he insists, is not the sort of gap that more research closes, because the auxiliaries we would need to fix the interpretation are themselves contested. When the evidence underdetermines the answer, he argues, the panel is not choosing a scientific fact at all — it is choosing how to distribute the risk of being wrong, which is to say how many avoidable cancers we are prepared to tolerate in exchange for how much economic disruption and how much loss of credibility when the next compound of this kind turns out to be benign. I think he is right about the logic and wrong about what follows from it, and I need something on paper before Thursday.

Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate to the panel in advance of the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — a classification, a deferral with a date and a specification of the evidence that would settle the matter, or something else — with a time frame, and it should be written so that the director could act on it without further elaboration from us. It must engage my colleague's position seriously rather than dismissing it as an abdication of scientific responsibility: if the classification really is underdetermined by the evidence, say so plainly, and then say what the panel should nevertheless do, including whether we ought to adopt a standing rule for cases of this shape and, if so, what that rule should say about who carries the burden of proof and about how much weight the agency should give to the fact that the exposure is involuntary and largely invisible to the people bearing it. Where you take a position on the science — the threshold question, the weight to give the unvalidated assay, the confounded epidemiology — state what would change your mind. Our panel's practice is to append a short section recording the strongest objection to our own recommendation, so please write that section too, in the voice of the member most likely to sign it. I would also like a brief passage on how the decision should be described to the public, given that someone on the panel will inevitably be quoted in the press saying the evidence proves nothing. Please write the memo in American English, in the register of a formal advisory opinion rather than a scientific review, and keep it to something I can read aloud in fifteen minutes.
