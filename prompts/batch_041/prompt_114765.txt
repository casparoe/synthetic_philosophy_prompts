I'm a backend engineer (Go and Postgres, mostly payments infrastructure) with zero formal philosophy training — one gen-ed ethics course in undergrad and that's it — but I've been running a lunchtime "weird ideas" reading group at work for a couple years and that somehow turned into an invitation to give the main talk at our local Philosophy in the Pub night next month. The crowd there is a very specific mix and I know most of them by now: maybe half are effective-altruism/rationality types who think in QALYs and have read a lot of Parfit and Singer; two bioethics grad students, one of whom works on chronic pain and honestly scares me a little; a retired GP who has watched alot of people die badly and is allergic to anything that smells like a tech fix; and one disability-rights lawyer who shows up every single month and asks the question everyone else is too polite to ask. Last time somebody talked about enhancement she basically ended the Q&A in four minutes. I'd like to not get ended this time.

Here's the thesis I'm defending, and I think it's the only version that survives contact. Everyone lumps this stuff together under one slogan — abolish suffering with biotechnology, I've seen the phrase "the hedonistic imperative" used for it, no idea who made it up — as if the target were just pain. I want to argue the target has to be narrower. There's the part of pain that tells you where it is and how bad, and then there's the part that makes it awful: it grabs your attention, it won't let go, it reorganizes your whole life around managing it. I'll claim the second part is what "suffering" actually names, that the two come apart at least in principle (I'm not claiming anyone can do it yet, only that the goal is even coherent), and that once you say the aim is to kill the awfulness while keeping the signal, a whole pile of standard objections miss — they're aimed at the stronger "no unpleasantness at all" version, which I'm perfectly happy to give up. I'll also bite the bullet that this means deliberately engineering people's affective lives and argue that's less frightening than it sounds. I'm not pretending I have a lab or a timeline; this is a "what should the goal even be" talk, not a roadmap. I'll be honest, I'm not sure the two parts are cleanly seperate in practice, and I don't yet know whether that's a problem for me or just a problem for the engineers.

What I want from you is my Q&A prep sheet. Which objections am I actually going to get from this room — not the ones I'd get at a transhumanist conference or from a journal referee — roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way that particular person would phrase them (the GP and the lawyer will not use the same words, and I need both versions), with a short spoken reply for each and a candid note on where the reply is soft or where I'm quietly changing the subject. Tell me what to concede up front so I don't look like I'm dodging, which objection has the most real force even if it's not the loudest one, and which single exchange I have to win for the talk to count as a success. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. And don't hand me a menu of positions and tell me to choose — take a side. Say which objections I should just accept and eat, which ones I should fight, and where my own view is genuinely weakest, even if it's the part I'm most attached to.
