I'm writing a novel set in the 1820s — one of my characters is a young radical who's fallen in with the Benthamite crowd, and I've been reading around to get the texture right. Last week a friend from my writing group, who did a philosophy degree and never lets anyone forget it, gave me his summary of Bentham's views on sex, and something about it smells off even to me, so I wanted to check before I put any of it in the book. Here's basically what he said: Bentham was the first major philosopher to defend gay rights, way ahead of his time. His argument was that consenting adults should be free to do whatever they want in private, because the state has no business legislating morality — the only legitimate reason for a law is to prevent harm to other people. He thought the laws against homosexuality were a violation of individual liberty, and that any law rooted in religious disgust was illegitimate on its face. So he was basically a proto-Mill, anticipating On Liberty by decades, and the reason none of us learned this in school is that his essays on the subject were considered too scandalous to publish and sat in a drawer for a hundred and fifty years.

I know enough to know that "gay rights" isn't a phrase Bentham would have used, and that the harm principle is more Mill's thing than his, but I don't know where the line is between "my friend is glossing" and "my friend is just wrong." Could you go through his summary claim by claim and tell me what a Bentham scholar would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's flatly false? In particular I want to know whether Bentham's actual case for decriminalizing homosexuality was a liberty-and-consent argument or something else entirely, and whether it's fair to call him a proto-Mill. And if the true story is more interesting or stranger than my friend's version — I get the sense Bentham's views on sex were alot weirder than "let consenting adults do what they like" — I'd love to know that, because honestly the weirder version is better for the novel.
