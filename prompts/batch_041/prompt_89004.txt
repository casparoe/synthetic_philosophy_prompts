Had a long, slightly drunken arguement with my mate Dan on Friday about whether charging interest is inherently sordid or just the fee for a service, and he kept saying Aristotle sorted all this out 2,300 years ago, and I kept saying Aristotle never had a mortgage. I want to see whether the argument looks any different as fiction, so: write me a short story that dramatises Aristotle's thinking about money and exchange. Set it somewhere invented — a fishing village, a hill town, a trading post on a river, whatever you like. No togas, no Athenians, no history lesson.

What I want it built on is the stuff people don't usually quote, because that's where the friction is. Three bits:

First, for Aristotle money is essentially a promise about the future — a guarantee that you'll be able to get what you need later, even though you need nothing right now, which is how it solves the problem that you and I rarely want each other's goods at the same moment. But he also thinks money exists only by convention, by custom or law rather than by nature, so it's a promise the community can rewrite whenever it likes. Those two claims sit badly together and I'd rather the story feel that than smooth it over.

Second, he says a shoe has two uses, wearing it and swapping it, and that swapping a shoe isn't really using it as a shoe — so every act of exchange is faintly a misuse of the thing exchanged. Get that into a scene without anyone explaining it.

Third, he splits getting a living from your own household and land, which has a natural limit, from the kind of moneymaking that aims at money itself and therefore has no limit at all, and he thinks the second kind rots people because it confuses living well with having more. The worst case for him is interest — the very word for it means offspring, and money giving birth to money is the most unnatural of the lot. He's also snide about middlemen, buying cheap in one place and selling dear in another, which he sees as skimming off the exchange itself.

Give both sides a fair hearing, though. I want a lender in there whose case is genuinely strong, and an opponent of lending who is genuinely persuasive, and no verdict at the end — just a particular person making a particular decision.

Constraints: under about 1,500 words. Nobody says the phrase use value or exchange value. Nobody explains Aristotle to anybody else; the ideas have to do their work in what people actually do. And give me one concrete object that gets used both ways at once — worn and traded, eaten and pawned, something like that — so the two uses collide in front of the reader.

One last thing. He's oddly confident that need is what really makes different goods comparable and that money is just the stand-in measure for it. But if need is doing the real work, the measure is arbitrary; if the measure is doing the real work, then need is just whatever the measure says. Either way there's a gap. Try to land the ending on that gap without spelling it out.
