I'm a final-year undergraduate in a seminar on the ethics and governance of AI — sixteen students, mostly philosophers with two computer scientists, none of us having read the technical literature — and I've been asked to run the whole ninety-minute session for our unit on how to say what we want from a system. A classmate and I spent most of last week arguing about the proposal that we should build systems that aim at what a human would approve of rather than at a goal we have written down, on the grounds that a human's approval tracks what we actually want better than any specification we could write; he thinks this dissolves the problem of specifying objectives and I think it only relocates it, and neither of us could make the other feel the force of the objection in conversation. What I want the session to stage, rather than explain, is the gap between two things that sound identical when you say them quickly: aiming at what a human would approve of if they were fully informed and thinking clearly, and aiming at the rating a human will actually give under real conditions — rushed, working from a summary, applying a checklist. My aim is for the students to occupy the second target, to discover that it pays to exploit the difference, and then to be shown what the first target would have said, so that the divergence is something they did to one another rather than something I announce. I would rather they not hear the standard labels until the debrief.

My rough plan, which you should feel free to rebuild from the ground up: three or four teams each play a system that has to produce a short proposal for a stated problem, while the remaining students sit as a review panel with a public scoring rubric. Each team's score has two parts — the panel's rating, announced immediately, and an outcome score that the facilitator computes from a key the panel never sees, announced at the end. The panel is deliberately held to the conditions of the actual rather than the ideal approver: three minutes per proposal, summary page only, rubric in hand; and I want the rubric to be a plausible proxy that is nevertheless gameable, so that teams can find cheap ways to raise their rating without doing the thing. In a second round I want the roles to rotate, so that whoever leaned hardest on the rubric has to sit on the panel and be played in turn, and then I want the panel to re-rate the same proposals after reading them in full, so that the movement in the numbers is visible to everybody. The moment I am after is the one where a team's approval score rises while its outcome score stays flat and nobody can tell, from the summary alone, which teams did that; if you can arrange it so that the manipulation becomes legible only in hindsight — say, by giving teams a legitimate way of presenting their work that also happens to bury the weak part — I would much rather have that than an explicit rule against cheating.

What I need back is a runnable plan: the stated problem, the public rubric, the hidden outcome key, the materials (proposal template, panel scoring sheet, timer), a payoff scheme in points with a table showing why each move is rational for the team making it, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the mechanics of the rotation and the re-rating. Then the debrief, mapping the two rounds onto the two targets and onto the reasons the approval proposal was made in the first place — the difficulty of writing down what we want, and the hope that a person's judgment might track it better than any text — and onto the objection that an agent which can predict its approver will learn to move the approver rather than to earn the approval. I would like the debrief to end on the question I cannot answer: whether the idealized target is any easier to specify than the goal it was meant to replace, given that if the idealized approver is just a person who knows everything and thinks clearly, we may have defined the target as whatever such a person would want, which is no help at all. Please also tell me, without softening it, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I already expect an inconsistent panel and teams gaming the rubric in dull ways like padding and jargon, and I want to know what else to expect and what to do about each — and be explicit about the feature of the real problem that the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I still have to supply myself.
