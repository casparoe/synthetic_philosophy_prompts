I have no formal training in philosophy, but for several months I have been trying to work out an idea that came up in an argument with friends about evolution, markets, and artificial intelligence. The idea is that a system can act for a reason even when no part of the system represents that reason: a vine grows toward a trellis because that brings more light, a market price rises because supply has fallen, a recommendation system keeps people scrolling because that maximizes an engagement score, a firm tightens its safety rules because a regulator might otherwise fine it. In each case there is a rationale that explains the behaviour, but the rationale may be nowhere present as a reason inside the thing doing the behaving. I have been calling these free-floating rationales, and I have been trying to distinguish three versions: one where the rationale is not represented anywhere at all, one where it is represented only in the history that selected for the behaviour, and one where it is represented only in some external designer or institution that shaped the system. I am not looking for a history of the phrase or for reading suggestions; I want to know what follows from taking the idea seriously.

Please give me the most important implications of this idea for how we should describe, predict, and respond to systems that look purposive. I am especially interested in what it implies for when it is legitimate to say that something acts for a reason, has a goal, or is responsible, and how the three versions above differ in their implications. Treat the following as the cases I care about: an evolved plant trait, a market price, an immune response, a reinforcement-learning agent, a large language model producing an after-the-fact justification, and a corporation with a stated policy. Where the implications run out or conflict, say so rather than smoothing them over.

Please return a single JSON object with the following fields. The field bottom_line is a string of no more than five sentences giving your overall verdict. The field implications is an array of seven to nine objects, ordered from most to least important, each with: rank (integer), implication (one sentence), what_it_changes (string), applies_to (string naming the cases above), why_it_follows (string), strongest_objection (string), test_case (string), and confidence (one of high, medium, or low). The field version_differences is an array of three objects, one for each version of free-floating rationale I described, each with: version (string), distinctive_implication (string), and whether_it_collapses_into_one_of_the_others (string). The field unresolved_tension is a string describing the most serious tension your implications leave standing. The field practical_rule is a string giving a rule of thumb someone without a philosophy background could actually use. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to particular authors; I cannot check them and they are not what I am asking for.
