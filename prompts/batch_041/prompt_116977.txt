Two things happened within the same week and I have not been able to reconcile them. The first was a hospital intake form that asked me to select a race and an ethnicity from a fixed list; none of the options matched how anyone in my family describes themselves, yet the form would not proceed without an answer. The second was an argument with a friend who insisted that money is nothing but a social construct and therefore not really real — a claim I found at once obviously true and obviously false, and I could not say why. It has become clear to me that I do not know what I mean when I say that a kind is socially constructed, and that the phrase appears to do quite different work when applied to race, to gender, and to money. I would rather settle this by building an instrument than by reading further, so I am asking you to construct one.

Please produce a decision tree, or an equivalent taxonomy, that sorts the positions on the single question: what is it for a kind — a category of people or of things — to be socially constructed?

Requirements:

1. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of theorists. Identify the diagnostic questions first, arrange them so that each internal node can be answered by someone who does not already know which position it leads to, and only then locate the positions at the leaves. No node may be answerable only by someone who has already accepted a position.

2. The tree must branch, at minimum, on the following: whether the social element is the boundary of the kind or its membership; whether the construction is causal (social arrangements bring it about that members have certain features) or constitutive (social attitudes fix what it is to be a member); whether the kind is interactive, so that classifying its members changes them, or indifferent; whether a constructed kind can be real and constraining, and in what sense; and whether the fact of construction has any bearing on which categories we ought to use.

3. The leaves must include, at least: an eliminativist or fictionalist position; a natural or biological realist position; causal constructionism; constitutive constructionism; the view that a kind is real precisely because it is constructed; the institutional-fact view that seems to fit money; the interactive or looping view; an ameliorative or pragmatic view that selects categories by their political usefulness; and a deflationary view on which the dispute is really about how a word is used. Name each position descriptively — I do not want the names of theorists — and add any further leaves the space requires. Under each leaf, state in one sentence the commitment that position would find hardest to defend.

4. Four cases must appear in the tree and be tracked through it: an official race or ethnicity question of the kind found on forms; a sex or gender category that now admits self-identification; a currency; and an administrative threshold that manufactures the behaviour of the people it classifies, such as a credit-score band or an insurance tier. For each case, say which leaves can accommodate it and which cannot, and whether the same sense of construction is doing the work in all four.

5. Where a combination of answers is occupied by no position, mark it and say whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect.

6. Flag any pair of positions the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to distinguish them.

7. Make explicit whether any leaf is committed to the claim that construction entails arbitrariness, or entails easy changeability, since I suspect a great deal of the confusion in my own thinking lies there.

Assume an intelligent reader who has not read the literature; keep it self-contained and free of reading lists. Aim for roughly eight to twelve internal nodes and six to ten leaves.
