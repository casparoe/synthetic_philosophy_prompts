A long-running dispute about the historicity of the Trojan War, of King Arthur, and of the Exodus has two coherent camps that mostly argue past each other. The record camp holds that a tradition is, at bottom, a degraded record of an event: something happened, transmission distorted it, and the task is to estimate what survives. The projection camp holds that a tradition is a composition of the community that produced it: its apparent reference to an event is a feature of genre and function, and the interesting question is what present need the story met. Write the joint statement that a committed member of each camp could sign without retracting anything, covering all three traditions.

Include, in this order:

1. A vocabulary section fixing the terms both camps will use. It must distinguish two modes of collective memory: memory circulating among living witnesses and their immediate descendants, kept honest by the presence of people who were there, and memory that has become institutional, ritual, and normative, and is therefore free to be reshaped to fit present needs. State what both camps take to follow from the fact that a tradition is first recorded only after the first mode has died out.

2. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should include claims about what a tradition is evidence of when its narrative is false; about what makes one item of evidence independent of another; about the worth of arguments from silence against a record whose survival is selective; about the independence of transmission fidelity from content truth; and about the conditions under which a legend becomes warranted history. Include agreed claims about each of the three traditions at whatever level of description both camps can honestly accept. Each claim should carry a sentence saying why the camp that finds it least congenial still signs it.

3. A disagreements table. For each item: the position in each camp's own preferred wording, a neutral restatement that neither camp rejects as a caricature, and the self-description each camp would give and the label each would refuse.

4. The cruxes, with the case or argument both camps accept as bearing on each, and both camps' predictions about it written down in advance. At least these are in play: how long and under what conditions an event-schema can survive transmission; whether topographical anchoring, that is, a story fastened to a ruin, a pass, a mound, or a name, is a mechanism of memory or a mechanism of invention; whether a tradition's founding or legitimating function counts against its historicity or is orthogonal to it; and whether the three traditions should be covered by one general thesis at all, or whether they are three different epistemic situations. For each crux, say which camp would move, in which direction, and how far: a change of verdict on one of the three cases, or only of confidence.

5. A closing section on residual verbal disputes. Where the camps differ only over what the unit of historicity is, an event, an agent, a sequence, a scale, or a meaning, or only over where the burden of proof sits, say so, and say what would have to be stipulated for the dispute to become empirical. End with what would move each camp and what both agree would not.

Do not manufacture finds, inscriptions, dates, or attributions, and do not cite scholarship; refer to positions rather than to authorities. Where a camp's case depends on a description of the record that the other camp disputes, that belongs in the crux section, not in the agreed claims. Do not manufacture agreement: the list of agreed claims should contain whatever survives honest drafting, and nothing more.
