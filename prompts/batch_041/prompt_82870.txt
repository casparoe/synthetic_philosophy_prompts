Okay, novelist here, mid-draft, stuck on something that's less plot than philosophy, and I need the actual machinery, not the TED talk version.

The setup: one of my characters is an entomologist who's come around, slowly and reluctantly, to thinking insects might matter morally. She has a scene with her brother, a large-animal vet, who is the most hard-headed person in the book, and she has to lay out the scale argument to him on the page. I understand the shape of it — there are so many insects that even a tiny probabilty that any given one is sentient, multiplied out, gives you a gigantic expected amount of something — but the brother is going to fight her, so I need her replies to be real, and I need to know where the argument is actually soft.

Things I specifically need untangled:

— What is the argument claiming, exactly? Is it about what's valuable in the world, or about what she should do? And does the probability attach to "insects are sentient" as one fact about the whole class, or to each insect separately? Does that distinction matter or is it a distinction without a difference?

— Why is it supposed to survive setting the probability arbitrarily low? My brother-character's line is going to be that this is Pascal's wager with antennae, and I half think he's right. At what point does tiny-probability-times-huge-number stop being rigorous and start being a trick? Is there a principled place to draw that line, or is the whole move just what you get if you take expected value seriously and don't flinch?

— Here's the one I actually care about, because it's my character's own position: she thinks sentience isn't a switch, it's a dial. An insect, if it has any experience at all, has a very dim one — a flicker with a tiny amount of welfare in it. She thinks that's the real answer to the scale argument: not that the probability is too low, but that the per-head weight is too small. Is she right, or does the dial idea just relocate the problem? If sentience comes in degrees, can you still get a huge total out of an enormous number of barely-sentient things?

— And the thing I can't shake: the same move seems to work for anything. Rocks, a computer, a galaxy of dust — run the multiplication and you get a huge expected something. If the argument shows insects matter, does it also show that everything matters, and is that the reductio her brother should be reaching for? Or is it just what the view honestly implies and everyone's supposed to live with?

— She definately wants to use a coin-flip line somewhere: heads, all insects are sentient, tails none are, so the expected number is half the total. Is that a fair illustration or a sleight of hand?

Don't mush it. Tell me which of these objections actually bite and which are the ones that sound devastating at a dinner party and fall apart when you push. And give me a couple of images or examples I can steal — I'm writing fiction, not a dissertation.
