I teach philosophy of mind, and for the past two years I have also served as the resident philosopher on a cross-departmental working group on model evaluation — an arrangement that has left me able to read a benchmark paper or an eval write-up without translation, and to speak, as a competent consumer rather than a contributor, the vocabulary of held-out test sets, contamination, construct validity and the gap between a construct and its operationalization, distributional shift, adversarial evaluation, capability elicitation, specification gaming, and Goodharting. I would like to use that vocabulary for a translation exercise. Take the Turing test as it actually functions in the philosophical literature — not only the imitation game in the form in which it was originally proposed, where the interrogator's task was to distinguish a man from a woman before a machine was substituted for one of the players, but the whole cluster of positions that has grown up around it: that behavioural indistinguishability is a sufficient condition for thought; that it is at most strong evidence and not a criterion at all; that it is a criterion of something narrower than thought, whether verbal fluency or the capacity to deceive; and the standing objection that no amount of conversational success settles what, if anything, is going on inside. Restate each of these positions, one at a time, in the evaluation vocabulary I have just described, as faithfully as you can. Be scrupulous about not importing the target framework's assumptions and passing them off as translation: if you find yourself writing that the Turing test is merely a benchmark and benchmarks are gameable, stop, because that is an argument rather than a restatement.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. For each position, tell me what survives the translation intact, what survives only in a distorted form, and what has no counterpart in the evaluation vocabulary at all. I am particularly interested in three items: where the notion of a constitutive criterion — a test that does not measure a property but fixes what the property is — goes; where the first-person dimension goes, if anywhere; and whether treating the test as a property of a system-plus-interrogator-population pair, which is what evaluation practice does, is something the philosophical debate should have been doing all along. My own suspicion, which you should feel free to reject, is that the test is best read as a stipulation about what we are going to count as thought, and that the evaluation vocabulary, for all its sophistication, has no room for stipulation of that kind. Finally, and this is the question I would most like answered: is the residue a defect of the target vocabulary — something evaluation practice cannot say and ought to be able to — or is it evidence that the original philosophical claims were less clear than their formulations made them look? I would rather you took a view than split the difference, and I would rather you tell me which single piece of residue is the most diagnostic.
