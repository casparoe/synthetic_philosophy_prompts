I'm a fourth-year PhD student who splits time between an evaluation-methodology group and a lab that works on alignment, and I've been asked to circulate a short position memo before a joint reading group on measurement and optimization. Before I do, I want a consistency check on the view I've actually been defending in meetings, because I keep noticing that different people there take me to be making different claims. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) Goodhart effects are not one thing; I count at least four mechanisms — regression on a noisy proxy, breakdown of the proxy-target relationship once you optimize far enough into its tail, the proxy standing in a causal relation to the target so that pushing on it moves the target, and strategic adaptation by whoever is being measured — and I think remedies have to be matched to mechanism. (2) Optimization pressure is the master variable: the same proxy that behaves fine when you nudge it fails when you optimize it hard, so the deepest version of the problem is about the strength of the optimizer rather than the cleverness of the metric. (3) Specification gaming in AI is the institutional metric problem with a more capable optimizer behind it, so lessons from audit design, accounting, and research evaluation should transfer. (4) The practical fix is better specification — formalize the objective more carefully, add constraints, use multiple and rotating measures, and hold some measures out. (5) In genuinely competitive or adversarial settings, secrecy and mechanism design are the only robust fixes: don't publish the metric, or arrange the incentives so that gaming is unprofitable. (6) Metrics remain indispensable for running institutions and for evaluating models; refusing to measure is worse than measuring badly. (7) We can often say in advance which metrics will be gamed, by looking at the incentive structure and at the causal relation between proxy and target. To make this concrete: in a recent argument about reward models, one colleague insisted the fix is better reward modeling while another insisted the fix is to stop optimizing the reward model so hard, and I found myself agreeing with both, which is part of why I'm asking.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and how badly does each genuine tension bite? Please order the genuine tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and what it would cost me, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at the least cost. I would rather you discriminate than flag everything as loosely in tension — if two of these are reconciled by a distinction I am already implicitly making, say so and say what the distinction is. And please don't tell me to just stop using metrics; that is not on the table for reasons that should be obvious from (6).
