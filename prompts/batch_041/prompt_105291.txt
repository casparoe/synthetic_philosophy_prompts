I teach the perception-and-empiricism unit in an undergraduate seminar, and this year I want to pair Molyneux's question with H. G. Wells's "The Country of the Blind" — both the 1904 text and the expanded 1939 version, because I suspect the two endings pull the philosophical reading in different directions. In the story a mountaineer from Bogotá named Nuñez falls into an isolated Andean valley whose inhabitants were cut off from the world by an earthquake and lost their sight over successive generations; they have built a whole culture around four senses — windowless houses, kerbs along every path, a cosmology with no sky, and no concept of sight at all. Nuñez arrives assuming that a sighted man must effortlessly dominate a community of the blind, discovers instead that he cannot even make himself understood, falls in love with Medina-Saroté, is refused by the elders on account of his obsession with the imaginary sense, and is then offered a cure: the village doctor diagnoses his eyes as diseased growths that irritate his brain, and proposes to remove them. He consents in order to marry her, then flees at dawn — in the original ending he lies down high on the mountainside at sunset, content to have escaped, and does not come back down; in the 1939 ending he sees a rock slide coming, is again dismissed as imagining things, and escapes the valley with Medina-Saroté. What I want is a reading of the story through Molyneux's question and the debate it set off, not a summary of either.

Take the philosophy seriously in both its early and its contemporary forms. Molyneux put the question to Locke, who printed it in the Essay: a man born blind, taught by touch to tell a cube from a sphere, is made to see — could he identify them by sight alone before touching them? Molyneux and Locke answered no, on the ground that the man has never learned that what affects his touch in such a way must affect his sight in such a way; Berkeley answered no more radically, denying any resemblance between the ideas of sight and touch and treating vision as a language we learn to correlate with tangible experience; the rationalist side, from Leibniz through Hutcheson, answered yes. Then bring in the modern material: the sight-restoration cases in which adults who gain vision after decades of blindness can see but cannot at first visually identify shapes and objects they knew perfectly by touch, with matching emerging only through practice; the sensory-substitution work in which blind subjects learn to extract spatial information from a camera translated into tactile or auditory patterns; and the answer associated with Gareth Evans, who thought the correct reply is yes, because sight and touch both inform the subject about one and the same objective spatial layout, so the newly sighted person can bring the seen shape under a concept of shape he already has. Note also that Molyneux's original question had a second half about distance — whether the man could judge, by sight alone and before reaching, whether the objects lay within arm's reach — which is exactly the kind of judgment the valley-dwellers never make visually. Which positions does the story dramatize, scene by scene? Is Nuñez's failure to explain sight to Medina-Saroté a failure of his vision or a failure of her conceptual repertoire — and does that tell us anything about the empiricist answer? Does the valley vindicate Berkeley (sight is one more language, and one nobody there needs to speak) or Evans (the spatial world is common to the senses, and the valley's kerbs and walls show the blind living in the same space), or does it evade the question by making the issue social rather than perceptual?

Then be an honest critic rather than an enthusiast. Wells is plainly also writing about the visionary among the complacent, the pressure to conform, and the medical framing of a difference as a disease to be excised — the eye operation is arguably closer to a story about normalcy and coercion than about the acquisition of ideas, and the hereditary blindness, the colonial explorer's presumption, and the romance with Medina-Saroté all carry their own freight that has nothing to do with Molyneux. Tell me where the philosophical reading illuminates a scene and where the story resists it and I should admit as much to my students. Which passages would you actually assign alongside the SEP-style exposition, which of the two endings serves the philosophical reading better and why, and is there anything the story makes vivid that the argument alone cannot — in particular, the thought that whether a perceptual capacity counts as knowledge may depend on what a community has any use for? I don't need quotations or a reading list; I know the text and the literature well enough to fill those in myself.
