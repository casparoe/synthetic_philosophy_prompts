I have recently taken charge of the education of my two children, aged six and nine, and in thinking through what I want for them I have been reading Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education. I find his general picture compelling — that the formation of character and of settled habits matters more than the stock of knowledge, that a child's love of liberty and of play should be turned to use rather than crushed, that esteem and disgrace move a child further than rewards and blows, and that the tutor's real task is to shape the mind rather than to fill it — but the parts of his advice do not obviously fit together, and I want the consequences drawn out before I commit to a plan. Please set out the most important implications of Locke's position for the following, treating each as a place where his principles either converge or collide: (1) the schedule on which a child should be governed by habit and mild authority versus brought to act from reason and the desire for approbation, including whether there is an age before which reasoning with a child is wasted effort; (2) the use of esteem and disgrace as the household's chief levers — what this implies for praise, for rivalry between the two children, and for a child who appears indifferent to both; and (3) whether his account favors a parent or a hired tutor as the primary educator, and what it implies for a household in which the working parent cannot be the daily instructor. I am also considering giving the children access to an AI tutor that will answer any question on demand; tell me what Locke's account of habit, esteem, and the love of liberty implies about whether such a tool should be welcomed, rationed, or refused. Where his scheme is simply wrong by the lights of later work on child development, say so plainly, and rank the implications by how much they should change what I actually do.
