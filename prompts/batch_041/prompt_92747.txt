I'm in a slow-reading group working through Hegel's Phenomenology and I've got to present the Lordship and Bondage section next week. I can recite the standard story — two self-consciousnesses, a fight to the death, one submits, the master wins but gets a hollow recognition, the slave gets to work and thereby gets further along — but I want to actually understand the sentences instead of nodding along to the summary everyone already knows. Here's the chunk I'm stuck on, from the old public-domain translation I'm reading (paragraphs 185–186 of the Self-Consciousness chapter):

"This pure conception of recognition, of duplication of self-consciousness within its unity, we must now consider in the way its process appears for self-consciousness. It will, in the first place, present the aspect of the disparity of the two, or the break-up of the middle term into the extremes, which, qua extremes, are opposed to one another, and of which one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes.

Self-consciousness is primarily simple existence for self, self-identity by exclusion of every other from itself. It takes its essential nature and absolute object to be Ego; and in this immediacy, in this bare fact of its self-existence, it is individual. That which for it is other stands as unessential object, as object with the impress and character of negation. But the other is also a self-consciousness; an individual makes its appearance in antithesis to an individual. Appearing thus in their immediacy, they are for each other in the manner of ordinary objects."

What I can't get past is the line about one being merely recognized while the other only recognizes. Taken literally, that seems to say the master is the recognized one and the bondsman is the recognizer — but then the very next paragraph says that as they first appear they are for each other in the manner of ordinary objects, which sounds symmetric, like neither is recognizing anything yet. So is the asymmetry already there before the fight, or is it something the fight produces? And what is the "middle term" that gets broken up into "the extremes" — is Hegel deliberately borrowing the vocabulary of the syllogism here, and if so, what are the two premises and what is the conclusion supposed to be? I have a hunch the syllogism language is load-bearing and not just decorative, but I can't tell if that's a real reading or me over-reading a metaphor.
