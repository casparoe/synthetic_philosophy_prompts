I've been trying to get my head around Bernard Williams's objection to utilitarianism, and I found this essay online that summarizes it — I think it was on some ethics blog, not by anyone famous — and I want to know if its a fair summary before I go repeating it to people. Here's roughly what it says:

Williams's famous objection to utilitarianism — the "integrity objection" — is really just one argument, and it's built on his example of a man who can save only one of two drowning people and chooses his wife. The man's integrity lies in not having to justify that choice: Williams says that if the man has to stop and ask whether saving his wife is morally permitted, he's had "one thought too many." The man's love for his wife is just a fact about him that sits outside morality altogether — there's no reason for it — and any theory that tries to justify it with a rule has already ruined the thing it's trying to justify. So the integrity objection shows that impartial theories like Kantianism and utilitarianism are self-defeating: no actual human being could really live that way. His objection, in other words, isn't that utilitarianism asks too much of us, it's that it asks for the wrong kind of thing entirely, and a theory that demands a reason for loving your own wife is basically incoherent.
