My father is in the late stages of frontotemporal dementia, and at a family dinner last month my aunt said something I keep turning over. She said he is mostly gone already, that you can see it in the scans — the shrinkage, the enlarged ventricles, the numbers the neurologist reads off — and that we should stop putting him through hospitalizations. She was not being cruel, and part of what she said I agree with. But she was also making an argument about moral status: less brain, less of a someone, a weaker claim on us. I want an argument I can actually hold on to, not a slogan.

Please argue for this position: brain size — volume, mass, neuron count, however it is measured — is never itself a morally relevant property. It can be a proxy for something that is, and it can function as a gate that settles which regime of treatment applies, but it can never be the ground. And once that is clear, the scans can do no moral work at all in my father's case, even though they plainly do a great deal of epistemic and practical work.

Keep the following three roles distinct throughout, and use them as the spine of the argument:

- a ground: the property that itself makes a creature morally considerable;
- a proxy: a correlate of a ground, good enough to estimate it in creatures we cannot interview;
- a gate: a bright line that fixes which set of rules applies.

Then argue that brain size is at best a proxy and sometimes a gate, and never a ground; that the proxy role is far weaker than it looks, because a proxy inherits every looseness of its correlation, and this particular correlation fails in both directions; and that the gate role, while real, is a fact about our institutions rather than about the creatures being gated.

Structure the response with these headings, in this order:

1. The question (about 150 words). What exactly is being asked, and why the quick reply — that size does not matter, only the capacities that matter — is too quick to be the whole answer.

2. The argument (about 700 words). The case for the position. Include at least one case where a small brain comes with rich capacities and one where a large brain comes with thin ones, and at least one case where a proxy does legitimate work (for instance, deciding what to test for, or which animals a welfare statute should cover). Say explicitly whether the conclusion carries over to the cross-species case, where brain-size arguments are usually made, and where it does not.

3. The strongest objection (about 250 words). Give me the best version of the other side, not a straw man. The version I find hardest is that mentality comes in degrees, that a proxy may be all we will ever have, and that refusing to use it is self-serving whenever the proxy is inconvenient. If you can think of a better objection, use that one instead.

4. My reply (about 250 words).

5. What would change my mind (about 100 words). Specific evidence or a specific argument, not a gesture at open-mindedness.

6. What I might say to my aunt (about 150 words). The same conclusion in the register of a family dinner — no lecture, no condescension, and no claim I have not earned in the earlier sections.

Do not give me citations or a reading list; I cannot check them and they are not what I need. Argue, and where the argument is weak, say so.
