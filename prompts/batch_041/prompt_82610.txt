For the past several years I have been writing fiction that treats the physical world as morally serious — not as a backdrop for human drama but as something whose smallest constituents might themselves be loci of value — and I have reached the limit of what I can do on intuition alone. I am applying for a one-year research fellowship at a university's interdisciplinary humanities center, and I need a two-page proposal. The half-formed idea I would like you to turn into that proposal is this: the literature on panpsychism and its relatives has largely settled into arguing about whether experience is ubiquitous, while the ethical question — whether anything in the fundamental furniture of the world can suffer — tends to be discharged with a gesture toward the combination problem, as though the difficulty of assembling a single mind out of micro-subjects also settled the difficulty of locating pain in an electron, a field, or a spacetime point. I suspect those are two different problems wearing one name, and that pulling them apart changes what an ethically serious physics would even look like. What I want the proposal to do is state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer: whether a claim like "fundamental physical entities can be subjects of suffering" is a claim about the intrinsic natures of those entities, about the structure of their states, or about something we project onto them, and whether any of those three readings gives a defensible reason to revise how we act toward, or think about, the physical world.

To keep the project from sprawling, I would like you to build it around a single device I keep returning to and cannot get to behave: a pair of imagined universes that are physically identical in every measurable respect but differ in whether their fundamental constituents are subjects of experience at all. The proposal should say what work that thought experiment can and cannot do — whether any difference between the two universes is a difference that could matter to anyone inside them, or to us, or whether the whole contrast collapses into a distinction without a difference — and it should use that pressure to sort the candidate positions. Then give me the method: what I would actually do, month by month, across the year, and why a conceptual project of this kind makes progress where empirical work cannot yet reach. Say what a successful result looks like (I imagine something like a small map of the positions with criteria that discriminate among them, plus a clear verdict on whether any of them survives the thought experiment), and say honestly what would count as the project failing — whether there is a real risk that the question dissolves on inspection rather than resolving.

I do not need a bibliography or a literature review; I will fill in citations myself, so please argue from positions and their internal logic rather than from names and dates. Write it as a proposal a philosopher would respect, even though I am a novelist and my eventual deliverable is a book. And one request about the substance: throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus — where you are giving me your own lean, mark it as such.
