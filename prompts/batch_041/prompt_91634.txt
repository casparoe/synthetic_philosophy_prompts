I'm in a seminar on the passions and we've just started on Spinoza's Ethics, and I've hit a wall on something everyone else in the room seemed to nod along to. Part III opens with three definitions before you get to any propositions, and the third one says: "By emotion I mean the modifications of the body, whereby the active power of the said body is increased or diminished, aided or constrained, and also the ideas of such modifications." Right after it comes the note: "If we can be the adequate cause of any of these modifications, I then call the emotion an activity, otherwise I call it a passion, or state wherein the mind is passive." And the preface just before all that is where he says he'll consider human actions and desires in exactly the same manner "as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids", and where he dismisses Descartes for thinking the mind might get absolute dominion over the emotions. The methodology I think I follow. It's the definition itself I can't make sit still.

Two people in the seminar gave me opposite readings and now I cant tell which is even the right kind of answer. One said this is basically a feeling theory: the emotion just is the bodily modification plus our registering of it, so the body comes first and the "idea" clause is just an awareness clause -- fear is feeling your power of acting get constrained. The other said no, the idea clause is doing all the work, so it's closer to an appraisal theory: the emotion is an idea of the body's power of acting, a kind of judgement about your own power, only a confused one, which is exactly why the passions come out as inadequate ideas. I want you to adjudicate by reading the sentences rather than handing me the standard line on Spinoza. (a) Is the emotion the modification, the idea of the modification, or the two as one thing -- and given his parallelism, am I even asking a well-formed question when I ask which of them it "really" is? (b) An idea of a modification is an idea of what, exactly: the body's change, the change in its power, or the thing that brought it about? Because when I'm angry at a particular person the emotion is about them and not about my insides, and the wording seems to say the opposite. (c) Does the definition give him any principled way to keep non-emotional bodily states out -- hunger, a headache, feeling cold, just feeling where my feet are -- or does anything bodily that gets registered count as an emotion? (d) Confused as opposed to what? If a passion is a confused idea of the body's power, what would the unconfused version be, and why can't an emotion be it? A friend suggested the real test case is an emotion with no obvious felt bodily change -- contempt, or a grudge you've carried around for years -- and said either the definition handles it or it doesn't, and whichever way it goes settles which of the two readings is right. If that's the right test, run it. If it isn't, tell me what the right test is. No reading list please, I just want to see how the wording forces the answer.
