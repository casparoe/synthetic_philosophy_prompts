I am a novelist in the last revision of a book whose climax turns on whether viruses are alive. My protagonist, a young virologist, demonstrates in the final chapter that viruses evolve by natural selection; the room — a grant committee, in my draft — concedes the point and funds her. A friend who teaches philosophy of biology read the scene and said it could not happen as written, then declined to explain, and I would rather understand the problem than cover it with a line of dialogue.

My reasoning was this. Living things are the things that evolve; that, as I understood it, is the criterion by which biology marks off the living from the merely chemical, and viruses satisfy it. So the question should be settled by evidence, and my protagonist has the evidence. If that is wrong, I want to know how. Which criterion should she be invoking, and what would actually count as settling the matter? It also puzzles me that the definitions I encounter in astrobiology writing are not the ones the origin-of-life chemists use; I had assumed a definition was either right or wrong, not fitted to whoever is asking.

I have also collected the borderline cases and cannot tell whether they are genuinely undecided or merely undecided to an amateur: a mule, which cannot reproduce; a prion, which propagates without nucleic acid; a viroid, which is bare RNA; a mitochondrion, which has its own genome but no independent existence; a virus that is itself parasitized by a smaller virus; a self-replicating pattern in a computer simulation. If a reader asked which of these are alive, I would not know what I was claiming.

One last thought, which I would like tested: I have considered having my protagonist argue for a criterion of her own — that life is whatever a lineage becomes when it is subject to selection — but I suspect this changes the subject rather than settling it. Tell me whether I am right, and if I am, what she could honestly say to that committee.
