I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on self-reference and formal systems, and our final assignment is a poem that embodies one of the constructions we studied rather than describing it. I have chosen the typogenetics system from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and the isomorphism he draws between it and the self-referential sentences of Gödel numbering. Since I cannot assume you have the system at your fingertips, here is the version our seminar worked from, and I would like you to build the poem out of this description rather than out of anything you half-remember. A strand is a string over four letters, A, C, G, and T. Decoded two letters at a time, it yields a sequence of amino acids; the amino acids are concatenated into a chain and then broken apart at punctuation points into separate enzymes. An enzyme folds into a shape that gives it a preference for one of the four letters; it binds to a strand at a letter it prefers and then runs through its amino acids in order, carrying a cursor along the target and cutting, deleting, inserting, copying, or switching segments as it goes. A strand whose enzymes, acting on that strand, produce a copy of it is self-reproducing, and that strand is Hofstadter's prize exhibit. The construction is legible at two levels at once: a syntactic level, where strings are rewritten by rules that attend only to shapes, and a semantic level, where a string means the operation it performs on other strings. His claim is that the relation between those two levels is the same relation that Gödel numbering sets up in arithmetic, and that this relation is what makes self-reference possible in either place.

Please write a pantoum, sixteen lines, with the second and fourth lines of each stanza returning as the first and third lines of the next, in which the form does this work rather than illustrating it. Let the returning lines be the strand, so that each time a line comes back it is doing a different kind of job: a line that read as a description on its first appearance should be readable as an instruction on its second, and a line that was an instruction should be able to settle into a report of what that instruction has just done. Somewhere in the poem, one stanza should be legible as an enzyme, a run of imperatives that, carried out on the poem's own text, would produce the stanza after it. Let the last line be the first line, so that the poem can be read again from the top, and let that closure feel like replication rather than like a remark about replication. Please keep the words typogenetics, enzyme, strand, codon, gene, Gödel, and isomorphism out of the verse entirely; I want the mechanism carried by the poem itself, not announced. A reader who knows the system should be able to point to the lines that are enzymes and say what each one does to the text, while a reader who has never heard of it should find a poem about copies, about meaning showing up inside a mechanism, and about a sentence that turns out to be about itself. Write in American English, do not quote anyone, and after the poem add two sentences identifying which stanza you meant as the enzyme and what operation you had it perform.
