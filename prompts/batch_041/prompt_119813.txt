I write about science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about prediction-market governance — the family of designs usually filed under futarchy, in which a polity votes on a single measure of collective wellbeing (GDP with some distributional weighting, say), and then hands the choice of policies to conditional betting markets: traders buy and sell assets that pay off according to whether that measure is higher in the world where a given proposal is adopted than in the world where it is not, and the proposal whose conditional price implies the larger gain is enacted without further deliberation. The scheme has been tried in a few small settings, is much discussed in blockchain governance, and has been refined with agenda-setting auctions, veto rules, and statistical tests for whether a price gap is significant. What I want from you is not a description of the mechanism or a recital of the standard objections, but a reconstruction of the philosophical commitments it presupposes: the views it only makes sense under, the rivals it quietly excludes, and the places where it is silent and several views would serve equally well.

Take these in turn. First, the split between ends and means — a vote fixes the measure of wellbeing, markets choose the policies that maximize it. What theory of value does that division require? Does it need the good to be a single scalar, measurable and comparable across persons? Does it need a sharp separation of fact from value, on which markets are competent over beliefs and inert with respect to desires? Second, the design assumes that traders bet on what will happen rather than on what they want to happen, trusting the profit motive to discipline expressive and strategic betting. Is that a presupposition or an empirical hope, and what conception of the trader does it commit the designer to? Third, enacting the market's verdict directly treats a decision's legitimacy as a matter of expected consequences. Which accounts of legitimacy does that rule out, and can any be recovered — does the prior vote on the measure carry enough of the legitimating burden to keep the arrangement democratic, or does the design concede that the substantive question is the choice of measure and then hand it to the very majoritarian procedure it was meant to displace? Fourth, access is typically gated by capital, so influence scales with willingness to stake money; what does that presuppose about political standing? Fifth, the conditional structure exists to avoid self-reference — if the forecast determined adoption, the forecast would alter the thing forecast — yet the assets still require that counterfactuals of the form "the measure would have been higher had we adopted this proposal" be determinate and eventually measurable. What must be true of the world, and of the measure, for those counterfactuals to have values at all?

Then tell me which of these the design strictly presupposes and which it merely fits, where the presuppositions are in tension — in particular whether the welfarism of the decision rule can be squared with the fact/value separation the market design assumes — and what would have to be rewritten in the mechanism if one replaced the welfarist-consequentialist commitment with a procedural or pluralist alternative.
