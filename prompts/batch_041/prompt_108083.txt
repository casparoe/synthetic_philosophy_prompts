For the past several months I have been running a small discussion circle — seven or eight of us, none of us with any formal training in the subject — that meets every other week to work through problems in probability and decision theory. Each cycle I set a written exercise, the members submit answers, and I write up comments to circulate. My own background is no deeper than anyone else's in the group, so before I send my remarks back I would like an outside read.

The exercise I set was the following.

Suppose two agents share a common prior probability distribution over a finite set of possible states of the world, and suppose each agent receives private information, modelled as being told only which cell of a partition of the state space contains the true state. Each agent then forms a posterior probability for some event by conditionalising the shared prior on the cell he has learned. Explain the result usually called agreeing to disagree, which concerns what follows when the two agents' posteriors are common knowledge — that is, when each knows the value, each knows that the other knows it, and so on. In doing so, address three points: (i) precisely what has to be common knowledge for the result to apply; (ii) whether the result shows that persistent disagreement between rational and equally well-informed people is impossible; and (iii) whether the agents' preferences and values, and not only their probabilities, fall under the same constraint. Illustrate your answer with a concrete two-agent example.

Here is the answer one member submitted.

The result in question is the agreeing-to-disagree theorem, usually credited to Aumann. In the set-up, two agents share a common prior over a finite state space; each learns only which cell of a partition contains the true state; and each forms a posterior for the event by conditionalising the shared prior on the cell he has learned. The theorem says that if the two posteriors are common knowledge, they must be equal.

The reasoning behind it is that a disagreement is itself evidence. If I learn that you, working from our shared prior and your private information, have reached a different probability than I have, then your private information is evidence that I lack, and I should move toward your value; the same holds in reverse; so there is no stable position in which we both knowingly hold different probabilities. Rational disagreement is therefore possible only where common knowledge is missing.

The common prior does a great deal of work here. If the two agents start from different priors, the theorem does not apply at all, and their reaching different posteriors is no failure of rationality. This is the chief reason the theorem does not show that disagreement in general is irrational: it constrains only agents who already share a prior and know that they do.

As to what must be common knowledge, the theorem requires the two posterior values to be common knowledge in the full sense — each knows them, each knows that the other knows them, and so on. But the higher-order steps come for free once the first step is secured. Once each agent knows what probability the other assigns, there is nothing further to be learned, because the values do not change as the knowledge is iterated. In practice, then, the condition is satisfied as soon as the two have compared notes.

The example makes this concrete. Let the states be 1, 2, 3 and 4, with a uniform prior, and let E be the event consisting of states 1 and 2. Agent A is told whether the state lies in {1, 2} or in {3, 4}; agent B is told whether it lies in {1, 3} or in {2, 4}. Take the true state to be 1. A is told that the state lies in {1, 2}, which is exactly E, so A's posterior for E is 1. B is told that the state lies in {1, 3}; among those two states E holds only at state 1, so B's posterior is 1/2. The posteriors differ. Since both agents know the shared prior and each other's partition, each can work out the posterior the other assigns, so the two values are common knowledge between them. But they are unequal, which is exactly what the theorem forbids. The example therefore does not so much refute the theorem as show how much it leans on the common prior: the natural diagnosis is that the agents' priors were not genuinely shared, and that once that assumption is relaxed the difficulty dissolves.

The same structure, finally, carries over to values. If two agents share a prior and it becomes common knowledge that they rank two outcomes differently — one preferring a certain policy to another and the other reversing that ranking — the same argument applies: the disagreement is information, each should move toward the other, and common knowledge of a settled difference is impossible. The result is thus a general constraint on rational agents who share a prior, and not a fact about probability alone.
