I am finishing a novel whose central object is a bronze equestrian monument — commissioned from a mould, cast in a foundry, unveiled, and then, three generations later, hauled down, cut apart, and in part melted and recast into something else — and I have found that I cannot keep my descriptions of it straight. Sometimes I write as though the bronze were the statue; sometimes as though the statue were something the bronze merely happens to make up; sometimes as though the monument were a third thing over and above the metal and its shape. Before I revise, I want to know exactly which metaphysics I have been writing, and to give each of my principal characters a different one without ever letting any of them break the rules of the view they hold. So please build me a decision tree of the positions on the material constitution of a statue out of its matter.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Each branch node should be a question about the monument that a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to: how many things stood on the pedestal at the unveiling; whether the ingot that predates the casting and the statue that follows it are one thing or two; whether the statue and the metal could have survived different histories, the one melted, the other merely dented; whether statue, lump, and war memorial name one thing under three descriptions or three things that happen to coincide; whether the relation of constituting is primitive or reducible to sameness of parts plus a difference in what could have been true of each; and whether the question of which of these is the real object has an answer at all. Each leaf should carry the name of a position — identity monism; eliminativism about one of the pair; constitution without identity; coincident-objects pluralism; relative identity by sortal; a four-dimensionalist reading in which the statue and the metal are different sums of temporal parts; a hylomorphic reading in which the form is not a further part; and a deflationary reading on which the whole dispute is a matter of which language-game we are playing — or should be marked unnamed if no one occupies it. Give me six to nine leaves, and at each leaf one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, tied in every case to my monument rather than to a philosopher's example.

Two further things. First, wherever the tree yields a combination of answers that no position occupies, tell me why: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Second, if any two leaves turn out to be indistinguishable by the questions you have posed — I suspect the constitution and coincident-objects views may be, and that the deflationary view may quietly swallow the rest — say so plainly rather than inventing a question that separates them on paper only. I would rather have a tree with a confessed blind spot than a tidy one that cheats. No citations or reading lists, please; I want the positions themselves and the questions that separate them.
