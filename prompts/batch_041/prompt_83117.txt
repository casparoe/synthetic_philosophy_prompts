So I retired from the bench a few years back — thirty-odd years in microbiology, mostly obligate intracellular stuff, plus a strain collection I still can't quite let go of — and since then I've been reading philosophy, badly but enthusiastically. Here's an argument a friend in our reading group built over dinner after I said viruses aren't really alive, and I cant get out of it. I'm writing it out in numbered steps because that's how it went down on the back of a menu.

(1) If "alive" is a real feature of things and not just a convenient word, then it has to be a feature of the thing itself — something about the thing, not about us or about its relatives.
(2) The only candidate that does the job is being the kind of thing natural selection can get a grip on — descent with modification. Everything else fails. Metabolism, membranes, information storage, growth: all of them turn up in things nobody calls alive (a flame eats fuel and spreads; a crystal copies its own lattice; a prion propagates its shape), and all of them can be missing or idle in things everyone calls alive (a mule, a dried-out tardigrade, a virus that's been sitting frozen in permafrost for thousands of years, the cells in my old glycerol stocks).
(3) Natural selection doesn't act on individuals. It acts on populations and lineages over generations — a single bacterium doesn't evolve, the population does.
(4) So being subject to natural selection is not a feature of any individual thing.
(5) From (1) and (4): no individual thing is alive. Only populations and lineages are.
(6) But an individual bacterium, a mule, my own body — if anything's alive, it's these.
(7) So one of (1) through (4) has to go.

I'd sooner keep (2) than give it up — the evolution line is the only one that ever looked like it carved nature at a joint — and I'm obviously keeping (6). So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine to me, and what does rejecting it cost? If the step I should give up is (2), tell me what to put in its place and what that costs, because I've heard the candidates and none of them ever seemed to work. And if the argument is actually fine and (5) is just true, say so, and tell me what that would mean for what I spent thirty years doing.

One request: seperate what's genuinely settled here from what's a live fight among people who know the topic. I keep getting told things as if they were the consensus and then finding out later that it's one camp's line, and I'd rather have the map than the sales pitch.
