I have been sitting on the board of a small grantmaking circle — nine trustees, one pot of money per quarter — and last winter we adopted a rule that has quietly taken over my thinking. Any trustee may nominate at most one proposal. If exactly one trustee nominates, that proposal is funded in full. If nobody nominates, or if two or more do, the quarter's money goes to a fallback grant none of us is excited about. I am told this rule has cousins in the decision-theory literature, discussed under names like the Luring Lottery and the Platonia dilemma, and that they have provoked a small war over what a rational participant ought to do. At our next retreat I am running a session on it, and I would rather the handout be a decision tree than a summary of schools: the trustees should work down the branches and discover which view they actually hold, not which view they have heard of.

Please build that tree. Aim for roughly eight branch nodes and six to eight leaves, each node phrased as a concrete case or claim that a non-philosopher can answer without already knowing where it leads; no node may simply ask whether they accept causal decision theory. Begin from something everyone in the room would grant: that the other eight trustees are relevantly like me, that my decision is not causally insulated from theirs, and that I still have to decide. I want nodes that separate people by their verdicts on the standard probes — Newcomb's boxes, a prisoner's dilemma played against a copy of oneself, the hitchhiker who pays only someone who would have promised first — and by whether they take the question to be about acts or about the policies and procedures that generate acts; whether the number of other participants changes the verdict (nine trustees who can talk, versus a million strangers who have each read the same argument); whether a binding pre-commitment matters morally or merely practically; and what they say about a variant in which a trustee may declare any number of votes for their own proposal, with the pot divided by the total number of votes cast.

Name the leaves wherever a name exists — causal, evidential, superrational, team-reasoning, updateless or functional, Kantian universalizability, mixed-strategy equilibrium — and leave a leaf unnamed if the tree lands someone where nobody has defended a position. Give one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment. Then flag the pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate and say which extra case would separate them, and list the combinations of answers nobody occupies, with a guess as to whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Three of my fellow trustees have already staked out positions I would like the tree to place: one holds that we should each nominate with probability one in nine; one holds that the only question is what I would have settled on in advance, before knowing which of the nine I would turn out to be; one holds that nominating is weakly dominant, since it can only help and never hurt. If the tree cannot place all three, tell me which one falls through the cracks.
