Okay so I've fallen down a rabbit hole about whether a person can be irrational just by having the preferences they have, or whether it's only their choices that can be irrational, and I keep landing on gut verdicts that I can't square with each other. Here are the cases and what my gut says, and I want to know if there's a principle that gets them all, or close.

Case 1: A guy has strict preferences in a cycle over three sandwiches. He prefers turkey to ham, ham to blt, and blt to turkey, and he'd pay a penny to swap any of them for the one he prefers. Each single trade looks fine to me: he prefers what he's getting, he pays a trivial amount, no problem. But after three trades he's back holding turkey and three cents poorer, and that whole run looks obviously irrational to me. So I want to say each step is fine and the loop is not.

Case 2: A woman has the exact same cyclic preferences but refuses to trade at all, because she can see where it leads. She never loses a penny. Is she rational? My gut is she's got the same defective preferences so something is still off about her, but she never does anything I can point at. I'm tempted to say she's irrational in her preferences and perfectly rational in her choices, which sounds like I'm letting her off on a technicality.

Case 3: Someone whose tastes genuinely change over time in a non-cyclic way. She loves opera at 20, hates it at 60, and there's no moment where she wishes she had her old tastes back. At each time she just does what she then prefers. My gut: fine, not irrational.

Case 4: Someone who at 8pm prefers to get up early, and at 6am prefers to stay in bed, and at 8pm she'd happily pay to force her morning self up. Her evening self and her morning self keep ending up worse than a plan they could have stuck to. My gut: the 8pm version of her is being irrational, or at least is losing a game she could win by precommitting.

Case 5: A person takes $100 today over $110 tomorrow. Fine. Asked today about next month, she prefers $110 on the 31st over $100 on the 30th. Also fine. Put together, my gut says something has gone wrong, she'll keep shoving the $110 off forever and end up with nothing, but I can't say which single choice was the bad one.

Case 6: Someone who, offered a choice, takes the option that is worse on every dimension she says she cares about. She'll tell you the other one is better and still take this one, no story about weakness of will, she just does it. That seems flatly irrational to me in a way that case 1's individual trades don't, even though both are just single choices favoring the thing preferred at the moment.

My rough guess at a principle is that rationality is about the shape of your preference structure across time and not about any individual act, but I honestly can't tell if that saves case 1 or wrecks case 3, and I suspect cases 1 and 3 can't both survive anything simple. Is there one principle that gives me all six? If not, which verdict do I have to give up, and what does the surviving principle say about a case I haven't listed: a woman who knows she'll be tempted to break a diet and pays a service to lock her fridge, where the lock costs her more than the diet was ever worth to her? Does it matter that she would in fact have stuck to the diet anyway? And whats the difference, if any, between her and the 8pm precommitter in case 4.
