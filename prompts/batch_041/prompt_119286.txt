I'm a novelist — my book-in-progress is about a forger — and I've been asked to interview a philosopher onstage at a literary festival. Her position is that a perfect forgery shares every aesthetic property with the original, since aesthetic value supervenes on how a thing looks or sounds; what the original alone has is an indexical standing, its being the one object causally connected to a particular act of making. Her own wrinkle is that this makes the wrong of forgery testimonial rather than aesthetic: the forgery is a false claim about its own provenance, owed to an audience rather than to the artist, and discharged by disclosure.

Draft eight to ten questions for the hour, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want one pressing the charge that her indexical standing is mere fetishism — a collector's superstition given a metaphysical costume; one on whether her account implies that a forgery destroyed before anyone ever believed it wronged no one; and one on the artist's own later replica of her earlier painting, made by the same hand, which her criterion seems to count as an original. Close with the case I'm writing toward: a replica commissioned to stand in for an original destroyed in a war, so that the public's experience of the work survives — lie or gift, on her view? Assume she knows the standard debate; no warm-up questions about her career.
