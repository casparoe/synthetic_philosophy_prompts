I've been going back and forth with a couple of grad students about free-floating rationales — you know, the idea (I'm fairly sure it's Dennett's phrase, don't quote me on the source) that there can be reasons in nature without any reasoner, that when we say the bird heads south 'because' winter's coming, or that the cuckoo chick 'manipulates' its hosts, we're citing a rationale that nobody actually entertains, one that's been installed by selection. I find it genuinely useful and also genuinely slippery, and I want to turn my half-formed suspicion into a proposal for our department's small internal seed grant (one semester, basically no money, so the scope has to be honest). My suspicion is that the notion does two jobs that pull apart. In one mode it's more or less a translation manual: 'this trait exists because it solved problem P' is fully cashed out by selection history, and the reason-talk is just shorthand — an 'as if' you could delete with no loss. In the other mode it's doing something more, carrying an implicit norm — the mechanism can succeed or fail at the rationale, the rationale explains why the thing is supposed to work that way, and that's what licenses us to call a maladaptive case a malfunction rather than just a difference. I want the proposal to pin down whether that second, thicker notion earns its keep or quietly collapses into the first plus alot of hand-waving about teleology.

I think the method is mostly conceptual: find or construct cases where the thin and thick readings come apart, and see which one our judgments actually track. Be honest about what would count as the project failing — e.g. if every case I cook up turns out to be decided by the thin reading alone, or if the distinction keeps sliding around under pressure.
