Write a short story of about 2,500 words about a historian at work. The drama I want is epistemic, not physical: no chase for a lost manuscript, no convenient last-minute discovery. The subject is how a careful person decides what happened in the distant past when the evidence is thin and compromised.

Build the story around a predicament of the following shape, and invent every particular yourself — the place, the period, the names, the stakes. A scholar is reconstructing a single consequential event: a treaty, a massacre, an abdication, a founding. Every surviving account of it descends, through an unbroken chain of copying, from one document written by a party with a strong interest in how the event would be remembered. The scribes who transmitted it were forbidden to alter a single word, and they knew it; their disagreements with the text, and with one another, therefore survive only in the features the rule did not reach — the size and placement of the letters, which passages they chose to ornament, the ink, the silences in the margins. The scholar's training descends from the oldest ambition of her discipline, to establish what happened by weighing witnesses against one another, and it has given her two habits of inference: that a detail damaging to the author's own cause is probably true, because no one invents a fact against themselves; and that an unexplained omission shows nothing, because absence is cheap. She has begun to suspect that both habits can be turned against her.

I want the story to earn its ending through visible reasoning. Let me watch her weigh a hostile source's damaging admissions, notice what the copyists' hands are doing, and revise her picture in front of me. Let the ending be honest about the limits of what she can know: she may reach a verdict that is probable and still wrong, or find that the record supports two incompatible stories and no more. Do not resolve the puzzle with a device the surviving evidence could not have supported.

Then, in a separate note of no more than 400 words, tell me where the story leans on a contested reading of how source criticism actually works, which of its inferences a working historian would push back on, and what would have to be true for the opposite reading of the evidence to be the better one.
