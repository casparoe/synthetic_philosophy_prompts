I hold a version of Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality: the internal states of the two-bitser, and of any artifact we describe in intentional terms, have their aboutness only relative to our purposes and conventions, whereas the states of a frog or a human represent the world in their own right. I want to know what would count against this, and I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would actually be rather than by how often they get cited. In particular: what would have to be true of a system's causal history, its internal organization, or its sensitivity to its own errors for the distinction to collapse? Consider the wandering two-bitser case, where the same internal state that once tracked quarters would track a different coin after the machine is moved, with no internal change; does that tell against the Searlean line, or can the line absorb it? What would a defender of the distinction concede as counting against them, and is there any observation, formal result, or possible organism that would be fatal rather than merely awkward? Finally, tell me honestly whether the distinction has been qualified into something that risks nothing, and whether that is a defect or just the price of a distinction drawn at the level of intrinsic teleology rather than mechanism.
