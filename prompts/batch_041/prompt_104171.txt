I have been working through Augustine's Confessions and have hit a passage in Book VIII, chapter ix that I cannot get a clean grip on. Here is the text:

"The mind commands the body, and it obeys instantly; the mind commands itself, and is resisted. The mind commands the hand to be moved; and such readiness is there, that command is scarce distinct from obedience. Yet the mind is mind, the hand is body. The mind commands the mind, its own self, to will, and yet it doth not. Whence this monstrousness? and to what end? It commands itself, I say, to will, and would not command, unless it willed, and what it commands is not done. But it willeth not entirely: therefore doth it not command entirely. For so far forth it commandeth, as it willeth: and, so far forth is the thing commanded, not done, as it willeth not. For the will commandeth that there be a will; not another, but itself. But it doth not command entirely, therefore what it commandeth, is not. For were the will entire, it would not even command it to be, because it would already be. It is therefore no monstrousness partly to will, partly to nill, but a disease of the mind, that it doth not wholly rise, by truth upborne, borne down by custom. And therefore are there two wills, for that one of them is not entire: and what the one lacketh, the other hath."

What I need help with is the move at the end. Augustine has spent the passage insisting that this is not two agents but one will that is not entire — partly willing, partly nilling — and then the last sentence says flatly that there are two wills, with the gloss that one is not entire and the other supplies what it lacks. I cannot tell whether the two wills are meant as a real division, a façon de parler for the two poles of a single divided act, or something in between, and I suspect the answer turns on the reflexive formula just before it: the will commanding that there be a will, not another, but itself. If willing is not an act the will performs on itself, then the command metaphor seems to have no purchase; if it is such an act, then the commanding will and the commanded will look like two things again, which is exactly what he says they are not. How is a single will that is not entire supposed to be a stable middle between one will that is whole and two wills? What work does the reflexivity do, and what would break if it were dropped?

The second thing I want is a comparison I keep failing to make cleanly. I came to this expecting a proto-hyperbolic-discounting picture — the present-biased self overriding the long-run self, in the standard present-bias or planner-versus-doer idiom — but the temporal structure seems wrong for that. In the discounting picture the conflict is between momentary selves at different times, and each momentary self has a well-defined preference; what is unstable is the ordering across time. Augustine's conflict seems to be inside a single moment: the will is not entire now, and the failure is not that today's preference diverges from tomorrow's but that the present act of willing does not come off as a whole. So: can a discounting or dual-self model represent not-entire willing at an instant at all, or does it need a kind of internal division its formalism cannot express? If it cannot, is Augustine describing a different phenomenon — call it synchronic akrasia — than the one the discounting literature formalizes, and if so, what does the modern account lose by having no place for it?

Third, and connected: what is custom doing in the phrase borne down by custom? Is it a force exerted on the will, a feature of the will's object, a habituation of the will's own movement, or something else? Does the discounting picture have anything corresponding to it, or is consuetudo carrying weight that the modern account has no slot for?

I would like the answer to stay close to the passage, to be explicit about where Augustine's vocabulary is doing conceptual work that a modern paraphrase would flatten, and to tell me which reading of the two wills the rest of Book VIII forces — in particular, whether the chapter that follows, where he ridicules the Manichean reading of inner conflict as two natures, settles the question or only sharpens it.
