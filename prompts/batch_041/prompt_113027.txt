For the past four years I have been writing a novel that braids three stories into a single continuous account — the fall of Troy, the last campaign of Arthur, and the departure of the Israelites from Egypt — narrated by one voice that believes all three happened exactly as the tradition reports, and written so that a reader cannot tell which of the three the narrator takes to be documented and which merely inherited. I am at the stage where the book's authority is the only thing left to settle, and I have been carrying around a thesis a historian friend put to me. I would like you to work out its consequences.

The thesis is this. The line between legend and history is not drawn by evidence about the events; it is drawn by two accidents of record-keeping. The first is institutional: whether some body with a standing interest in the past — a palace, a temple chancery, a monastery, a bureaucracy — had a reason to write the thing down, and a reason to prefer the version it wrote. The second is material: whether the surface it wrote on survived. Fired clay outlives parchment, parchment outlives papyrus, and nothing outlives a story that was never written down at all; there is even a small irony in the fact that the fires which end a city are what bake its paperwork hardest. On this view, what separates my three traditions is not what happened but where each sat relative to an archive that had both a motive and a durable surface.

The three cases seem to me a fair test of it:

Troy is a real citadel, excavated, with more than one destruction layer, and its excavators have spent a century arguing about which layer, if any, answers to the story. Hittite tablets name a western vassal city that most scholars connect with the Greek name for Troy — they give its kings, its treaties, its depositions and restorations — but no text names the war or anyone in it, and the tablets never describe Greeks attacking the city.

Arthur is named by no text that survives in a copy made within centuries of his supposed lifetime. The earliest surviving narrative that gives him twelve battles and treats him as a leader of the Britons is a compilation from around 829, and it supplies no dates; the annals that do date him are later still.

The exodus is recorded by no Egyptian source at all. The one mention of Israel in any Egyptian text is a victory inscription from about 1208 BCE, in the course of a Canaan campaign, listing a people of that name among defeated groups — the hieroglyphs give Israel the sign for a people rather than a city — and it says nothing about Egypt, slavery, or a departure.

What I want is the most important implications of that thesis for my book, not for historiography but for the concrete decisions a novelist makes. Please work through them in roughly this order, then rank them by how much they should actually change what I write.

First, what the thesis implies for the narrator's authority — whether a voice that believes all three stories equally is now a confidence trick, an act of piety, or something with a stronger claim than either.

Second, what it implies for the ethics of the gaps. If the archive is the only difference among the three, then my invention is doing the work a lost archive would have done, and I want to know what that makes of the novelist: forger, witness, or substitute record-keeper.

Third, what it implies for the reader's experience, given that I have deliberately made the three traditions indistinguishable on the page.

Fourth, whether the thesis changes what the novel can claim to be about. If historicity is an artifact of media and institutions, the honest subject may not be the events at all but the record-keeping itself, and I want to know what is lost if I concede that.

Then tell me which of the three traditions your answer treats most differently from the other two, and whether that asymmetry embarrasses the thesis or supports it.

Finally, I would like the reasoning to be self-critical rather than tidy. Say where your own argument is most likely to fail — which step I should distrust — and say what would change your mind: what argument, or what kind of discovery, would lead you to conclude that the boundary between legend and history really does track the events after all, and that the archive is a side effect rather than the cause.
