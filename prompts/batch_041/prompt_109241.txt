Can you write me a short story that actually puts the Epicurean argument about death under pressure instead of just restating it? Setup I keep circling: a retired archivist in her sixties, dying slowly, spends her last months writing very specific instructions about what happens after — which letters get destroyed, which old colleague finally gets the apology she never delivered, how she wants to be described in the department newsletter, a correction she wants made to a public record that miscredits her. She isn't afraid of being dead; her whole view is that she won't be there, so nothing afterwards can land on her. Then the executor she chose ignores about half of it, and the apology goes to the wrong person. I want the story to make the reader feel she's been wronged while also taking seriously the thought that there's no one left to wrong — that the harm, if there's any, has no subject and no moment at which it happens. No ghost, no twist reveal; I want the reader stuck between "obviously she was wronged" and "wait, who exactly was wronged?" And if the idea that death is bad because of the life it cuts off sneaks in, let someone notice that the same logic would make a person who was never born unlucky — I'd like that discomfort in the background, not spelled out. Leave us with the exectuor's discomfort rather than a verdict.
