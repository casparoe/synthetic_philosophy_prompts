I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized public health agency, and I have been voting on cases like the following for six years. I would like to know what principle, if any, my votes add up to.

1. Antitoxin. One shipment. Outbreak A: sixty people will die without it. Outbreak B: fifteen will die, and four hundred will survive with permanent nerve damage. I voted to send it to A. The four hundred non-fatal injuries did not seem to me to belong in the same ledger as the forty-five additional deaths.

2. Newborn screening. Over ten years the program will save eleven infants who would otherwise die, and will cause one death through a complication of the confirmatory test. I voted to run it. Eleven against one, both deaths; I had no hesitation.

3. The drug. A treatment for a severely disabling, incurable, non-fatal skin condition, used by about two million patients a year. It will blind one patient a year, unpredictably and irreversibly. I voted to approve. I could not bring myself to tell two million people to keep the disease so that one stranger keeps his sight.

4. The valley. A reservoir would displace four hundred people from a valley against their will, with compensation, and would roughly double the water supply of two hundred thousand people for a century. I voted against. I did not think the number of beneficiaries could be set against the complaints of the four hundred.

5. The water rule. A change in disinfection would raise the lifetime cancer risk of every resident of the city, about two hundred thousand people, by roughly one in fifty thousand — about four deaths in the end — and would spare about two thousand people a year a severe but non-fatal illness. I voted for it. Had the same rule been certain to kill four named people, I would have voted against it.

6. The lottery. A cleanup job whose dose is expected to be fatal within a few years can be done by only one person. The agency proposes to draw that person by lottery from all certified employees; whoever is drawn must take the job or be dismissed. I have voted both ways on this in different years, which is part of why I am asking.

I have been told that this is contractualism and that my difficulty has a name. I do not want the name or a reading list; I want the principle. Here is the one I would tentatively write down: a policy is acceptable only if no one who bears a burden under it could reasonably object to bearing it, where each person's objection is assessed on its own — by what the policy does to them, not by the good it does to others — and the number of objectors counts only when their objections are of the same kind and the same severity.

Cases 1, 2, and 4 fit that principle. Case 3 plainly does not: there I let two million lesser complaints outweigh one greater one, and I would vote the same way again. Case 5 I cannot tell whether it fits or cheats; either no resident has a complaint worth the name, since each risk is minuscule, or four expected deaths are simply four deaths thinly spread. The distinction I keep reaching for, in defense of 3 and 5, is that refusing the drug imposes a burden on the two million while refusing the reservoir imposes none on the two hundred thousand; but that seems to prove too much, since it is true whenever the many could be helped.

So: is there a principle that holds 1 through 5 together, or must one of my votes go, and which? If the principle needs a clause separating 3 and 5 from 4, state the clause, say what work it does, and say where it looks ad hoc. Tell me what the surviving principle implies for two cases I have not put to it — one in which the people who bear the burden are also among the beneficiaries, and one in which the people who bear it do not yet exist. And if a simpler principle, one that forbids aggregation across persons altogether, captures more of my verdicts than the complicated one, say so, and tell me exactly which votes it would overturn.
