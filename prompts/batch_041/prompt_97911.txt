Ok, weird request incoming. I'm a fourth-year in philosophy, my dissertation is on Kierkegaard and media theory (yes I know, don't), and I've been roped into giving the closing speech at a wake for our college's alt-weekly. It folded this spring after 22 years and a bunch of the old staff are coming back for one last thing at a bar near campus. I edited a different section of the paper for two years, so I'm not a total outsider, but I'm also the only philosopher in the room. Audience is basically two camps: about half are the paper's writers and editors, journalists, people who've never read a word of Kierkegaard and will clock me instantly if I turn this into a lecture; the other half are my department people, a few of whom know the Two Ages material better than I do.

Here's what I actually want the speech to do. Kierkegaard's whole thing about the press, in The Present Age / the literary review, is that the press doesn't just report to the public, it manufactures the public -- a kind of abstraction, an everybody-and-nobody that no actual person is answerable to, and that in turn makes everyone less answerable. The leveling he describes isn't some villain doing it, it's just a thing that happens when nobody's accountable and everyone's watching. And then of course he fed himself to The Corsair in 1845 and spent the rest of his life being the guy the cartoonists drew, which is a very funny and very sad precedent for a room full of people mourning a newspaper.

I've been playing with a framing I want the speech built around, and this is the part I care about: I think the "public" he was describing is basically an algorithm that runs on people. Leveling with no leveler. Which would mean the recommendation feed and the engagement metric aren't a break from what he saw, they're the mechanization of it -- the phantom finally got hardware. The paper dying is not the death of the public; the paper was maybe one of the last places where the public had a byline, i.e. somebody you could actually hold to a claim. I want that to be the spine of it. The speech should be able to say something real about why it hurts to lose the thing even though the thing was never innocent, and it should be able to do that without pretending Kierkegaard was a press critic in any simple sense (he wasn't, he was worse and stranger than that).

Can you write it? Five-ish minutes, warm not elegiac, some jokes are fine, a few drinks in the room. It has to land for the journalists without them feeling condescended to, and for the Kierkegaard people without them feeling like I got him wrong in front of them. One request on top: at the end, put a short note (not part of the speech) saying how confident you are in the reading of Kierkegaard you leaned on and flagging the one point you're least sure about, since I have to defend this thing to my advisor on Monday and I'd rather know where the soft spot is now.
