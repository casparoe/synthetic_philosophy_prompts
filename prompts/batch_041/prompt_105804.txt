For about thirty years I worked on numerical weather prediction — running forecast models, sitting in review meetings arguing about whether a change to the physics scheme was a real improvement or just a lucky fit to one bad winter. I retired four years ago and have been reading philosophy of science properly for the first time, and the thing I most wanted to be true is Popper's idea of verisimilitude, because as far as I can tell it's a formal version of what we were doing all along: this model is closer to the truth than that one. So I was a bit disapointed to learn that the whole programme apparently fizzled. The story as I've pieced it together: Popper said one theory has more verisimilitude than another if it has more true consequences and fewer false ones; then in 1974 a few people (Tichý, Miller, and one or two others?) showed his own definition has the absurd consequence that no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other, and Popper accepted the point fairly quickly. Then the rescue attempts: some kept the content/consequence idea but made 'relevant' consequences do the work, others went for likeness — posit a space of possible worlds with some distance measure and say a theory is closer to the truth the closer its nearest worlds are to the actual one — which is roughly what I'd have naively expected anyway. The distance measure is where I get stuck, and I gather I'm not alone; I've also seen it said that these comparisons come out differently depending on the language you formulate the theories in, and there's apparently an impossibility theorem of some kind floating around from the 2000s. Meanwhile plenty of people seem to have quietly walked off — either to accuracy-first / epistemic utility work, where you score a credence function against each possible world, or to the line that science only ever needed empirical adequacy and never needed truthlikeness at all.

What I'd like from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a substantive impasse — a real disagreement about what a formal explication of closeness to the truth is supposed to deliver — or is it partly verbal, with one camp wanting a definition that does explanatory work in the realism debate (explaining why science succeeds) and another wanting a language-invariant mathematical object, so that a single word is doing two jobs? Or is a hidden question carrying all the weight: whether the notion has to be usable in practice, whether the world comes with a metric on it at all, whether the trivialization results should push us to a pluralist or comparative-only notion rather than a single measure? And if it really is stalled, tell me what kind of progress is even available here — a theorem, a distinction, a change of question, or nothing at all.

I have a suspicion of my own that I'd rather you test than agree with, since it comes out of my old job rather than out of the literature. When we said a forecast was closer to the truth, the space was given to us independently — pressure, temperature, wind on a fixed grid — so distance from the truth meant something before we chose a metric, and the metric was then a separate, arguable question (do you care about the deep tropics or the 2-metre temperature over Ohio?). With a scientific theory I can't see what plays that role: the space seems to be constituted by the theories themselves, so any measure of closeness has to be built out of the vocabulary and ontology of one of the theories being compared, which makes the whole exercise circular and lets you gerrymander the verdict. If that's the real problem, the formal wing was doomed from the start and the live question becomes what a scientific realist can still say about progress without a metric — and I want to know whether that's a genuine option or whether it just collapses back into 'theories that work'. If my suspicion is wrong, tell me where it goes wrong, and be concrete about which of the current proposals (consequence, relevant-consequence, likeness, or something else entirely) actually escapes it.
