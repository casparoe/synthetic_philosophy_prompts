I sit on the disciplinary panel of a state bar association and need to circulate a written opinion before Friday's hearing. A defense attorney, in an unrecorded hallway exchange with a prospective witness, said, in substance, "I'd hate to see you dragged through a deposition; you have enough going on at home." Nothing in the words is a threat, and the attorney says he meant only sympathy and a nudge about the witness's time. The witness says she heard a warning that testifying would cost her. Our rule bars a lawyer from attempting to influence a witness by means of threats, and the panel must decide whether a threat can be communicated without being stated. Draft the opinion: a clear disposition — formal discipline, a private admonition, or dismissal — with reasons the attorney could actually answer, and an honest account of the strongest case for him, which is not merely that he says he meant no harm, but that our rule, read fairly, may reach only what a speaker asserts or requests and not what a hearer infers. Because the exchange was unwritten and the recollections conflict, the reasoning must proceed from what a cooperative speaker in that setting would have been taken to convey. Address two points specifically. First, what weight the attorney's later public insistence that he "wasn't threatening anyone" should receive, given that the hearer had already taken up the implication — can an implication be cancelled after the fact? Second, whether it matters that the implication seems to depend on this particular context rather than on the words themselves, and whether the test the panel has been using — drawn from Grice's account of conversational implicature, roughly that content the speaker could have disclaimed without self-contradiction was implicated rather than said — settles the case or merely narrows it.
