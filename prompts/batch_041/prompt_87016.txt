ok so I'm running a grad seminar on personal identity and I've somehow become the referee in a two-year argument between two of my students, and I want to force them to produce the document they should have written a year ago instead of just going in circles. can you draft it for me.

here are the two camps, roughly. one of them takes what she calls the pattern line: what carries a person forward is the right kind of information-processing organisation, so if you replace someone's neurons one at a time with functionally equivalent parts -- silicon, whatever -- the person is there the whole way through, no moment where anyone dies. the other one takes the biological-process line: a person is a particular ongoing physical process, and you can't swap out its parts for functionally equivalent ones without at some point ending that process. on his view the thing that walks out the other side is a very good successor, not the original, even if nobody can tell from the outside.

I've been feeding them little cases to fight about, and I'd like the writeup to be built around three of them. (1) the long swap: units replaced one at a time, spaced over years, subject awake and living her life throughout. (2) the table swap: same start, same end state, same fidelity, but it all happens in one session under anaesthesia. (3) the kept original: gradual replacement, but the biological parts that come out are preserved and later reassembled, so at the end there are two candidate people who both remember being her. and a fourth wrinkle I want them to address: suppose the replacement parts are only 99.9% faithful and the errors slowly accumulate, so the organisation itself drifts over the swap, the way it would drift over ordinary aging anyway.

what I want out of it: a numbered list of claims both of them can actually sign -- about what's at stake in the question, what the cases do and don't settle, what would count as evidence either way. then the real disagreements, each one written in a form that its own side would accept as fair, not a strawman. then the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling things would actually move one of them, and for each crux a concrete case or argument that they both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. if any part of their fight turns out to be purely verbal -- same facts, different words -- say which part and why. keep it constructive; the whole point is a text both of them could put their names to.
