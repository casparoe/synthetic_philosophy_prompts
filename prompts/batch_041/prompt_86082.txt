I'm a high school student writing a paper for my philosophy club on whether I am one persisting person over time — endurantism, I think it's called — or a succession of momentary selves. A few ordinary institutions seem to take opposite sides on this, and I can't tell whether that is a real inconsistency or whether they never had to commit themselves. I can sign a contract at eighteen that binds me for thirty years, and I can write an advance directive that doctors are supposed to follow decades later, withholding treatment from a person who by then seems content and asks to live. Yet almost everywhere I am forbidden to sell a kidney, and one argument for the ban is that the money would go to me now while the harm would fall on a later self, a different person, who never agreed to the deal. What view of personal identity across time do these practices presuppose? Do they presuppose it strictly, or do they merely fit well with it and sit just as comfortably with the view that I am a series of person-stages, one at each moment? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practices pull in different directions, and tell me which rival theories of identity would license exactly the same rules.
