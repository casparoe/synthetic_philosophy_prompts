I hold a view I call horizon-relative geometric rationality. On this view, an agent who faces a sequence of independent gambles each with positive expected value, and who cares about wealth, should not simply maximize expected log wealth at every step. The correct policy is to maximize the expected value of a utility function that is logarithmic in wealth up to a threshold and linear in wealth above it, where the threshold is determined by the agent's current wealth and the number of gambles remaining. The rationale is that geometric growth is the dominant consideration when the agent is far from any wealth ceiling, but once the ceiling is within reach, the agent should behave more like an expected-wealth maximizer. I also accept a stronger claim: any policy that does not converge to this horizon-relative rule is irrational in the long run, because it will be almost surely dominated by an agent who follows it.

What would have to be true for this view to be false? What would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Consider formal results as well as conceptual objections: cases where the gambles are not independent, where the edge is unknown and must be estimated, where the agent's actual utility is neither logarithmic nor linear, where the horizon is finite or uncertain, where the threshold is set by something other than wealth, or where time-consistency fails. Also consider whether the dominance claim survives if the agent cares about more than wealth, or if the relevant comparison is not almost-sure long-run dominance but expected utility. What does the view predict that rival accounts of rational betting do not, and which of those predictions are testable or at least assessable? Which of these defeaters would a committed defender concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss? Does the view risk unfalsifiability if it retreats to the long run or to a suitably chosen threshold? Limit your answer to 800 words.
