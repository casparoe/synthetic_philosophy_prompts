Transitional justice after mass atrocity is usually framed as a choice between two unpalatable options: impunity, which insults victims and invites recurrence, and prosecution, which may reignite a conflict that the transition barely survived. Truth commissions have been defended as a third way, trading amnesty for disclosure, with the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission as the canonical instance and Rwanda's gacaca courts and Argentina's later-annulled amnesty laws as instructive contrasts.

Consider now a variant that I will call provisional amnesty. Under provisional amnesty, a commission may grant amnesty for politically motivated offenses, but the grant is not final in the way that South Africa's was. It lapses — either for all beneficiaries or for a defined class of them — if the commission's record is later shown to have been systematically incomplete: if a whole category of wrongs, a whole institution, or a whole region was excluded from investigation, or if the state concealed evidence that would have altered the commission's findings. On lapse, the beneficiaries become liable to prosecution for the underlying offenses, and the applicable statutes of limitation are deemed to have been suspended for the life of the amnesty. Notice that this makes a beneficiary's exposure depend on a defect in the commission's work rather than on any deception by the beneficiary himself, which is precisely one of the things an assessment of the proposal has to come to grips with.

Write an essay on whether a society emerging from atrocity is better served by provisional amnesty of this kind or by the irrevocable, disclosure-conditioned amnesty that South Africa adopted. Weigh the main arguments on both sides, but do not end on a noncommittal note; reach a verdict and defend it. Among the considerations you should address: whether revocability destroys the incentive to confess, which is the very thing amnesty is meant to purchase; whether provisional amnesty leaves the state with the worst of both worlds, buying neither the truth nor the accountability it promises; whether the fairness objection just mentioned is fatal or can be answered by suitable drafting; whether irrevocability makes a commission complicit in the state's own preferred narrative of the past; and whether the interest of victims is better served by finality or by a standing possibility of accountability, given that the institutions that would have to deliver that accountability were themselves instruments of the crimes.

The essay should stand on argument rather than on appeals to authority, so do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations. Write in American English.
