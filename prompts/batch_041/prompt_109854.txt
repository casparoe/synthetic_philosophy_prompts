I sat in on my reading group's session last week on whether the numbers count — the puzzle about whether, when you can save either one person or five and not both, the fact that there are five gives you any reason at all, given the argument usually credited to Taurek that saving the five rather than the one leaves no particular person worse off than they would otherwise have been, so that all six have equally strong claims and the choice between them is a matter of indifference, to be settled by a coin if it is to be settled at all. I said almost nothing, partly because I couldn't decide, but two of the others, Ravi and Jess, went three rounds and are still cross with each other. I've been reading rationalist blogs for a decade and have a lot of patience for people who will say exactly which premise they reject, so I'd rather have a move-by-move verdict than a scoreline.

Here is the exchange as best I can reproduce it. (1) Ravi said the argument is circular as stated: if we save the five, the one is worse off than he would have been, and if we save the one, each of the five is worse off than he would have been, so there is no asymmetry in *who* is worse off — the only difference is how many people are in that position, which is precisely the thing the argument says doesn't matter. (2) Jess granted that the five claims cannot be summed into one bigger claim, but said this is not the same as the number of claimants being irrelevant: we can give each claim equal weight and still notice that there are five of them, much as five ten-pence coins are worth more than one, and summing equal claims is not the same as summing happiness. (3) Ravi said that the moment you count claims you have already aggregated, because deciding that claims are the things to be counted, and that they are to be weighted equally, is itself a distributive decision; once you have a common currency for claims you have no principled place to stop. He added that the coin at least refuses to weigh anything, and merely declines to prefer one person's survival to another's. (4) Jess said that a view on which a coin is the right procedure must equally accept a rule that always saved the single person, or always saved whoever happened to be standing on the left, and that nobody could accept such a rule without knowing which side of it they would fall on; she then put a case of her own — one person will die unless treated, or two people will each be permanently blinded unless treated — and said Ravi's argument must make that a coin flip too. (5) Ravi said her case shows only that blindness and death are hard to compare, and that the veil-of-ignorance move assumes we can identify with a chance of being any of the six, which quietly treats the group as one person writ large; he said that if he is wrong about this, Jess owes him an account of why the numbers stop counting somewhere. (6) Jess said she is happy to give one: the numbers count when the claims on each side are equal in strength and not otherwise, so five weak claims do not beat one strong one, whereas five claims of the same strength do.

Please go through the six moves one at a time, grading each on a scale from plainly mistaken through questionable to plainly right, and giving the reason for each grade rather than a summary judgement at the end. Then tell me what each of them is missing — in particular whether the "no one is worse off" argument survives Jess's two cases, whether Ravi's coin really escapes the charge of aggregation, and which of the terms the two of them throw around (claim, weight, count, worse off) is doing work in the argument without either of them having defined it.
