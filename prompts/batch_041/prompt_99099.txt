I'm a graduate student in the history of economic thought, and I've been working through Hume's essays on commerce from the 1752 Political Discourses — the essay on money and the companion piece on the balance of trade, with the essay on interest alongside them. I've hit an argument I can only give you in paraphrase, since I'm working from a library copy I can't quote at length, and I'd like your help getting it straight. Hume begins by insisting that money is not itself one of the subjects of commerce but only an instrument men have agreed upon to facilitate exchange; he compares it to a lubricant rather than to a working part of the machinery of trade, and he claims that the absolute quantity of money in a kingdom makes no difference to its real wealth, because prices simply rise to absorb whatever quantity is present. So far I take myself to be following a quantity-theory line. But then he turns around and argues that an increase in the quantity of money can quicken industry, and that this effect depends on the increase not being generally perceived or anticipated: the benefit lies in the interval before prices adjust, during which some people find themselves with more money in hand while prices are still low, and the resulting activity draws labour and commerce forward. He seems to add that a sudden drain of money out of a country can be harmful in the same way, and — this is the part that really puzzles me — he appears to suggest that the good policy of a magistrate consists in keeping the quantity of money slowly increasing, precisely because that keeps alive a spirit of industry, even while he also warns that this cannot safely be done by debasing the coin. What I want from you, acting as a careful historian of eighteenth-century political economy who is also comfortable with mechanism-talk and counterfactuals, is a patient reconstruction of what Hume is actually committed to in this stretch of argument.

Before you begin writing, think through the possible readings rather than settling on the first one. Is the interval argument consistent with the neutrality claim, or does it quietly concede that money is non-neutral — and if the latter, is the non-neutrality a mere transition phenomenon that leaves the long-run doctrine intact, or does it run deeper? Does the whole mechanism depend on the increase being unanticipated, and if so, what does Hume think happens once everyone learns to expect it: does the effect evaporate, or is it simply priced in from the start? How does this square with what he says elsewhere about interest depending on the state of industry and profit rather than on the sheer quantity of money, and with his account of how money flows between nations to correct a trade imbalance? And what is the magistrate's role supposed to be — is Hume describing a self-regulating system in which the interval is a transient disturbance no one should try to manage, or is he offering a genuine if fragile policy recommendation, and if the latter, why does he distrust the obvious instrument for carrying it out?
