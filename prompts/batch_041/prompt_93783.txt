I'm a senior in a philosophy program, and this term I'm in a seminar on decision theory and ethics that has spent the last several weeks on idealized-preference accounts of practical reason — the family of views on which what you have reason to do, or what is genuinely good for you, is fixed not by your actual preferences but by the preferences you would have if you were somehow improved: better informed, more coherent, more vividly aware, unclouded by addiction, depression, or adaptive resignation. The cases that do the motivating work are the familiar ugly ones — the contented slave, the person who has trimmed her ambitions to fit a life she cannot escape, the addict who endorses his craving — and the proposal that keeps coming up is the one that extrapolates a whole community's values rather than a single person's, on the thought that what we really want is what we would converge on under idealized reflection rather than what any of us wants now. My roommate and I argued about this until two in the morning last week and got nowhere, which is roughly where the seminar is too: everyone grants that actual preferences cannot be the last word, and then every attempt to say what the last word is either quietly collapses back into actual preferences or smuggles in the theorist's own values. For my final paper I want to stop defending another contender and instead write the specification.

What I want from you is the desiderata list: what any adequate theory of this kind — any account that identifies an agent's "ideal" or "extrapolated" preferences — would have to deliver. Which cases must it get right, beyond the adapted-preference ones above: the case where the idealized agent would, on reflection, choose to be less idealized; the case where two different idealization procedures return different verdicts about the same person; the collective case, where the extrapolated volition of a group matches no individual member's ideal; and the self-modification case, where the idealized agent would want to change what counts as idealizing in the first place. Which distinctions must it not collapse — between what an agent ideally wants and what is good for her; between idealizing an agent's preferences and replacing them; between the claim that idealization is genuinely indeterminate and the claim that it is merely hard to compute. And which formal constraints must it satisfy — that the idealization not presuppose its own output, that iterating it converge to a fixed point rather than drifting further with each pass, and that it be defined at all in the self-modification case. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about which requirements are genuine and which are just commitments of one camp.

The part I care about most is the conflict report. I suspect that the requirement that the ideal agent be recognizable as the agent's own — that she not be a stranger whose preferences merely happen to be better — cannot be jointly satisfied with the requirement that idealization correct for adaptive and distorted preferences, since the very preferences we most want to correct are the ones the agent most identifies with; if you agree, say so and lay out the shape of the tradeoff, and if you can find other pairs that cannot be jointly satisfied, that is the most interesting result the paper could have. Finish with a short assessment of which single requirement does the most to rule out the accounts people actually defend — the fully informed account, the coherent-extrapolation account, and the ideal-advisor picture — but don't give me a full scorecard; I'll be running the comparison myself.
