I am a novelist, and between drafts of a novel set in the 1840s I have written a short essay — part author's note, part argument — on how practices like dueling and slaveholding actually ended, and I would like your comments before I send it out.

The thesis: moral argument did very little of the work. Both practices were argued against for a century without any visible effect on conduct, and what changed was not conviction but the price of contempt. Every such practice is held up by an economy of contempt — who is entitled to look down on whom, and for what — and it ends when contempt is redirected from those who refuse the practice onto those who keep it. A corollary I want to press: contempt aimed at the act leaves the practice standing, since the practitioner can always plead exception or necessity; only contempt aimed at the sort of person who does it moves anyone. On this account abolition succeeded where slaveholding became a mark of coarseness rather than mastery, and dueling died in England while the statutes against it sat unenforced — the last fatal duel on English soil, at Priest Hill in 1852, was fought not by Englishmen but by two French exiles who had carried their quarrel and their code over with them, which suggests the English stopped believing before they stopped legislating, and that those still dueling were the ones with no English reputation left to spend. That is the other half of the thesis, and the part I trust least: I claim that revolutions run fastest where the old practitioners can convert their standing into a new currency — the planter into a man of commerce, the duelist into a man of business — and stall where no conversion is available, so that the same arguments fail in one place and succeed in another.

What I want from you is pressure, not encouragement. Is this a mechanism or a redescription — have I said more than that norms change once enough people stop minding? Is it genuinely distinct from the honor-centered accounts of dueling's decline (Appiah's, for instance) and the interest-centered accounts of abolition (the Williams line), or have I merely re-labelled one of them? Does the conversion claim do independent work, or is it decoration any story could accommodate? And give me the strongest counterexample you can: a practice that ended with no revaluation of contempt, or a contempt redirected while the practice survived. Then tell me which paragraph you would cut as the weakest, and why. I do not want a reading list; I want to know where the argument breaks.
