I am the technology director for a mid-sized public school district — roughly nine thousand students, forty-odd buildings — and I am the person who signs off, or refuses to sign off, whenever a school wants to adopt a piece of software that touches student data. This comes up maybe twice a month, sometimes more, and the stakes are real: these tools run on children's information, the district is small enough that a bad headline would end careers, and I have no legal training and no budget to hire anyone who does. The superintendent signs the final contract, but nothing moves without my recommendation, and I usually have a week or less to produce one.

I have been trying to find a way to make these calls that isn't just asking whether the data is sensitive, because that test gives me the wrong answer constantly. What I have landed on, after a lot of reading and arguing with colleagues, is this: privacy is not mainly about keeping facts secret. It is about whether a particular flow of information — from some party, about some aspect of a person, to some other party, under some rule governing how it moves — matches what people would reasonably expect in the setting where it happens. The same fact can be unremarkable in one setting and a betrayal in another. So the real question is never whether the data is sensitive; it is whether this flow fits this setting, and who set the expectation in the first place.

I want you to turn that into a procedure I can actually run. Specifically: a fixed list of questions in the order I should ask them, with a note on each about what would count as a sufficient answer and what would count as a dealbreaker; the point at which the procedure stops and returns a no; and the points where it stops and hands the decision to a person — me, the superintendent, the parents — because the question is one of values rather than facts. Keep it short enough that I can run it in an afternoon and write the result up in a paragraph for the superintendent, and make it honest about which of its steps are just my judgment dressed up as method.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show the reasoning at each step:

1. A reading-comprehension app that the special-education teachers love and that has visibly helped struggling readers, but whose vendor collects keystroke-level data on every answer, keeps it indefinitely, and uses it to train models sold to other districts.
2. A request from the district's school resource officers for routine access to the discipline and attendance records of students on their watch, which the principals are inclined to grant because it is easier than saying no.
3. A tool that would let parents see, in real time, the location of the bus their child is riding — requested by a parents' group after a scare last spring.

Case 1 is the one where I suspect the procedure is going to tell me to say no to something I badly want to say yes to, and I want that worked through rather than softened. Finally, tell me plainly what this procedure cannot do and where it will misfire — including what happens when the setting's own norms are unsettled or openly contested, and when a single person sits in two settings at once, as a student who is also a patient. If the way I have framed this leaves some of these calls genuinely undecidable rather than merely hard, say so, and say which part of the framing is doing that.
