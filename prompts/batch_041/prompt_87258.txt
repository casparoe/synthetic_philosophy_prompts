I have been defending a principle for years, and a colleague has finally constructed an argument that seems to make it devour everything. I would like help locating the break, if there is one.

(1) If we can prevent intense suffering at a cost that is trivial to us, and the suffering is neither deserved nor a necessary means to some compensating good, then we are morally required to prevent it.
(2) Suffering of exactly that kind occurs in the wild at astronomical scale: predation, starvation, disease, exposure. A fawn that starves over several days, nestlings that die slowly under parasites, animals that freeze — none of this pain is lesser for having been caused by a predator or a pathogen rather than by a person.
(3) We already accept that we may reduce such suffering when it is easy: vaccinating a wildlife population against an outbreak, freeing an animal trapped in a fence, treating mange in a local fox population.
(4) The cost to us of doing far more — research, oral vaccines delivered by bait, contraception or genetic interventions to reduce predator or prey populations — is small relative to what we already spend on things we would not call morally urgent.
(5) Therefore we are required to intervene in nature on a large scale to reduce wild animal suffering.

I am prepared to accept all of that. Here is the part that alarms me.

(6) The reason to intervene is that the world contains a vast quantity of suffering that we could reduce, and that reason makes no mention of whose suffering it is or when it occurs. If suffering counts the same wherever and whenever it occurs, then the same reasoning that commands me to vaccinate the fox also commands me to weigh the suffering of animals in the distant past, which I cannot reach, and — more troubling — the total sum of suffering across the future.
(7) And if what is being minimized is the total quantity of suffering rather than the suffering of any particular animal, then the most efficient intervention may be not to help any existing animal but to prevent future animals from existing: to drive a predator species to extinction, or to sterilize a prey species whose members are destined mostly to starve, on the ground that no animal is harmed by never being born.
(8) Taken seriously, then, the principle appears to require me to work toward the deliberate extinction of animal lineages and to divert nearly all of my resources from humans who need them, on the ground that the numbers in the wild are larger.

I will not accept (8). But (1) is the only premise I am willing to fight for, and my suspicion is that the trouble lies in the extension rather than in the seed — somewhere in the move from (5) to (6), or from (6) to (7), rather than in (1) itself. Tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked compelling in the first place, and price the alternatives: what does each candidate rejection cost someone who wants to keep (1)?

Two further things. First, be honest about the practical disanalogies — that ecosystems are largely zero-sum, so helping one population often harms another, and that our ability to predict the net effects of large interventions is poor — and say whether you think these considerations belong to the moral argument or only to its application. Second, in your answer separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this question, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
