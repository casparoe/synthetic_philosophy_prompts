I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, spending a fellowship year embedded with the interpretability team at a mid-sized AI company. The engineers keep asking, in informal terms, a question I need to turn into something repeatable: when some internal state or feature of a model is causally implicated in its behaviour, is it legitimate to say that the state represents anything, and if so, what does it represent? I want a decision procedure that a competent engineer with no philosophy training can run on a single feature in an afternoon, and whose verdicts can go into the model card that ships with the product; our research lead signs off on those, so the procedure has to survive someone who will push back on it. Build it on the etiological tradition in the theory of content — content fixed by the conditions under which a state is supposed to be produced and the work it is supposed to do downstream, with the possibility of misrepresentation rather than mere correlation doing the diagnostic work. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure halts with a negative verdict, and where it hands the question to a person because what is at stake is what we have decided the system is for rather than any fact about the system. Then run it on three cases: a hidden unit in a vision model that fires reliably for dog snouts; a state in a closed-loop neuroprosthetic controller that the engineers describe as the user's intended grasp; and a distilled copy of one of our trained networks, which behaves identically but has no training history of its own. At least one verdict should be one the engineers will not like. Close by saying what the procedure cannot settle — in particular where indeterminacy of content, and the gap between what a system does and what it was supposed to do, leave it stuck.
