For a philosophy club essay, I want to read Groundhog Day through the idea of sphexishness, and I would like you to work out what the film actually does with it. The term comes from the digger wasp Sphex: she paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold while she goes inside to inspect, and then emerges to haul the cricket in; if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she is inside, she fetches it back to the threshold and inspects the burrow all over again, and in one well-worn retelling she will repeat the whole sequence as many as forty times rather than simply drag the cricket inside. Douglas Hofstadter coined the word for this in 1982, and Daniel Dennett later pressed it into service in his arguments about free will. The point of the idea, as I understand it, is not that the wasp's behaviour is caused -- everything is caused -- but that her routine runs to completion, cannot be interrupted or revised in the light of what the situation actually calls for, and preserves the appearance of purpose after the purpose has been defeated. I have also read that the anecdote is disputed, that after many repetitions the wasp does adapt and that re-inspecting a disturbed burrow may itself be adaptive; I mention it in case it bears on the reading, though I am not sure how much it should.

Here is the question I want to put to the film. Early in the loop Phil Connors is sphexish in an almost literal sense: he wakes to the same clock radio, steps through the same day, learns the town's residents as a set of triggers (the insurance salesman, the innkeeper, the boy who falls from the tree, the man who chokes at the diner), and rehearses Rita until he can predict her to the second. But the film is not about a man who stays in the loop; it is about a man who leaves it, and I want to know what the film thinks the difference is between his rigidity and his eventual flexibility. Is it a difference of degree, his repertoire simply growing as he accumulates memory and skill, or a difference of kind, with him beginning to act for reasons rather than being tripped into action? Does the film need freedom in the libertarian sense, some choice that nothing caused, or does it in fact dramatize a compatibilist picture in which being caused to act and being unable to act otherwise come apart? And what should I make of the fact that Phil is the only thing in Punxsutawney that accumulates while everything around him replays exactly? Is the film locating flexibility in learning within a mechanism, or is that a cheat, since the loop itself is a device the screenwriters switch on and off?

Please also say honestly where the reading breaks down, because I suspect the ending resists it. The loop is broken by Phil becoming a decent person and by Rita's love, which is a satisfying resolution but may be a payoff of plot rather than a thesis about agency, and the groundhog, the blizzard and the radio alarm are comic machinery rather than a metaphysics. Tell me where the film is doing philosophy and where it is only doing story, and finish by saying what the film contributes to the debate that an argument about the wasp could not.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
