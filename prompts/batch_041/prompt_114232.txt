I am working through a difficult stretch of Aristotle's Metaphysics — Book VII (Zeta), chapter 3 — and would be grateful for a careful reading of it. The translation I have in front of me runs as follows:

"Now in one sense we call the matter the substrate; in another, the shape; and in a third, the combination of the two. By matter I mean, for instance, bronze; by shape, the arrangement of the form; and by the combination of the two, the concrete thing: the statue. Thus if the form is prior to the matter and more truly existent, by the same argument it will also be prior to the combination.

We have now stated in outline the nature of substance — that it is not that which is predicated of a subject, but that of which the other things are predicated. But we must not merely define it so, for it is not enough. Not only is the statement itself obscure, but also it makes matter substance; for if matter is not substance, it is beyond our power to say what else is. For when everything else is removed, clearly nothing but matter remains; because all the other things are affections, products and potencies of bodies, and length, breadth and depth are kinds of quantity, and not substances. For quantity is not a substance; rather the substance is that to which these affections primarily belong. But when we take away length and breadth and depth we can see no thing remaining, unless it be the something bounded by them; so that on this view matter must appear to be the only substance.

By matter I mean that which in itself is neither a particular thing nor a quantity nor designated by any of the categories which define Being. For there is something of which each of these is predicated, whose being is different from that of each one of the categories; because all other things are predicated of substance, but this is predicated of matter. Thus the ultimate substrate is in itself neither a particular thing nor a quantity nor anything else. Nor indeed is it the negations of these; for the negations too will only apply to it accidentally.

If we hold this view, it follows that matter is substance. But this is impossible; for it is accepted that separability and individuality belong especially to substance. Hence it would seem that the form and the combination of form and matter are more truly substance than matter is.

The substance, then, which consists of both — I mean of matter and form — may be dismissed, since it is posterior and obvious. Matter too is in a sense evident. We must consider the third type, for this is the most perplexing."

Immediately before this, the chapter has canvassed the candidates for what substance is — the essence, the universal, the genus, and the substrate — and has characterized the substrate as that of which everything else is predicated while it is itself predicated of nothing.

I can follow the sentences one at a time, but I cannot see how the argument as a whole is supposed to work, and what I want is a reconstruction of it rather than a summary of the chapter. Four things in particular.

First, set out the argument that arrives at the conclusion that matter alone is substance, making clear what is being "removed" and in what order, and then explain exactly what the appeal to separability and individuality contributes at the end. What would matter have to possess in order to qualify, and what precisely does the bronze fail to possess?

Second, explain the remark that the ultimate substrate is not even the negation of any of the categories, "for the negations too will only apply to it accidentally." Why does the argument need this, and what would go wrong if the substrate were allowed to be, say, non-quantitative in its own right?

Third, the passage first ranks form and the compound above matter as "more truly substance," then sets the compound aside as "posterior and obvious" and matter as "in a sense evident," leaving the third type as "the most perplexing." What is the force of "posterior and obvious" here? Why does the compound — the statue, the very thing whose constitution is in question — drop out of the inquiry instead of serving as the paradigm case?

Fourth, and for me the hardest point: if substance is what is not predicated of a subject, what is the author's position on the sentence "the statue is bronze"? Read as predication, it makes bronze a predicate rather than a substance; read as identity, it makes the statue and the bronze one thing, and the distinction between the compound and its matter seems to vanish. Using only what the passage commits him to, say which reading he must take and what he must give up in taking it. Then test the result against a case: a bronze statue is melted down and recast as a different figure, so that the bronze survives while the statue does not. Does the passage require us to count the bronze, and not the statue, as the substance — and does any of its own wording pull the other way?

Where the argument is genuinely aporetic, say so and explain what the difficulty is, rather than dissolving it by appeal to what Aristotle is generally thought to hold.
