I am a high school student who competes in competitive debate, and I have spent enough time in that world to be fluent in its vocabulary: constructive cases and rebuttals, cross-examination, claims and warrants and impacts, burdens of proof and rejoinder, topicality, fiat, and the everyday business of extending an argument or letting it drop. I have also recently become interested in a puzzle from philosophy. When someone asserts that something is the case, what has to be true of the speaker for the assertion to count as being in good standing? One answer is that the speaker must know it; another is that the assertion merely has to be true; another is that the speaker only has to believe it. I would like you to restate that debate in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. You do not need to cite any sources; I am asking for your own reconstruction, not a literature review.

Please organize your answer in three parts.

First, translate the competing norms into debate terms. Treat a round as a system in which speakers make claims to a judge, and ask what each proposed norm would amount to as a rule for when a competitor is entitled to make a claim, when an opponent may object, and what the judge is entitled to do in response.

Second, give the residue report: for each norm, say what translates cleanly, what survives only with distortion, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. I especially want you to address three things. One is that debate plainly allows a competitor to assert what they do not believe, and it has notions like fiat and arguing a position for the sake of the round, so a belief-based norm looks either invisible or already rejected in that setting. Another is that the philosophical debate seems to treat assertion as a distinctive act whose norm flows from what assertion essentially is, whereas debate treats a claim as a move in a game, judged by its effects on a judge. The third is the relationship among knowledge, truth, and belief, and whether debate can distinguish them at all.

Third, give me a verdict rather than a survey. Is the residue a defect of my framework, something debate genuinely cannot represent, or is it evidence that the philosophers' question is less substantive than it looks, so that assertion in their sense is a stipulation and debate simply stipulates differently? Take a definite position and defend it. Where you are genuinely unsure, say so plainly, but still commit to the reading you think is best and tell me what would change your mind.
