In my graduate seminar on the epistemology of practical reasoning we have spent several weeks on the family of views that make knowledge — or the propriety of assertion and practical reasoning — sensitive to practical stakes. For the seminar's case-study unit I would like you to write an analysis of roughly 1500 words of the Challenger launch decision of January 1986, treated as a test case for those views. Work from what is common knowledge about the episode and from the stipulations below; do not pad the piece with references, reading suggestions, or appeals to a literature you cannot consult, and where you are unsure of a factual detail, mark it as an assumption rather than asserting it.

Set out the three positions cleanly before applying them. The first holds that practical stakes are themselves among the conditions on knowledge: raise the stakes enough and a true, well-evidenced belief that would otherwise count as knowledge stops counting. The second holds that knowledge is invariant, but that the standards governing the word "knows" in a conversational context shift with what is at stake for the participants, so that the same belief can be truly described as knowledge in one room and truly denied in another. The third holds that knowledge is one thing and the stakes another: what changes under pressure is not the epistemic position but the practical reasoning built on top of it.

The facts you may take as given: engineers at the solid-rocket-motor contractor had for years documented erosion of the field joints' seals, had argued against launching at a temperature for which they had no data, and in a pre-launch teleconference were pressed to show that the cold would be unsafe rather than that it would be safe; under pressure from the launch schedule the objection was withdrawn, the launch proceeded, and the joints failed. Assume, for the sake of argument, that the evidential situation of the engineers did not change across the days before the teleconference, only the stakes did — that the same people who were later described as certain enough to stake a career on the objection had been content, days earlier, to fly.

That assumption is the point of the exercise. I want three things, in order. First, a clean statement of what each of the three positions implies about the engineers' epistemic position at the moment the question was put to them: did the stakes bear on what they knew, on what "knows" meant in that room, or on neither? Second, a sorting of the case's features into those that genuinely bear on the choice among the three positions and those that are atmosphere — the schedule pressure, the hierarchy, the fact that the decision was made in a teleconference rather than a memo, the fact that the engineers were asking management to act against its own institutional interests. Third, and most important, the reverse direction: what the episode does to the positions. Does it confirm the stakes-sensitivity of knowledge, or expose a cost — that on the first view a sufficiently expensive decision can strip a person of knowledge, so that the more it mattered to be right, the less there was to be right about? Is that a reductio or a description? Then consider whether a fourth position does all the work without stakes entering the epistemology at all: that the stakes govern the credence threshold at which one may act, or the amount of evidence required for rational belief, while knowledge stays fixed. If that position explains everything the first two explain, say so, and say what would remain of them.

Do not settle the case by appealing to what the engineers should have known in hindsight. Keep the epistemic question fixed on the moment of decision, and be explicit where the episode underdetermines the choice among the positions rather than pretending that it decides.
