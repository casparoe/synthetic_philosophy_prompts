I have been reading Hume's political essays with a reading group — the ones on money and on the jealousy of trade, where he calls money the oil that renders the motion of the wheels of trade more smooth and easy rather than one of the wheels themselves, and where he argues that one nation's commercial prosperity tends to promote its neighbours' rather than to come at their expense — and I have come away with a set of verdicts about monetary cases that I cannot get to line up. What I want from you is not a summary of the essays and not a reading list; it is the principle, or small set of competing principles, that actually captures my verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see what each clause is doing.

Here is what I think, case by case. First, if a country's stock of money doubles overnight and nothing else changes, I think nothing real changes: prices settle at twice the level, wages and rents and debts adjust, and the country is neither richer nor poorer. Second, when the increase actually arrives the way increases arrive — gradually, through the hands of merchants and landlords and the people they employ, so that some are spending new money at old prices before anyone has marked prices up — I think there is a real stimulus to industry during the interval, not a mere illusion, and that this interval is where the interesting economics lives. Third, if the new money instead lands in a single grandee's cellar and stays there, I think there is no stimulus at all; that case collapses back into the first, with a longer wait. Fourth, when a country grows rich through trade I think its trading partners generally grow richer too, and that a policy of beggaring them is a mistake; but when a country grows rich by extracting silver from a colony and spending it abroad, I find I do not think the partners are enriched in the same way, and I cannot tell whether that is a different principle or the same one misapplied. Fifth, I am uneasy about banks and paper credit in a way I cannot derive from anything above — it feels like a separate worry about what foreigners will accept in payment and what survives a political crisis, and yet Hume seems to fold it into the same argument. Sixth, I think a country drained of coin but not of goods is not thereby made poorer in any sense that matters.

The difficulty is that the first verdict and the fifth seem to pull against the second: if money is only the oil, why should it matter so much how it enters, and why should it matter whether it is metal or paper? Please state the principle or principles that accommodate as many of these six verdicts as possible, be explicit about the mechanism each clause rests on, say what each candidate principle predicts for cases I have not listed — in particular a modern fiat-money system in which a central bank can create money at will, and a case in which the new money reaches wage-earners first rather than merchants — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to give up.
