I'm a novelist — about 200 pages into a book set in a failing utopian community in upstate New York in the 1840s, and I've been reading Mill's Chapters on Socialism to get the intellectual weather right. One of my characters is a lapsed Owenite who quotes Fourier at people when he's drunk. There's a passage I can't get my head around, and I'd rather understand it than fake it.

Here's the bit, from the third chapter:

It is impossible to deny that the considerations brought to notice in the preceding chapter, make out a frightful case either against the existing order of society, or against the position of man himself in this world. How much of the evils should be referred to the one, and how much to the other, is the principal theoretic question which has to be resolved.

Then a few paragraphs later:

Though much of their allegations is unanswerable, not a little is the result of errors in political economy; by which, let me say once for all, I do not mean the rejection of any practical rules of policy which have been laid down by political economists: I mean ignorance of economic facts, and of the causes by which the economic phenomena of society as it is, are actually determined.

What is he doing with that second one? I keep reading it as a bit of a dodge — like he wants to say the socialists are wrong without being the guy who defends the existing order, so he splits the difference: bad facts, maybe fine politics? Or is the distinction actually load-bearing and I'm just not seeing it? Walk me through what it means to be ignorant of the causes by which the economic phenomena of society as it is are actually determined, as opposed to rejecting practical rules of policy. Why bother saying it once for all right there, immediately after conceding that the allegations are largely unanswerable?

And then the bigger thing, which is really what I need for the book: if much of the indictment is unanswerable, and the real question is just how much of the misery belongs to our social arrangements versus to the human condition, what does Mill think follows from that? Does the frightful case give him a reason to move toward socialism, or does it only mean he can't use the present order as a baseline? My Owenite needs a position and I don't want to put words in Mills mouth that he wouldn't own.

One more, smaller thing, and tell me if I'm mashing two passages together: I have a memory of him saying somewhere in there that the thing everyone took to be socialisms fatal weakness might turn out to be one of its strengths, and that the thing is population — that communism would have to deal with over-population just like any other system, but might deal with it better. Is that in the same chapter, and does it connect to the frightful case bit or is it a separate argument I'm stapling on? I'd like to know before I have my character say it out loud and get it wrong.
