I'm in a philosophy of social science seminar and last week the prof spent most of the class on Friedman's essay about methodology — the one that opens his 1953 book of essays — and I filled a few pages of notes, but I'm now worried I wrote down his gloss on it rather than what Friedman actually said, and I have a paper due. Here's basically how he put it, as best I can reconstruct it: Friedman's big idea, which Samuelson nicknamed the F-twist, is that the assumptions of an economic model don't have to be realistic — all that matters is whether the model's predictions come out right. He even claims that the more unrealistic the assumptions, the better the theory, since a theory that described reality in full detail would just be a description and not a theory at all. So when a model says firms maximize profit, or that agents have perfect information, it doesn't matter that no real firm ever sits down and equates marginal revenue with marginal cost; the assumption is doing its work as a useful fiction, and if the model's predictions about prices are good, the assumption has earned its keep. The upshot, according to him, is that criticizing a model for having unrealistic assumptions is a category mistake — you're testing the wrong thing. You only ever test a theory against its predictions and never against its assumptions, and once the predictions check out the assumptions are simply none of your business.
