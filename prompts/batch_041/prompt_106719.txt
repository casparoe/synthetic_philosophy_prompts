Each year a set of world happiness rankings is published, ordering countries by how happy their populations are said to be, and I have been trying to work out what they actually tell us. I have no academic background; I read the news and think about these things on my own. As I understand it, the ranking rests on a single survey question: respondents place themselves on a ladder running from the worst life they can imagine to the best life they can imagine, scored from 0 to 10, and a country's score is the average of its residents' answers, pooling three years of polling. The same reports also list six factors — income per head, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceived corruption — which are said to explain much of the difference between countries, though they are not ingredients of the score itself. Last week two friends of mine, Marta and Owen, argued about all this at dinner, and I could not decide who had the better of it. I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication.

Marta argued that the rankings do measure happiness, or at least the best available stand-in for it, and that a country aiming to climb them is aiming at something worth having. First, the ladder asks people to judge their lives as a whole, which is the only way to get at something like happiness, and the six factors explain much of the variation between countries, which shows the measure tracks real features of life rather than noise. Second, the obvious alternative — asking people how they felt yesterday — would be worse, because moods are volatile and a government can do little about a mood. Third, the ranking is a legitimate target for policy precisely because it summarizes how people's lives are going; a government that lifts it has done good. Fourth, any measure can be gamed, but that argues for watching the gaming, not for abandoning the measure.

Owen replied with four objections. First, the question never asks about happiness; it asks for a judgment about one's life, and the two come apart, since someone can judge her life satisfactory while feeling wretched most days, and the reverse. Second, because the ladder is anchored to each respondent's own imagination of the best and worst life, a 7 in a poor country and a 7 in a rich one need not mean the same thing; if your picture of the best possible life is humbler, your 7 is cheaper. Third, the published number is an average, so a country can rise by making the comfortable a little happier while leaving the miserable where they are. Fourth, if a government sets out to top the table, the number becomes a target, and answers will drift upward for reasons — national pride, a wish to be seen doing well — that have nothing to do with how anyone's life is going.

Marta answered each in turn. To the first: the feeling-versus-judgment distinction is real but irrelevant to policy, since what a government can affect is people's overall assessment, and the reports never claimed to be measuring a mood. To the second: the anchoring worry is a reason for care in comparing very different countries, not for abandoning the measure, and it would apply to any subjective measure whatever. To the third: fair, but the underlying data allow a distribution to be computed, so this is a complaint about what gets headlined rather than about the measure. To the fourth: any target can be distorted, and if people's answers rise because they feel better about their country, has something not improved in a way that matters?

Owen's rejoinders were these. To the first: if happiness is simply defined as whatever the survey asks about, the claim that the rankings measure happiness becomes empty and cannot support the policy conclusion — the interesting claim was that the score tracks how well people's lives are actually going, and that is precisely what is in dispute; and in any case a factor can predict a score without the score measuring what its label says. To the second: the problem is not care in comparison, since the ranking is nothing but a comparison, and if the meaning of the scale shifts with people's expectations, part of the ordering is an artifact of differing expectations; he added that people adapt, so someone in bad conditions may report a decent number because he has lowered his sights, which means a high score can sit alongside real deprivation. To the third: conceded, but what people respond to is the ranking, and the ranking is an average. To the fourth: Marta's point proves too much, because if rising answers count as an improvement, a country could improve its score by exhorting its citizens to be more patriotic — which is not an improvement in anyone's life.

Please work through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which point, and what each of them is missing. Then say, on the strength of that, whether a country has a good reason to try to top the rankings.
