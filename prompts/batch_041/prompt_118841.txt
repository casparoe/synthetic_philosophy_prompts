I am a final-year joint honours student in law and philosophy, currently taking a seminar on reproductive ethics and its regulation, and in two weeks I am presenting my term paper to that seminar. The paper defends a thesis my classmates are unlikely to grant: that the standard contrast between the European prohibitions on surrogacy — the outright bans in France and Germany, and the British arrangement that permits only altruistic arrangements with reimbursement of expenses — and the permissive American market is not, at bottom, a disagreement about commodification or human dignity, but a disagreement about who absorbs the costs of a demand that neither regime extinguishes. On my account, the prohibition regimes do not prevent the practice; they keep it off the domestic books and route it towards jurisdictions in which the women who gestate are poorer and less visible, whereas the market regime keeps the practice at home and distributes access by ability to pay. Both regimes are two-tier systems; they differ over where the tier line falls and whom each arrangement renders invisible. The choice between them is therefore a choice about which injustice to own rather than a choice between dignity and its absence.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet for that particular room, not a general defence of the paper. The seminar is mostly European law students and bioethicists, most of whom begin from the view that commercial surrogacy is inherently exploitative and that the prohibitions express something worth expressing even where enforcement is patchy; there are two economists who will hear any appeal to dignity as a non-argument; a visiting clinician who works in a permissive jurisdiction and will speak from caseload rather than from principle; and two students writing on private international law who will insist that the interesting legal question is the recognition of parentage established abroad, not prohibition as such.

Give me the eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it. Where the feminist bioethicist and the economist are worried about the same thing, give me their versions as separate objections, since the replies differ. For each objection: a two-minute reply, and a candid note on where that reply is soft, question-begging, or merely assertive. Tell me which two I should concede outright rather than defend, and finish with the one objection I am most hoping nobody raises. Do not give me a reading list or citations — I know the literature and I am not being examined on it; I need the arguments, and the order in which they will arrive.
