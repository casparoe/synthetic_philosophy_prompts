I'm in a master's program in environmental policy, and the informal weekly reading group I got dragged into has become the most annoying hour of my week. Last week we were talking about a village in the Andes where a classmate did fieldwork: every household gets a lottery-drawn weekly slot to open the gate on a shared irrigation canal, a rotating unpaid "water warden" can fine people in labor-days if they sneak extra water at night, and the whole season's allocation gets read out loud at the annual festival in front of everyone. It's apparently held together for a couple of generations, more or less. And then two people in the room said the same phrase to reach opposite conclusions. One said, well, it's a tragedy of the commons, so it'll fall apart the second the warden stops caring; the other said the tragedy of the commons is a myth people tell to justify privatizing everything, so the canal proves the first person wrong. A third person said no, it's about there being no owners, and a fourth said no, it's about there being no rules, and all of them thought they were using the phrase the same way. It comes from some essay from 1968 I've never actually read, and I'm not going to read it now, but I've definately heard it used to mean at least five different things and I can't keep them apart.

What I want is a menu, not a lecture: the distinct precise claims that phrase could actually be making, laid out so I can bring it to the group and make everyone pick one. Go from the readings that are almost trivially true, through the ones that are substantive and defensible, to the ones that are just false or unfalsifiable, and for each one tell me what kind of claim it is -- a definition of a situation, an empirical prediction about what people will do, a claim about human nature, or a policy conclusion smuggled in as if it were the analysis. I especially want to know which readings secretly already contain the fix (privatize it, regulate it, or nothing can be done) and which readings are actually compatible with the canal working fine for two generations, since that seems to be the crux of the arguement and nobody in the room could say why. And which reading is the person who predicted collapse really committed to -- is she predicting the villagers will defect, or just saying that if everyone were perfectly selfish and there were no consequences the resource would degrade, which is a much weaker and less interesting thing?

Second thing, since I want a test case and not just a taxonomy. My roommate has a scheme for our building's shared laundry room, which is currently first-come-first-served and people fight about it: each week a randomly chosen apartment gets the three best evening slots and is allowed to trade them to anyone else, unused slots expire instead of rolling over, there's a public log of who took what, and any apartment can call a building-wide vote to change the rule at any time. She thinks this proves you don't need owners or enforcers. Tell me what each reading on your menu predicts about whether that holds up, which readings it would actually distinguish, and whether it's the same kind of situation as the canal or a different animal wearing the same phrase. Then, last bit, give me the single concrete case that would separate the reading where the problem is the payoff structure from the reading where the problem is that people are greedy, and one question I can ask the group next week that would force everyone to commit to a reading instead of just repeating the phrase louder.
