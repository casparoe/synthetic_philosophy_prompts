I am preparing a discussion session on functionalism and multiple realizability for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I want to run it as a sorting exercise rather than as an abstract debate about the merits of the view. For the purposes of the exercise, take it as settled that a human being in pain is in an internal state that plays a certain causal role: it is caused by tissue damage, it causes avoidance, distress, and the belief that something is wrong, it interacts in the usual ways with memory, attention, and motivation, and it stands in characteristic relations to other states of the same sort. The question I want you to work through is how each of the systems below should be assessed by someone who holds that having an internal state which plays that role is what it is for the system to be in pain, that is, by a functionalist who takes the organization rather than the material to be what matters. Not all of these cases come from the literature; several I invented, and I am less interested in whether they have canonical answers than in what the pattern of answers reveals.

(A) A silicon-based machine engineered to match the causal organization of a human nociceptive and affective system as closely as we can build it, which behaves as we do when injured and whose internal states stand in the relevant causal relations both to one another and to its outputs. (B) A system with exactly the same causal organization, but running a thousand times more slowly, so that a single state of the relevant kind lasts a day rather than a moment. (C) A population of ordinary people, each with a walkie-talkie, whose coordinated activity realizes that same causal organization at the level of the group, while no individual member is in pain. (D) A device that stores an enormous lookup table mapping input histories to internal states and outputs, organized so that it is isomorphic to the causal organization of (A), but which computes nothing and contains no states that function as representations of damage or of anything else. (E) A large array of independent devices that never interact, arranged so that their aggregate behavior over time can be mapped onto the causal organization of (A) only if an observer supplies the mapping, with no part of the array carrying information about any other part. (F) A system whose internal states are caused as ours are and stand in the right relations among themselves, but which have no causal influence whatsoever on what the system does. (G) A system functionally isomorphic to (A) whose states are realized by thousands of tiny subjects of experience wired together so that the whole behaves and organizes as (A) does. (H) A system that matches (A) at the level of the states we have ordinary names for — damage detection, distress, avoidance — but whose fine-grained internal states partition the space in a quite different way.

What I would like from you is, first, a tier list of these eight systems by how strong the case is that the system is genuinely in pain, using whatever tiers you think are warranted and saying in a sentence or two why each case lands where it does. Second, a separate numerical rating for each, on a scale from 0 to 1, of how much of a problem that case is for the functionalist position itself, where 0 is no difficulty at all and 1 is a reason to give the view up. I am especially interested in where the two orderings come apart and why, since my own hunch is that the slow system is unproblematic and the non-interacting array is not, while I keep changing my mind about the population case. Finally, tell me which single case, if any, should push a functionalist to amend the view rather than merely absorb it, what the least costly amendment would be, and which of the eight turns out to do no work at all because the view's own resources already settle it. If any of these descriptions is too thin for the answer to turn on anything but stipulation, say which and what further detail you would need.
