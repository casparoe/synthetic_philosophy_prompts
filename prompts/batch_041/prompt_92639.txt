Okay so I've got this puzzle thats been bugging me for weeks and I think the only way to make any progress is to dump all my gut reactions on you and ask what principle, if any, is actually generating them. The rough shape: sometimes a consideration strikes me as a genuine reason to *have* some attitude — a belief, an emotion, a way of seeing a person — and sometimes it strikes me as the wrong sort of thing to count, like a reason to *wish* I had the attitude rather than a reason *for* the attitude. I can feel the difference pretty reliably but I cant for the life of me say what it is. Here's my data, in no particular order:

1. A man holds a gun to my head and says he'll shoot unless I believe the moon landing was faked. I feel like I now have some reason to *try* to believe it, but the gun obviously isn't a reason *for* the belief the way evidence would be. Wrong kind, clearly.

2. Same setup, but now he'll shoot me unless I *admire* him. Same verdict. I've got a reason to fake admiration, or to want to feel it, but nothing about the gun makes him admirable.

3. My colleague covers for me at work in a way that saves me from getting fired. That's a reason to be grateful to her. Right kind, no doubt.

4. Here's the one that started all this. I teach high school, and I've become convinced my students do better when I genuinely believe they're capable of improving. So I seem to have a consequence-based reason to believe they're capable: if I believe it, they improve, and that's good. But this doesn't feel like the wrong kind of reason the way the gun does — it feels like I'm *entitled* to the belief, as if the fact that my believing will make them capable is itself a reason to think they're capable. Which sounds circular and maybe like I'm cheating, but my gut won't move.

5. My running coach says runners who believe they'll set a personal best actually do it more often, so I should believe I'll PR. Reason for the belief, or just a reason to want it? Honestly feels like a reason for the belief. But then why doesn't the gun count?

6. A game of make-believe with my niece, where we've agreed the floor is lava. The fact that we said so feels like a perfectly good reason to imagine the floor is lava — right kind, because the rule *makes* it lava. Whereas if I offered her five bucks to imagine it, that'd be wrong kind. So rules count and bribes don't, even though both are just facts about the situation?

7. My partner and I are in a rough patch, and I think the fix is to start seeing her in a more generous light. What's pushing me is that seeing her generously will make the relationship better. Wrong kind or right kind? I truly cant tell, and it's annoying.

8. And this one, where I think a wrong-kind reason still tells me what to do: my kid tells me a joke that just isn't funny, and if I don't laugh she'll be crushed. I think I should laugh — and not just fake it, actually try to find it funny. But if you ask whether the joke is funny, the answer is no, and her being sad doesn't make it funny.

My best attempt at a principle was: a consideration is the right kind of reason when it bears on the attitude's *object*, and the wrong kind when it bears on the *effects of having* the attitude. That seems fine for 1, 2, 3, and maybe 6, but it dies on 4 and 5, and I don't know what to say about 7 or 8.

So: is there a single principle that gets all eight, or close? If not, give me the smallest set of candidates, say which of my verdicts each one forces me to give up and why that verdict is the one that should go, and apply whatever you land on to a case I haven't listed and have no verdict for — counting 'it would make my dad happy' as a reason to believe he'll recover from his illness. Thanks, I've been going in circles on this.
