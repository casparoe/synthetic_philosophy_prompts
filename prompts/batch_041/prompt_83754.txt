For the past month I have been the graduate student member of a research-integrity inquiry into a paper from my own lab, and a case that should have been about a retraction has turned into an argument about what a person is doing when he testifies. The facts are not much in dispute: every sentence in the disputed passage was true as written, and the first author knew that readers would come away believing something false — that an effect held across the full sample rather than in one prespecified subgroup, and that a hypothesis had been registered in advance when in fact it was written after the data arrived. Under questioning he said, evenly, that he had never said anything false; and the chair of the panel, a chemist with no patience for what she calls word games, wrote in her summary that the applicable standard is the one from the witness oath, "the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth," and then treated it as satisfied by the first clause alone, on the grounds that no one tells the whole truth and no one could be required to. I keep answering with "a half-truth is a whole lie," which I believe, but I have begun to notice that the two of us are using the same handful of formulas to mean things neither of us has spelled out. I would like them spelled out.

Give me the distinct precise theses these formulas could be standing for, ordered from the nearly empty to the very strong, with a verdict attached to each: (1) the minimal reading, that one must not assert what one believes to be false — trivially true, but is it even the whole of the duty not to lie? (2) that one must not produce or intend a false belief, which would make lying a species of a broader wrong rather than the wrong itself; (3) that a speaker who volunteers any truth thereby takes on a duty of completeness, so that omission becomes misrepresentation once one has begun to speak; (4) that "nothing but the truth" is a separate, relevance-shaped constraint, forbidding a witness from padding a true account with irrelevant truths whose only function is to push the hearer toward a false conclusion; and (5) the semantic question underneath all of them, whether the content of an assertion is fixed by the sentence uttered, by the speaker's communicative intention, or by what a reasonable hearer would take from it — since "technically true" is a defense under the first of these, a much weaker one under the second, and a confession under the third. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive, or false; whether it draws the line between lying and misleading where people actually draw it or somewhere else; and what it licenses in the cases I actually care about, namely a flat falsehood, a true sentence carrying a false implicature, a true sentence with a material omission where disclosure was plainly expected, a true sentence deliberately engineered to be misread, and a true but irrelevant revelation dragged in to discredit a witness. Two fragments I can offer you: some negotiation researchers have a term, "paltering," for the deliberate use of true statements to create false beliefs, and I have a vague memory that the misrepresentation cases in tort law treat a statement true so far as it goes as fraudulent when what it leaves out is material — I would rather you tell me what those traditions are committed to than tell me where I read them. I have also heard it claimed that Kant located the wrong of lying in the falsification of a declaration rather than in the deception itself, which would mean that misleading someone while saying nothing false was not, for him, a lie at all; assess the distinction rather than the attribution, and say whether it can be sustained.

Do not send me to a reading list; I want the analysis, and I want you to take a side. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — state which reading the chair is entitled to and which reading I should be pressing the panel to adopt — then give the reasoning that earns it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Where two readings are both defensible, say which you would adopt and what it costs to adopt the other; and if the honest answer is that the panel's question is ambiguous between two different wrongs, say so and say which of them a panel of this kind is actually in a position to adjudicate. Lay the readings out as a numbered list with a verdict on each, so that I can carry it into the next hearing.
