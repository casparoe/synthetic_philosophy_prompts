I've been working through Hume's essay on suicide — the one he withheld from publication during his lifetime — and I've hit a wall on a specific stretch of the argument. I'll paraphrase it rather than quote, since I don't have a reliable text in front of me and don't want to misattribute wording.

Hume grants, for the sake of argument, that God is the author of the universe and that human life is his gift. He then asks what it would even mean for a person to usurp God's authority by ending his own life. His answer seems to be that the whole category of resisting or usurping providence collapses once you notice that our voluntary actions are themselves part of the general course of nature that providence ordains. If the Almighty governs the world through general laws rather than by intervening in each particular event, then a man who takes his own life is not stepping outside the order of providence but executing a part of it — no more and no less than a man who preserves his life, or a farmer who plants in spring. Hume then pushes this further: if God had reserved the disposal of life to himself alone, then to preserve one's life would be as much a violation of that reservation as to end it, since in both cases one would be acting where one has no authority. Hence the ordinary religious prohibition on suicide, he concludes, cannot be grounded in the idea of a divine prerogative over life without equally condemning every act of self-preservation.

What I can't get past is how this is supposed to work as a reductio. It looks to me like Hume is trading on an ambiguity between two very different claims: (i) that God's will is expressed in the general laws of nature, so that anything that happens is in some sense willed by God; and (ii) that we have no special authority over our own lives because that authority belongs to God. Claim (i) seems to make the concept of resisting God's will vacuous — but only by making it vacuous for everything, including the paradigmatic sins, which is surely not what Hume's religious opponents meant. Claim (ii), by contrast, seems to be doing real work, and it doesn't obviously depend on (i) at all. So is Hume's argument best read as a genuine reductio of the divine-prerogative view, or as a bait-and-switch in which a trivializing premise is used to discredit a claim that was never committed to that premise?

I'd also like help with a related puzzle. The essay seems to presuppose a thoroughgoing denial of any doing/allowing asymmetry in God's governance, but Hume elsewhere (in the Dialogues, if I remember rightly) treats the existence of evil as a genuine problem precisely because a good God would not permit it. If God's permission of an evil is just another way of willing it, why is the problem of evil a problem at all for Hume? Is there a consistent position across the two texts, or is the suicide essay's argument simply local and polemical, not meant to cohere with his broader theodicy?

I've seen this kind of move described on rationalist blogs as dissolving a distinction rather than refuting a claim, and I suspect that's the right frame here — but I can't tell whether the dissolution is legitimate or whether it just changes the subject. I'm not asking for a reading list or for citations; I'd just like someone to walk me through the strongest version of Hume's argument and tell me whether my diagnosis of the equivocation is fair.
