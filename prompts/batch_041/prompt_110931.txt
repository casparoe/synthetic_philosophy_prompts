I am writing a novel whose engine is a forty-year quarrel inside a small research institute — the Calder Institute, in my pages — between its two surviving founders. The first holds that a comparison of one person's welfare with another's is not, at bottom, the kind of thing that can be true or false: that the settlement which stripped welfare economics of cardinal utility was correct, and that the comparisons people make in ordinary life are moral verdicts or guesses wearing the costume of fact. The second holds that we compare welfare between persons constantly, competently and unavoidably — in medicine, in law, in ordinary kindness — and that a theory of social choice which forbids itself such comparisons has bought rigour at the price of having nothing to say.

I need the document those two would sign together, because I keep writing one of them as a caricature, and my readers will notice. Please draft it: the claims both can put their names to, each stated so that neither is signing a truce in words; the residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as fair to itself; the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them; and, for each crux, a case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. Where the quarrel has been about words rather than about the world, say which part, and why. Format the agreed claims as a numbered list, and set out the disagreements as a table — one row per disagreement, with its crux, the case that bears on it, and each side's prediction.

I have already put a house rule into the novel's world, and I should like the document written in its vocabulary. The Institute admits a comparison of two persons' welfare only if it can answer three questions: is there a determinate answer at all (determinacy); could we in principle reach it by a route you are prepared to name (access); and could the answer be used in a decision that has to be taken (service). Both men have signed the rule; they read its three limbs very differently, and I want those readings set out side by side.

One scene turns on whether a woman's chronic pain is worse than her brother's grief. If that case serves one of the cruxes, use it; if not, invent a case that does, and make it one both would accept as relevant.

I want no citations, no reading lists, and no appeals to who said what — the arguments must stand on their own, since my characters cannot be seen quoting a literature. Write in British English and in a formal register, and do not tidy the disagreement into something more comfortable than it is.
