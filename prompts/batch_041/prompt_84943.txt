I'm in a small reading group on consciousness (three of us, mostly phd students, we meet in a cafe and argue way too much) and last week we did the color phi thing. You know the one: flash a dot at one spot, flash it again a little way off and in a different color, short gap between, and people swear they see a single dot travelling and changing color partway along the path -- and here's the bit that wrecked us -- the color change seems to be experienced as happening *before* the second dot even comes on, which is presumably impossible, since the second dot's color is what's driving it. We read Dennett on this and his Orwellian vs Stalinesque framing and it turned into a whole fight about whether the question "when did you actually experience that" even has an answer. So before we read any more theories in this area, I want a proper spec first -- good enough that we could actually use it to decide whether Dennett is right that the timing question is empty. Write me the desiderata for a theory of the temporal structure of experience: what any such theory has to deliver. The phenomena it has to account for, and why each one is a real constraint rather than a curiosity (color phi itself; metacontrast-type masking, where a later stimulus can decide whether you saw an earlier one at all; the fact that stretching the gap too far kills the illusion, so whatever the mechanism is it's sensitive to timing; the tactile-rabbit kind of saltation where taps at two points get felt in between; and the boring fact that we *can* often correctly say which of two events came first, so the system isn't just noise); the distinctions it mustn't blur (the time an experience occurs vs. the time that experience presents its object as occuring; the time of my report vs. the time of the experience I'm reporting on; order of processing vs. order in experience); the puzzles it has to actually dissolve rather than just rename (how information arriving later can shape an experience that supposedly already happened, without anything travelling backwards in time; whether there's a real "filling in" process or that's a bad description); and any hard constraints from neuroscience it can't contradict. For each requirement, tell me the case or consideration that puts it on the list.

The thing I most want from you: which of these can't be satisfied together. My hunch -- say if it's wrong -- is that "every experience happens at some definite moment" and "what an experience is like can depend on stimuli that arrive after that moment" are the pair that fight, and that if you take both seriously you're forced to give one up, so the list ends up being a kind of proof all by itself. Lay out that conflict properly, and say which of the other requirements line up on either side of it. One more thing, and it's really why I'm asking now: a friend in the group wants to actually run a variant -- circle flashed at A, square at B, short gap -- and then ask people to sketch the shape they saw when the thing was halfway across, and separately to hit a key the instant the shape starts to change. If people draw some circle-square blob that was never on the screen, or if their key-press lands before B is even shown, what should a theory be committed to saying, and does the list I asked for already have a slot for that? Then, briefly at the end, score the obvious contenders against the list -- Dennett's multiple drafts, the naive "experience is just delayed until all the info is in" view, and a predictive-processing "the brain infers when things happened" story -- but keep that part short, the criteria are the point.
