I've been going round in circles on a passage from that old Lewis Carroll dialogue where the Tortoise and Achilles wrangle about Euclid, and there's a bit near the end I can't crack. Here it is:

"You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."

"And why must I?"

"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

Two people in my reading group have takes on this and I can't decide between them. The first says the regress only ever gets going because Achilles keeps writing the conditional down as another premise — something to be believed — when what's really doing the work is the rule of inference, the licence to move from A and B to Z, and a rule just isn't a premise, so the Tortoise's demand for one more thing to accept is a category mistake and the whole thing dissolves. The second says that's a nice distinction but it only relocates the problem: if the rule isn't a premise, the Tortoise just asks what justifies *following the rule*, and now you're trying to justify a rule, which you can't do by handing over yet another rule or yet another premise, so the regress hasn't stopped, it's just changed shape. She adds what I think might be the real nub: imagine someone who didn't already infer by modus ponens — could you get them to start by giving them a premise that says modus ponens is valid? If not, then the thing the Tortoise keeps asking for (a reason to accept Z that isn't just "accept the rule") is something no argument could ever supply, and that would be a result about deduction itself, not just about this puzzle.

So: what is the Tortoise actually demanding, and is he cheating or onto something? Is the rule-versus-premise distinction a genuine resolution or just a relocation, and how would I tell the difference? And what work is the word "must" doing in "Z must be true" — is that where the trouble lives, or am I chasing the wrong word? I want to actually understand the passage, not just pick a side, so tell me what would have to be true for each of my friends to be right, and where you think the confusion really is.
