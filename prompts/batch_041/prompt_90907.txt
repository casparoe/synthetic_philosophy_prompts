I am putting together a unit on anthropic reasoning for an advanced undergraduate seminar, and I need a fresh thought experiment that isolates the difference between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption. Please construct one. The scenario should be set in an ordinary contemporary institution—a hospital, a fertility clinic, a university, or a workplace—and it should culminate in a practical decision that one person must make under uncertainty, not merely a credence question; the decision may be altruistic or self-interested, but its expected value must depend on the population-size hypothesis. The uncertainty should concern how many observers (or observer-moments, i.e., individual moments of experience) exist in some relevant population, and the person's only evidence relevant to that uncertainty should be indexical: she knows she is one of the observers in that population, but she does not know how many others there are. The case must be such that a reasoner who accepts the self-sampling assumption (roughly, the idea that you should reason as if you are randomly selected from the relevant reference class) and a reasoner who accepts the self-indication assumption (roughly, the idea that the fact that you exist is evidence in favor of hypotheses that posit more observers) reach different recommendations, and the difference must trace to those assumptions rather than to different priors, different values, or different empirical information. To keep the exercise honest, avoid cosmology, doomsday arguments, infinite universes, amnesia, brain duplication, and exotic physics; if you need a modest technological or institutional stipulation, keep it mundane and flag it explicitly.

After the scenario, do the following: (1) state the competing hypotheses about population size and the agent's indexical evidence; (2) derive the posterior and the decision for each assumption, showing the arithmetic in a way an undergraduate can follow; (3) identify the exact step in the derivation that the two assumptions treat differently; (4) explain whether the case is a version of the presumptuous philosopher problem or whether it avoids that problem, and why; (5) say what happens if the agent's reference class is widened to include non-human animals or narrowed to include only people with a certain trait; (6) explain how the analysis changes if we count observer-moments rather than observers; and (7) tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and whether removing it collapses the disagreement. The scenario itself should be no longer than two paragraphs, and the analysis should be rigorous but accessible to someone who has read only an introductory discussion of anthropic reasoning.
