Build me an original case of the liberal paradox — the conflict between a unanimously preferred alternative and two individually decisive private choices — to the specifications below, and then work it through as instructed. The case is for a seminar on rights and welfare, so it has to earn the paradox out of the preference orderings rather than announce it: if I have to take your word that the orderings generate the problem, the case has failed.

Specifications:

1. Two people, three alternatives, ordinary domestic or working life. Nothing about censorship, nothing about what anyone reads, and nothing that would let a reader say the example was reverse-engineered from the conclusion.
2. Each person must be decisive over a pair of alternatives — whichever of the two they prefer, society is to follow — and that pair must be uncontroversially their own business: their own body, their own possessions, or their own actions. The liberal assignment itself must not be what is doing the controversial work.
3. In the pair over which the unanimity judgement is made, at least one preference must reach beyond the holder's own private business, and it must be a preference a liberal cannot dismiss as censoriousness, envy, or busybodying. Not 'I want to stop them doing what is none of my concern', but something of the shape of a preference about shared time, shared space, or a shared plan — the kind a committed liberal would refuse to let the law override and would still be unwilling to strike from the domain as illegitimate. If you conclude that no case can meet these specifications, say so and prove it; do not quietly relax a constraint.

Then:

1. Set out both preference orderings in full, name the pair over which each person is decisive, and derive the result step by step, labelling each step as coming from a liberal right or from the unanimity principle. State precisely what goes wrong — a cycle of social preference, or an outcome that both people rank below an alternative that was available.
2. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work: the one whose removal would dissolve the problem. Demonstrate this by giving the variant case without that feature and showing that nothing goes wrong there.
3. Say which of the two conditions the case really indicts — the unanimity principle or the assignment of decisive private pairs — and commit to one answer rather than laying out both and leaving the choice to me.
4. Give the strongest objection a liberal could make to your case, in one of its two standard forms: exclude the offending preference from the domain, or refine the alternatives so that the preference is absorbed into the description of what is being chosen. Say whether your case survives that objection, given specification 3, and what the objection costs.

Begin with the case itself, with no preamble and no restating of these instructions; then state your verdict on point 3 in a single sentence before you argue for it.
