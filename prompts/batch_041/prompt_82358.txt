A colleague of mine who works on consciousness defends Dennett's heterophenomenology against the complaint that no third-person method can capture experience by saying the whole thing is just like reading a novel: the subject's reports are the text, the heterophenomenological world you build out of them is the world of the story, and you can describe that world exhaustively without ever committing to whether any of it exists. That, he says, is all the neutrality the method needs — the subject is authoritative about how things seem to her, the theorist needn't say anything about how they really are — and nothing is lost. Audit that. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of author, narrator, text, world, reader — then the places where the mapping breaks, separating the breaks that matter for the conclusion he's drawing from the ones that are idle. I'm especially interested in whether a story's world is the right model for something that also has a brain and a causal history behind it, and whether the analogy has quietly imported a verdict about whether the subject's inner life extends beyond what turns up in that world. Then tell me whether his neutrality conclusion survives the disanalogies or whether the analogy was carrying it, and if there's a better comparison for what the heterophenomenologist is actually doing, what it is.
