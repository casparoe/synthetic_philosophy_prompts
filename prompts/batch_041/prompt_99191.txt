I have been sitting in on a reading group devoted to Augustine's ethics, and we have spent three weeks circling his treatment of lying: the absolute prohibition, the ranked taxonomy of lies that runs from the gravest to the lightest, the concession that a jocose lie is still a lie even though it is the least of them, the refusal to permit even the officious lie that harms no one and saves a life, and the long quarrel with Jerome over whether Paul's rebuke of Peter at Antioch was sincere or a piece of pious play-acting undertaken for the good of the church. Our argument has narrowed to a wrinkle I cannot get out of my head: on his account, is a lie located in the words or in the mind? A sentence that is literally true, but arranged so that the hearer will certainly draw a false conclusion, seems to leave the words intact while corrupting the intention — and if the intention is what makes the lie, then a great deal of ordinary tact and every well-designed evasion would fall under the prohibition, a conclusion the group has not been willing to accept. We have also invented, half in jest, a practice we call the ledger of unsaid things: a private record of truths deliberately withheld, kept so that a person can prove they never spoke falsely even while admitting they never spoke fully.

I would like you to write a short story, roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words, that dramatizes this problem rather than discusses it. Set it in the present day, in a mid-sized town under a public-health tracing regime, and center it on a small fellowship — a dozen adults, no formal clergy — that has adopted a rigorist rule of truthfulness in the spirit of Augustine. Give the fellowship its own vocabulary and customs, including the ledger of unsaid things, and let the plot turn on a tracing interview in which the protagonist can answer every question with a sentence that is strictly true while arranging matters so that the interviewer will reach a false conclusion. The story should also contain: a jocose lie told to a child, and the argument it provokes among the members about whether the lightest lie is still a lie; a dying man who asks whether his son died well, and the fellowship's refusal or permission of an officious lie in that room; and a visiting scholar who arrives to argue that the Antioch quarrel settles the matter in favor of useful deception, on the ground that Scripture cannot be read as recording a sincere error by an apostle. Let the members disagree about whether the true-but-engineered answer is a lie, and let the disagreement track the real positions rather than a caricature of them.

Write in restrained third person, with no sermons, no authorial verdict, and no scene in which the rule is simply declared right or wrong; a reader should finish the story able to reconstruct the actual Augustinian positions from what the characters say and suffer. Do not quote Augustine or any other text verbatim, do not cite particular treatises, letters, or chapter numbers, and do not name real scholars or historical figures beyond Augustine and Jerome themselves; keep all other names invented. Avoid period clichés and modern sneering at religion alike, and give the ledger at least three concrete entries, each one a truth withheld for a different reason, so that the reader can see where the practice is honest and where it is a dodge.
