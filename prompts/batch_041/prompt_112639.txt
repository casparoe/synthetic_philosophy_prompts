I am a high school student working through Schopenhauer's philosophy of art for an independent project, and I have hit a passage in his discussion of music, in the third book of The World as Will and Representation, that I cannot make consistent with itself. Because I do not read German and the library copy I have is an old translation, I am going to paraphrase the passage rather than reproduce its wording, so please treat the sentences below as my summary of his claims and not as his own words.

The claims, as I understand them, are these. The other arts, such as painting, sculpture, and poetry, are supposed to copy the Ideas, which are the will's first level of objectification; they give us a temporary escape from the will, because in contemplating the Ideas we stop being individual desiring subjects. Music is different: it does not copy the Ideas at all but is instead an image of the will itself, and therefore it bypasses the whole world of phenomena and representation. Music, on this account, could exist even if there were no world of bodies and no Ideas, and in some sense it would still be the same music. Yet Schopenhauer also wants to say that the parts of music correspond to the parts of the will: the bass notes to the lowest grades of the will's objectification, harmony to the will's satisfaction, dissonance to its dissatisfaction, and melody to the history of an individual will's strivings, with its delays, satisfactions, and final resolution. And he says music is the most powerful of the arts, and that it moves us in a way that no mere picture of Ideas ever could.

My problem is that these claims seem to pull against each other in at least three places, and I cannot tell whether Schopenhauer has a way out or whether the passage is simply loose.

First, if music is a direct image of the will, and the will's nature is endless, groundless striving that Schopenhauer elsewhere treats as the root of all suffering, then why is listening to music pleasurable rather than horrifying? A faithful portrait of something unbearable ought to be unbearable, or at least ought to feel like knowledge; but music feels like relief, and he even ranks it with the arts as a way of escaping the will. So is music an escape from the will or the most direct expression of it?

Second, how can something as structured as music, a fugue for instance, or a piece that is almost pure pattern with no emotional program, be an image of something he describes as blind and formless? If the structure is the point, then music looks like a representation of a very rational order, which is the opposite of what he says the will is. If the emotion is the point, then the structure seems to be doing no metaphysical work at all.

Third, when I actually listen to something sad, I take pleasure in the sadness itself, and I do not think I am taking pleasure in someone else's sadness the way I might in a tragedy. Is that a different kind of pleasure from the contemplation of Ideas, or is Schopenhauer quietly relying on the same explanation for both and hoping no one notices?

What I want is not a general summary of his aesthetics; I have read enough of those to know the outline. I want help working through the passage itself. For each of the three tensions, tell me what Schopenhauer's resources are: whether he has a distinction I am missing, for instance between the will as it is in itself and the will as it appears in us as individuals, or whether the tension is real and the text is inconsistent. If there is a reading that saves the whole picture, I would like to see the argument spelled out; if there is not, I would like to know which claim I should give up first, and why. Please also tell me what a piece like a fugue is supposed to be an image of, on his view, since it does not seem to be about anyone's strivings.
