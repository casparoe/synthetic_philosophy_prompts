I've got the case-study slot in a seminar on religion and politics next week and I've bagged the Swiss minaret vote, mostly because everyone else wanted to do Rawls-versus-the-religious-right in the abstract and I'd rather do a real vote. What I don't want is the essay everyone writes — should religious arguments be allowed in the public square. I want you to actually run this episode through the public reason machinery and then tell me where the machinery jams.

Facts so you're not guessing: popular initiative launched in 2007 by a committee of right-of-centre politicians, an amendment to the constitutional article on religion and the state, the Federal Council and both chambers against it, the vote in late 2009, 57.5% yes on a turnout north of 53%, only a handful of mostly francophone cantons and half-cantons against, the four minarets that already existed — Zurich, Geneva, Winterthur, the little six-metre one in Wangen bei Olten — all left standing, and calls to prayer already regulated by ordinary cantonal noise rules. The framing is why I picked it: the public case the yes side made was mostly civic rather than religious. A minaret isn't really a religious structure anyway, it's a political symbol, a claim to power, a marker of a parallel legal order; there were arguements about integration and about women. So on its face the campaign met the standard — it argued in terms everyone could share, on a constitutional matter, which is exactly where the duty of civility is supposed to bite hardest. In my notes I've been calling the move 'secular ventriloquism': the religious reason gets translated into the lingua franca, the translation passes every formal test, and the conclusion still lands on a minority as a message about them.

So map it first. Who are the actual reasoners here — individual voters, the initiative committee, the Federal Council arguing against, the cantons? What were the options, and what would even count as evidence about what the voters were moved by? Then run it: does public reason forbid the ban, permit it, or have nothing to say, on the assumption the civic reasons were sincerely held? Then re-run it assuming they were a costume — and tell me wether the theory has any way at all of telling those two cases apart, or whether its structurally blind to motive. Where does it go quiet? And then the other direction, which is the part I actually care about: what does this case show about public reason — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated? Does it show the constraint is too weak, since any conclusion can be laundered into shared language, or that the whole business of shared reasons was doing less work than we assumed? Analysis only, no reading list, and flag the step in my framing that's weakest — I suspect it's the ventriloquism charge itself, but I'd like to be told.
