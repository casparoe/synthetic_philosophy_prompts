I have been trying to set out, in a form I can actually examine, what the law of armed conflict assumes about why killing is wrong, and the assumptions it seems to need will not sit together. The same legal order that treats intentional killing as the gravest of crimes licenses a soldier to shoot an enemy soldier on sight; forbids him to shoot that same man once he has been captured or lies wounded; and forbids torturing him under any circumstances, including circumstances in which killing others to save lives would be permitted. The soldier who kills under authorization is not a murderer even if the war he is fighting is itself unlawful, while he is a murderer if he kills without authorization or kills someone who is not a lawful target. And the highest honors the institution confers are ordinarily justified by the risk the recipient accepted rather than by the number of enemy dead; medics and stretcher-bearers have received them without firing a shot.

What view of the wrongness of killing does this pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose that killing is wrong in itself and is made permissible by authority, so that the operative question is who may do it rather than what is done to whom? Or does it presuppose that the wrongness turns on the liability of the person killed, with the codified rules serving only as a rough working proxy for a liability no one can assess in the field? Can both readings license the practice, leaving it philosophically underdetermined between them, or is one strictly required and the other merely compatible with it? Where the pattern is not underdetermined but strained, say so plainly: why should liability to be killed lapse at the moment of capture while liability to be tortured never arises at all, and why should the lawfulness of the war be irrelevant to the guilt of the man who fights it?

I am drawn to pacifism and want to know exactly what I would be denying. Tell me which parts of the practice presuppose that killing is sometimes permissible, which parts could survive a pacifist reading of them, and what would have to change if liability rather than authorization were made the governing idea.
