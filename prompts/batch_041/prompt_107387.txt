I'm a hospice volunteer, and I also just read a book about medical ethics that left me more confussed than when I started, so fair warning that this may be a naive question. Here's a pattern I keep bumping into and I don't know if it has a name.

There's a man on our unit with fairly advanced dementia. Years before any symptoms, he wrote down that if he ever got like that he did not want to be kept going — no feeding tube, no antibiotics for a pneumonia, none of it. Now he's here, and honestly he's happy. He likes the music therapy, he asks for ice cream, he says he wants to see his granddaughter graduate. The family and the doctors are going with the piece of paper. Then there's a friend of mine who promised a kidney to her college roommate back when they were twenty-two and inseparable; twenty-five years later she's backing out, and everyone, including the roommate, agrees that's completely her right, no question about it. And then a third case, a woman who wrote her advance directive as a committed atheist and has since become devout, and now wants everything done; the team genuinely can't tell which self to listen to.

What I keep noticing is that we treat an old statement as binding on a person exactly when we suspect the person in front of us isn't really the author of their current wants — and we let the old statement go when the change looks like ordinary growth or a change of heart. The earlier self wins, in other words, not because earlier is wiser, but almost as a penalty for the later self failing some test of being a genuine agent. Is there a name for that specific configuration? I don't mean the general practice of honoring advance directives — I mean this conditional structure, where the past self's authority seems to be derived from a judgment that the present self is no longer a proper author. I know there's the Ulysses-and-the-mast thing and a lot about promises and personal identity over time, and I have a feeling the answer will be "this is just X, go read Y," but X never quite fits, because Ulysses was competent the whole time and could feel the ropes, and a promise is made to other people, whereas this man's directive is made to himself. If there's no name, could you coin one and then tell me exactly where the edges are — what it is, what it isn't, and how it differs from the nearest things? I'd much rather understand the shape of it than get a label.
