I am a doctoral candidate in metaphysics, working at the boundary between philosophy of art and conservation studies, and I keep returning to a mismatch in how museums actually reason about the identity of the objects in their care. Consider the ordinary conservation and authentication policies of a large encyclopaedic museum. A marble sculpture loses its nose; a later restorer carves a replacement from a different block, and the museum continues to catalogue, display, lend and insure the work as the same sculpture. A panel painting is transferred from its warped wooden support to a canvas; the painted surface is preserved, the original support discarded, and the work is still treated as the same painting. A bronze is damaged beyond repair, melted down, and recast from the same metal in the original mould; the resulting object is normally treated as a copy or reproduction rather than as the surviving work. A three-dimensional scan of a destroyed statue is used to carve a new marble version, and that too is a replica. Yet the same institution will refuse to deaccession a work on the ground that the object itself is unique, and its insurers will pay for total loss when a fire destroys a painting even though the matter remains in the ash.

What theory of material constitution, if any, does this pattern of practice presuppose? I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want the implicit theory reconstructed from the practice itself. Which view does it commit the museum to: that a work of art is not identical with the matter that constitutes it at any time, that identity is relative to a sortal such as sculpture or painting, that the work is a four-dimensional entity whose later stages are parts of it, or something else? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely fit without requiring? Is the practice internally consistent, or does the treatment of the recast bronze and the scanned replica conflict with the treatment of the restored marble and the transferred panel? I am especially interested in where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same decisions.

A colleague in conservation science has proposed replacing the museum's informal rule about original fabric with a two-part test: first, material continuity, understood as a causal-historical chain from the original making; second, what she calls curatorial warrant, the accumulated record of custodial acts, scholarly attributions and institutional narratives that authorises treating a later state as the same work. Please assess that proposal. Does it capture what the existing practice is already doing, or does it introduce a new commitment? Run it on the four cases above, including at least one where it gives an answer the museum would resist. Then say what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its implicit theory with mereological essentialism, on the one hand, or with a straightforward constitution view, on the other. I would like a polished essay in British English, with the argument organised around the reconstruction, the consistency test, and the underdetermination claim; no bibliography or citations are needed.
