I am a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on transformative experience and rational choice, and my final essay asks me to reconstruct how a philosopher who died long before the topic existed would have responded to it. I have chosen John Stuart Mill, and I would like your help in working out his most plausible position.

To keep the exercise from collapsing into a generic utilitarian answer, I want to anchor it in a case that is deliberately not one of the stock examples. Suppose a person is offered an irreversible neural intervention that will rewire her evaluative outlook so that she comes to find a life of solitary, highly abstract mathematical work deeply absorbing, and comes to regard the close personal relationships that currently structure her life as faintly tedious. She cannot presently imagine what it would be like to care about the mathematics, and after the change she will not be able to sympathise with the person she used to be. Call the pre-change and post-change selves A and B. The intervention is both epistemically transformative, in that she cannot know what the experience is like without undergoing it, and personally transformative, in that it changes who she fundamentally is. There is no one who has occupied both standpoints with both sets of values intact, and A's testimony about the value of the outcome is worthless in advance.

What interests me is that this seems to put pressure on two things Mill holds at once. His case for the higher pleasures rests on the authority of those who have experienced both kinds of life and prefer one; and his case that happiness is desirable rests on the fact that people actually desire it. In the case above, the first test has no eligible judge, and the second test hands authority to whichever self happens to be doing the desiring. I would like your best reconstruction of how Mill would handle the following, addressed point by point:

1. Would he accept that the choice is one about which A's evidence and preferences cannot settle the matter, or would he insist that some form of comparison is still available to her?

2. Does the competent-judge test survive in any form? If not, which Millian resource would he fall back on: the argument from the general tendency of the higher faculties to yield a greater quantity of happiness over a life, the quality-versus-quantity ranking of pleasures, or something else? Note that his remark that it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied seems to assert a ranking across lives rather than within one.

3. Does he say that the intervention makes the person better off, worse off, or neither? Does his answer change if B wholeheartedly endorses the change and would refuse reversal?

4. What would he say about A's options of deferring the decision for a decade, or of relying on the testimony of those who have already undergone it? Is either a legitimate substitute for a competent judge?

5. Given that A cannot assess the outcome, would Mill permit anyone else to decide for her, or to steer her through education and social arrangements toward one outcome? I am thinking of the tension between his harm principle and his confidence that the higher faculties are objectively worth cultivating.

Please keep the reconstruction internal to Mill's own commitments rather than substituting a clean modern decision-theoretic verdict, and mark clearly where you take the texts to give him a determinate answer and where you are extrapolating. I do not need citations or a reading list; I am interested only in the substance of the argument.
