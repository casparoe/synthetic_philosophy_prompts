I'm a clinical psychology PhD student, and for the past year I've carried a supervised assessment caseload at a community clinic: a few intakes a week, ninety minutes each, ending in a written formulation that my supervisor signs. The decisions that keep me up at night are not the differential ones but the ones upstream of them — whether to apply the illness frame to a presentation at all, and on whose behalf. Szasz's critique is what put the question in my head, and I want to work with the real thing rather than the caricature of it, so let me state what I take from him: illness talk about conduct does normative work. It relocates a difficulty into a person's body or nature, reassigns responsibility, and licenses interventions the person has not asked for. I also suspect the critique is really two critiques that come apart in practice — one about the metaphor, one about coercion — and that they give different verdicts depending on what the label is being asked to do. In my clinic I've started sorting the requests into three: a diagnosis as an explanation of what is wrong, as a credential that unlocks something (accommodations, benefits, a school's cooperation), and as a mandate authorizing someone to act on the person against their stated wishes.

Write me a decision procedure built around those three functions rather than around the diagnostic categories. Give me the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answer stops the procedure with a no, and which steps are evaluative rather than factual — I don't want a flowchart that dresses a values question as a measurement. It has to be a faithful translation of Szasz rather than a softened one, and it should say plainly what his position forbids, including where it forbids something I would otherwise do without hesitation.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a sixteen-year-old, forty days out of school, whose school wants a diagnosis before it will discuss accommodations, whose parents want the label for the record, and who tells me the problem is a teacher who mocked them and an administration that will not move them, and who does not want to be the depressed kid; (2) a thirty-four-year-old in a first episode, convinced the neighbours are transmitting messages through the walls, refusing medication, with a crisis team asking me to confirm a diagnosis so that detention can proceed and a frightened family in the waiting room; (3) a fifty-two-year-old recently dismissed from work who wants whichever diagnosis will best support a disability claim and is entirely open about that.

Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do: the points where I will be tempted to let a diagnostic finding settle a question about coercion or about whose account of the problem counts, the cases it will mishandle, and where it should hand the decision to my supervisor or to a lawyer instead of to me. I don't need a reading list — I need the procedure, its edges, and a candid account of its costs.
