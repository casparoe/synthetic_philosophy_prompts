For several years now I have been writing a novel that follows a single woman from her twenties to her eighties, and the question that will not leave me alone — the one my philosopher friend insists is a technical problem rather than a novelist's problem — is whether the woman on the final page is the same person as the woman on the first. My friend is a perdurantist. She holds that a person, like any persisting thing, is a four-dimensional whole assembled out of temporal parts, that each moment of a life contributes its own part and nothing further, and that there is no fact about identity over and above those parts. Her favourite argument for this is an analogy I have come to distrust even as I keep reaching for it. A life, she says, is like a melody. No single note is the melody; the melody exists only as the notes in their order, and to insist that the melody be wholly present in any one note is simply to misunderstand what a melody is. So too, she concludes, it is a mistake to ask whether the woman is wholly present at any one moment; she exists as the whole sequence, and each moment is one note. This, my friend says, disposes of endurantism — the rival view that the very same person is wholly present at each moment of her life — as a confusion born of treating a process as though it were a thing.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the metaphysics. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the note, the melody, the performance, the score, the listener, the rests between notes — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Which features of a melody does the analogy quietly import into a person: that a melody is repeatable and can be played again; that it is a type rather than a particular performance; that it is heard in succession and never grasped all at once; that its division into notes is to some degree a matter of notation and convenience rather than something in the sound itself? Separate the disanalogies that bear on my friend's conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, say whether the conclusion — that there is no wholly present person, only parts — survives once they are on the table, and tell me what a better analogy would be, and why.
