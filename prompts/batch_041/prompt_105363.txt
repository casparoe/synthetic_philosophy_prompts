I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and I am preparing a teaching dialogue for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of uncertain evidence. I would like you to draft that dialogue. Please take on the role of a philosopher of science who works on Bayesian confirmation theory and who is equally at home in the formalism and in the homely examples that motivate it. Before writing, think carefully through the technical structure of the two positions you will be putting in each other's mouths. Open your response with a brief brainstorm — a handful of bullets identifying the strongest formulation of each side, the two or three points on which the exchange should turn, and any place where a careless formulation would turn one of the characters into a strawman — and then write the dialogue itself.

The disagreement concerns how a rational agent should revise her credences when her experience is inconclusive. Two rules are on the table. The first says that when you learn a proposition E for certain, you should replace your old credence in any proposition A with your old conditional credence in A given E. The second, usually called Jeffrey conditionalization after Richard Jeffrey, says that experience often leaves you with no proposition you are certain of, only a redistribution of credence across the cells of a partition; in that case, if your new credences in the cells are q1 through qn, your new credence in A should be the weighted sum of your old conditional credences in A given each cell, weighted by those new credences, with the conditional credences themselves held fixed from before the experience. The second rule is meant to generalize the first, since the first is simply the special case in which one cell is assigned credence one.

The dialogue should be between two proponents of opposing views about this material. One holds that the second rule is genuinely needed, because experience frequently delivers evidence that is uncertain through and through; this character should be able to say precisely what that means and why the first rule cannot accommodate it. The other holds that the first rule suffices for every case, because experience always supplies some proposition the agent is certain of — typically a proposition about how things seem or appear to her — so that the apparent need for the second rule is an artifact of describing the input of experience at the wrong grain. Neither character may be a strawman; both should be technically competent Bayesians, and each should answer the other's actual arguments rather than sliding past them. The exchange should be real back-and-forth, with each turn responding to the last, and it should not end in a tidy consensus unless the argument has genuinely earned one.

Ground the abstract dispute in a concrete case that both characters return to more than once: a colleague working late reads a dimly lit instrument panel and comes away 0.65 confident that the reaction has occurred. What, if anything, did she learn, and what should she have done with it?

I want the dialogue to work through at least the following points, and to get the technicalities right rather than gesturing at them:

- whether the seeming proposition is really one the agent is certain of, given inattention, peripheral vision, and the possibility of confabulation, and whether the version of it that is certain is too thin to do the work the orthodox view needs;
- whether a proposition about how things seem to one is evidence about the world only in conjunction with further, uncertain assumptions about the reliability of seemings;
- the tension between a thin input that is certain and a rich input that carries the information, and whether the orthodox rule either loses the richness or smuggles the uncertainty back in;
- the fact that repeated applications of the second rule are path-dependent: two pieces of uncertain evidence arriving in one order need not leave the agent where the same two pieces would leave her in the opposite order, when the two partitions are not nested. One character may treat this as a defect; the other should say exactly what it does and does not show;
- the diachronic Dutch book worry: that an agent who updates this way can be committed to a series of bets, each of which strikes her as fair when it is offered, that together guarantee a loss. Have the character who raises it say which cases it is meant to cover, and have the other reply on the merits;
- the self-referential repair, if one of the characters proposes it: that the agent can always be certain of a proposition about her own posterior credence — that her credence in the reaction's having occurred is now 0.65 — and conditionalize on that instead. The other character should press on whether such a proposition is about the world or only about her, whether it is guaranteed by the very procedure she is trying to justify, whether a well-defined prior for it could exist, and whether the original problem simply reappears one level up;
- whether the disagreement is substantive or turns on how the input of experience is described, so that the two rules coincide once the partition is specified carefully enough.

The dialogue should run roughly 1,500 words, in American English, with two clearly distinguished speakers. Do not include citations, references, or appeals to authority; the characters should argue from the substance. Write it as polished philosophical prose that a well-prepared graduate student could read aloud.
