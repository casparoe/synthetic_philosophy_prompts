I am a high school student preparing an independent project on the philosophy of history, and I have become stuck on a passage in Immanuel Kant's essay "Idea for a Universal History from a Cosmopolitan Point of View" (1784). I am reading it in English translation, so the wording below is from the version I have.

The passage that first stopped me is this: "Individuals and even whole peoples think little on this. Each, according to his own inclination, follows his own purpose, often in opposition to others; yet each individual and people, as if following some guiding thread, go toward a natural but to each of them unknown goal; all work toward furthering it, even if they would set little store by it if they did know it."

Other sentences seem to state something much stronger about nature: "All natural capacities of a creature are destined to evolve completely to their natural end. ... An organ that is of no use, an arrangement that does not achieve its purpose, are contradictions in the teleological theory of nature." And: "In man (as the only rational creature on earth) those natural capacities which are directed to the use of his reason are to be fully developed only in the race, not in the individual."

My difficulty is not with the individual sentences but with the kind of claim they make. Calling the goal of history a "guiding thread" suggests something a historian adopts in order to write a coherent narrative, yet the sentences about organs and capacities read like assertions about how nature actually works. I would be grateful for help with three questions.

First, is the "natural purpose" of the human race meant as a fact about the world, or as a way of organizing a history? Is there anything in the passage itself that decides between these readings, or does the text leave it open?

Second, I tried to test the thesis about natural capacities against what I have learned in biology. The appendix, the tailbone, and other structures are usually described as vestigial, and species go extinct with their capacities unfulfilled. If the thesis is an empirical claim about nature, this seems to refute it; if it is not, what work is it doing in an argument about history, and can the argument survive without it?

Third, my teacher connected the essay to the familiar idea that history bends toward justice. But in the passage the goal is unknown to those who pursue it, and they would "set little store by it if they did know it." Does Kant's argument entitle him to a morally good outcome, or only to an outcome of some kind? Can the passage support the reading my teacher offered, or does it support something weaker or stranger?

I would much prefer your reasoning about these specific lines to a general summary of the essay, and if the text is genuinely ambiguous on one of these points, I would rather be told so, with the evidence on each side, than given a confident answer.
