Last term my religious studies class covered the idea of karma, and I have been thinking about how my relatives actually use it, which doesn't seem to match the textbook version. When someone is diagnosed with a serious illness, or a family stays poor, they say that person is working off the results of actions from a previous life; but when someone is wealthy or healthy, they call it a blessing or the reward of hard work rather than the payoff of past merit, and nobody ever addresses the obvious objection that we cannot know what anyone did in a previous life. The same relatives who explain a person's suffering as deserved also insist that we have a duty to help that person and must not judge them.

Please tell me what theory of moral desert this practice actually presupposes. In particular: does it require that the suffering be deserved, or only that it be caused by the sufferer's own past actions — that is, is karma functioning as an impersonal causal law, like gravity, or as a verdict handed down by a judge, which would seem to need a judge to hand it down? Does it require a self that survives death and remains the same person who acted, given that the later self remembers nothing of the act? Which elements are strictly presupposed by the practice, and which are merely things people happen to say? And is the whole package — deserved suffering, plus an obligation to aid the sufferer, plus the admission that we cannot know anyone's past actions — coherent, or does it hold together only because the karma explanation is never actually applied when people decide how to treat someone? Conclude by saying what would have to change in the practice if the karma explanation were dropped and replaced by a purely causal one.
