I am a junior in high school and the editor of our literary magazine, and I would like you to write a poem for our spring issue. It will also be read aloud at the final meeting of our philosophy club, which has spent the year on free will, so it has to survive both audiences.

The question I have not been able to put down is the one our club kept circling: when someone says that free will is worth wanting, which freedom are they talking about? Three candidates kept surfacing, and I want a poem that holds all three in view without letting any of them win.

The first is the plainest: nothing and no one is preventing you from doing what you have already decided to do, which is the freedom of the person who is not locked in, held down, or overruled. The second is the one I care most about, and it is less obvious: the freedom not just to act on your strongest desire, but to have some say over which desire is strongest, which is the difference between a person and someone who merely wants whatever he wants and never once asks whether he wants to want it. The third is the stubborn one: the freedom to have done otherwise, in exactly the same situation, with everything up to that instant just as it was. I have been thinking about how, in the hardest cases, this third freedom shrinks almost to nothing. A person who could not have chosen any differently at all, except that somewhere in him there was a flicker, half a second of hesitation or resistance or turning away, and some people say that flicker is everything and others say it is nothing whatever.

What I would like is a poem of roughly twenty-five to thirty-five lines, in free verse, built on one constraint that I hope is more than a stunt. It should turn on a single sentence that returns several times, and each time it returns exactly one word should have changed; the word that changes must be the word that carries the sentence from one of these three freedoms to the next. The poem should not name or announce the three candidates the way I have named them here. A reader should feel the ground shift beneath the repeated sentence before understanding why it shifted. And when the sentence comes back for the last time, I do not want the poem to settle which freedom is worth wanting. I want all three left standing, close enough to touch, with the question of what each one is worth, for blame, for dignity, for love, for the ordinary business of being a person, still honestly open.

Make it accurate enough that someone who has studied this for years would not wince, and clear enough that a reader who has never thought about it would still be moved. I do not need citations or references to anyone's work; I want the poem to do the work by itself.
