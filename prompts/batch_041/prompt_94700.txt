Ok so I'm a novelist, which means I spend most of my working life trying to get other people's inner lives onto the page, and I've ended up with alot of opinions about what emotions actually are that I'm no longer sure I can hold all at once. Here's roughly where I've landed, in no particular order:

1. An emotion is at bottom a felt bodily state — the tight chest, the heat in the face, the pit in the stomach. Take the feeling away and I don't think there's anything left that deserves the name.
2. Emotions are also appraisals. When I'm angry I'm construing someone as having wronged me; when I'm afraid I'm construing something as dangerous. The appraisal isn't just a cause of the emotion, it's part of what the emotion is.
3. Emotions are a kind of perception — they're how we take in the value of things, the way the eyes take in color. My fear is presenting the spider to me as threatening, the way sight presents it as brown.
4. Recalcitrant emotions are real and they're everywhere. A character (or a person) can know perfectly well that the plane is safe and still be terrified. I write this constantly; it's most of what a novel is for.
5. Emotions are the kind of thing that can be rational or irrational, fitting or unfitting, and that's exactly what makes them worth writing — the wrong feeling at the wrong moment tells you something about a person.
6. You can't just decide to feel something. A character who wills herself into loving someone reads as false every time.

So: do these hang together? Which ones are actually pulling against each other and which only look like they do? Rank whatever real tensions you find by how bad they are, and for each one tell me what I'd have to give up and what that would cost me on the page — because I use all six when I'm drafting, and I'd rather not loose more than I have to.
