I'm teaching the Hart-Fuller exchange to a jurisprudence seminar next week and I want to use Antigone as the centrepiece instead of just lecturing about the grudge informer cases. The problem is every reading of the play I can find does the same move -- Antigone stands for natural law, Creon for positivism, tragedy because both are right -- and then it stops. Thats boring and I think its also wrong about the philosophy. Can you give me a reading that actually takes both the play and the debate seriously?

Some things I want you to wrestle with. Fullers natural law isnt a higher moral code, its the inner morality of law -- the eight principles of legality: generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, feasibility, constancy, and congruence between the rules and how officials actually behave. So Antigones appeal to the unwritten laws of the gods isnt Fullerian natural law at all, its a rival sovereign, or maybe just divine command. If thats right the standard pairing is backwards: Antigone isnt Fullers heroine. Does the play then show that Fuller isnt really a natural lawyer in the old sense, or just that the way we usually teach the play is wrong? And check Creons edict against the eight principles yourself -- my instinct is it passes most of them (its general, published, prospective, clear), which is awkward for Fuller, since he wants wicked laws not to count as law. The one I keep coming back to is congruence: Creon eventually does relent and goes to free her, but too late, and only after Teiresias frightens him. Does that late reversal show his regime fails the congruence requirement -- that the officials werent actually being guided by the rules they enforced?

Then the Hart side. If the edict is law, Antigones problem isnt validity but obedience -- shes a civil disobedient, not a natural lawyer, and the play is about whether shes right to disobey rather than about whether the edict is really law. Harts open texture and the core/penumbra distinction are useful here: is Antigones case in the core of the edict, obviously covered, or could you argue the edict is in the penumbra -- does what she actually does even count as burying Polynices? And the grudge informer and the post-war German cases are the obvious modern parallel, along with Radbruchs idea that a law can be so intolerably unjust that it stops being law. Is Creons edict over that line or not, and does the play give us any way to tell?

Where I want you to push back on the reading is this: Antigone isnt a liberal defending rights, shes defending family and religious duty, and shes pretty cold to Ismene, so shes an awkward heroine for a modern natural lawyer. And Creon isnt a caricature positivist -- he has real reasons about the city and the traitor. So Id like you to say honestly where the play resists the philosophical reading, and whether it dramatizes something the Hart-Fuller arguments miss -- maybe something about the tragic, that law and morality are both genuinely at stake and neither can be reduced to the other.

Please dont quote the play at me line by line, just describe the scenes, and I dont need a reading list. I mostly want to know whether the pairing is defensible and how Id present it without misleading my students.
