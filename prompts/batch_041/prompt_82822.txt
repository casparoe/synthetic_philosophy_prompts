I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so go easy on me if I mangle the vocabulary. I'm about 200 pages into a book about a woman named Ruth who's taking care of her mother, who has fairly advanced dementia and mostly doesn't know who Ruth is anymore — calls her by her dead sister's name, flinches when Ruth tries to help her into the bath, that kind of thing. The whole book is basically an argument for care ethics, or at least its supposed to be: the idea being that the moral weight of a life sits in these particular relationships and not in some abstract calculus of who deserves what. So I've been reading alot of care ethics to keep myself honest, and I came up with this argument to try to pin down exactly what makes Ruth's situation admirable. Trouble is, it seems to prove care ethics is false, and I really don't want that.

Here it is:

(1) On the version of care ethics I keep gravitating to, what makes an action morally good is the caring relation between the person doing the caring and the person being cared for — not the carer's intentions, not the results.
(2) But a relation takes two. For there to be a caring relation, the person being cared for has to actually be in it — has to receive the care, respond to it, be moved by it. Otherwise it's just one person doing stuff near another person.
(3) Ruth's mother can't do any of that. She doesn't recognize Ruth, she pushes her away, she isn't receiving anything in any meaningful sense.
(4) So there's no caring relation between Ruth and her mother.
(5) So by care ethics's own lights, what Ruth is doing isn't care at all.
(6) But Ruth's care for her mother is the most obviously good thing in the whole novel. If a moral theory can't call that care, something's wrong with the theory.
(7) So caring can't be a relation after all — it has to be something inside the carer, or in the act itself, that doesn't depend on whether it lands.

I keep feeling like step (2) is smuggling something in, but I can't say what, and if I give up (2) I'm not sure what's left of the relational picture that made me like care ethics in the first place. Is the argument sound? If not, which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather keep (6) than (1), if that helps, but I want to know what I'm paying either way. And the part that actually matters for the book: if I keep (2), what am I supposed to say about Ruth?
