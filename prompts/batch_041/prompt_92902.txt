I host a monthly interview podcast on practical rationality; the audience is mostly graduate students and interested non-specialists. My next guest is a philosopher who has spent a decade arguing that satisficing is not merely a description of how resource-limited agents happen to decide, but the correct normative account of practical rationality — correct for ideal agents as much as for us. I want the interview to be substantive rather than promotional, so I need help preparing it.

Act as a philosopher working in decision theory and the theory of practical reason, with a good ear for where a position is genuinely vulnerable. Before drafting anything, take time to reconstruct the view below in your own words, identify where it is strongest and where the real pressure points lie, and only then write the questions. Work through this carefully; the quality of the interview depends on getting the reconstruction right. If you conclude that the view as I have stated it is internally unstable, say so briefly before the questions — I would rather know in advance.

The view, as my guest states it:

- An action is rational just in case its outcome clears a threshold of adequacy that is relative to the agent. No comparison with better available outcomes is required, and the absence of such a comparison is not a defect.
- The threshold is fixed by the agent's aspiration level, which is inherited from her history, habits, and social setting. She may revise it, but the revision is not itself a matter of optimization. Aspiration levels are given to deliberation rather than chosen by it.
- The account is not a fallback for agents with limited time or computational power. It is claimed to be correct even for an agent who could compute the value of every option before her.
- Because it demands only adequacy, it is claimed to dissolve the demandingness objection to consequentialism and the problem of having to trade off incommensurable goods.

Now draft eight to eleven interview questions. Each should be a genuine probe that turns on the content of the position, not an invitation to restate it. At a minimum, cover: the arbitrariness and possible regress of thresholds; whether satisficing collapses into constrained maximizing once the threshold is treated as a constraint; the self-prediction problem (an agent who knows her own threshold and search procedure seems able to choose how hard to look); whether the view can make sense of ex post regret and the thought that one could have done better; the leveling-down or slacker objection, and whether the theory can condemn anything at all; whether setting a threshold presupposes the very comparability the view was meant to avoid; whether aspiration levels themselves can be criticized as reasonable or unreasonable; and whether the position is a normative theory of reasons or a description of well-adapted heuristics, dressed up. Close with a question about what would count as evidence or argument against the view.

For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give, and a one-line bracketed note to me naming what the question is doing — for instance, presses the regress, tests the self-prediction worry, forces a choice between criterion and procedure. Keep the language speakable aloud. No questions about how he came to the topic, and no requests for reading lists or for names of people he is responding to: assume neither I nor the listeners have read the literature.
