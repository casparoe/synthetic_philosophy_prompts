I've been reading about the harm principle — the idea that speech should be free unless it harms others — and I want to test it against my own gut reactions. I work on content moderation, so these are cases I actually see, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

I'd allow: a long, civil essay arguing that a particular religion is false and dangerous; a comedian's routine mocking a public figure; a post accurately reporting that a politician was arrested decades ago; a novel with a graphic torture scene.

I'd remove: a post falsely naming a private individual as a sex offender; a thread where users coordinate to flood a specific journalist with abuse; a post falsely claiming a named local restaurant is laundering money; a livestream where someone reads out his ex's home address and urges viewers to go there.

At first I thought my rule was about targeting: allow general claims, remove attacks on a specific person. But two cases break it. I'd allow a calm, well-argued essay claiming that a named ethnic group is genetically inferior, which targets a whole group; and I'd remove a viral post falsely claiming a new drug is safe, which names no one. So targeting isn't it — and neither is truth, since the group essay is, as far as I can tell, baseless.

My best guess at a unifying principle is that speech is permissible as long as its targets keep a fair chance to answer it back, but I can't make that precise and I doubt it handles the drug post. What principle — or small set of principles — actually fits these verdicts? Please state it precisely, tell me what it implies for a case I haven't listed (a platform's recommendation algorithm amplifying a false but legal health claim), and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.
