I teach the final unit of an upper-level seminar on moral philosophy and the ethics of partiality, and every year I run into the same wall. My students can recite Bernard Williams's integrity objection to impartialist morality — the complaint, often referred to as the one-thought-too-many objection, that an agent who must first secure an impartial license before acting on a deep personal attachment has already been corrupted in the very structure of his deliberation — and they can also recite the drowning-spouse case and the Jim-and-the-Indians case, and yet they cannot say what the objection is actually targeting. What I want them to see is that there are two quite different charges in the vicinity: (a) the charge that impartial morality misranks the agent's attachments, treating them as less weighty than they are, and (b) the charge that it imposes the wrong deliberative order, by demanding that the attachment present itself as a candidate consideration standing in need of an impartial justification rather than as the reason it already is. Most of them collapse (b) into (a), and I would like a piece of fiction that makes the gap between the two impossible to miss.

So please write me a short story, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. Set it inside a volunteer mountain search-and-rescue outfit, where the protagonist is a coordinator who works from a written triage protocol that ranks stranded parties by survivability and accessibility; one of the parties she must rank includes her own younger brother. I want two scenes that sit in tension with each other: one in which she deliberates before acting, working the protocol, and one later in which she is made to justify her choice to an institutional review board that wants her reasoning on the record. The story should be constructed so that a careful reader can conclude either that the protocol shielded her from the objection or that the protocol is precisely what instantiates it — I do not want the narrative to settle the question, and I do not want a narrator who moralizes, editorializes, or tells us which scene is the corrupted one. Let the contrast between the two scenes carry the philosophical weight, and keep the prose restrained and concrete rather than lyrical. Please avoid the standard drowning-spouse and trolley-style setups, since my students have memorized those and will pattern-match instead of thinking. American English, please. After the story, add an endnote of no more than 120 words stating plainly which of the two charges — the misranking charge or the deliberative-order charge — the story is engineered to isolate, and naming the single moment in the text where, in your view, the two come closest to collapsing into each other.
