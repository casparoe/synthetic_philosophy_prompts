I am trying to get a stable reading of a passage in Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste," and I keep talking myself out of every interpretation I arrive at. The passage, as it appears in the Wikisource text of the essay, is this:

"The great variety of Tastes, as well as of opinions, which prevail in the world, is too obvious not to have fallen under every one's observation. Men of the most confined knowledge are able to remark a difference in the narrow circle of their acquaintance, even where the persons have been educated under the same government, and have early imbibed the same prejudices. But those who can enlarge their view to contemplate distant nations and remote ages, are still more surprised at the great inconsistence and contradiction. We are apt to call barbarous whatever departs widely from our own taste and apprehension: But soon find the epithet of reproach retorted on us. And the highest arrogance and self conceit is at last startled, on observing an equal assurance on all sides, and scruples, amidst such a contest of sentiments, to pronounce positively in its own favour."

A few paragraphs later he writes:

"In all matters of opinion and science, the case is opposite: The difference among men is there oftner found to lie in generals than in particulars; and to be less in reality than in appearance. An explication of the terms commonly ends the controversy; and the disputants are surprized to find, that they had been quarrelling, while at bottom they agreed in their judgment."

Here is the difficulty. I am trying to hold apart two ways in which someone might take up a critic's judgment. The first I call Verdict-Deference: you believe that a work is good because a trusted critic says it is, and you treat the critic's say-so as evidence for a proposition about the work. The second I call Sensibility-Deference: you do not form the belief that the work is good, but you try to acquire the critic's way of attending and discriminating, on the assumption that if you succeeded you would come to feel the pleasure the critic feels. My initial thought was that Hume must reject Verdict-Deference, because he insists that beauty is not a property of objects but a sentiment in the beholder, and must accept something like Sensibility-Deference, because the whole point of the true judge is to model a corrected sensibility. But the passage above seems to cut the other way. Hume says that in matters of opinion and science, disagreement is often merely verbal and evaporates once terms are explicated, whereas in matters of taste the disagreement is real and does not evaporate. If the disagreement is real, then there seems to be something to disagree about, which makes Verdict-Deference look intelligible after all. And if the disagreement is not real but only a clash of sentiments, then Hume should have said that explication of terms would resolve it by showing that the parties were not really disagreeing about the same thing — which is the opposite of what he says. So I cannot tell whether the contrast between "opinion and science" and "taste" is a contrast between resolvable and unresolvable disagreement, or between two different kinds of disagreement that both have a fact-like structure.

What I want is a reading of this specific passage, not a survey of Hume scholarship. Does the passage commit Hume to the view that aesthetic disagreement is about something, or only to the view that it is not merely verbal? Where exactly does the ambiguity lie, and what are the competing readings? And does the passage support the Verdict-Deference/Sensibility-Deference distinction, or does it collapse the distinction? If it collapses it, which side does it collapse it into?
