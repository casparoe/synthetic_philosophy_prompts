Chesterton's fence gets cited constantly as a burden-of-proof rule: don't tear a thing down until you can say what it's for. But the metaphor smuggles in a picture of the fence as protecting travellers, when plenty of fences exist to keep people out, and the cost of the fence lands on whoever is on the wrong side of it. Burke's presumption in favour of inherited arrangements looks like the same rule with a different backing (accumulated wisdom of generations vs. plain ignorance of what a practice does). My hunch is that once you seperate three things -- (1) the survival of a practice as evidence that it has some function, (2) whose function it is and who pays for it, (3) how strong the presumption should be given what kind of process kept the practice alive (competitive selection, sheer inertia, or a group with the power to keep it going) -- the rhetorical force of the tradition comes from running them together, and what's left standing is a defensible but much narrower epistemic claim. I also want to press the rule with cases where you don't know the fence's purpose but can see it's redundant, or where you can see it costs more to maintain than any function it could plausibly serve.
