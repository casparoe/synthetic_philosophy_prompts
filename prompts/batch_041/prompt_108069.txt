For a long piece I am writing on the revived argument over whether history admits of laws — the argument usually associated with Popper — I have spent several months attending a monthly workshop run by the philosopher of history Vera Lindqvist, who for a decade has invited readers and students to submit candidate laws of history and has ruled, in public and usually in a single line, on whether each candidate is a law or something else. Her rulings are half the reason for the piece. Everyone who works on this question knows how the classical arguments are supposed to cut, and almost everyone I have spoken to has been wrong about what she will say next.

Here is the archive as I have transcribed it. Each item is a submitted candidate; the ruling is hers.

1. "In any society whose ruling group justifies itself by success in war, a long peace corrodes its claim to rule." Accepted.

2. "Between 1750 and 1900 the states of Western Europe became steadily more centralized." Rejected: "a fact about a place and a century, not a law."

3. "In any society, the arrival of writing moves authority over the past from those who remember to those who keep records." Accepted.

4. "The twentieth century was the century of the masses." Rejected: "that describes an age; it names no mechanism."

5. "In any society, an institution that persists is one that serves a function for someone." Rejected: "nothing could count against it."

6. "Once a society's productive work is done entirely by machines, the state will wither away." Rejected: "a prophecy wearing the costume of a conditional."

The item circulated for next month's session is already being fought over:

7. "In any society that acquires a cheap and reliable test for whether a speaker believes what she is saying, the state will regulate political speech the way it regulates fraud."

What will she say? I am less interested in the verdict than in the reasoning, so please give me the following. State the rule she appears to be running. Then give the other rules that fit every ruling in the archive just as well, and say what each of those predicts about item 7. Tell me which of them you think is really hers, and on what evidence — and if the archive genuinely does not decide between them, say so plainly rather than choosing for me. Finally, name the one further candidate I should submit to the workshop to force the question, the case on which the surviving rules part company, and say exactly what each verdict on it would show.
