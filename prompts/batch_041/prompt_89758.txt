I've been reading a lot lately about the idea that the far future could contain vastly more suffering than the present does — not just more beings, but suffering on a scale that makes everything so far look like a rounding error — and that we might be able to do something about it now. The more of these discussions I read, the more I notice a move I don't have a name for, and I don't think it's just optimism.

Here's the shape of it. Suppose some technology has, say, a one-in-a-thousand chance of eventually producing a future with enormous amounts of suffering in it. The standard reply is that this is speculative, that you can't plan around a scenario like that, that it's sci-fi. Fine. But give the same technology a one-in-a-thousand chance of wiping us out instead, and a lot of the same people treat it as a serious reason to slow down. And it isn't a numeracy problem — these are people who will act on a one-in-a-million risk when it's a drug trial or a launch window. The number doesn't change. What changes is what the bad outcome is made of. It's as if the probability is being read off the description of the outcome instead of the other way around.

Is there a name for this? I know about availability, and about people dropping probabilities entirely once they get emotional about an outcome, and about motivated reasoning — but those all seem to be about how people handle numbers badly in general, and what I'm after is the bit where the content of the bad outcome is what sets the discount rate. If nothing established fits, coin something and tell me where its edges are. Does it cover the mirror case, where the huge outcome is a good one and the small probability suddenly gets taken very seriously? Does it cover a bad outcome that's just a large setback rather than a catastrophe? And what exactly separates it from plain wishful thinking?
