I teach a seventy-five-minute session in a second-year undergraduate course, and this year I have to introduce Russell's theory of definite descriptions. The students have had one term of formal logic: they can read and write quantified formulas, they know what a bound variable is, and they are comfortable distinguishing a sentence from its truth conditions. They have not met scope ambiguity, and no one has ever asked them to separate grammatical form from logical form. Plan the explanation for me; do not give me the explanation itself.

1. The order. Tell me what has to be in place before the theory can be stated as a solution to a problem, and what depends on what. In particular, say where the grammatical-form/logical-form distinction has to be introduced relative to the problem the theory is meant to solve, and whether that problem should be presented as the failure of reference to non-existent things or as something else. If you think the standard entry point through empty names and the present king of France is the wrong place to start for this audience, say so and give me the entry point you would use instead, with your reason.

2. The carrying example. One example has to do the heavy lifting for the whole session. I do not want the present king of France and I do not want the author of Waverley; both are in every textbook, and the students will pattern-match instead of thinking. Give me the example, tell me what it must contain for the machinery to be visible, and tell me what it must lack. In particular, address whether it can be a case where the description succeeds in picking out its object, and whether the example should fail on the existence clause, on the uniqueness clause, or on both. I am also considering an organizing image for the session: presenting the theory as a claim about where in a sentence the commitment to there being such a thing actually sits, rather than as a claim about what exists. Tell me whether that framing earns its keep or misleads.

3. Misconceptions, in the order they will arise. I expect at least these: that Russell is claiming the sentence is meaningless; that he is claiming it is straightforwardly false and nothing more needs saying; that the theory is a paraphrase trick rather than a claim about logical form; that it is a psychological claim about how people process sentences; that it commits him to the separate thesis that ordinary proper names are abbreviated descriptions, which I do not want them to absorb; and that it is really the doctrine that existence is not a predicate, which they will have met in the ontological argument and will import here. For each, tell me where in the sequence to head it off, and whether heading it off early costs me anything later in the session.

4. The incompleteness objection as a hinge. My students will push back with an ordinary sentence in which uniqueness plainly fails and yet the sentence is perfectly fine — someone says the car is in the driveway when there are three cars. I want that objection to do real work rather than sit there as a distraction. Tell me whether to let it surface as a defect in the theory, as the bridge to the presupposition alternative, or as both, and exactly where in the sequence to let it in. If there is a point in the sequence where letting it in too early would wreck the session, say where that is.

5. The ending. I want to finish on the ambiguity of negation: a negated sentence whose description fails to refer, read one way comes out true and read the other way comes out false. From there I want to move to the presupposition view, on which such a sentence is neither true nor false, and I want the students to leave able to state both positions and genuinely unsure which is right. I do not want to hand them a verdict. Tell me how to set that up so the last ten minutes land, and what I must not say earlier or I will spoil it.

6. What to leave out, and saying so. Tell me what to refuse to cover at this level, and what to announce out loud so that the students know it was left out on purpose rather than forgotten. I am considering whether to bring in the referential/attributive distinction and whether to say anything about the complaint that the theory mishandles proper names. Advise me on both, and tell me the cost of each omission.

7. The check. Give me one question I can ask in the last five minutes that will show whether they have the analysis or have merely memorized a formula, and tell me what a wrong answer would look like and what it would reveal.

Throughout, keep the dependency structure explicit: I want to know why each piece has to come where it does, not just a list of topics in a sensible order.
