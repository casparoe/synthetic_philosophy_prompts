I am a novelist, and I am in the early stages of a book whose central character is a philosopher of mathematics — an anti-realist of a stubborn and rather beautiful kind — and in the course of researching her I have been reading around her subject in a haphazard, novelistic way, which is to say I follow whatever seems dramatic and skip whatever seems merely technical. A few weeks ago a friend of mine, who teaches in a philosophy department and is generally reliable on the broad history of the subject though he has never worked on this particular problem, gave me over dinner a brisk account of Fitch's paradox of knowability. I wrote it down almost as he said it, because it had exactly the shape a novelist wants: a modest premise, a short argument, an intolerable conclusion. His summary ran roughly like this. "Fitch's paradox is at bottom quite simple. Take the anti-realist's central claim, that every truth is knowable. Now take some truth that nobody in fact knows. Since it is a truth, the anti-realist is committed to saying it is knowable. But suppose someone did know it: then they would know both that the thing is true and that nobody knows it, and that is a contradiction, since knowing that nobody knows it is incompatible with knowing it oneself. So there cannot be any truth that nobody knows — from the single premise that all truths are knowable it follows that we know everything already. Since that conclusion is obviously false, Fitch's argument refutes anti-realism outright and vindicates the realist, who has always held that truth may exceed anyone's knowledge. The reasoning itself is a parlour trick; it turns on a quirk in the logic of the word 'know.' What it really shows is that the very idea of a truth that will never be known is incoherent. I mention it because your anti-realist heroine would have to have an answer to it."

I half-believe him and half-don't, and the half that doesn't is precisely the half that matters to me, because I would rather not build a character's intellectual life on a summary that a specialist would quietly wince at. What I would like from you is an accuracy audit I can actually act on. Go through his account sentence by sentence — not to score points, but to tell me, for each move, what someone who has worked on this problem would nod at, what they would recognise as a common simplification of something more delicate, and what is simply wrong or misleading. I am especially suspicious of three things and would like them addressed directly. First, the leap from "the premise entails there are no unknown truths" to "we know everything already": is that a fair statement of the conclusion, or has he smuggled in something about who knows, and when? Second, the claim that the paradox refutes anti-realism outright and vindicates the realist: is that what the argument establishes, or is it a reductio that leaves a defender of the knowability thesis room to manoeuvre, and if so, what room exactly? Third, his parting claim that the argument shows the very idea of a never-known truth to be incoherent: does that follow from anything true in his summary, or is he stating categorically what is at best a conditional consequence of a premise he was supposed to be examining?

I would also be grateful if you would tell me what he has left out that a specialist would consider essential — whether the argument really does turn on a "quirk in the logic of the word 'know,'" or on substantive principles about knowledge that a careful opponent could contest, and which of those principles the summary has quietly assumed without stating. I don't need you to send me to any literature or tell me who said what when; I want the substance, in your own words, in a form I can put in a notebook and use when I write her. Where his summary is defensible, say so plainly — I am not looking for a takedown, only for an honest inventory of what survives.
