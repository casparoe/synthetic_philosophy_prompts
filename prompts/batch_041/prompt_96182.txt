I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and we've spent the last two sessions on the color phi phenomenon, and I've come out of it with an argument that I'm fairly sure proves too much, but I can't find the loose thread. Here's the setup we were given, in case it's not the version you know. You flash a dot on the left of a screen in one colour, blank it for a fraction of a second, then flash a dot on the right in a different colour. With the right timing you don't see two dots — you see a single dot travelling across the gap, and it switches colour roughly halfway along the path. The odd part is that people report the switch as happening at the midpoint, which is a moment before the second dot has even gone up on the screen. Nobody thinks perception runs backwards in time, so something has to give. The two options we were offered: either the brain holds everything and assembles the whole moving, colour-changing dot after both flashes and only then hands it to you as a finished product, so what you think you saw at the midpoint was actually built later and dated earlier (my friend called this the show-trial option — the evidence is manufactured before the public ever sees it), or you really did see the first colour, and your memory of it was quietly rewritten after the fact, so the record you consult later says the other colour (the ministry-of-truth option). We found out afterwards that we'd reinvented a distinction somebody famous already made, which was deflating.

Anyway, here's the argument, and it's the generalising step I don't trust but can't break. (1) In this case a bit of experiential content — the new colour, located at the midpoint — shows up in experience at a time earlier than any stimulus that could have caused it. (2) Nothing causes anything earlier than itself. (3) So that bit of content was produced later than it appears to have been, and was given a false earlier date. (4) Nothing in this depends on the particular colours, or on dots, or on the gap being a fraction of a second: whenever the content of an experience is richer than what the stimuli available at that moment could have supported, the surplus must have been added afterwards and back-dated. (5) But every experience is like that, because the brain is always integrating over a window, and there's always later processing that can enrich or overwrite what came before — and you can't introspect the overwriting, because from the inside the final version just is what you experienced. (6) So for any experience, the question 'when exactly did that occur, and was it revised before I got it?' has no answer that my experience could ever reveal to me. (7) And if no experience has a determinate time of occurrence, then the order of my experiences isn't a fact about me either — the stream of consciousness is just something I tell myself afterwards, out of whatever the machinery last wrote down. (8) So there's no fact of the matter about what I experienced a second ago, or in what order.

I can't accept (8). I also can't give up (2), obviously, and I'd like to keep (1) and (3) — they feel like just physics. My friend says the mistake is upstream in (1), because it treats the experience as if it had parts that can be matched one-to-one with the stimuli: she says the content is simply 'a dot moves and changes colour at the midpoint', and asking when the new colour happened is like asking which half of the word 'cat' you heard first. That seems to me to just relocate the problem rather than solve it, but I dont know. So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? I especially want to know whether I can keep the phenomenon and the physics while refusing (8), or whether the only honest options are the two unpleasant ones I listed at the start — and if it turns out the argument is actually sound, tell me what I'd be buying, because one of the things it seems to cost is my memory of what I just experienced, which is the very thing I'd be using to check the argument.
