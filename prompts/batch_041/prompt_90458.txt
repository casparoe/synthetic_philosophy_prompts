Next Saturday my oldest friend, Dana, leaves the country for good, and I'm giving the send-off speech at her going-away dinner. There will be about thirty people there, and they divide into two camps that don't much like each other. Her parents and her two brothers have spent the past year trying, gently and then not so gently, to talk her out of it; as far as they're concerned she is dismantling a perfectly good life — the job, the flat, the long relationship — to move somewhere she doesn't speak the language and knows almost no one. The other half of the room is the crowd from the climbing gym she fell in with two years ago; they think she has finally stopped pretending and started living. I'm the only person there who is close to both halves, which is presumably why I've been handed the microphone and why I've been dreading it.

The trouble isn't stage fright. Every send-off speech I can imagine runs on the same promise: you will be happy there; this will be the making of you. I can't make that promise, and not because I doubt Dana. A few weeks ago she said something I haven't been able to put down. She said she can't work out whether to go, because the woman who'd be doing the judging on the other side isn't the woman doing the choosing now — she'll want different things by then, and there's no way to ask that future self for advice, since she doesn't exist yet and won't be the same person when she does. I've been trying to say it back to myself in plainer words, and the best I've managed is that the witness is disqualified either way. If Dana ends up glad she went, her gladness is the very thing that was bought by going, so it can't count as evidence that going was right; and if she ends up miserable, she'll have spent years arranging herself not to notice, so her misery can't count either. Whichever way it comes out, the only testimony on offer is from a witness who can't be sworn in.

What I want from the speech is the thing I can't build myself. It has to be honest — no promises about how it turns out, and no pretending that her parents are simply wrong or that the climbers are simply right. It should land for both halves of the room at once, without one half feeling lectured at or patronized. It can't sound academic, and it can't name any books or thinkers; the moment I start explaining a theory I've lost the room, and Dana will hate it. And it should end by giving Dana something she can actually carry onto the plane — not reassurance, since I don't have any, but something true. Please write the speech, four to six minutes spoken, in my voice: plain, a little dry, affectionate, and not afraid of the fact that I don't know how this ends.
