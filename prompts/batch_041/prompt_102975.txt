I am a fourth-year PhD student in cognitive science, and there is a slogan in my lab that I have never seen given a careful reading. A senior colleague is skeptical of the computational account of creativity my group works with — a knob-twiddling picture in the tradition of Hofstadter, on which creative production is largely a matter of turning the dials a domain already provides, so that a new work is a re-setting of existing dimensions rather than the arrival of something from outside. When I presented a model that generates melodies by re-parameterizing a small set of continuous dimensions, he waved it off with a line from Ecclesiastes: 'there is no new thing under the sun.' He treated this as decisive. If every new work is a variation on what came before, he said, then the model has explained nothing, or has shown that there is nothing to explain.

I want to know what that sentence can actually be claiming before I concede or push back. Lay out the distinct theses it might express as a menu, ordered from the trivial to the radical, give a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, or false — and then say which reading my colleague most likely intended and whether any reading would in fact support his dismissal of the model.

In sorting the readings, keep the following axes distinct:
- the kind of novelty being denied: novelty of tokens, of types, of properties, of dimensions, of possibilities;
- the scope of 'under the sun': all actual things, all human artifacts, all possible things, or only what has been generated so far;
- the modal status of the claim: whether it is meant analytically, as an empirical generalization, or as a claim about what is physically or logically possible;
- whether it concerns the products of creation or the processes that generate them, and whether the two can come apart.

I would like the readings tested against two cases: a variation that introduces a dimension of variation where none existed before — a knob that was not previously on the panel — and a variation so degenerate that it merely restates the theme. Which readings would count the first as a genuinely new thing, and which would count the second as nothing new?

Finally, tell me whether the strongest defensible reading of the slogan is actually in tension with the knob-twiddling picture or is instead its philosophical expression. If recombination is what creation is, the Preacher may be agreeing with us rather than refuting us, and I would like to know which of us is confused.
