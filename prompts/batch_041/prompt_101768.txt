I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. In a recent postmortem, my team lead kept repeating the one-liner that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, using it to argue that when two engineers assign different probabilities to the same root-cause claim—both having read the same incident logs, both knowing the other read them, and both knowing that the other knows, and so on—at least one of them must be irrational. I suspect the line is doing more work than it can bear, especially because the two engineers may also have different loss functions, one weighting downtime heavily and the other data loss, so even with the same probability they might choose differently. Please lay out the distinct precise claims this one-liner could actually be making, from the trivial to the radical: a descriptive claim about actual humans, a formal claim about Bayesian agents with a common prior and common knowledge of one another’s posterior beliefs, a claim about higher-order evidence, a claim about what counts as rational, and a claim about disagreement in action or preference rather than in belief. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say which reading would actually support my lead’s conclusion that one engineer is irrational. At the end, state what would change your mind about your verdicts and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
