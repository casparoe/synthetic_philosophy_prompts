I took early retirement from a zoology department a couple of years ago — forty years of it, mostly running pigeons and starlings through delayed-reward schedules — and I've been filling the time sitting in on an undergraduate philosophy course at the local university. So I've known the hyperbolic discount curve since forever: reward value drops off steeply at short delays and flattens out further away, which is why a bird takes two seconds of grain now over ten seconds a minute from now, but waits happily if both options are a minute out. That much is just my old trade. What I keep bumping into on the philosophy side is the old puzzle of akrasia — weakness of will — presented as though it were a genuine paradox: how can someone judge that A is better than B, all things considered, and then go and do B? Honestly it doesn't strike me as a paradox at all, it strikes me as precisely what the curve predicts, and I half suspect the philosophers are describing in fancy vocabulary what Ainslie and the behavioural-econ people describe with a graph. But when I try to say that out loud at the seminar I can't tell wether I'm offering an explanation of weakness of will or quietly dissolving it and pretending I did something cleverer.

What I'd like from you is a proper comparison of the two — the Aristotelian/Davidsonian thing on one side, the delay-discounting account of preference reversal on the other — and I want you to be careful about a distinction I think does a lot of the work. There's acting against your present, considered judgment at the very moment of acting, and there's settling on a plan and later reversing it because the later self wants something else. The discounting story seems to only really cover the second, and only if you grant that the person at t2 literally prefers what they do at t2 — in which case nobody has done anything against their own better judgment at all, there's just a succession of short-lived preferrers, and 'weak' is a word we use afterwards when we disapprove of the one who happened to be holding the wheel. The first kind is the one that nags at me: the clear-eyed case, someone who says I know I shouldn't, I'm doing it anyway, and I still think I shouldn't — the discount curve seems to have nothing whatever to say about that, and I suspect it's the case the philosophers actually care about. I also notice the discounting literature makes a lot of the difference between a chooser who doesn't see the reversal coming and one who does and arranges things in advance so it can't happen (tying himself to the mast, so to speak), and I'd like to know whether the akrasia literature has anything to say about that, or whether it just treats all such cases as the same failure of will.

So: which of the two accounts has the better claim to the phenomenon, where are they genuinely in conflict and where are they just two vocabularies for one thing, and if you had to come down on it, is hyperbolic discounting a rival theory of akrasia, a replacement for it, or a redescription of a neighbouring phenomenon that happens to share its symptoms? And since I'm still enough of a scientist to want the question to have an empirical edge — what would you actually measure, or what pattern of behaviour would you go looking for, that would tell the two apart?
