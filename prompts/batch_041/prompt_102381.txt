A picture keeps surfacing in my seminar on political obligation, and I want to know whether it can bear the weight placed on it. The picture is that a legal penalty is a price: the statute forbids an act, but the attached fine or term of imprisonment is, in effect, an offer to permit it for a fee — a toll booth rather than a wall. My students invoke it to conclude that a civil disobedient who accepts the penalty has done nothing the law really forbids, so that civil disobedience is not a distinct category of action but ordinary lawbreaking with the fee paid; one of them adds that this vindicates the state, since no one is compelled. Please audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of seller, buyer, price, offer, refusal, and toll booth — then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the failures that matter for that conclusion from the differences that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in what the picture imports from markets that law lacks: whether an offer can be refused, who sets the price and to what end, and what becomes of those who cannot pay. Finally, say whether the conclusion survives, and if the toll booth is the wrong vehicle, what would be better.
