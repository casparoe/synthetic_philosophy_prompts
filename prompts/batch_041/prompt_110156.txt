I'm a novelist, about three years into a book whose main character inspects welds in a shipyard, and I've written a short craft essay that turned into something I didn't plan. I'd like you to tear it apart for me.

The setup, in case it matters: Nell runs an ultrasonic probe along a hull seam and knows, before the readout confirms anything, that there's a hairline crack hiding under the paint. I've asked her a hundred times how she knows and she can never tell me. She says the sound goes flat, or the feel goes greasy. Neither of those is an explanation. Hand me the same probe and I feel nothing but my own pulse.

Here's the draft. Its rough, the middle is a mess, and I already know the ending is too tidy.

---

THE RESIDUE (draft)

Everyone reaches for the same comparison here: knowing how versus knowing that. Ryle's old point, which I will paraphrase badly: if doing a thing well required first knowing a rule for doing it, then applying the rule would itself be a thing you'd have to know how to do, and you would never get started. The rule can't bootstrap the act. Nell has no rule. That's the whole appeal of the distinction for a novelist — the character who can do the thing and can't say the thing is a gift, because her silence looks like proof.

But I don't buy that the inarticulacy is the point. Plenty of things I know I can't say either, and they're still facts.

So here's what I actually think, and this is the part I want you to attack. Knowing that is always about something already settled. The fact sits still while you circle it. Knowing how is never about a settled thing; its whole shape points at a future that hasn't happened yet. Nell can't tell you how she does it, but she can tell you with total confidence what she'd do if the reading jumped. I don't think that would is a proposition she's consulting. It's the shape of her next move. So: know-how is the part of knowledge that has a future tense. What I call the residue is just this forward-leaning part, the bit that can't be handed over in a sentence, because a sentence is a thing that stops and the skill doesn't stop, it keeps going.

Which brings me to the instructor problem, my best shot at a test case. Nell is a terrible explainer and a brilliant teacher. She can watch an apprentice's hand come down wrong and fix it — a word, a nudge, two inches of wrist — and she never states a rule, because she doesn't have one. If knowing how were really knowing that under some special practical wrapping, then the correction would have to be a proposition she's passing across. It doesn't feel like that. It feels like she's steering the kid's trajectory rather than describing it. Teaching a skill might not be transfer of facts at all. It might be transfer of the residue.

Two things I can't patch. First, the future-tense thing leaks: I know that the bus will be late tomorrow, and that's future-directed too, so tense alone won't seperate the two kinds. Second, maybe I'm just redescribing a disposition — saying she's disposed to do X, with more adjectives and a shipyard. If that's all I've got, tell me, and tell me what I'd have to add to make it more than that.
