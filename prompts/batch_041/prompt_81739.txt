I teach a third-year decision theory module and I'm setting the final. Write me one essay question on Pascal's mugging that a student cant answer by reciting the standard mugger setup or by shrugging that the threat is obviously absurd. What I want them forced to confront is that the mugging is a schema and not a single case: whatever rule you adopt to turn the mugger down, there's a version built to break it — so make them choose a fix (a bound on utility, a floor on the probabilities you'll multiply, a restriction on which possibilities count as live, or biting the bullet) and defend it against the case aimed at that fix. I use two variants in class that they should have to bring in: the mugger who threatens a very large number of people with a small harm rather than one person with an absurd harm, and the point that the same tiny-probability-enormous-stakes reasoning is what a lot of people lean on for existential risk — I want to see whether they notice they can't have it both ways.

Then a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 with five must-hit points, the marks for each, and one line on the mistake that costs most students that point. Don't let the marks hang on naming who said what — the moves are what count. And in the model answer keep what's actually settled among people who work on this (that the mugger can always rescale, that naive expected-value reasoning tells you to pay) apart from what's genuinely contested (whether bounding utility is a live option or just a reductio, whether the mugging really carries over to existential-risk reasoning) — don't sell a contested line as the consensus.
