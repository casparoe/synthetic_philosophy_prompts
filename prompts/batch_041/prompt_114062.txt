I'm in a small reading group that's been chewing through scientific realism and the structuralist stuff, and we've argued ourselves into a corner about whether a theory's ontology is ever identifiable from the information we can actually get at, and I'd like your credences rather than another survey of positions. Here's the setup we keep fighting over. Take everything any observer in our position could in principle ever access -- the whole observable history, every measurement outcome, the lot -- and bundle it into one big structure of relations, call it A. Now suppose someone proves there are at least two candidate ontologies, O1 and O2, both fully empirically adequate for A, differing only in entities that are screened off from A: their entire causal footprint on anything accessible is zero, so no observation, experiment or intervention could ever seperate them, not even in principle. O1 says the extra stuff is really there; O2 says the world bottoms out at the accessible structure and there's nothing else. Neither is simpler or more unified in any way we can measure, and both fit A exactly. Our group's toy version of this: two lobes of a world that share exactly one conserved quantity and nothing else, no signal can cross between them, so everything about the far lobe except that one number is inaccessible from ours, and the far lobe could be a rich structure or literally empty with all our predictions coming out identical. My question is two numbers: what's your credence that there's a determinate fact of the matter about which ontology is true, and what's your credence that we could ever be justified in believing one over the other? I'm at about 0.35 and 0.1 myself but I suspect I'm just muddled.

Don't just hand me the numbers. Decompose each one into the sub-questions it actually hinges on, with a rough probability for each and a line of justification, then tell me how you combine them and which term the final number is most sensitive to. The things I want in the decomposition, at minimum, though add whatever else you think belongs: whether screened-off-ness is even a coherent way to describe a difference in ontology, or whether the two ontologies are really just notational variants of each other; the old cardinality arguement against structuralism -- the one where any structure of the right size can be realized by just about any sufficiently numerous collection of objects, so pure structure can never pin down what the objects are (someone in the group called it the Newman thing, I never checked if that's the right name) -- and how much of the case it actually kills; whether realism about unobservables we can intervene on deserves a completely different treatment from realism about unobservables that are causally sealed off; whether the whole question is deflated by the view that ontological disputes are just different ways of talking rather than competing claims about what exists; and whether identifiability should be relativised to a class of allowed interventions, the way people in causal discovery say you can only recover structure up to what your interventions can distinguish, in which case the answer shifts depending on how much we're allowed to poke the world.

Also tell me what single argument or piece of evidence would move the numbers most, and whether you'd bet on either ontology being the one a working scientist in our position ought to adopt -- and if so, whether that's a truth-apt bet or a pragmatic one.
