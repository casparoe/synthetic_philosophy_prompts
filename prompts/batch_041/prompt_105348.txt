I recently had to set priors for a diagnostic model that will be used in my own clinic, and the experience has turned the principle of indifference from an abstraction into a practical problem. Here are my verdicts on several cases. A fair die: each face gets 1/6, and I take this to be correct. A factory produces cubes whose edge length is uniformly distributed between 1 and 3 centimeters: I do not think there is a fact of the matter whether indifference should make the edge length or the volume uniform; either is a modeling choice. A chord of a circle: the three standard constructions yield 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, and I think none is uniquely correct. A coin with unknown bias: I am drawn to a uniform prior on the probability of heads, but a uniform prior on the odds feels equally defensible, so I cannot see that indifference selects either. A point chosen uniformly from a square: uniform over the square seems right, but I am unsure what indifference says about the distribution of its distance from the center. A real number chosen uniformly from the unit interval: I think the probability that it is rational is 0, and this seems determinate. A uniformly chosen integer: I think there is no such distribution, so there is no probability that it is even. Is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? I suspect the answer involves invariance under some group of transformations, but I want it stated precisely: which transformations, which measure, and what it implies for the coin prior and the square-distance case. If the principle forces me to abandon one of my verdicts, which one, and what does it predict for a case I have not mentioned?
