I am a third-year philosophy student and have been asked to lead a single seventy-five-minute meeting of a comparative-epistemology seminar: the session on Nyāya and the pramāṇas. The group is mixed. About half are analytics who have done a unit on justified true belief and the Gettier cases; the rest come from religious studies and have read some Buddhist material but no Nyāya. Nobody reads Sanskrit, and I have no wish to turn this into a survey of who accepts how many pramāṇas. I do not want the pramāṇas explained to me. I want a plan for how I should explain them, organised around the question I keep getting stuck on: whether the four are four independent instruments, each with its own domain, or four descriptions of the single process by which one cognition comes to be warranted. My instinct is that this is the hinge of the whole session, and I would like the plan to make the group feel that question as a real one rather than a piece of scholarly bookkeeping.

What I need from you:

1. The order in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit. My hunch is that the means/result/knower distinction has to come before the list of four, and that inference cannot be broached before some account of pervasion is in place. Tell me whether the hunch is wrong, and if so what actually has to come first and what each later piece is waiting on.
2. The one example that carries the session. I have been toying with a single afternoon: a neighbour tells me my rear tyre is flat (testimony), I go out and look (perception), I hear the hiss and infer the puncture (inference), and I only grasp what the mechanic means by "run-flat" because I have handled ordinary tyres and can see what is missing (comparison). Assess that candidate: what would it have to have and lack to do the work, whether the textbook wild-ox case should be the spine or demoted to a footnote, and whether my scenario is even the right kind of case, given that on the Nyāya account comparison seems to deliver knowledge of a word's relation to a thing rather than of a fact about the world. I want the plan to face that, not paper over it.
3. The misconceptions I should expect, in the order the group will actually form them. I predict: "a pramāṇa is just a source of belief, so this is reliabilism with Sanskrit labels"; "testimony is hearsay, hence the weak one"; "inference must be deduction"; and "comparison is an embarrassing leftover kept only by tradition". Add the ones I am not seeing, and note any that follow from the mix of backgrounds rather than from the material.
4. For each misconception, the exact point in the sequence where it has to be headed off, and why there rather than earlier or later.
5. One question to put to the group at the end that would separate "they can list four pramāṇas" from "they see what the list is for".
6. What I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how I should say so out loud.

Format: open with the single sentence I could write on the board to fix what a pramāṇa is, then the plan as a numbered sequence keyed to the dependencies, then a short table pairing each misconception with the step at which I kill it. Hard limit: 700 words.
