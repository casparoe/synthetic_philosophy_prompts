I'm in a graduate seminar on the governance of emerging technologies, and this week we were assigned John von Neumann's June 1955 Fortune essay on whether we can survive technology. I expected a period piece about the bomb and instead got an argument whose shape I can't quite bring into focus. As I reconstruct it, von Neumann's central claim is that the coming crisis is not produced by any particular technology or by anyone's blunder, but is inherent in two relations at once — technology's relation to geography and its relation to political organization. His picture seems to be that the industrial revolution had been carried along by a kind of safety factor: an ever-widening geographical scope for technological activity combined with an ever-widening political integration, so that the tensions thrown up by progress could be absorbed by an expanding frame. That frame has a hard boundary, the actual size of the earth, and we are now pressing against it. The step I find genuinely puzzling sits in the middle of the argument. He says that the rising speed of technological operations did not shorten the time required for processes but instead enlarged the size of the units — political, economic, organizational — affected by them, because the relevant time scales are fixed by human reaction times, habits, and physiology, so that faster technology gets spent on doing larger things in the same time rather than the same things faster. And then there is his further thought that the environment for progress has become not only undersized but underorganized, which I take to gesture at a mismatch between the scale at which problems now arise and the scale at which decisions are actually made.

What I want is an explanation pitched at the level of the argument rather than the historical illustrations: what exactly the safety factor is doing in this argument; why the finiteness of the planet, rather than any specific danger, is supposed to be the source of the crisis; why the speed-enlarges-units step is needed at all rather than being a digression; what "underorganized" adds beyond "undersized"; and what the argument implies would count as genuinely resolving the crisis rather than merely postponing it. Treat the essay as an argument to be reconstructed and assessed, not as a prediction to be graded, and where the argument has a gap or trades on an ambiguity, say so rather than smoothing it over.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
