Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on no-trade theorems and I think I've talked myself into a position I want you to try hard to break, because right now it feels unassailable in a way that makes me suspicious of it.

Here's the position. The Milgrom-Stokey result says that if traders share a common prior and the info-acquisition structure is common knowledge, then no speculative trade happens -- any trade you actually observe has to be explained by something non-epistemic (endowments, risk tolerance, hedging, liquidity) or by a difference in priors. Aumann's agreement theorem is the same theme from the other side: agents with a common prior who commonly know each other's posteriors can't disagree. I want to read these as an epistemological thesis about real markets, roughly: in a reasonably liquid market, watching someone take the other side of my bet is almost never evidence that they know something I dont. If they knew something and I knew they knew that I knew, the trade would be off. So the info content of an observed trade is carried almost entirely by the possibility that our priors differ, or that they have non-epistemic reasons to trade -- and differing priors isn't information, it's just a different starting point. Which means: when I see volume and a price move, my default should be risk-sharing / disagreement / noise, not "somebody knows something," and "the market disagrees with me" should barely move me when I think I've got genuine private info. The market price is closer to the common prior than to a summary of what everyone knows.

Now: what would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones the position's own defenders would have to concede.

Some things I specifically want you to address: (1) how much of this is just the common-prior assumption doing all the work, and whether "heterogeneous priors" is a legitimate epistemic state or just a euphemism for one party being wrong; (2) what happens when you relax common knowledge to approximate common knowledge -- does the conclusion survive in any usable form or is it knife-edge; (3) whether informed trading and adverse selection (lemons-type markets) actually refute the epistemic reading or are consistent with it; (4) the argument that if prices were fully revealing then nobody would bother gathering info, so equilibrium prices have to be only partially revealing -- does that rescue the market as a source of evidence or undercut my claim; (5) whether I'm conflating a theorem about speculative trade with a claim about information transmission through prices, since those seem to come apart. And finally, tell me straight whether I've insulated this into unfalsifiability -- like if every counterexample just gets waved away with "well, your priors differed," is that a real problem for the view or is it just what the theorem says?
