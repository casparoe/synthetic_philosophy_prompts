I'm a retired experimental physicist — forty years of laser spectroscopy, mostly — and since I shut the lab down I've been teaching myself philosophy of physics. I've hit a wall on Bell's theorem and I want a number, not another tour of the landscape.

Here's the claim, as tight as I can make it:

(P) Nature contains causal influences linking spacelike-seperated events that are not carried by anything propagating through spacetime at or below c — the correlations Bell-type inequalities forbid for locally caused theories really are produced by superluminal goings-on, and by nothing else.

Ten years ago I'd have said 0.9; every popular account told me Bell had proved the world is nonlocal and to get over it. Now I'm at maybe 0.45, and I think the drop is just confusion, because every time I think I have the full list of escape routes someone hands me another one. What I want:

1. Your number for (P).

2. The decomposition. In particular, Bell's 'locality' seems to come apart into two assumptions — one that the detector settings are statistically independent of the state the pair is in before measurement, and one that the two outcomes are independent of each other once settings and that pre-measurement state are fixed. As far as I can work out, quantum mechanics violates only the second, and the second is the one that can't be exploited to send a signal. So: your credence that the real diagnosis is a failure of the first (the settings are conspiritorially correlated with the state — the superdeterminism option); that a failure of the second earns the word 'nonlocal' at all; that the influence runs backwards in time instead (retrocausality); and that there's no pre-measurement state for the correlation to be about, so the question is malformed (QBism, if I've got them right). Numbers for each, and tell me if they don't sum to one and why.

3. Which sub-number the total actually hinges on — if I could settle exactly one of them tomorrow, which moves (P) most?

4. A separate itch I can't scratch: Bell's theorem is a theorem about theories, not about the world. If every theory that fits the data has to be nonlocal, does that make the world nonlocal, or only our best descriptions of it? Does that distinction shift your number, and by how much?

5. What would move you. Is there an experiment left to run — a better Bell test, a loophole still open, something cosmological — or is this the sort of thing no experiment can touch, because every reading of the formalism is empirically equivalent by construction? (Somebody also told me that if I'm genuinely free to choose the settings then the particles must be free too, and I can't tell whether that's deep or a pun. If it bears on (P), say how.)

Two things I'd ask you not to do. Don't tell me it all depends on what I mean by 'nonlocal' and stop there — I know it depends on that. I want to see exactly where the dependence bites, and what you'd have to believe for the number to go to 0.9 or down to 0.1. And don't send me a reading list; there's already a stack of books on my floor and I'm 71.
