I'm a retired philosophy professor, and I've been asked to give the address at our town's Remembrance Day ceremony — the one at the cenotaph, with the Legion, the cadets, a few hundred locals in heavy coats, and my old department chair in the front row pretending not to know me. The trouble is I've been a Hegelian about war for thirty years and I still dont know how to say what I actually think in a room like that without either lying or getting run out of town. What I think, roughly: war isn't just an atrocity that interrupts an otherwise normal peace. For Hegel it's a moment in the life of a state — the occasion on which people show that all the particular things they care about, property and comfort and even their own lives, are subordinate to something they hold in common, and it's that willingness to stake yourself that makes a political community actual rather than just a crowd of people minding their own business. And then the bit everyone hates: he thinks peace is what lets a people go stagnant, that war clears the air the way wind keeps a sea from going foul. That's a genuinely hard thing to say at a cenotaph, because half the room wants to hear that they gave their lives so we could be free, and the other half wants to hear that war is hell and we should mourn and never do it again, and I think both of those are false, in different ways.

So please write me the address. Something like 700–800 words, spoken register, no footnotes, no reading list. I want the Hegelian structure to be doing the real work — the ideality of the particular, sacrifice as what actualises the state, the thought that a state is partly constituted by its readiness to risk itself, and the refusal to treat peace as the highest good — but it has to land for the veterans and the families who didn't sign up for a lecture. Here's the wrinkle, and I want you to feel it rather than smooth it over: the war this cenotaph is about is one I think was wrong, a stupid imperial adventure that nobody should have faught. So the speech can't quietly sanctify it, and it also can't resolve the tension by turning the dead into victims of history, because I think that quietly strips them of agency and is its own kind of lie. And the cadets are sitting in the front row in uniform, so I can't just be the old man who's against everything.

One more thing I'd like you to try, if you can do it without wrecking the tone. I've always thought the Hegelian structure has a hole in it where remote, riskless killing goes — that if what makes the sacrifice ethical is the actual staking of your own life, then a war fought by people sitting safely in a trailer an ocean away is doing something the concept of war can't quite contain, and I'd like one clean sentence somewhere that lets that idea surface. But the priority is the room: by the end, someone who came in expecting either a patriotic speech or a pacifist one should feel why a person could hold all of this at once without being a monster, and I'd honestly rather the speech end unresolved than falsely comforted.
