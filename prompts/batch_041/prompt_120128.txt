I run a fortnightly quantum foundations reading group that has drifted into being a mixed seminar: fourteen people, mostly graduate students in physics and philosophy with a few interested outsiders, ninety minutes together, and a room with a whiteboard, a projector, and nothing that could be called laboratory equipment. We have worked through the standard material on Bell's theorem and I have discovered that everyone can recite the conclusion — no local hidden-variable theory — while almost nobody has felt what the theorem actually forbids. They treat it as a claim about spooky influence at a distance, or about measurement disturbing the system, and when I press them on why an arbitrarily clever shared plan cannot rescue the correlations they go vague and start gesturing at the experimental literature. I would like an activity that repairs this, and I would like the participants to walk into the wall themselves rather than be shown where it is.

Here is the shape I have in mind, though I want you to design the details. The group should be split into pairs acting as two separated agents who answer one-bit questions posed by a referee, where the pair wins the round just in case a specified parity relation holds between their two answers; the arrangement should be such that any single pair of answers looks perfectly innocent while the four families of correlations cannot be produced by any one pre-agreed plan. The pairs may meet beforehand and agree on any strategy whatsoever, shared randomisation included, but may not communicate once play begins. I want them to spend the first twenty minutes cheerfully winning roughly three quarters of the rounds and then to discover, when they push against that ceiling, that it is not a ceiling imposed by their own ingenuity. Ideally they reach the counting argument on their own — that every deterministic plan must fail on at least one of the four question combinations — and I would like the activity to stage the two neighbouring tiers as well, so that the surrounding geography becomes visible: a variant in which the pair is permitted to signal, where a perfect score is trivially available, and a variant in which the pair is handed a hypothetical box whose correlations are stronger than any quantum-mechanical ones while still incapable of transmitting a message. Give me the rules, the materials (I would rather use cards and dice than anything electronic), the scoring scheme, a precise timeline for the ninety minutes, and the exact moment at which the facilitator should stop talking and let the group sit with the failure.

The debrief is the part I care about most. Map each stage of the game onto a piece of the theorem: the pre-agreed plan onto the assumption of pre-existing values, the inability to exceed three quarters onto the inequality itself, the signalling variant onto what the theorem does not say, the stronger-than-quantum box onto the fact that a no-signalling principle alone does not single out the quantum correlations, and the referee's random questions onto the assumption the theorem needs but cannot prove. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect pairs to leak information through the room, to mistake shared randomness for a loophole, to think they have found a winning strategy when they have merely been lucky, to accuse me of running the game unfairly, and to conclude that the exercise was about communication rather than about the impossibility of a joint distribution for four observables that can never be measured together — and how to handle each failure without giving the answer away. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: it can show that no classical strategy reaches the quantum value, but it cannot show that anything in nature reaches it, so I need to know exactly what the facilitator must supply after the game, in what order, and how to do so without collapsing back into the lecture that the activity was meant to replace.
