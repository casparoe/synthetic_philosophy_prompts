I have spent most of a seminar this term arguing with two friends about whether I could have had a different origin, and we keep reaching the same stalemate: we agree about every concrete case and disagree about what the cases add up to. I would like you to tell me what principle, or small family of principles, my verdicts actually commit me to, and where they conflict, which verdict I should give up. Here is what I think. (1) Had a different sperm fertilized the egg my mother released that night, the resulting child would have been a sibling of mine, not me. (2) Had the same sperm met a different egg, likewise: not me. (3) Had that very fertilized egg — my sperm and my egg — been flushed from my mother's body at the eight-cell stage and implanted in another woman, who carried and bore me, that would still have been me; who gestated me, and where I was born, strike me as incidental. (4) Had my parents conceived me by IVF and left the embryo frozen for a decade, that too would have been me, merely born later. (5) Had someone taken a skin cell from me and grown a clone with my exact nuclear DNA — no sperm, no egg, different mitochondria — the clone would not have been me; a copy, or a very late twin, at most. (6) And the case I cannot settle: had that same fertilized egg, my sperm and my egg, split at the two-cell stage and had both halves developed, so that I had an identical twin, would I have existed, and as what — as one of the twins, as both, or as neither?

My friends infer from (1) and (2) that I am essentially the product of one particular sperm and one particular egg, and from (3) and (4) that nothing which befalls the embryo after fertilization belongs to my essence. That already sits oddly with (5), where the genetic material is held fixed and yet I deny that the resulting individual is me. And (6) seems to break the approach from the other side: the single fusion of that sperm and that egg does not by itself pick out one person, since it can be followed by two. I keep wanting to say that in (6) I simply would not have existed — that the brief single cell was a stage of me which ended when it divided — but I can also feel the pull of saying I would have been one of the twins, and nothing in (1) through (5) seems to decide it. What makes me most uneasy is a case I have not listed: had that same two-cell embryo been split and only one half implanted, with the other discarded, I would have no hesitation in saying the surviving half was me; yet it is hard to see how the mere existence of the other half could determine whether I survive.

Could you set out the principle or principles that best fit my verdicts, stating each precisely and identifying for each clause the case that forces it; then say what the principle predicts for two cases I have left open — whether I could have existed with a different mitochondrial genome, and the split-and-discard case just described — and be candid about which of my six verdicts it cannot keep, and why I should give that one up?
