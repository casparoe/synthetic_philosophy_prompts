I'm writing a paper on Turing's philosophy of machine learning and want to check my reading before I commit to it. Here is what I hold. (1) Turing's 1948 unorganised machines are an early connectionist proposal: intelligence emerges from the self-organization of initially random networks under selection pressure, not from explicit rule-programming. (2) His 1950 child machine is continuous with this, except that the learning mechanism is now education, understood as the systematic application of rewards and punishments. (3) Turing places real weight on initiative in machines and warns that excessive discipline produces limited intelligence. (4) He treats the problem of machine intelligence as primarily an engineering problem rather than a philosophical one. (5) His account of learning is behaviourist in spirit: what matters is the shaping of input-output relations, not the internal representation of rules.

I suspect these hang together, but I'm not confident. (2) and (5) seem to leave no room for (3): if learning is reward-and-punishment conditioning, where does initiative come from? And (1) and (2) may be describing two different mechanisms of learning — structural self-organization versus behavioural conditioning — that Turing never reconciles. Can you tell me which of these are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what the cheapest revision would be if something has to give?
