I'm a science journalist, and I foolishly agreed to guest-grade a stack of essays for a friend's history-of-science seminar — its finals week, bad idea, I'm alot behind. The question was whether Newton's private religion actually did any work in his physics, and I've pasted the exam question and one student's answer below. I keep flip-flopping on what this deserves, so grade it for me. Give it a letter grade, then go through it point by point — what's solid, what's actually wrong, what's just vague — so I can defend the mark if the kid pushes back. Don't just hand me a vibe; name the specific claims.

EXAM QUESTION:
"Newton published almost none of his theology, yet historians insist it shaped his natural philosophy. In two or three paragraphs: (a) describe what Newton's private theological views actually held, and (b) assess this claim: 'Strip away the church politics and Newton's God is the deist's God — a clockmaker who built the machine and walked away.'"

STUDENT'S ANSWER:
"Newton's God is not the deist's absentee watchmaker. The deists wanted a creator who set the world going and then left it alone, but Newton kept hauling God back into the machinery. In Query 31 of the Opticks he argues that the regular motions of the planets could never have arisen from blind fate, and that the system tends to run down and needs an occasional reformation — which is exactly why Leibniz accused him of making God a shoddy clockmaker who has to keep winding his watch. So Newton's God is constantly active and present everywhere, and the world depends on him at every moment. That's the opposite of deism, and Newton says so in the General Scholium he added to the second edition of the Principia, where he insists God governs all things by his will and dominion, not as a mere soul of the world.

On the private theology: Newton was a committed anti-Trinitarian. He thought the Trinity was a fourth-century corruption foisted on the church by Athanasius, and he spent years on church history trying to prove it. He was, in the standard term, a Socinian — he held that Christ was not God but a uniquely exalted man and prophet. He also rejected the immortality of the soul. Because denying the Trinity was still a capital offense in England, he kept all of this quiet, and he never let the anti-Trinitarian manuscript he sent to Locke see print.

So the claim in (b) is wrong about God's relation to the world but right that Newton's physics can't be cleanly separated from his religion — the same God who governs the cosmos by his will is the God whose worship Newton thought the church had corrupted. Where I think the prompt oversells it is the suggestion that the physics was just a vehicle for the theology: Newton's mechanics stands or falls on its own and doesn't need the theology to work."
