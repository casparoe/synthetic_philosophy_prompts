Retired now after a career in experimental biology, I have spent the past two years teaching myself moral philosophy, and I have reached the stage where I would rather have a principle than a reading list. The question I keep circling is moral worth: what distinguishes an action that is merely right, or useful, from one that reflects well on the person who performed it — what is it, exactly, to act for the right reasons?

I have been working through cases and recording my honest reactions to them. Here they are.

1. A man wades into a cold river and pulls out a drowning stranger. He takes no pleasure in it; he finds the stranger repellent, resents the interruption, and feels only irritation throughout. He does it solely because he takes it to be required of him. My reaction: the act has moral worth; he acted for the right reasons. This is the verdict I hold most loosely, but it is my verdict.

2. A second man does the same thing purely from a spontaneous rush of pity, without ever considering what is required of him. My reaction: this also has moral worth, and I cannot see why the first man should receive credit that the second does not.

3. A third man pulls the stranger out because he recognizes him as wealthy and hopes for a reward. My reaction: no moral worth at all. The outcome is fortunate; the reason is bad.

4. A fourth man pulls the stranger out both because he takes it to be required and because he wants the reward; either motive would have sufficed on its own. My reaction: I want to say the act retains some worth but that the reward taints it, and I reverse myself every time I think it through. I cannot settle this one.

5. A woman raised in a community that taught her it is gravely wrong to aid members of a certain group nonetheless hides such a person from a mob, out of simple compassion for the person. She believes she is doing something forbidden. My reaction: this is the most admirable case of the lot, better than the men in (1) and (2), even though by her own lights she is sinning.

6. A woman gives a large sum to a charity that turns out to be a fraud, out of genuine compassion for the people she believes it serves. My reaction: her act was not right — it did harm — yet she strikes me as no less creditable than anyone else here.

7. A man refrains from cheating on his taxes only because he fears an audit. My reaction: he does the right thing, and it has no moral worth.

8. A man refrains from cheating because he holds honesty to be required of him, and would not cheat even if audits did not exist. My reaction: moral worth.

Here is the tentative principle I have been working with. I am a scientist, and what I reach for is a control experiment. To ask whether someone acted for the right reasons, I propose to vary the motive while holding the situation fixed, and to ask whether the consideration that made the action right was doing any work in producing it. Call a reason load-bearing when, had that consideration not obtained while everything else stayed the same, the agent would not have acted as she did. The principle would then be: an action has moral worth just in case the right-making consideration was load-bearing in this sense.

The principle handles (1), (2), (5), (7) and (8) as I would like. It stumbles on (6), where no right-making consideration obtained at all, since the charity was a fraud, and yet I want to credit her. And it issues no verdict on (4), where each motive is load-bearing on its own and the test returns two answers at once.

What I would like from you is not a survey of the literature — I do not care who said what — but an answer on the merits. State the principle, or the small set of competing principles, as precisely as you can; for each clause, identify the case that forces it; tell me what your preferred principle implies about the two cases I could not settle and about two further cases you devise yourself; and tell me honestly which of my eight verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I should give that one up.
