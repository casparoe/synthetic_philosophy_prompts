I have been trying to disentangle two things that I keep conflating under the single phrase the value of information, and I would like you to compare them and tell me precisely where they come apart.

The first is a result in decision theory usually credited to I. J. Good. The setting is an agent who must choose among acts, who represents her uncertainty as probabilities, who updates by conditionalization on any evidence she receives, and who will then choose whichever act maximizes her posterior expected utility. Suppose the evidence is free, arrives before she must choose, and does not alter either her preferences over outcomes or the set of acts available to her. Then the expected utility of the strategy of receiving the evidence and then choosing is never lower than the expected utility of choosing now, on the information she already has; the value of information, measured this way, cannot be negative. The reason, as I understand it, is that the informed agent can always do whatever the uninformed agent would have done, so the informed strategy dominates the uninformed one in expectation. I am confident about the shape of the argument; what I want is to see how much of the phenomenon of the value of information it actually captures.

The second is the ordinary and, I gather, well-documented tendency of people to prefer not to be informed. A patient declines a test whose result he cannot act on; a man avoids opening a letter he is nearly certain contains bad news; someone asks a doctor not to tell him the details of a diagnosis he already has. These people are not, on the face of it, making an arithmetic error, and it is not obvious that their preference is irrational; something in them prefers the state of not knowing. Call this, loosely, the preference for ignorance.

What I want from you is a comparison of these two pictures. Are they in genuine conflict, or do they measure different quantities under one name? In particular I would like you to separate, as cleanly as you can, the senses of value at work on each side: value computed before the fact from the agent's prior versus value as it is realized after the fact; the instrumental value of a decision versus the value of the state of knowing itself; the value of information to the agent who will act on it versus to the agent who cannot or will not. Then tell me which of the theorem's assumptions the preference for ignorance actually violates, whether the cases turn on the cost of the information, on the agent not being a Bayesian, on her preferences or options shifting once she knows, on her inability to commit to ignoring what she has learned, or on something else entirely. I am especially interested in whether the theorem's escape clause, that the informed agent may always act as if she had never learned, is available to the kind of agent the second picture describes, or whether knowing something you cannot un-know forecloses that option.

Finally, in the spirit of the comparison: in which ways is the decision-theoretic notion of the value of information special or narrow, and is there any defensible sense in which ignorance is valuable that is not merely the reverse image of the theorem's assumptions? I would rather have a careful comparison that shows me where the two pictures touch and where they do not than a verdict about which one is correct.

Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a long letter to someone who has asked you a question.
