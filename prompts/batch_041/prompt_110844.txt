Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on consent for something I'm writing and there's a pattern I can't stop noticing, and I want to know what it adds up to.

In a hospital you sign a form before anything happens. Someone has to walk you through the risks, and if they don't, your signature basically doesn't count for much. But you can be in agony, you can have no good option, you can be told it's this or you die — and none of that stops it from being valid consent. Nobody treats the fact that you had no acceptable alternative as a problem.

Then in a police stop: "mind if I take a look in your car?" and in alot of places the officer isn't obliged to mention that you're allowed to say no. Courts look at the "totality of the circumstances" and usually call it voluntary.

Then sex. Lying about some things voids consent, lying about others doesn't, and the line people draw is usually whether the lie went to the nature of the act itself. Lie about being sterile, lie about loving someone, lie about what happens to the condom — a lot of jurisdictions shrug. Lie about what act you're doing and it's a serious crime.

Then contracts: a deliberate material lie gets you out of the whole deal.

So: what theory of consent does this patchwork actually presuppose? I don't want a survey of the literature, I want the implicit theory dug out. Specifically: does the practice commit to a moralized view of coercion — a threat only counts as coercing you if it was wrong for them to make it — as opposed to a plain "you had no acceptable alternative" view? Does it treat consent as an attitude in someone's head or as an act that authorizes and waives? Is voluntariness a threshhold you either cross or don't, or a matter of degree? And which parts of the pattern are strictly forced by those commitments and which are just things that happen to be compatible with them?

Then tell me where the practice is underdetermined — several theories license the same behaviour — and what would have to change in each of those four settings if we swapped in a reasonable-alternatives account of voluntariness instead.
