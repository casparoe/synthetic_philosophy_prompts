I am a novelist at work on a book about first contact, and for the past several weeks I have been rereading Stanisław Lem's Solaris as a way of testing my own assumptions against a story that refuses to console them. What I would like from you is a critical essay: a reading of the novel through the philosophical problem of what it would take to recognize a mind on the far side of an interstellar distance. Not the sociology of the search for extraterrestrial intelligence and not a survey of astrobiology, but the epistemology of contact — what could count as evidence that something out there thinks, and what would count as understanding it.

Solaris, as I read it, gives us a planet-wide ocean that is very likely intelligent, a century of "Solaristics" that has produced little more than a taxonomy of its shapes and a literature that has quietly died, and a set of visitors the ocean appears to lift out of the crew's own memories — for the novel's narrator, a psychologist named Kelvin, the figure of his dead wife. No one on the station can say whether these visitors are messages, experiments, tortures, or gifts, and the ocean never once tells anyone anything about itself.

I want the essay organized around a tension between two positions, and I want you to say which one the novel actually supports. The first is the view I associate with the principle of charity in radical interpretation: that we could not so much as count something as having beliefs or a language unless we could in principle translate it, and that radical incommensurability between minds is therefore not a genuine possibility but the artifact of our own failure of imagination. The second is the view I take Lem to be dramatizing: that intelligence may come in forms whose thoughts are not merely untranslated but untranslatable, and that our insistence on charity as the price of admission to mindedness is itself a piece of anthropocentrism — a demand that the universe be legible to us before we will grant that it is inhabited.

Note, though, that contact of a sort does occur. The ocean evidently reads Kelvin, and it answers him with his own past; it mirrors rather than replies. Is that contact, or only the sound of our own voice coming back? Does the ocean refute the charity view, or confirm it in a way that ought to embarrass us?

Three further threads, if they earn their place. First, the novel never settles whether the ocean is a mind at all; it withholds the confirmation that would make "contact" a well-defined goal, and I want the essay to say what that refusal costs and what it buys. Second, the visitors raise the question of moral status: they are manufactured, made of the ocean's own substance, and at least one of them becomes a person by any test the crew can administer. The crew disagrees about whether such visitors may simply be destroyed as simulacra, and Kelvin cannot bring himself to. I would like to know whether the novel thinks the question of their personhood is answerable, and what it means that the crew's answer seems to track what they can bear to do rather than what they can establish. Third, the book keeps circling a creator who is powerful but not wise, making things it does not understand. If greater intelligence does not bring greater comprehension, what becomes of the assumption that a more advanced civilization could explain itself to us if it wished to?

Keep the reading faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a thesis, and do not bend the philosophy to make the novel look prescient. Where the book resists the reading — where the failure of contact looks like a failure of Lem's characters rather than a fact about minds, or where melancholy is doing work that an argument ought to do — say so plainly.

End with a verdict: what does Solaris establish about the possibility of knowing an alien mind that the philosophy of interpretation does not, and where does it merely stage a conclusion the philosophy had already reached? No bibliography is needed; I will take the plot as given, and I care about the argument rather than the citations. Write in American English.
