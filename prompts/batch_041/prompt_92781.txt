I am a statistician who works on Bayesian inference and robust estimation, and I am comfortable with the standard apparatus: a probability space over an algebra of propositions, priors and likelihoods, conditionalization as the update rule, posteriors, model comparison, and the machinery of contamination models and outlier-resistant estimators. I also habitually think of a probability-one prior as an absorbing state — once the mass sits there, no observation can move it.

I would like you to take a puzzle from epistemology — the one usually called the dogmatism paradox — and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in that vocabulary. As I understand the puzzle, it runs roughly as follows: if I know some proposition P, then I also know that any evidence that later turns up against P is misleading evidence, since P is true and evidence pointing away from P must be leading me astray; and if I know that the evidence against P is misleading, I have a reason to disregard it. The conclusion is that knowing P licenses me to ignore any future evidence against P, which is absurd. I want to see what this argument looks like once "knowledge," "evidence," "misleading evidence," and "having a reason to disregard" are all translated into my terms.

Be as precise and as honest as you can. In particular, I want a residue report, and I expect the residue to be the interesting part:

- Which steps of the paradox translate cleanly? For instance, does "evidence against P" become an observation with a low likelihood under P, and does "misleading evidence" become something I can actually define — perhaps as an observation that would lower my posterior in P even though P is true? Does "knowing P" become a degenerate prior, or a posterior of 1, and is that a translation or a distortion?
- Where does the translation force me to say something I would not otherwise say? Conditionalization is mandatory in my framework — there is no update rule that lets me simply refuse a likelihood — so if the dogmatist's move is "disregard the evidence," I need to know whether that is expressible as anything other than a mistake, and whether calling it a mistake is faithful to the puzzle or just my framework talking.
- What has no counterpart at all? I suspect at least three candidates: the first-personal, forward-looking structure of the paradox (it concerns evidence that has not yet arrived, and what I may do now); the distinction between justified and unjustified certainty (my absorbing state cannot tell those apart); and the notion of an epistemic permission or entitlement, as opposed to a posterior or a decision rule. Tell me whether I am right about these, and whether the higher-order move — treating "I know the evidence is misleading" as itself something to conditionalize on — dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it. I have the reflection principle in mind here, and I want to know whether it is doing real work or papering over a gap.
- Finally, the verdict I actually care about: is the residue a defect of my framework — a place where the Bayesian apparatus is simply blind, say to the difference between certainty and knowledge, or to the temporal asymmetry between evidence past and evidence future — or is it a sign that the paradox's grip depended on a confusion that my vocabulary, by refusing to make certain distinctions, quietly exposes? Do not settle this by assuming the Bayesian answer; tell me which parts of the original puzzle survive as genuine problems and which evaporate, and say plainly which of the two you think the evaporation shows.

No citations or reading lists, please — I want the translation and the residue, not pointers.
