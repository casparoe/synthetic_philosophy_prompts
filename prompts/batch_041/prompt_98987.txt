I have no formal training in philosophy, probability, or economics, but I have been working through Aumann's agreement theorem on my own, and I have gotten stuck on a disagreement between two friends. I would like you to act as an expert in formal epistemology and game theory, think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the different ways the theorem can be interpreted and the conditions it actually requires. Then adjudicate the exchange below move by move.

Aumann's theorem, as I understand it, says roughly this: if two agents are Bayesian rational, share the same prior probabilities, and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, then their posteriors must be equal. It does not say that any two people who share evidence must agree, and it does not say that every disagreement is irrational. My friends Ravi and Lena both know this much.

Ravi and Lena are trying to forecast whether a local referendum will pass. They have read the same polls, the same economic reports, and the same local news. They have also told each other their reasoning and their final probabilities: Ravi says 70 percent, Lena says 40 percent. Neither is trying to deceive the other, and each regards the other as generally intelligent and sincere.

Their exchange goes like this.

Ravi: If we are both rational and we have shared all the evidence, we cannot really disagree. Aumann's theorem says common knowledge of our disagreement is impossible under those conditions. So either one of us is being irrational, or we do not actually have common knowledge of each other's reasoning.

Lena: That is too quick. The theorem assumes a common prior. We did not start from the same prior. I grew up in a place where the polls systematically undercounted a certain kind of voter, and Ravi grew up in a place where they overcounted that kind of voter. Those experiences shaped our priors. Different priors, same evidence, different posteriors—no contradiction.

Ravi: But we have now told each other about our priors. I know your prior and you know mine. If we are both rational, should we not now be able to compute what a rational agent with our combined information would believe? And if we can, why do we not both move to that number? If we do not, it seems we are each saying the other is making a mistake.

Lena: Because we do not just have different priors; we also have private evidence about how reliable our own priors are. I know things about my hometown's polling history that you do not know, and you know things about yours that I do not know. And even if we shared all of that, the theorem still requires common knowledge of the priors themselves, not just mutual knowledge. You knowing my prior and me knowing yours is not the same as each of us knowing that the other knows, and so on indefinitely. That common-knowledge condition is doing real work.

Ravi: Then the theorem is almost empty as a practical tool. Any two people can always say, different priors, or not quite common knowledge, and walk away. It can never tell anyone that they are being unreasonable.

Lena: It is not empty. It tells you where to look. If two people are rational Bayesians and genuinely share a prior and have common knowledge, then disagreement has to trace back to a failure of common knowledge or a failure of rationality. In our case, it traces back to different priors and private evidence. But there is a further point: the fact that you disagree with me is itself evidence. Since I think you are probably rational and probably have some information I lack, your 70 percent should push my 40 percent upward, and my 40 percent should push your 70 percent downward. We should not fully converge, because we are not certain about each other's priors and rationality, but we should not ignore each other either.

Ravi: But that is circular. If I raise my probability just because you disagree, I am treating your disagreement as evidence about the referendum. But your disagreement is just a report of your private reasoning. If I already think your reasoning is flawed because of your prior, why should I let it move me? And if I do let it move me, why stop at 55 percent instead of going all the way to 40 percent?

Lena: It is not circular; it is Bayesian. Your belief about the referendum should depend on your belief about my reliability and about what my disagreement tells you about the evidence I might have. And we stop short of full convergence because neither of us is certain that the other is more reliable. If I became certain that you were perfectly rational and had all my evidence plus more, I should just adopt your probability. Since I am not certain, I should move partway.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Ravi's and Lena's turns, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and explain briefly what is correct or incorrect in it. Then answer these broader questions:

1. What exactly does Aumann's theorem require, and which of those requirements are missing in Ravi and Lena's case?
2. Is Ravi right that the theorem is nearly empty in practice, or is Lena right that it still constrains what a rational disagreement can look like?
3. Is Lena right that disagreement itself should move each of them partway, or is Ravi right that this is bootstrapping?
4. If they both know they have different priors and both know the other is rational, does that make their disagreement common knowledge in the relevant sense?

Is Maya's addition a legitimate extension of Aumann's framework, a confusion of it, or a separate principle that needs its own justification?
