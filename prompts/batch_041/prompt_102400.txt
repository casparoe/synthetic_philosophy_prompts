I chair the trustee board of a small occupational pension scheme, about 40,000 members, and I have to bring a written opinion to our meeting on the 14th. The platform we use has offered members a new feature: they can move money between our two sub-funds -- a growth fund and a capital-protection fund -- as often as they like, each switch costing a flat £5. The platform's own data, and annoyingly our actuary's too, shows that the typical member who uses it ends up several hundred pounds worse off over three years than an otherwise identical member who never touches it, because people tend to jump into protection right after a drop and back into growth right after a rally, and the £5s pile up. The funny thing is that each individual switch, judged by what that member wanted at the moment they made it, looks like it served them -- I think this is what philosophers call a money pump. Our actuary has now started reporting a 'regret-adjusted return', which scores every switch against what that same member would say they wanted twelve months later, and by that measure most switches are mistakes. But some members use the feature sensibly and come out ahead, and they're furious that we might take it away.

I don't want you guessing what our regulator would say; I want the board's actual opinion. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion, in British English. Across the five, cover: the recommendation itself (approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- pick one and be concrete), the main reason for it, the strongest case for the other side and why it doesn't win, which standard we should be useing to decide whether a member is better or worse off (their preferences at the time of switching, their preferences at the end, their calm or considered preferences, or something else entirely), and any conditions plus a time frame. No citations, just argue it.
