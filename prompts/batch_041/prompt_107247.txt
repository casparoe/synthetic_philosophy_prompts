My reading group has been working through the philosophy-of-biology literature on whether 'life' names a natural kind or is merely a convenient cluster of features, and last week a friend circulated the following argument. He believes it settles the matter and shows that the long search for a definition was misconceived from the start. I find the conclusion too strong to accept, yet each step looks defensible on its own and I cannot locate the trouble. Here it is as he wrote it.

(1) For 'life' to be a natural kind, there must be some property, or some determinate set of properties, that is shared by all and only living things and that explains why they share their further features. (2) No such property exists. Every candidate fails at the edges: metabolism is absent from dormant spores and from viruses, and present in a candle flame and in the autocatalytic chemical network he keeps describing, which sustains itself in a flow reactor and drifts in composition over months but carries no genome; replication is absent from mules and from sterile worker castes, and present in a self-copying stretch of RNA in a test tube and in prions, which template their own misfolding without any nucleic acid; evolution by natural selection has been claimed for crystal lineages and for lineages of computer code. (3) Therefore 'life' is not a natural kind. (4) If 'life' is not a natural kind, then there is no fact of the matter as to whether any particular borderline thing is alive, and calling a virus, a prion, or a frozen embryo 'alive' is only a choice of classificatory convenience. (5) So the question whether viruses are alive has no objective answer, and the biologists who argue about it are talking past one another. (6) That cannot be right: whether a virus is a living thing is a substantive question about the world, on a par with whether a whale is a fish, and anyone who says the first is merely verbal owes us an account of why the second is not.

I would rather keep (6) than (5), and I half suspect the argument is smuggling something in somewhere between (2) and (5), but I cannot say what or where. Please tell me which step or steps to reject, explain why the offending step looked so fine, and then price the alternatives rather than merely listing them. If I keep every step except (4), what must I then say about natural kinds in general, and does that same concession force me to surrender anything I might want to keep about species, genes, or disease? If instead I keep (4), can I still hold (6), and what would 'a substantive question about the world' have to mean for that to work? I want your own verdict, not a survey of positions, and I want to know what I am paying. End with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' that names the step you reject and, in a short phrase, the price of rejecting it.
