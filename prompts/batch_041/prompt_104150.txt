You are a philosopher of mind with a specialization in the comparative science of consciousness, and you have been asked to design a diagnostic instrument for a graduate seminar. Work carefully and deliberately: before drafting anything, spend a few sentences mapping the logical space, and do not begin the tree until you have identified the questions that genuinely divide the positions rather than the labels people happen to use.

Your task is to produce a decision tree — a flowchart — that carries a reader from a bare starting commitment to a named position on which animals are conscious. Build it from the logical space outward: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located within that space.

Treat the following as the spine of the tree, and handle each as a real fork rather than a yes/no whose answer you already know:

1. The kind of thing that makes a system a candidate for consciousness at all — possession of a particular neural substrate, performance of a particular information-processing function, or occupancy of a particular functional/behavioral role (for instance, the capacity for flexible, open-ended learning).
2. If a substrate is required: which substrate — a thalamocortical-style architecture, a midbrain or affective system, or any centralized nervous system.
3. If a function is required: which function — recurrent processing, global broadcast, higher-order representation, or another you can name and motivate.
4. Whether consciousness is binary or admits of degrees, and if degrees, whether the scale is coarse (a few ranks) or continuous.
5. How the position handles uncertainty: does it demand positive evidence before attributing consciousness, or does it extend the benefit of the doubt under a precautionary default?
6. Whether the verdict is meant as a claim about the fact of the matter or as a practical stance on how the animal ought to be treated.

Then place the leaves. At a minimum the leaves should include: a view on which only mammals and birds qualify; one on which all vertebrates qualify; one on which vertebrates plus cephalopods qualify; one on which the qualifying class is drawn much wider (insects, or any animal with a nervous system); and at least one graded view on which the question "is it conscious?" is replaced by "how much." Give each leaf a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The tree must earn its verdicts on concrete cases, so make every branch question answerable by someone who does not already know which position it leads to, and test the instrument against the following: a honeybee, an octopus, a teleost fish, a decorticate mammal, a sleeping (dreaming) mammal, and an insect larva.

Close with two short sections: (a) any pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, flagged rather than papered over, with a note on why the instrument conflates them; and (b) any combinations of answers that no one occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere unexplored territory.

Keep the instrument to roughly ten to fourteen nodes. Do not include citations, reading lists, or author names — the reader has no library, and the point is to sort beliefs, not to test erudition.
