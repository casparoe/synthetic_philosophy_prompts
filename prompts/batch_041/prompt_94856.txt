I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, and I've been reading Aquinas's Summa Theologiae on my own. I'm stuck on the reply to the third objection in the article on whether sedition is always a mortal sin (II-II, q. 42, a. 2). The objection had argued that since it is praiseworthy to deliver a multitude from a tyrannical ruler, and that can hardly be done without dissension among the people, sedition cannot always be a mortal sin. Here is the reply:

"A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely; for this is tyranny, being conducive to the private good of the ruler, and to the injury of the multitude."

What I expected was something like "sedition is normally a mortal sin, but overthrowing a tyrant is an exception." Instead he seems to say that this simply is not sedition in the first place. Does that shift do any real work, or is it just a way of talking? Second, how should I read the proviso about the disturbance being "inordinate" and producing greater harm than the tyranny itself — is he covertly weighing consequences here, which seems to sit oddly with everything I've heard about his ethics? And why does he end up pinning the charge of sedition on the tyrant rather than on those who move against him, given that elsewhere in the same article he treats the defenders of the common good as the ones who are not seditious?
