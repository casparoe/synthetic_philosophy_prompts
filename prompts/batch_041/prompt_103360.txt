I am a doctoral student in formal epistemology, at work on a chapter about uncertain evidence, and I want to state precisely what I actually believe a rational agent should do when the evidence does not arrive as a certainty. I have a stable set of verdicts on particular cases and a tentative principle that I suspect is too crude. I would like you to find the principle, or the small family of principles, that best fits my verdicts, and to tell me honestly which of them has to go. No bibliography is wanted; I care only about the content of the answer.

My verdicts:

1. Dim light. I glance at a coin in poor light and come away in a perceptual state that makes me 0.7 confident it landed heads; a moment earlier I was at 0.5. I think my credence in heads should become 0.7 exactly — not 0.5, and certainly not 1. I also think that something has become certain, though not that the coin is heads: namely, that I am in the perceptual state I am in.

2. Testimony. A forecaster whose track record I trust announces a 70% chance of rain. I do not think my credence should jump to 0.7; it should go to whatever the bare fact that he said so supports. If I would have been surprised by any other announcement, it should move a lot; if I fully expected him to say 70%, it should barely move. In the dim-light case I set my credence to the number my experience delivers; here I refuse to.

3. Garbled instrument. A gauge displays a number I take to be its estimate of some quantity, but I am only 0.8 confident that I have read the display correctly; it may have said something else, or nothing at all. I still think I should update, to something between my old credence and the reading. I find it unacceptable to say that there is no verdict here because I do not know the new probabilities of my evidence. Whatever rule I use should extend to this case or be replaced by something that does.

4. Order of learning. Suppose I acquire two pieces of uncertain evidence in succession — first the dim-light look, then a partly reliable test, or the reverse. I am confident the final credence should not depend on which came first. Evidence is evidence, and the order of receipt is an accident of biography.

5. Forgetting the experience. After the dim-light look I am at 0.7; then I forget the look entirely but remember that I was once at 0.7 for some perceptual reason. I think the 0.7 should persist. Once absorbed, evidence should not leak back out through forgetting.

6. An uninformative look. Suppose I know in advance that my glance is statistically independent of the coin — it will leave me 0.7 on heads whether or not the coin is heads. Then looking should leave my credence in heads unchanged, at 0.5. An experience unrelated to what it purports to be about is no evidence at all.

My tentative principle is this: whenever my new total evidence can be represented as a probability distribution over a partition of the possibilities, I should set my new credences in the cells of that partition to the given values and leave the conditional credences within each cell as they were, which recovers ordinary conditioning as the special case in which one cell goes to 1.

What I want from you is: (a) the principle, or small set of principles, that best captures verdicts 1 through 6, stated precisely enough to apply, with a note on which case forces each clause; (b) whether my tentative principle is salvageable, and if not, what should replace it; (c) your principle's verdicts on two or three further cases that you think discriminate between the surviving candidates; and (d) an honest statement of which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate. If verdicts 1 and 2 pull in opposite directions, or if 4 and 5 conflict with whatever rule handles 1, I would rather be told which verdict to abandon than be handed a principle engineered to save all six.
