I sit on the programming committee of a small literary festival, and at our last meeting a dispute was closed with a sentence: when one member objected to featuring a novelist who has done something genuinely bad in his private life, the chair replied that we must 'separate the art from the artist,' and that was taken to settle the matter. I am not sure the chair's conclusion follows from any one thing that sentence might mean, and I suspect the reading that would support his conclusion is not the reading most people would endorse. What I want is not a defense of the slogan but a map of it.

Set out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that 'separate the art from the artist' could be expressing. I would expect the readings to diverge along several axes, and I want them separated: claims about what we must do in order to appreciate or interpret a work; claims about what belongs to a work's meaning; claims about what is relevant to its aesthetic value; claims about the ethical standing of the artist and of those who consume the work; and claims about what institutions — festivals, museums, publishers, prize committees — should do with it. Some of these are surely trivial, some are substantive and defensible, and some may be false.

For each reading, please do four things. First, state it precisely enough that it could be assessed. Second, give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. Third, say what it implies for a harder case than the one we faced — specifically, a case in which the wrongdoing is not merely a fact about the maker's life but is implicated in the work's own content or in the conditions of its making, so that the work's achievement seems to depend on the very thing we object to. Fourth, and most important, say which of these readings, if any, would actually license the chair's conclusion that the objection should simply be dropped, as opposed to reframed or answered.

Where a reading needs a distinction the slogan does not mark — for instance between a work's being aesthetically flawed and its being morally flawed, or between the value of a work and our reasons for engaging with it at all — name the distinction and say whether the slogan can be repaired to mark it, or whether it is irredeemably ambiguous. Please lay the whole thing out as a numbered menu with the verdicts beside each entry, in a form I can circulate to the committee before the next meeting.
