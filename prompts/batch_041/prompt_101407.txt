I am a novelist at work on a book about a prison governor who must decide whether to recommend a man for early release. I want to practice defending the idea that the man deserves what he got against someone who has thought harder about it than I have. Please play a committed hard incompatibilist: someone who holds that because no one is the ultimate author of their own character, no one ever deserves to suffer in the basic sense—deserved punishment just because of what they did, apart from any forward-looking benefit—and who therefore thinks criminal justice should be modeled on public health quarantine rather than retribution. Make the character sharp, patient, and genuinely responsive; let him concede that retributive attitudes can have good effects, but never that they track a real desert fact. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, and argue through concrete cases rather than slogans. Begin by responding to my opening.

Here is my opening. My governor has a distinction she calls ledger desert. A debt is real because we keep the ledger; it does not have to be written into the fabric of the universe. When she says the man owes something for what he did, she means that he is a participant in a practice that assigns liabilities, not that he is the unmoved origin of his own character. If you quarantine him instead, you treat him as a hazard rather than a debtor. Isn't that a colder view of him, and doesn't it lose something we need in order to hold people responsible?

At the end of your in-character reply, add a brief out-of-character note stating how confident you are in the claims you have made and identifying the point you are least sure of. Please write in American English.
