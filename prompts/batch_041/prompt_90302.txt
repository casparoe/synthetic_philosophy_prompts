In my history class we were discussing the collapse of the Soviet Union, and my teacher said there was no single cause: economic stagnation, the nationalist movements in the republics, and the arms race all played a part, and he wouldn't rank them. Two classmates of mine took this out into the hallway and kept arguing about it.

Marcus said that if several factors were each sufficient on their own, then none of them can be called a cause at all — pick any one, and the collapse would have happened anyway, so it made no difference. Historians who still cite one factor, he said, are just picking the one that interests them.

Dana said that is an artifact of describing the outcome too coarsely. If you describe what actually happened — the collapse happening in that way, in that year, with those republics going first — then the stagnation mattered, the nationalisms mattered, and the arms race mattered, and nothing was redundant. The difference-making test is fine, she said; it was the description of the outcome that was sloppy.

Marcus answered that this trick makes every factor necessary for some outcome description or other, since you can always redescribe the outcome narrowly enough to fit, so the test now proves nothing. Dana replied that the alternative is worse: keep the coarse description and the only honest thing to say is that the set of factors caused the collapse rather than any one of them, which is what historians mean by multi-causal explanation. Marcus said a set you can always pad with a backup cause is not a real thing in the world.
