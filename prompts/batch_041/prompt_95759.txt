Assume that Keynes had lived into the 1960s and encountered the Ellsberg problem in the form in which it is usually presented: an urn known to contain fifty red and fifty black balls, and a second urn containing a hundred balls in an unknown proportion of red to black. Offered a bet on red, most people prefer the first urn; offered a bet on black, most again prefer the first urn; and this pair of preferences cannot be represented by any assignment of probabilities to the composition of the second urn together with a single utility function, since the agent who prefers red from the first urn to red from the second, and black from the first to black from the second, is committed to the probability of red being higher in the first than in the second and the probability of black being higher in the first than in the second, though the two probabilities sum to one in each case.

I want Keynes's reaction rather than Savage's, and I want it argued from the parts of his own theory of probability that bear on the case. Three such parts, as I understand them. First, probability is a logical relation between propositions, not a subjective degree fixed by an agent's willingness to bet. Second, degrees of belief are not always numerically measurable and in many cases not even comparable, so that for some pairs of propositions one can say only that the first is more probable, and for others one can say nothing at all. Third, probability is only one dimension of the epistemic situation, the other being the weight of the evidence, so that two propositions may carry the same probability while one rests on a much larger body of relevant evidence than the other.

My questions:

1. Would Keynes treat the usual preference for the unambiguous urn as rational, as a bias of the kind Savage's framework diagnoses, or as a sign that the agent has misdescribed the situation — in particular, as a sign that the composition of the second urn is not a proposition with a determinate probability at all, so that the sure-thing principle simply has no application and the charge of inconsistency misfires? If he would accept the preference, does he accept it in both directions — is an agent who prefers the ambiguous urn equally licensed, or does the weight of the evidence make caution rational and boldness irrational?

2. What would he do with cases that his apparatus appears to divide more finely than the risk-ambiguity contrast allows?

(a) An urn whose composition was fixed by a fair coin toss, so that the chance of red is fifty-fifty by symmetry, but whose actual contents no one has seen; and a second urn whose composition was fixed by a process nobody can inspect. Both are unobserved; the first yields a numerical chance of red and the second does not. Where does the weight of the evidence fall in each case, and is the pair of urns that Ellsberg's subjects treat as known and unknown the pair that Keynes would have treated that way?

(b) Two urns neither of whose compositions is known, but one of which is the subject of a large body of inconclusive evidence and the other of no evidence at all. Does Keynes's weight rank these, so that an agent may consistently prefer the better-evidenced but still ambiguous urn to the worse-evidenced one, and how does that preference compare in rationality to the original preference for the fifty-fifty urn?

(c) A choice between two options whose probabilities are, on Keynes's own view, not comparable — a bet on a well-understood physical process against a bet on a scientific hypothesis of a kind he thought could not be assigned a numerical probability. What is the agent supposed to do here? Is there a decision rule at all, or does the theory leave the choice undetermined, and would he have regarded that as a defect or as a correct description of the situation?

(d) An agent who, offered the choice between the fifty-fifty urn and an urn about which he knows only that it contains some red balls and some black balls, is willing to pay a premium for the ambiguous urn on the grounds that it might be heavily stacked in his favour. Does Keynes's framework give any verdict here, or is this outside the reach of a theory of probability altogether?

3. How much of the modern discussion would Keynes have recognised as his own? I take it that his distinction between probability and weight is doing some of the work that ambiguity is now asked to do, and that his refusal to make all probabilities comparable is meant to block exactly the move by which the standard argument turns the two preferences into a contradiction. I would like an assessment of whether the two notions come to the same thing: whether weight is the same phenomenon as ambiguity, whether non-comparability is a stronger or a weaker claim than ambiguity aversion, and which framework handles the cases above better. Where the two conflict, tell me which verdicts Keynes would have had to give up, and which of them you think he should have given up.
