I am currently taking a graduate seminar in decision theory, and we have spent the past two weeks on cases in which the expected value of a prospect is undefined rather than infinite or finite. The example that has lodged itself in my mind is a game in which a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if it lands heads on the nth toss, the payoff is the quantity negative one raised to the n minus one, multiplied by two to the n, divided by n. If one computes the expectation in the natural way, the terms reduce to the alternating harmonic series, which converges to the natural logarithm of two. But because that series is only conditionally convergent, rearranging its terms can make it sum to any real number one likes, or make it diverge. So the game appears to have no well-defined expected value, even though every outcome is a definite amount of money and the probabilities are perfectly ordinary.

Our instructor then introduced a variant: the same game, but with one dollar added to every payoff. Call it the shifted game. Every outcome of the shifted game is exactly one dollar better than the corresponding outcome of the original game, so it seems obvious that the shifted game is better. Yet if the original game has no expected value, the shifted game also has no expected value, and expected utility theory cannot say that one is better than the other. That bothered me, and I would like to explore it in writing.

Please write a dialogue between two graduate students in the seminar, both of whom are fully competent and neither of whom is a strawman. One should argue that the original game and its shifted variant reveal a genuine gap in expected utility theory: the theory cannot rank prospects whose expectations are undefined, but we have strong pre-theoretic judgments about some of them, such as that the shifted game is better than the original, or that either game is worth playing for a small fee. The other should argue that the undefinedness is not a defect of the theory but a correct diagnosis: the game simply has no expected value, and expected utility theory is silent rather than wrong; the intuition that the game is worth playing may come from confusing it with a truncated version, or from a mistaken application of the law of large numbers, or from a non-expected-utility principle that we can adopt as a tie-breaker without revising the core theory. They should go back and forth, responding to each other’s actual points. The first should press on whether the theory can accommodate the shifted game’s dominance without invoking something outside expected utility. The second should press on whether the first’s intuitions survive when we consider the long-run behavior of the game, or when we compare it to a sure gain.

Let them also discuss at least one further variant, such as a version with a finite cutoff, or a version in which the player is allowed to choose the order in which the outcomes are summed, or a version in which the payoffs are made nonnegative in a way that makes the expectation infinite rather than undefined. The dialogue should be philosophically rigorous, should not pretend to resolve the issue by fiat, and should not attribute views to named historical figures unless the attribution is uncontroversial. It should be clear at the end where the two students agree and where they still disagree.
