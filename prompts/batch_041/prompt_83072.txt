Your job here: be a philosopher of medicine who's spent way too long on the naturalism vs. normativism fight — i.e. whether calling something a disease is reporting a fact about malfunctioning biology or making a value judgement about what we don't want to happen, and whether 'health' is anything over and above the absence of the former. Before you write a word, take a minute to brainstorm: what does each side actually claim, and where does each one start to crack? Then write the piece.

Here's the setup to work with. The fictional Republic of Vellaria has passed the Species-Typical Function Act. Section 1 says a disease is a statistically abnormal reduction in the functional efficiency of some biological mechanism, judged against the reference class of the organism's species, sex and age. Section 2 creates the Standing Committee on Nosological Integrity, which each year publishes the Schedule of Recognised Diseases. Your piece is the Committee's press release announcing this year's Schedule: deadpan, around 650 words, in the flat self-congratulatory register of a real government bulletin — numbered items, a footnote or two, a soundbite from the chair.

The comedy has to come from the definition actually breaking on the cases, not from the committee being stupid or venal. So the Schedule has to rule on all of: a condition that's universal in the population (so under the letter of the Act nothing about it counts as abnormal, and it can't be a disease, however bad it is); a rare trait that's atypical but harmless or even handy; a condition whose disease-status flips depending on how narrowly you draw the reference class (all humans? people their age? people who work in this factory?); and something the whole committee agrees is bad but that nobody can call a dysfunction without first settling what counts as harm. Invented conditions are fine — you dont need real ones — and please don't name any real textbook, author, body or living person. Also work in at least one item where something gets onto the Schedule, or drops off it, purely because its frequency in the population moved.

Two more things. Somewhere in there, a dissenting committee member gets a short footnote or bracketed objection, and it should be the most sensible thing in the document. And the release must never let on that anything is going wrong — the reader should work out on their own that the committee is quietly making value judgements while insisting that it isn't. Someone who knows this debate should laugh rather than wince, and someone who's never heard of it should still enjoy it and end up wondering whether the whole definitional project was ever going to work.

Give me the document only — no preamble, no explanation of the jokes, no moral tacked on at the end.
