And the script says:

Williams's target here is utilitarianism, and his example is the man who can save either his wife or a stranger from drowning. A utilitarian tells him to save whichever of the two lives produces more good overall, and since the lives are worth the same, the theory can only let him save his wife if he first registers that doing so is permitted — he has to think something like: in a situation of this kind, saving one's own wife is allowed. Williams says that is one thought too many. His reason for pulling her out should simply be that she is his wife; the moment he needs a moral licence for it, the theory has already turned her into one person among others, standing to him only under the general requirement to maximise.

The objection is really about how much utilitarianism demands of the agent. In the Jim and the Indians case, where Jim has to kill one prisoner in order to save nineteen, Williams's complaint is that the theory requires the agent to subordinate his ground projects to the general welfare, and the wife example is the same point in miniature: the husband is asked to treat his deepest attachment as one item in the sum, to be set aside whenever the arithmetic goes the other way. That is what people mean by the integrity objection, and the wife case is just a cleaner version of it.

The best reply is the two-level one, put most sharply by Railton. Railton separates the objective standard of rightness — an act is right if it maximises welfare — from the agent's subjective deliberation, and argues that a sophisticated consequentialist can hold the first without ever letting it into the second. He saves his wife because she is his wife, not because the sums favour it, and he never has the licensing thought Williams complains about. That answers Williams, because the objection only lands against a theory that tells the agent to run the sums at the moment of action, which is naive act-utilitarianism. So the objection shows at most that utilitarianism needs a two-level structure, not that it is false: once the theory is stated properly the wife is not one person among others in the husband's deliberation, and he saves her for the right reason. Williams's objection therefore fails, though it did force utilitarians to be much more careful about the difference between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure.
