I've been roped into running one 90-minute session of our public philosophy evening series — about 15 adults, mixed backgrounds, most of them not academics. I retired from the chemistry department a couple of years ago and have spent the time since catching up on philosophy, so I'm apparently the obvious person to do the weakness of will slot.

Here's what I want the session built around, and it's not Aristotle. I want them to catch themselves doing the thing. The setup I have in mind: offer someone a small reward now versus a bigger one later and they'll usually wait; move both rewards further out and the same person flips. That much is familiar to them. What I'd like to hang the session on is a finding I'm fairly sure is real in the psych literature, that the steepness of the discount depends on how you carve up the delay — discounting over one undivided stretch comes out gentler than discounting over that same stretch chopped into subintervals. If that's right, it means preference reversals can be manufactured or made to vanish just by changing how the interval is described, which is a much stranger and more useful thing than "people are impatient."

So: give me a runnable activity with rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (a small real prize is fine if it helps). The rewards must not be described to them in the abstract — the delays have to be compressed into the session itself, so they actually sit there waiting. Two features I want built in. First, one subgroup faces a single long gap while another faces the same total gap in pieces, so we can put reversal rates side by side on the board and see if the carving did anything. Second, if you can fit it without eating the session, a moment where they can trade their claims with each other, so the price of their own future cooperation becomes public and they find out whether it matches what they just told the room about themselves.

Then a debrief that maps the moments onto the philosophy: the difference between acting against what you judge best and failing to stick to a plan you had already made, and what it does to that distinction if the shape of your preference over time is partly an artifact of how the interval was described.

Tell me what will definately go wrong the first time — I assume several things will, starting with nobody caring about the tokens. And be honest about what this can't reproduce. A game over tokens gives you inconsistency across time, not weakness of will in the strict sense, and I want to know exactly where the gap is so I can say it out loud myself rather than have someone in the audience say it for me.
