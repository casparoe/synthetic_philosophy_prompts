My epistemology group is finishing with Aumann's agreement theorem and I want to close the seminar with a poem instead of a recap. Two voices, antiphonal, alternating lines, 16-20 lines total. They start with different credences about the same thing, and each line moves one of them toward the other, because each treats what the other says as evidence. I want the form to do the theorem's work: by the end, the same words should be coming out of both mouths. But here's the part I actually care about, and the part people always fudge -- the theorem doesn't say rational people can't disagree, it says that if they share a prior and the disagreement is common knowlege, the disagreement can't survive. So make it clear the convergence is forced by each one hearing the other, not by anyone being persuaded or being polite. Last beat: let it turn out that the reason they could ever meet is a prior neither of them can see, so they don't get to congratulate themselves. Keep it accurate enough that someone who knows the result won't wince, and don't let it read as if the theorem rules out ordinary private disagreement -- it only bites once the disagreement itself is common knowledge.

The theorem's got a contested reading or two, so end with a short note, three sentences max, on where the poem takes liberties -- including the point that the result is really about posterior expectations of any random variable, not just credences in a proposition. Poem itself no longer than 20 lines.
