I'm a second-year PhD student in a history-and-philosophy-of-the-social-sciences reading group, and I've been asked to open next week's session on why the "lessons of history" so reliably misfire. My friends keep reaching for the familiar illustrations — the Munich analogy as it was pressed into service during the Vietnam era, and the current vogue for treating a rising power and a ruling power as fated to collide — but what I actually want, and what I'd like you to supply, is the conceptual machinery underneath the cases rather than another tour of the cases themselves. Please act as a methodologist of historical inference, and think carefully about the structure of the problem before committing to anything. I want you to explain why a historical analogy can look impeccable in every respect we ordinarily check and still license no conclusion about the present: what it means to say such an analogy is an inference from a single case; what has to be true for two situations to share a relevant structure as opposed to merely telling a similar story; why the reference class that would give the inference a base rate is almost never available for a historical "lesson"; how the choice of which past to analogize to is itself shaped by the conclusion one already wants; and the peculiar reflexivity of the historical case, namely that the actors being forecast are the same actors who have read the lesson, so that applying the analogy can alter the very outcome it was meant to predict. I've been calling the conclusion an analogy is meant to carry across its "payload" and the incidental similarities that merely make it feel apt its "scaffolding," and you should feel free to work with that contrast if it earns its keep.

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each point should isolate one distinct reason the analogy misfires, and the fifth should be the one that only starts to bite once all the others have been handled well.
