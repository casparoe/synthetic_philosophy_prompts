I'm preparing a seminar presentation for a course on personal identity and the metaphysics of mind, and I want to rehearse the hardest objection I can against a position I find tempting but have not yet managed to defend. Please play the part of a committed defender of the following view, and stay in that role until I say "out of character": that what matters in survival is the continuation of a single unbroken causal process — the same going concern, carried forward by lawful step-by-step dependence from one moment to the next — and that this is a stricter requirement than the preservation of a pattern, a copy of one's memories, or any amount of qualitative similarity. You should be a serious defender of this view rather than a caricature: you may concede that identity comes in degrees, or that identity is not what ultimately matters, but you should never simply give up the claim that continuity of process rather than similarity of pattern is what does the work. Argue in plain prose, one or two paragraphs per turn, no bullet points, and no references to the literature — I don't need citations and neither should you; if you want to invoke a case, describe it yourself. Don't break character to explain what you are doing or to ask whether you are doing it right.

Here is the case I want to put to you. A surgical procedure replaces a person's neurons one at a time with silicon units. Each step is tiny, and the patient, who is under general anaesthesia throughout, notices nothing: there is no lapse of consciousness, no behavioural change, and by every test available the end result is indistinguishable from the original. But two features of the procedure are unusual, and I want you to take them literally rather than idealise them away. First, the replacement changes the grain of the functional decomposition: each biological neuron is retired by a pair of silicon units that only jointly realise that neuron's role, so at no point does a single new unit stand in for a single old one — the very units the process is made of are redrawn as we go. Second, the silicon units are only statistically faithful: each reproduces its predecessor's firing statistics but not its exact spike times, so the trajectory of the system diverges at every single step from what it would otherwise have been, and after enough steps no downstream state is what it would have been had nothing been replaced.

Now: you say that what matters is one unbroken causal process. But if the person survives this, then survival does not require the continuation of any same process, only the production of a successor that is statistically like its predecessor, and your view collapses into the pattern view you meant to reject. If the person does not survive it, then you owe me an account of exactly which step kills them, given that every step is as innocuous as the one before it and no step is any less innocuous than its neighbours — and you cannot appeal to the patient's testimony, since anaesthesia makes first-person report equally available to a freshly woken copy. Which horn do you take, and why isn't it fatal to your view?
