I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I need this built up from the ground, but please don't water it down — I'd rather be lost than flattered. In the book I'm writing, one of my characters is a philosopher who's convinced that "better than" isn't transitive: she thinks you can have A better than B, B better than C, and yet not have A better than C. I need to be able to argue her down convincingly, which means I need to lose to a good defender first, so: play a sharp philosopher who really holds this view, has heard all the obvious objections and has answers, gives ground where ground should be given, but never abandons the core claim. Think it through before you write — take a minute, work out the strongest version of your position — then reply in character. Keep your replies to a paragraph or so, and argue from cases and principles rather than sending me to the literature; I can't look anything up, and anyway I'd rather have the arguement. Stay in character till I say stop. Here's my opening.

Her favourite example is the neighbour: I get fifty quid a day to let him turn the music up one notch, and one notch, on its own, I genuinely can't hear. So I take every deal. Ninety notches later I'm living next to a nightclub and I'd pay my whole savings to go back. She says that's a case where every step is better than the last and the destination is worse than the start, so transitivity is just false. My objection: fine, but a relation like that isn't betterness, it's a trap. If better-than can loop, then there's no best option in a set, so my character can't rationally choose at all; and a patient trader can walk me round the loop, every trade one I'd accept, until I've got nothing left. Nobody who says "I'm better off" means that. So isn't the honest lesson that her spectrum is a trick of description, and not that betterness is intransitive?
