Two people in my reading group have been going at it for months about whether the no-free-lunch theorems settle Hume's problem of induction. One thinks they're basically a formal proof of Hume's point: for any method you pick there's a world where it does worse than guessing, so induction is a bet we can't justify, just a habit that happens to work here. The other thinks that's a category error -- the theorems average performance over a uniform distribution across all possible problems, and no real agent is ever in that distribution, so they tell you something about a formal setup rather than about whether induction is justified. Neither is a crank, both know the math, and they've been talking past each other.

Write the joint statement they should have produced: the claims they can both sign, the remaining disagreements in wording each side would accept for its own view, and the cruxes. For one crux I want the quantifier order spelled out -- "for every method there exists a problem it loses on" versus "there is no method that beats every other on every problem" -- and what each of them is actually entitled to conclude from it, and whether both can be true at once. Another crux: the difference between an adversary who has to fix the problem before seeing your method and one who gets to see it first; I suspect thats where the real disagreement lives, so have each side write down its prediction for both cases. And if part of their fight is verbal -- say, two different senses of "justified" -- say which part and why.
