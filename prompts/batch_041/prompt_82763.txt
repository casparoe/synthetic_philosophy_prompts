Slightly random, but this has been eating at me since our neuro seminar and I want it out of my system. On the ward I volunteer on the protocol is dead simple: every vitals check they ask you to rate your pain 0-10, and the number decides everything. 4 or above and the nurse has to offer something, 7 or above and someone gets a talking-to about charting. The whole thing runs on the idea that pain is just whatever the patient says it is and nothing else, and that a higher number is definately worse. Even the official definition has it baked in, the International Association for the Study of Pain calls pain "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage." So unpleasantness is supposedly part of what pain is.

But then we read about asymbolia, where a stroke (insular damage I think) leaves people who can feel pain and report it -- they'll say yeah, that hurts, call it an 8, point right at it -- but they don't pull away, don't grimace, aren't distressed, and seem to find the whole thing mildly funny. The sensation is there, the minding isn't.

So what is our ward protocol actually committed to? It obviously assumes pain is bad, but bad in which way: is the badness in the sensation itself, so that these patients are in something bad whether or not they care, or is it in the aversion, in which case they might be in nothing bad at all and the 8 is meaningless? Does the score track intensity or badness, since those come apart here? And when we say the patient's report is authoritative, authoritative about what, the sensation or the suffering?

I think the practice is more committed than anyone notices, and also kind of inconsistent -- officially we treat the reported sensation, but if one of these patients landed on the ward I'm pretty sure everyone would shrug and say they're not really suffering, so nothing to treat, which is a totally different theory sneaking in. So which theories does the practice genuinely rule out versus just happen to sit well with, and where is it underdetermined? And if we took seriously the view that pain is only bad because we're averse to it, what would actually have to change -- the protocol, the definition, or just the paperwork? Don't let me off with "it depends," I want the hidden commitments named.
