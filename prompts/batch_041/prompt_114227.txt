I am preparing a session on infinity in literature and want a critical reading of Borges's "The Book of Sand" through the philosophy of the actual infinite: Cantor's completed infinite as against the merely potential, Hilbert's hotel, and the finitist and intuitionist objection (Brouwer, Weyl) that a completed infinite is either meaningless or illegitimate. Do not worry about references or citations; the ideas are what matter.

The story hands us what looks like an actual infinity in physical form: a book with neither beginning nor end, whose pages appear to grow out of the covers as the narrator turns them, which he can never exhaust, and which he finally abandons, hidden, in the basement of the National Library. Is this Hilbert's hotel made into an object, and if so, does the story grasp that the object a finitist says cannot exist is exactly the one the narrator is holding? Hilbert's hotel is meant to show that the oddities of infinite collections are simply their arithmetic rather than paradoxes; Borges's narrator, by contrast, is appalled, calls the book monstrous, and cannot bring himself to burn it for fear of an endless supply of smoke. I want to know whether that reaction dramatizes a legitimate intuition the finitists had and Cantor waved away, or whether it is a failure of nerve that Hilbert's argument exposes — and which the story itself seems to endorse.

Then set "The Library of Babel" against it, because there the totality runs the other way. The narrator tells us that the number of books, though vast, is not infinite, so the librarians' despair is the despair of finitude, of a closed totality no one can survey; yet his closing thought is that the Library is unlimited and cyclical, an endless repetition. Is that a reversion from the actual to the merely potential infinite, and does it make the second story the finitist's ally rather than the first? Where does the reading break down — where is Borges neither a Cantorian nor a finitist, and where does the parable trade on a confusion the mathematics would not permit (an infinite book is still a book, with pages, covers, and a language)? And what, in the end, does the fiction show that the arguments cannot?

Format: "The idea in brief" (Cantor, Hilbert, and the finitists — roughly 200 words); "The Book of Sand" (roughly 400); "The Library of Babel" (roughly 300); "Where the reading breaks down" (roughly 200); and "What the fiction adds" (roughly 150).
