I am also used to the distinction between a position that is theoretically won and a position an engine can actually win within its time limit, and between a game that has been solved and one that is merely solvable in principle.

In a philosophy class we have been reading about computational complexity, and there is a debate I cannot follow. On one side, people treat results such as NP-completeness as revealing something deep about the limits of what can be known or discovered — as though the existence of problems that are easy to check but hard to solve shows that insight or creativity must be a genuine faculty rather than an algorithm. On the other side, people insist that these are facts about one particular idealised model of computation rather than about knowledge or reality, and that the apparent limit is an artefact of how we have formalised the word 'efficient'.

Please restate this debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use — positions, search trees, tablebases, heuristics, the horizon effect, practical versus perfect play, solved versus solvable games — and then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion, and precisely what gets bent in the process? And which parts have no counterpart in the chess vocabulary at all? I am most interested in that last question, and in whether the residue is a defect of the chess way of thinking or evidence that the philosophers are trading on a distinction that does not survive being made precise. I would also like you to flag anywhere the chess vocabulary is quietly doing the philosophers' work for them — for instance, if describing an engine's heuristics as its intuition begs the question in favour of the deflationary side.

Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options: tell me which side you think has the better of it, and defend that choice. Write in British English.
