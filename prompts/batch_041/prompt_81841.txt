I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of religion seminar, and we have just spent two sessions on Pascal's wager — the argument that since the payoff to believing in God is infinite if God exists and the loss is finite if he does not, a rational person should believe, or at least set about acquiring belief. What has stayed with me is not the wager itself but the way a classmate shut down an objection I raised afterward. I had argued that belief is not something one can simply decide to have, and that taking on a faith can cost a person things of real value — a settled habit of proportioning belief to evidence, certain friendships, a kind of intellectual honesty. He dismissed all of it with a single line: 'you've got nothing to lose.' Several people nodded as though the matter were settled, and I could not articulate why the phrase felt to me like a trick rather than an argument.

Please take that one line seriously and tell me what it could actually be claiming. I want a menu rather than a verdict: the distinct precise theses that 'you've got nothing to lose' might be standing for when it is used to recommend the wager. Some of these readings will be nearly trivial, some substantive and defensible, some plainly false, and at least one, I suspect, is doing quiet work that the person repeating the line has not noticed. For each reading, say whether it is true or plausible on its own terms, and then say which of them would actually license the move my classmate was making — that is, which would show that my worries about the costs of belief and its involuntariness are simply beside the point. I am especially interested in whether any reading survives once one grants that a finite loss is still a loss, and that the same reasoning appears to recommend belief in any number of mutually exclusive gods.

Finally, tell me which reading the people who repeat this line most probably have in mind, and whether that reading is one they could defend if pressed. Please lay the readings out as an ordered list I can bring back to seminar, so that I can see exactly where his few words would have to be doing their work.
