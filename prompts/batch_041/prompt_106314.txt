I'm part of a small reading group that has spent the past few months on the metaphysics of consciousness, and we have gotten badly stuck on panpsychism — specifically on the combination problem, which I have come to suspect is usually framed in a way that quietly begs the question. The familiar setup asks how a great many tiny subjects, each with its own rudimentary experience, could ever add up to a single unified subject like you or me, and the familiar escapes — phenomenal bonding, cosmopsychism, panprotopsychism, plain brute emergence — each strike me as either mysterious or a change of subject rather than a solution. What I want from you is an argument, not a survey of the literature.

Here is the thesis I would like you to defend: that the combination problem is best addressed by rejecting the assumption that experience must be owned by a subject at the micro-level, so that what is fundamental is a field of subjectless qualitative character, and subjectivity itself is something that comes into being when that character is organized in the right way — a phase transition in which a genuinely novel higher-level unity appears while remaining fully dependent on the underlying character. Treat the phase-transition analogy as load-bearing rather than decorative: say what plays the role of the order parameter, what distinguishes a real subject from a merely convenient description, and why the resulting view does not simply collapse back into either emergentism or eliminativism. Then turn it against the strongest objections you can muster — above all that "subjectless experience" is incoherent, and that any such transition is just the combination problem renamed — and close by telling me where the position is weakest, what it would cost to hold it, and whether you would actually hold it. Please keep the argument self-contained; I am not looking for citations or a reading list, just the case itself and an honest account of its limits.
