I retired from a career in plant physiology about four years ago and, to my own surprise, I've spent most of the time since reading philosophy — philosophy of science first, then causation and probability, which is how I ended up back in statistics at an age when I should be gardening. Here's my problem. I got talked into sitting on the quality-and-data subcommittee of our regional health authority, and four or five times a year we're handed a comparison — hospital A vs hospital B on 30-day readmissions, our surgical unit vs the state average on complications, the district's staff absence figures broken down by age band — and asked to sign off on whether it goes into the public report. Twice now the headline number has flip-flopped once somebody stratified it, and each time we spent an hour arguing and then voted on vibes. I know this is the Simpson's paradox business (I first met it as a grad student with catch-per-unit-effort data that made no sense until you split by gear type). What I want from you is a procedure the committee can actually run in the room — 45 minutes, one part-time statistician who isn't always there, and a report that has to be defensible to people who will write letters to the paper.

So: give me a decision procedure, in the form of the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it hands the decision to a human because the question has turned out to be about values or about assumptions we can't test rather than about the numbers. I want it to say something usable about the awkward cases, and I'd rather you tell me honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. In particular: what to do when the variable that reverses the comparison can't be measured or isn't in the dataset; when the strata are so small that the reversal could just be noise; when the reversing variable is something that happens *after* the thing we're evaluating (my instinct says that changes everything but I can't articulate why); and how to tell the difference between "hospital A is better" and "you'd live longer if you went to A", because I suspect our committee keeps sliding between those two without noticing. Then run it on three cases: (1) the readmission comparison, where I'd like you to be clear about which of the two numbers belongs in a public report that people will use to choose where to be treated, and please don't fudge it in my favour; (2) the staff absence numbers, where I think the reversal is real and boring and tells us nothing about management; (3) a paper I've been asked to referee for a small ecology journal where the authors report a pooled effect across sites and one referee has already complained that it reverses within sites — say what my referee report should actually demand, given that I can't ask them to collect new data. And at the end, tell me what this procedure can't do, because I've noticed the philosophers are much better than my old colleagues at saying where the floor is.
