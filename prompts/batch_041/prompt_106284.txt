I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've fallen down a hole I need to climb out of before it eats another month of my book. Here's the hole. David Lewis — the Princeton metaphysician, possible worlds, died in 2001 — wrote a short piece late in his life about penal substitution, the Christian doctrine that Christ was punished in our place. He wasn't defending the doctrine. His claim was narrower and stranger: that the standard objection to it, the one that says punishing an innocent person in place of the guilty is obviously unjust, morally barbaric on its face, doesn't survive contact with what we actually think. Because we accept substitution all the time. Somebody pays a friend's fine. Somebody volunteers to take the fall, serve the sentence, absorb the hit. We don't call those unjust. We call them admirable, or at worst a bit unwise. So the doctrine isn't absurd at the level of moral principle; the objection that it is has to do more work than its proponents think.

I've more or less bought this. It's tidy, it's deflating in a way I like, and I've got a character who'd say it at a dinner party. Now I want you to tell me what counts against it, and I want it sorted by how much damage each thing does — fatal, serious, merely awkward, nitpick. I'm not interested in a survey of atonement theology, I'm interested in where this specific argument is exposed.

Some of what I'm already suspicious of, so you can tell me if I'm onto something or wasting my time. Most of his examples might be debt transfers rather than punishments — paying a fine discharges a liability, nobody gets punished, so they don't show we tolerate punishing the innocent, they show we tolerate one person covering another's bill. The volunteer-prisoner case is the one that has to do the work, and I suspect it's doing it by sleight of hand: consent is carrying all the weight, and if consent can make it just to inflict retributive suffering on someone who doesn't deserve it, that seems to license a lot more than he wants. Then there's the structure of the doctrine itself. If the penalty has to fall on the guilty to satisfy retributive justice, substitution can't satisfy it; if retributive justice doesn't require that, it's unclear what the penalty is paying for. And in the trinitarian version the substitute is also the judge and the offended party, which makes the transaction look like a man paying his own fine to himself — no net transfer, so did anything get paid? Does the argument survive that, or does it only survive because it was never asked to?

Also: what would a defender of Lewis concede here? Not what critics say, what the person who likes this argument would themselves admit counts against it. And be honest with me about whether the thing has been insulated into unfalsifiability — if the claim is only ever that the doctrine isn't *obviously* unjust, then maybe nothing could ever count against it, and I want to know whether that's a bug or just the price of a modest claim. What would actually make me give it up? Is there a case, an intuition, an argument, a fact about how real legal systems treat volunteers that should move me? And if there's something that would only move me if I already thought punishment must be deserved — say so, because that's the thing I can't tell whether I believe.
