I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory, and for the past year I have been keeping a file of my own verdicts on cases in which the other player is, in one sense or another, me. The view I have been defending to my friends is the one usually called superrationality, that between identical reasoners in a one-shot dilemma cooperation is rationally required, but I can no longer say precisely what makes two reasoners identical in the sense that matters. Assume throughout a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with temptation 5, reward 3, punishment 1, sucker 0; no repetition, no reputation, no communication; both players fully informed. My verdicts are these. (1) When the opponent is an exact copy of me, made from my blueprint before either of us deliberates and given identical information, I cooperate without hesitation, and a version of me who defected would seem to me to have made a mistake, not merely to have been selfish. (2) When the opponent is not a copy but a stranger whom I know to be running the same deliberation procedure I am, someone who has reached the same conclusion about how to act in this kind of situation and knows that I have reached it, I cooperate too, though less confidently. (3) When the stranger shares my preferences but reasons causally, so that he defects whatever I do, I defect, and cooperating would be pure sacrifice. (4) When the copier is only ninety per cent faithful, so that the opponent does what I do with probability 0.9, I cooperate, but my confidence visibly tracks the fidelity number, which embarrasses me, since the story I tell myself is that he is me, and he either is or he is not. (5) When the copy is exact but our payoffs diverge, so that he receives 4 rather than 1 when we both defect and therefore prefers mutual defection to mutual cooperation while I prefer the reverse, my pull toward cooperation disappears; yet he is as exact a copy as before, and I cannot say what has changed. (6) The case I keep going back and forth on: there is no copy, no causal connection and no shared history, only a distant agent running the same decision procedure in a mirror-image situation, so that helping him at a cost to myself is the counterpart of what I would want from him in his place. Some days I think there is a reason to help him, and that it is the reason operative in case 2; other days I think this is mysticism. For what it is worth, I also one-box against a predictor who has already settled what I will do, though I have never been able to say whether that is the same intuition as in case 1 or a different one.

The best I have managed on my own is something like: cooperate just when the probability of the opponent's cooperating, conditional on my cooperating, is high enough to make cooperating better in expectation than defecting. But that makes everything turn on my credences, seems to license self-serving ways of acquiring them, and says nothing about what the correlation between his action and mine is made of, whether common cause, common algorithm, or something else again.

What I want from you is a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could compute verdicts from them; for each clause, the case above that forces it; what the candidate implies about a case I have not listed, and please invent one that discriminates between the candidates, ideally one that separates correlation from identity; and an honest account of which of my six verdicts the best candidate cannot save, together with whether you would give up the verdict or the principle. I do not need a survey of the literature or suggestions for reading; I need an answer I can use to fix my own view.
