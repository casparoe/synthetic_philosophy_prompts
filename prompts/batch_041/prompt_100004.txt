I have just come back from a long argument with a friend who works on reduction and emergence in the philosophy of science. Over the years she has settled into a fairly stable pattern of verdicts about particular cases. She classifies thermodynamic temperature as reducible to statistical mechanics. She classifies phase transitions, such as the onset of magnetization in a ferromagnet, as emergent. She classifies protein folding as reducible to physics and chemistry. She classifies phenomenal consciousness as emergent and not reducible. She classifies turbulence in a fluid as emergent. She classifies genetic information as reducible to molecular biology. She classifies the arrow of time as emergent. She classifies the mass of the Higgs boson as reducible to quantum field theory. And she classifies market prices as emergent relative to individual trading decisions. I have noticed that these verdicts are jointly compatible with more than one plausible rule, and I cannot tell which one she is actually running. One candidate is an aggregate-versus-organization rule: a higher-level property is reducible when it is a coarse-grained aggregate of lower-level quantities, and emergent when it is a collective organizational pattern or a functional role. Another is a derivability rule: a higher-level property is reducible when its behavior can be derived in principle from lower-level laws, and emergent when the derivation requires infinite-system limits, singularities, or step-by-step simulation. A third is a multiple-realizability rule: a higher-level property is reducible when it is tied to a specific lower-level configuration, and emergent when it can be realized by many different configurations. A fourth is a mechanistic rule: a higher-level property is reducible when there is a complete mechanism that produces it, and emergent when no such mechanism is available.

The new case we were arguing about is the generalization error of a large language model: the gap between its performance on its training data and its performance on new inputs. She is about to give a verdict on whether that error is reducible to the model's architecture, parameters, and training data or whether it is emergent. I would like you to predict her verdict, tell me which of the candidate rules, or some other rule, she seems to be running, explain what each of the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts would predict about the generalization-error case, and name the further case that would best distinguish those rules. I am less interested in the bare yes-or-no than in the structure of the inference: which of her earlier verdicts are doing the real work, and where the rules come apart.
