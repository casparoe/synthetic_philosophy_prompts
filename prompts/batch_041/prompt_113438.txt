I'm in my third year, and this term I'm taking a course on the British empiricists alongside a seminar on the history of monetary thought, so the two have started to bleed into each other. Until now I had no idea that Berkeley wrote anything about money; we read parts of The Querist, the book of queries he first published in Dublin in 1735 (and which in a later edition he cut down, removing the sketch of a national bank he had floated), and it has been at the back of my mind ever since. What grips me is how much of it turns on a single thought — that money is not itself wealth but a token, and that its whole purpose is to stir up and circulate the industry of a people, so that the real riches of a nation lie in the labour and skill of its inhabitants rather than in the quantity of coin it holds. He asks, in one of the queries, whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter", and treats the denominations — pound, crown, livre — as so many names for a proportion rather than claims on anything intrinsically valuable.

For a paper I want to carry this forward and ask what Berkeley would have made of a monetary world that appeared long after his death. Concretely: money that is nothing but fiat, with no metal behind it, its value resting on the credibility of a central bank and on a state's willingness to accept it in taxes; central banks that create reserves electronically in vast quantities and use them to buy assets; and then the more recent inventions — Bitcoin and its fixed-supply imitators, so-called stablecoins tethered to conventional currencies, and central bank digital currencies. I want a projection that is genuinely argued from his commitments rather than a slogan dressed up as history: which of these he would greet as a fulfilment of the ticket idea and which as a perversion of it, and where the ticket idea itself would come under strain — say, from a currency whose supply is fixed by algorithm rather than by any authority, or from the thought that a token's usefulness depends on a stable demand that nothing guarantees.

I would rather you push back than indulge me. If projecting a writer of the 1730s onto proof-of-stake consensus or CBDCs is anachronistic in a way that makes the question ill-posed, or if The Querist is too occasional and polemical a text to yield these predictions, say so plainly and tell me what can and cannot be responsibly inferred, rather than manufacturing a verdict. Where you do project, mark the difference between what follows from his stated views and what is your own extrapolation. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
