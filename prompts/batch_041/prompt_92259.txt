Ok, I'm writing a piece on the oldest headache in epistemology: you believe something, you back it with another belief, that one needs backing too, and next thing you know you're either going in circles, signing up for an infinite chain, or just planting a flag and declaring some belief "basic." Before I write the explainer I want the spec first. What does a theory of justification actually have to be able to do?

Give me the requirements list, and for each one the case or intuition behind it. I'm thinking stuff like: it has to make room for my ordinary beliefs — that there's a cup on my desk, that 7+5=12, that the friend who told me the party's on Saturday isn't lying to me — while still explaining why the flat-earthers equally sincere belief comes out worse. It has to keep "justified" and "true" from collapsing into each other. It has to handle the fact that justification comes in degrees and can get defeated later on (I'm justified right up until you spring the fake-barn setup on me). It should say something about whether "I've got no reason to doubt it" is ever the same thing as "I've got a reason to believe it." And for the piece I really want a requirement about action: everybody decides things on less-than-airtight evidence, so a theory that only speaks to the ideal case isn't going to help me explain anything real.

Then the part I actually care about. Which of these are genuine requirements and which are just house rules of one camp — the foundationalists insisting the chain has to stop somewhere, the coherentists saying it can loop so long as the loop is big enough, the infinitists refusing to stop at all. Is there some subset of the requirements that no view can satisfy together? If there is, say which, and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most contenders. Skip the ranking of the three views, I'll handle that myself in the piece.

One page, 700 words max.
