On Saturday I give the closing speech at the twenty-fifth anniversary dinner of the Halstead Fund, the small family foundation I have served as a trustee for a decade. Two months ago the board rewrote our grant guidelines around a single rule: every discretionary dollar goes to the worst-off applicants we can identify. Our chair defends the rule with a sentence that makes half the room flinch — that she would rather everyone improve a little less than watch the gap between our neighborhoods widen. The donors need to leave convinced their money is not being spent holding anyone back; the community partners have heard "worst-off first" used for years as an excuse to fund the same three organizations, and will not sit still for a lecture. One of our trustees, who teaches philosophy, warned at the last board meeting that the chair's sentence defends equality rather than priority, and that it is an open invitation to the leveling-down objection; we will end up defending a view we do not hold. Write me the six-minute speech. It should honor the worst-off-first rule, explain in ordinary language what that rule is and is not committed to, say plainly what we are forgoing by adopting it, and answer the case already circulating in the room: a proposed literacy program that would raise every child's reading level but raise the strongest readers furthest. Our largest restricted fund, left by the founder, can be spent only on a program that mostly serves middle-income families, and the speech will have to account for that too. I want an answer that survives the leveling-down worry rather than dodging it.
