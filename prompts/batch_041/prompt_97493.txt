I'm on the steering committee for a local currency thing in my town — the main employer shut down two years ago and a group of us are trying to launch a scrip that only circulates locally, plus maybe a credit-clearing scheme between the small shops. I somehow got designated the "philosophy guy" because I did a year of a PhD before dropping out, so now every meeting someone asks me what the *point* of money even is and I mumble something about trust and then feel like an idiot.

Anyway I've been reading Berkeley's economic stuff — the Querist, and the thing he wrote about a national bank for Ireland in the 1730s — and it's been weirdly useful. His basic move seems to be that money isn't really a thing, it's a ticket or a counter, and what actually matters is whether there's enough of it circulating to keep people working, and that the supply can be expanded by public credit without any gold behind it. Which sounds kind of modern.

So here's what I want you to do: predict Berkeley's views on stuff that came way after him. Not the vague stuff — I want the specific positions. What would he say about central bank digital currency, where the state issues the token directly and there's no bank in the middle? Would he see Bitcoin as vindicating his view (money with no intrinsic value, purely a convention) or as a disaster (no sovereign issuer, no tie to the industry of the people)? Where would he come down on the modern argument that a sovereign that issues its own currency can't be forced into default, and on the counterargument that printing to fund deficits just inflates? And would his idealism about matter make him more or less comfortable with money being pure information, or is that a false parallel that I should stop bringing up at meetings?

Be concrete and take positions. If his views are genuinely underdetermined by what he wrote, say which way he'd probably lean and why, rather than hedging everything.
