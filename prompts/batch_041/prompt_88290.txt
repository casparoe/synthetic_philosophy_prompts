For the past three years, since retiring from a career spent largely on low-temperature calorimetry and the thermodynamics of small systems, I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in the philosophy of physics at the university near my home — a pleasant and faintly humbling way to discover how much of what I took for granted as a working scientist was never as settled as I assumed. This term the seminar has been working through the foundations of statistical mechanics, and we have given two sessions to Maxwell's demon. I have been asked to contribute one essay question to the take-home final, and I would like you to draft it for me, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The room is mixed — philosophy graduate students and physics graduate students, all with a first course in statistical mechanics behind them — the exercise is a 90-minute in-class essay, and a good answer should run to roughly 1000 words.

The difficulty with the topic is that everyone arrives already holding the standard resolution, which has hardened into a kind of catechism: the demon must record what it measures, the record must eventually be wiped, and Landauer's bound — at least kT ln 2 of dissipation per bit erased — cancels whatever work the demon extracted, so the second law survives. I do not want an essay that merely recites this. I want a question that compels the candidate to say precisely what is doing the explanatory work in that resolution and then to test it against cases where the accounting turns genuinely uncomfortable. Specifically, I would like the question to force confrontation with three things: first, the demon whose memory is never erased but merely appended to, so that the compensating cost is relocated into the entropy of a growing register rather than concentrated in a discrete erasure event; second, the demon that measures a single molecule once and then stops, where the Landauer bound — being a statement about the average over many runs — need not bind on any single trial, so that the demon appears to win on some individual runs; and third, the suspicion, which I confess I share, that the whole exorcism is a bookkeeping convention about where one draws the boundary of the system rather than an independent physical constraint, and that the appeal to Landauer's principle is therefore either circular, if the principle is itself a consequence of the second law, or else doing no work at all. The question should also require the candidate to say which reading of the second law — as a strict law governing individual systems or as a statistical statement about ensembles — the exorcism presupposes, and to commit to a verdict rather than retreating into a survey of opinions. If one of my three probes strikes you as misconceived, replace it with a sharper one, but keep the question trained on the exorcism itself.

Please write the essay question (with whatever brief framing paragraph an exam would naturally carry), a model answer of about 800 to 900 words of the sort a very strong candidate might produce, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks. The scheme should name the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from a merely competent one — for instance, distinguishing the cost of measurement from the cost of erasure; noticing that erasing a bit whose value is already known to the eraser is not the same operation as erasing one whose value is unknown, so the demon's memory is not automatically the sink that rescues the second law; and recognizing that logical irreversibility and thermodynamic irreversibility are independent notions. For each must-hit point, give the marks it carries and a one-line note on the way candidates typically get it wrong. Please do not ask for citations, dates, or any review of the literature: the candidates will have no library access during the exercise, and what I want to see is whether they can think the problem through rather than whether they have read around it. Likewise, do not let the question lean on any particular author's formulation — it should stand on its own physics and its own philosophy.
