Okay so I keep tripping over the same thing when I think about iterated elimination of dominated strategies, and I'm hoping you can lay out the actual conceptual structure for me, because the textbook version feels like its hiding the interesting part.

The part I get: if you iterate eliminating strictly dominated strategies, the order you do it in doesn't change where you end up. But if you iterate eliminating weakly dominated strategies, order totally does matter — different orders leave different sets standing, and you can even delete a strategy that gets used in some Nash equilibrium. Fine, I believe it.

What I don't get is the mixed-strategy bit. Take a player with three rows, against an opponent with two columns, and suppose the row player's payoffs are: row A = (2, 0), row B = (0, 2), row C = (0.9, 0.9). Row C isn't beaten by A on its own (it beats A in the second column) and isn't beaten by B on its own (it beats B in the first). But it's strictly worse than a 50-50 mix of A and B, which gives (1, 1) in both columns. So C is strictly dominated — just not by any pure strategy.

Here's what I want to understand, at the level of "why is this true and not just a technicality":

1. When people define iterated elimination, does it matter whether the thing doing the dominating is allowed to be a mixed strategy? If a strategy is only ever dominated by a mixture and never by a pure strategy, is it "in" or "out" of the surviving set, and does that choice change the final set once you iterate? I have a vague memory that letting mixtures do the dominating lines the procedure up with some other solution concept — never-a-best-response / rationalizability type stuff — but I can't reconstruct why.

2. Why is the strict-versus-weak asymmetry the way it is? What is it about weak dominance specifically that makes iteration order-dependent and lets it kill off equilibrium strategies, whereas strict dominance is immune to all that?

3. The epistemic story: why does "everyone is rational and everyone knows it" seem to license eliminating strictly dominated strategies but not weakly dominated ones? What extra assumption, if any, is needed to justify weak elimination, and is that assumption about knowledge, about trembles, or about something else entirely?

I don't want a formal proof dump — I want the conceptual skeleton, including the one or two spots where the intuition usually goes wrong. And if the whole mixed-dominators business turns out to be a red herring for any of the above, just say so.
