I teach a graduate reading group that is half philosophers and half theoretical computer scientists, and next month I have a single ninety-minute session on the hole argument. Do not explain the hole argument to me; I want a plan for how to explain it. The audience can handle finite structures, permutations, and isomorphism, but has almost no physics and no prior exposure to the argument; the one thing I can count on is that everyone has met the idea of a symmetry of a structure. Plan the session as a sequence of ideas, with the dependency order made explicit: which piece has to be in place before which, and why the sequence cannot be permuted without the argument arriving as a conjuring trick.

The example. I do not want the smooth-manifold picture of a diffeomorphism sliding the metric inside a region to carry the session. I want the load-bearing example to be a small finite structure the audience can enumerate by hand — something like a graph with a field on it, with a designated region fixed and a symmetry that fixes everything outside that region while moving something inside — so that the point is visible before any physics vocabulary arrives. Tell me what that example must have and what it must lack if it is to do the work without begging the question, and say where the standard continuous diagram should be introduced, if at all, and what it would add that the finite example cannot.

The misconception map. I expect this audience to reach for at least the following: that isomorphic means identical; that the argument is simply the observation that unlabelled structures have automorphisms and is therefore trivial; that any apparent indeterminacy is an artefact of how we model and so is harmless; that the argument refutes substantivalism outright; that it establishes indeterminism; and that it is about our ignorance rather than about what the theory says is the case. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be headed off, and which one, left standing, will make everything after it incoherent.

The ending. Give me one question I can put to the room that will show whether they have understood what the argument is an argument about — that it concerns what a theory of a manifold-with-fields admits as possible, and the commitments it forces on whoever holds a given view of what the fields are fields on — rather than whether they can recite a conclusion. As part of this, tell me whether the finite example can separate the reading on which the argument is a reductio of a principle of individuation from the reading on which it is an argument about ontology; if it cannot, say so and say what further case would. Finally, tell me what I should explicitly refuse to settle in the session and announce that I am refusing, and why settling it would spoil the session.

Keep everything reconstructible from the board; the session must not depend on any reading.
