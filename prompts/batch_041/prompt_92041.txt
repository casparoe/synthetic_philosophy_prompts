I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on consent — the group mixes law students, medical ethics students, and philosophers, so the instrument has to sort readers by the view they actually hold rather than by the label they would pick for themselves. Produce a decision tree on the validity of consent: the branch nodes are questions, the leaves are positions.

Every branch node must be answerable by taking a side on a concrete case rather than by reciting a definition, and each node must be one on which two otherwise similar views deliver different verdicts. Use the following cases, all of which should appear somewhere on the tree, and add at most one of your own invention:

1. A surgeon omits a one-in-ten-thousand risk of a serious complication; the patient, had she been told, would have gone ahead anyway.
2. A agrees to sex with B under a false belief about B's intentions, marital status, or identity, while the physical act is exactly the act A took herself to be agreeing to.
3. A did not cause the harm now threatening B — illness, a third party's cruelty, an accident — and offers to remove it for a price B can pay. B pays.
4. A first worsens B's position and then offers to restore it in exchange for B's agreement; the offer is better than B's new baseline and worse than the old one.
5. B says yes while not wanting to, or wants to while saying no; treat the two variants as a single node with three possible answers.
6. B authorizes in advance whatever A judges necessary, in ignorance of the particulars, and the particulars turn out to be something B would have refused.
7. B consents at the time and revokes afterwards; ask whether the revocation reaches back and turns the earlier act into a violation.
8. B's preference for the arrangement is itself a product of long dependency, or of the practice in question; ask whether that defect, if it is one, lies in B's consent or in B's situation.
9. A threatens something trivial, B has no better option, and B agrees.

Build the tree in two stages. First state the two or three dimensions you are classifying along — for instance whether validity is a psychological fact or a normative status, whether it comes in degrees or all at once, and what baseline a threat must cross to count as coercive — and show which combinations of answers those dimensions allow. Then locate positions within that space and name the leaves. A leaf may be marked unnamed if nobody defends it, and a combination nobody occupies should appear as an empty branch with one sentence diagnosing it as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The tree must be able to reach at least the following verdicts, though the leaves need not be worded as I have worded them: consent is a state of the will, so a yes without the will is void; consent is a communicative act, so the will behind it is irrelevant; both are required; coercion always voids consent; coercion never voids consent but only makes the act wrong for some other reason; coercion voids consent only when the threat crosses the agent's moral baseline; coercion degrades consent without destroying it; deception voids consent only when it concerns the identity of the act; deception voids consent whenever it would have changed the decision; consent is necessary but not sufficient for permissibility. At least eight leaves, no more than nine branch nodes.

No citations, no author names, no reading list. Where you are confident that a position has a standard name, use it; where you are not, describe the position in a phrase and mark it unnamed rather than guessing at an attribution.

Close with two short notes: which pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate because they agree on every node, and for each such pair whether the disagreement is merely about the rationale; and which single case, added to the tree, would separate each pair.
