I finished a mathematics degree last spring and started a PhD in philosophy of science in the autumn, and I have ended up as the only person in my cohort who arrived already persuaded that credences are the whole story. I have been drawn into a reading group on formal epistemology that meets on Friday afternoons in a seminar room whose radiator does not work, and two weeks ago we spent a session on how belief changes with evidence. Afterwards I had an argument with a colleague from the social-science side of the building that I have not been able to let go of. She holds that the phrase uncertain evidence names a confusion: whenever it looks as though you are revising your beliefs on the strength of something you are less than certain about, what is really happening is that you are certain of some fact about your own experience — what the dial looked like, what your memory felt like, what you took yourself to have heard — and you are simply conditioning on that. I answered that the manoeuvre either relocates all the uncertainty into an ever more elaborate description of the experience, or else it turns every careful researcher into a dogmatist about her own inner life, and we went round in circles until the building emptied.

Please write the dialogue we should have had instead. Let one speaker defend the orthodox line: that there is a genuine and important distinction between evidence you are certain of and evidence you are not, and that learning is, at bottom, conditioning on the former. Let the other defend the view that certainty is nothing more than a credence of one, that all evidence is in principle uncertain, and that the correct picture of learning is one in which an experience re-weights a set of propositions you have already divided the possibilities into, leaving the conditional probabilities within each of them untouched. Neither should be a straw man; each should be able to state the other's position fairly, and each should concede a point when a point is owed. When one raises an objection, the other should take it up and answer it rather than sail past it.

I would like them to grind through at least two concrete cases. One should be the kind that seems to embarrass both sides: a researcher who has believed a theory for years and then comes to see that the theory entails a fact she has known all along, so that the evidence cannot be news to her in any ordinary sense. The other should be one of your choosing, but please make it a case on which the two views deliver different verdicts rather than merely different descriptions of the same verdict. I also want them to settle two things I keep worrying at. First, I have a half-memory of being told that the rule the second speaker favours can give you different answers depending on the order in which two pieces of uncertain evidence arrive, whereas conditioning on certain evidence cannot; I want them to establish whether that is so, and if it is, to argue about whether it is a fatal defect or an artefact of an idealisation. Second, I want them to say where the substance of their disagreement ends and the bookkeeping begins — I have a strong suspicion that part of what separates them is a quarrel about how to describe a single process dressed up as a quarrel about how learning works, and I would like to see them locate the exact point at which the one turns into the other.
