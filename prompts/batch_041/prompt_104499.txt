Hi — I'm putting together the final for a third-year seminar on consciousness that I run, and I need one essay question plus a model answer and a marking scheme to go with it. The topic I want to hit is Dennett's distinction between "Orwellian" and "Stalinesque" explanations of the colour phi effect — you know the trick: two differently coloured spots flashed in quick succession at different points, and subjects report a single spot that travels across and changes colour mid-flight, which appears to happen before the second spot could possibly have been seen. Dennett says the two styles of explanation (the memory gets rewritten after the fact, versus the content being quietly resolved before it ever reaches experience) come to the same thing, and that there's no fact of the matter as to which is true, because both are just relapses into the Cartesian theatre picture. My problem is that the students can recite that. Half of them will write "Cartesian theatre, multiple drafts, no privileged moment" and think they're finished.

So I want a question that forces them to do something with it. What I have in mind is roughly this: a researcher claims to have settled the Orwellian/Stalinesque dispute empirically, by locating in the brain the moment the colour-change content gets stored in memory relative to the moment it becomes available for report, and says whichever comes first decides the case. I want students to work out whether that could possibly settle anything given what Dennett argues, and — this is the bit I actually care about — to seperate the claim that the dispute can't be settled by the subject's reports from the much stronger claim that there's no fact of the matter at all. A first-class answer should also say what a defender of the distinction has to give up in order to keep it, and what Dennett has to give up in order to deny it; I have a nagging feling his argument smells verificationist and I'd like to see them grapple with that rather than wave it away.

Could you write the exam question itself (aimed at a 45-minute essay), a model answer of about 600–700 words, and a marking scheme that genuinely separates an A from a B — name the specific moves a top answer has to make, the specific moves that earn a solid-but-not-top answer its marks, and the common misreadings that should cost marks, with marks attached (out of 20, say). Please write the whole thing in British English. And in your model answer, tell me how confident you are in the claims you're making and which single point you're least sure of — I don't want to hand out something I can't defend when a student pushes back.
