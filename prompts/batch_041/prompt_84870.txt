I run the performance dashboards for a mid-sized city agency — I'm the guy who stands up in the monthly stat meeting and walks each division through its numbers in front of the deputy mayor — and there's something about the whole arrangement I can't stop chewing on, though I don't know if its a real philosophical problem or just me being tired of the meetings.

Almost every number we use is a ratio. Numerator: the thing we want more or less of. Denominator: the population it could happen to. And the definitions are alive. When a division is going to miss, nobody in the room asks how to do better; they ask whether the case really counts. Is that call a request for service or an informational inquiry. Is that person an admission or an observation. Is that complaint founded, unfounded, or unfounded-with-a-note-in-the-file. Nobody lies, exactly — every reclassification has a reading you could defend to a judge — but the number moves. Everyone here calls it gaming, which I think is just a name for the symptom. We even have a change-control rule: definitions are supposed to be locked for the fiscal year so the trend line means something. We break it constantly, and everyone agrees that breaking it is sometimes the right call.

Here's the part that gets me. The same figure lands in the annual report as the record of what happened in the city this year, and it also sets next years budget and decides who gets promoted. It's the scoreboard and the archive at once. So when a reporter finds that a category got redefined and the count falls off a cliff, the official response is always the same: the underlying problem didn't get better or worse, the data is just noisy. Which assumes the real rate of X is sitting there behind the count, untouched, and all we did was fumble how we look at it.

I'm not sure there's a real rate behind the count for a lot of these. Whether it happened at all seems to depend on whether someone wrote it down and how they wrote it down. A robbery that nobody files as a robbery — is that a robbery we failed to count, or not a robbery? I genuinely can't tell if that's a distinction or a sophistry. A friend of mine who does sociology says it's a known thing, that once a number is used to judge people the number stops measuring the same thing, and that sometimes the thing being measured only exists in the shape the number takes.

So what does a city running itself on ratios actually have to believe? Does the practice need the assumption that every case has a fact of the matter that holds still independently of the form we file it in — that what's measured is indifferent to being measured, the way a weight is? Or does it survive on the other view, where the count partly makes the thing it counts? If the second one is right, does the practice fall apart, or does it just change what fixing the data means — are the audits and the independent re-coding we keep bolting on pointless, or do they do some other job? And is it incoherent to treat the number as gospel in the budget meeting and as mere noise when someone criticizes it, or is there a principled way to hold both? Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely happens to fit, and where it's underdetermined — I suspect several theories would license the same meetings.
