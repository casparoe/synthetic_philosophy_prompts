I'm a novelist by trade, though the book I'm finishing has pushed me into territory I'd always assumed belonged to other people: for the last two years I've been reading philosophy of science in a piecemeal, autodidact's way, mostly because I have a character who builds economic models for a living and I wanted to write his arguments from the inside rather than from the outside. Somewhere along the way I stopped being a spectator and started holding a view, and now I'd like to know how much trouble it's in. The view is this: the long quarrel over whether a model's assumptions must be realistic has been aimed at the wrong target. What matters is not whether an assumption is false -- nearly all of them are, in some measure -- but whether its falsity is of a kind that washes out as the model is made finer, or of a kind that propagates and infects the conclusion. A frictionless plane is a falsehood that annihilates itself in the limit; a representative agent who knows the future, or a market that clears by assumption, may be a falsehood that gets baked into the result. I want to say that the second kind is what critics of idealization should be attacking, and that the first kind deserves the immunity that defenders of unrealistic assumptions have always claimed for it -- the immunity I've seen called the F-twist when it's attributed to Friedman.

What I'd like from you is a map of what would count against this. Not a survey of the literature -- I can't follow citations and don't want them -- but an account of what the evidence and arguments would be if I'm wrong: which arguments, which concrete cases (historical or invented), and which formal results show that the distinction I'm drawing either collapses or fails to do the epistemic work I'm asking of it. I'd especially like you to press on the possibility that "washes out in the limit" is doing no real work, since any assumption might be redescribed as converging or not depending on how one sets up the limiting procedure, and the distinction might then be a rhetorical device rather than a criterion. Sort whatever you give me by how decisive it would be rather than merely how awkward, tell me which of these the view's own defenders would concede count against them and not only what its critics would say, and be candid about whether I have quietly insulated the position from any possible counterexample -- and if I have, whether that is a fatal defect in a claim of this kind or simply the price of any criterion worth having.

For what it's worth, the objection that would unsettle me most is not the charge of instrumentalism but the charge of emptiness -- the suggestion that my distinction is just the ordinary demand for good modelling dressed up as a philosophical thesis. So tell me, if you can, how my view stands to the position usually attributed to Friedman: whether I have merely restated his "assumptions don't matter" with better manners, or whether there is a genuine gap between saying that assumptions don't matter and saying that only certain of their falsehoods matter.
