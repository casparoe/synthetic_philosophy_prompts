For about a year now a reading group I sit in on has been working through the literature on the procreation asymmetry — the cluster of claims according to which bringing a miserable life into existence is bad, bringing a happy life into existence is not thereby good, and we are under no requirement to create happy people even when we easily could. Every session runs the same course. Someone states the asymmetry as if it were self-evident, in the form of a pair of verdicts about two imagined worlds that differ only in whether an extra life is added and whether that life goes well or badly; someone else observes that this looks like it lets existence function as a condition on being better off in the one case and not in the other; and then we spend an hour disagreeing about whether the second person has changed the subject. Nobody's position has budged, and I have started to suspect that the disagreement is not really about the cases at all. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than another round of the argument: is this a substantive disagreement about value, a verbal one about what words like 'good' and 'better' are doing here (for instance, whether 'better' presupposes someone for whom things are better), or two groups answering different questions — one about reasons for action and one about the ranking of outcomes — under a single label?

Here is a hypothesis I would like you to take seriously and then attack. The asymmetry, as usually stated, requires us to admit impersonal badness (a world is worse for containing suffering that belongs to no one in particular) while refusing impersonal goodness (a world is not better for containing extra happiness). I suspect the impasse is generated by that combination being a halfway house: it is stable only so long as we never ask what makes the two halves hang together, and each side in the argument is quietly pressing toward one of the pure positions — a fully impersonal axiology, on the one hand, or a strictly person-affecting restriction on reasons, on the other — from which the asymmetry looks either unmotivated or incoherent. If that is right, then the dispute is not stalled because the parties disagree about the facts or even about the intuitions; it is stalled because they are each treating a mongrel position as a fixed point. But I may be wrong, and I would rather you tell me so than agree. Someone also mentioned to me that there is a formal result showing that no way of ranking populations can satisfy a modest list of conditions we would all want it to satisfy; I cannot tell whether that result is the real engine of the impasse or a red herring that keeps getting dragged in.

So: diagnose the disagreement, using what the two sides actually say rather than a survey of who has said it, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, a demonstration that one of the positions collapses into its rival, an argument that the question is ill-posed, or a reason to think the impasse is simply what a hard question looks like. If you conclude that no progress is to be expected here, I would like to know why, and whether that is a defect in the question or a feature of it.
