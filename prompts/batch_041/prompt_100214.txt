On the whiteboard of the team I work on, someone has written as a kind of house proverb that the numbers worth watching are exactly the numbers that stop being worth watching once you start watching them, and it gets invoked to settle an unusually wide range of arguments. Last month it was used to justify keeping our service dashboards deliberately coarse and to refuse any numerical target for a support team's resolution times; this month a colleague is using it to argue that we cannot improve the reward model we are training for a summarization system, on the grounds that the moment you optimize such a measure it gets gamed, so any attempt to sharpen it is self-defeating. I do not believe these are all the same claim, and I cannot tell whether the load-bearing word is "worth" (the measure has stopped being an accurate indicator), "gamed" (agents begin optimizing it directly), or something definitional (a measure that survives optimization is by definition not the kind you optimize) — and someone insists it is just Goodhart's law, which I take to be a name rather than an argument.

Set out the distinct precise theses this proverb could be asserting. Where they are relevant, include a statistical claim about selection among cases, a causal claim about the world changing under the measure, a claim about behaviour far out in the tails where optimization drives you, a claim about deliberate gaming by parties with their own objectives, and a claim that holds only by definition. For each thesis, say whether it is a genuine law, a contingent empirical regularity, or a tautology in disguise; say which theses would actually license each of the three uses above and which one the colleague genuinely needs for the reward-model argument; and say plainly which reading leaves the proverb false or trivial rather than merely sobering. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
