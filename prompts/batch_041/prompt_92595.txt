I'm the instructor of record for a graduate seminar, cross-listed between cognitive science and philosophy, called "Analogy and the Architecture of Thought," and I'm drafting the take-home final. The seminar spent most of its time on the thesis that analogy-making is not a special-purpose faculty but the very fabric of cognition: that every act of categorization, every act of perception, and even the effortless retrieval of an ordinary word is itself an act of analogy-making, so that concepts are not fixed mental files but fluid, context-sensitive bundles of past analogies renegotiated on each occasion of use. The recurring frustration is that students find the thesis seductive precisely because it is so easy to restate and so hard to test — they will write a fluent page about the two-year-old who reports that she has undressed her banana, and about the scientist who saw light as particles after a century of wave theory, and never once say what would make the claim false or what it actually predicts. For the final I want a single essay question, answerable in ninety minutes, that cannot be answered by that kind of recitation.

I've decided the question must turn on the identity claim that categorizing and analogizing are one and the same process, and on a tension I have been trying to make them feel but have not yet found the right way to pose: if concepts are themselves nothing but the sediment of prior analogies, then there is no stable, pre-given representation for a later analogy to map onto, and the familiar picture in which an analogy is a mapping between two already-structured representations appears to have no room left to operate — yet the theory still owes an account of why a distant analogy can be retrieved, mapped, and then fail, and of what "distance" could even mean once concepts are fluid. I want the question to force students to state the strongest non-trivial version of the thesis, to specify what a decisive counterexample would look like and whether the thesis has instead been qualified into unfalsifiability, and to adjudicate this tension with the mapping picture rather than dissolving it into verbal agreement. To give the question teeth, I have been using a constructed case in lecture: an artificial system whose internal "concepts" are fixed, high-dimensional vectors, which nevertheless passes a whole battery of analogical-transfer tests drawn from the kinds of problems the seminar treated. I want students to say what the fluid-concepts theory predicts about where such a system must eventually break down, whether that prediction is a genuine risk the theory runs or merely an article of faith, and how the mapping picture would assess the same case differently.

Please write the essay question itself; then a model answer of roughly 900 words, in continuous prose rather than a bulleted list, of the sort a first-rate student could plausibly produce in the time allowed; and then a marking scheme worth 30 marks that names the specific moves a top answer must contain — including at least one move that separates an A from a solid B — with, for each move, a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong and where the marks are lost. The model answer and the marking scheme should not require students to name, cite, or quote any published works; the exam is closed-book and the library is shut over the break, so the question has to be answerable from the ideas alone.
