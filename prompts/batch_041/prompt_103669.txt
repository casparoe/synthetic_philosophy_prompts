I run a fortnightly seminar for graduate students in philosophy and cognitive science. They can all state the difference between evidential and causal decision theory and have argued about Newcomb's problem more than once, but almost none of them has thought seriously about Roko's basilisk; the few who have know it as a piece of internet folklore and are confident that it is either a joke or a straightforward blackmail scenario. Next term I have seventy-five minutes with them on this. I want you to plan the session, not to give me the argument.

What I need from the plan, in this order. First, the sequence in which the components have to be introduced, and why each depends on the one before it: what has to be in place before a punishment that no one is threatening can even be intelligible as a reason for acting now, and which of the premises is genuinely load-bearing rather than decoration. Second, the one case that should carry the whole session. I am inclined to build on Newcomb's problem, since they already own it, but I suspect that is a mistake and I want you to say whether it is and why. If you propose something else, it should be a case I can present cold to this audience, and you should tell me what it must have and what it must conspicuously lack -- for instance, whether it may involve anything that looks like a threatener, backward causation, or an actual communication from anyone. Third, the misconceptions they will arrive with or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each has to be headed off: my guesses are that they will treat the basilisk as a threat from a future agent, so that ignoring it is like ignoring a blackmailer; that they will file it under Pascal's wager and recycle the standard objections; that they will think the punishment matters only if they are in fact simulated, so the expected disvalue is negligible; that they will conclude the safe move is simply never to have considered it; and that they will hear the whole thing as a claim about a particular future machine's power rather than about the logic of their present choice. Fourth, the one question I should put at the end to tell whether they have understood the argument as an argument rather than merely been made uneasy -- something whose answer would come out differently for a student who has actually located the premise she rejects. Fifth, what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say out loud that I am leaving out.

One constraint on the arc: I would like the session to be able to reach the argument's conclusion, and its weakest joint, before the name is ever mentioned, so that they are assessing reasoning rather than reacting to a meme; tell me where, if anywhere, naming it becomes unavoidable, and what changes once it is named.

Give me continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to a colleague -- no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold or italic for emphasis, and no tables.
