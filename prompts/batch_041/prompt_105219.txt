A sustained disagreement has run for years between two camps concerned with whether fundamental physics is a site of morally weighty suffering, and you are to draft the joint statement the two camps could jointly sign.

The first camp, the valence-realists, holds that if experience is fundamental, whether in a panpsychist or a protophenomenal form, then the minimal experiences attaching to fundamental entities and events may already carry negative valence, and because microphysical events so vastly outnumber neural ones, the moral stakes of fundamental physics could exceed those of all of biology. The second camp, the integrationists, holds that valence cannot attach to an isolated micro-event at all, since it requires something like a unified subject for whom states can go better or worse; no accumulation of micro-experience therefore amounts to suffering without a certain kind of organization.

To force the dispute into contact, both camps have provisionally adopted the following shared vocabulary, which you must use throughout: a flicker is a candidate minimal episode of experience attached to a single fundamental entity or event, such as a field excitation, a decoherence event, or a vacuum fluctuation; a valence assignment is a rule mapping a flicker onto a good-bad axis; an aggregation rule specifies how flicker-valences combine into moral weight; and the subject-formation threshold is whatever is required for a collection of flickers to belong to a single subject.

Produce the document the collaboration would produce:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims, covering what panpsychism in the broad sense does and does not entail, what would have to be true for physics to count as an ethical domain, and what shape any defensible aggregation rule must take.

2. The precise points of residual disagreement, presented as a table with a neutral formulation of each dispute alongside each camp's own preferred wording, phrased so that the camp holding it would accept the wording.

3. The cruxes, that is, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. I suspect one crux is whether valence requires temporal extension or a subject at all, and another is whether moral weight is linear in the number of flickers or is instead gated by organization. State plainly whether you think any part of the dispute is merely verbal, and if so, which part.

4. For each crux, a case or argument both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Consider, for example, a long-lived coherent quantum state, a Bose-Einstein condensate, the interior of a black hole, or an error-corrected logical qubit, and say what each camp predicts about the flickers involved.

Write in American English and keep the register formal, as a document both camps could put their names to.
