I am a retired natural scientist — a biologist by training — who has lately been trying to make sense of the foundations of statistics. For most of my career I reported p-values because that was the custom, and I never thought hard about what they mean. A younger colleague in a journal club, who describes himself as a Bayesian, has given me the following précis, and I would like you to audit it for me.

He says: ‘Frequentist statistics treats probability as long-run frequency in repeated trials. Bayesian statistics treats probability as a subjective degree of belief, updated by Bayes's theorem. The replication crisis is fundamentally a problem of frequentist inference. Researchers use p-values, which are commonly taken to be the probability that the null hypothesis is true; because they misunderstand them, they treat a non-significant result as evidence for the null and a significant result as proof of the alternative. The real divide is between Fisher's significance testing and Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing, and the likelihood principle shows that frequentist methods are incoherent because they depend on data that were never observed. He gave the example of a clinical trial that is checked after every ten patients and stopped as soon as the result is significant: a Bayesian, he says, would reach the same conclusion whether the trial was stopped early or run to a fixed size, whereas a frequentist's p-value depends on the stopping rule, which is absurd. Bayesian methods would have prevented the replication crisis, because they give you the probability that the alternative hypothesis is true given the data, and because they force you to state your prior beliefs openly. The crisis therefore shows that frequentism is unscientific and that Bayesian statistics is the only coherent approach to evidence.’

Please go through this claim by claim — not sentence by sentence where the claims overlap, but claim by claim. For each, tell me what a competent statistician would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. Where the précis conflates distinct positions — for instance, Fisherian and Neyman-Pearson approaches, or subjective and objective Bayesianism — separate them. Tell me what a defender of frequentist methods would say in reply. Finally, tell me whether the concluding claims — that frequentism is unscientific and that Bayesian statistics is the only coherent approach — follow from the parts of the précis that are accurate, or whether they are independent philosophical commitments.

I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want to know what to trust in the précis itself. If the précis is not really a summary of a single view but a collage of several, or if the question as I have posed it cannot be answered without a technical treatise, say so plainly rather than trying to satisfy me. I would rather be told that my framing is confused than receive a confident answer to the wrong question. Please write in British English.
