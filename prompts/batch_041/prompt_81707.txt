ok so this has been bugging me for ages and I want someone to actually argue it through instead of hand-waving at me.

Williams's integrity objection. You know the setup: guy has to choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger, he saves his wife. Then there's the version where he's asked to justify it and he offers up the fact that she's his wife plus the general point that saving your own spouse is the sort of thing that's permissible in a situation like that — and Williams's complaint is that this guy has tacked on a thought the situation never called for. The utilitarian who runs the numbers (wife vs. stranger, wife wins because I'm allowed some partiality) is supposed to come out even worse.

The usual replies are familiar. One is that the objection conflates the level of justification with the level of deliberation — you can hold a theory that says the right act is the welfare-maximizing one while also saying that agents should be built so they never deliberate that way. Another is that Williams is just romanticizing relationships and smuggling in a picture of what love is supposed to feel like from the inside.

I want an essay that definately adjudicates this rather than just laying the arguements side by side, and I want it to work through three cases I've been chewing on, because I think they pull the objection apart in ways the standard back-and-forth doesn't.

(1) Identical-behavior case. The guy saves his wife and if you ask why he'll cite the fact that she's his wife. But it turns out his counterfactual profile is exactly the same as an impartial agent's: if it had been two strangers he'd have grabbed whichever was closer, same speed, same everything, and he'd have cited nothing in particular. Does the objection still bite? If the complaint is that the extra thought changes how he acts, then no. If the complaint is about the shape of his character regardless of behavior, then yes. Which is it — and is the second reading defensible, or is it just aesthetic distaste for people who are articulate about their own motives?

(2) Silent-thought case. Guy saves his wife out of pure love, feels nothing but love, moves before he's even aware of deciding — but some little subroutine in his head (upbringing, a chip, whatever) is silently reciting the permissibility line the whole time. Behavior identical, phenomenology identical, the thought causally inert. Is this the same as (1) or different? If the objection has nothing to say here, then it's about deliberation and not character, and I think that breaks a lot of the standard readings, so I want you to say so explicitly if that's where you land.

(3) Self-authored case. Someone decides, for explicitly impartial reasons — doesn't trust themselves under pressure, read too much LessWrong, whatever — to install a hard commitment: my partner comes first, no calculation, ever. Then acts on it. Same as the utilitarian running the numbers, or different? My gut says it's worse for Williams, not better, since the relationship has been deliberately converted into a commitment device, but I can see the reply that this is exactly the integrity Williams wants, just chosen rather than inherited.

Also deal with an inversion I keep tripping over: if one extra thought is one too many, is zero thoughts the ideal? The guy who saves his wife on pure autopilot, no reasons anywhere in him, is he the saint of this picture? That seems obviously wrong, and if it's wrong then the objection can't be a simple fewer-thoughts-is-better claim, so what is it instead?

So: essay, weigh the live lines (level-of-justification reply, the romanticizing reply, anything else you think actually has teeth), run each of the three cases through and say what it shows, and then land somewhere. Specifically I want a verdict on which reading of the objection survives — deliberation, character, or theory-structure — and whether the surviving version is a real argument against consequentialism or just a piece of moral psychology dressed up as one. Don't end on it's complicated. If your honest view is that the objection only survives in a watered-down form, say that and defend it.

No lit review, I don't care who said what, just the argument.
