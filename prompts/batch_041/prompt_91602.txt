Bit of an odd request, but here's where I'm at. I facilitate a standing stakeholder table on a regional river-basin water dispute — three camps, roughly: an irrigators' association, a land-conservation trust, and a tribal nation whose treaty water rights are in play. We've been meeting monthly for about a year, and the strange thing is that all three can sign off on the same package on the numbers and still leave feeling like something was done *to* them rather than *with* them. I've started to suspect the problem isn't the substance, it's that we keep treating "meet in the middle" as if it were a genuine improvement when really it's whoever is loudest getting a discount on what they wanted. A friend in my evening ethics class — we've been doing a unit on whether values can be traded off at all — put a half-formed idea to me: a compromise is only a real compromise, the kind where everyone is actually better off by their own lights, when each side gives up something it ranks *low* in exchange for something it ranks *high*, basically a trade; and that this whole picture only works if there's some common currency of concession, which for values there maybe isn't. I half believe it, but I can't run a philosophy seminar in a room where people have lawyers.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually use at the table — something I can walk through in the twenty minutes before a session, without any training in formal decision theory, and that I can say out loud to the parties afterward so it isn't a black box. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it has to hand the call to a human because the thing being asked is a values question rather than a factual one. I care a lot about two distinctions surviving the trip: (a) the difference between a side ranking something low and a side being *unable* to rank it at all — some things are, for these people, conversation-stoppers, and a procedure that tries to price them is worse than useless; and (b) the difference between a concession that's freely made and one that's just coercion with a nicer name, since a "trade" extracted by the fact that one party can hold the whole basin hostage isn't a gain from trade at all. Then run it on three proposals: a winter-release-for-irrigation-cap package that the irrigators and the trust both like; a "cultural water" allocation that's hard to fit into any of our usual units; and a package where two of the three trade away something the third treats as non-negotiable. At least one of the three should come out as a case where the procedure tells me the deal I personally favor is illegitimate, because if it never bites me it isn't a real test. And be honest in the write-up about which steps are just my judgment dressed up as procedure — I'd rather know where the seams are than have it look cleaner than it is.
