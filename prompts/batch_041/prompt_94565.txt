Two camps in normative economics and ethics have argued for decades about what Harsanyi's aggregation theorem establishes, and have largely talked past each other. The first camp holds that the theorem is a proof of utilitarianism from premises nobody can reasonably reject: if each individual's preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms, if the social ordering does too, and if the social ordering respects unanimous individual preference, then the social ordering must be representable as a weighted sum of individual expected utilities, with a suitable impartiality condition fixing the weights as equal. The second camp holds that the theorem is at best a conditional representation result: the weights are supplied by a substantive assumption about how the social evaluator is to imagine herself as one of the members, and the unanimity principle on which the argument turns is defective in precisely the cases that matter, namely those in which individuals hold divergent beliefs about the world.

Write the joint statement the two camps should have produced after a genuine adversarial collaboration. Assume nobody will consult the literature: state whatever machinery the document needs in the document itself rather than gesturing at sources, and do not include citations, reading lists, or attributions.

The statement should contain the following.

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. These should cover what the theorem does and does not establish given its premises; the gap between the weighted-sum conclusion and the equal-weight utilitarian conclusion; which premises carry substantive moral content and which are merely formal; and which cases the two camps agree about (for instance, cases in which all members share a single probability distribution and identical risk attitudes, or cases in which one option is better for every member in every state).

2. A table of the disagreements that remain. For each row, give the question at issue, then each camp's answer worded as that camp would accept it. At minimum address: whether treating each member's prospects as equiprobable is a requirement of rationality or a moral postulate; whether the binding expression of respect for persons is unanimity over ex ante prospects or over realised outcomes; whether a lottery that everyone prefers ex ante but that is contested in every state counts as genuine agreement; and whether the theorem's conclusion justifies a social ordering or merely represents one already assumed.

3. A crux section. For each crux, state the sub-question, what each camp predicts, and a case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it. Work through the following, among others. Take two individuals, two states, and two options, L and M: in state 1, L yields payoffs (1, 0) and M yields (0.6, 0.6); in state 2, L yields (0, 1) and M yields (0.6, 0.6). Suppose the first individual takes state 1 to be much the more likely and the second takes state 2 to be much the more likely, so that each is confident of the state in which she does well, and both therefore prefer L. Record each camp's verdict on what the social ranking should be here, and its reason. Then record what each camp predicts for a case in which the two individuals share their probabilities but differ in risk attitude, and for a case in which the social evaluator is told the actual proportions of the population instead of being left ignorant of which member she is.

4. A short section on whether any part of the dispute is verbal -- in particular, whether the two camps mean different things by social welfare and are therefore answering different questions -- and, if so, which part.

5. A closing section giving, for each camp, what it would count as evidence or argument against its own position, and anything both camps agree would actually move the debate.
