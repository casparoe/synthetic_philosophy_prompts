You are a philosopher of language who works on vagueness, and I would like you to build me a teaching artifact rather than summarize a literature I already know. I am assembling a graduate seminar unit on the sorites, and I have grown dissatisfied with the standard illustrations — heaps, bald heads, colour strips, Theseus's ship — because they let students recite the four familiar positions without ever having to see what those positions actually disagree about. What I want instead is a case constructed to order: a sorites series engineered so that the four positions are forced to disagree not merely about where a vague predicate stops applying but about what makes it vague in the first place. Think the design through before you commit to it, and quietly discard candidate predicates that fail the constraints below; I want the finished construction, not the search that produced it.

The specifications are these. First, the predicate must be graded but non-perceptual, and its application must carry real institutional consequences — someone's entitlement, liability, access, or obligation should turn on it — so that the metaphysics has something riding on it. It must not be one of the textbook examples. Second, the scenario must present two versions of the same series: same underlying quantity, same items, same stakes, same institutions, differing in exactly one respect. In version A the operative standard is precise by stipulation, an explicit threshold written into a rule, statute, or contract; in version B the operative standard is left vague, in the way that terms such as substantial, significant, or reasonable are vague. Hold everything else fixed. Third, version B must exhibit higher-order vagueness: there must be no item that is definitely the last to satisfy the predicate, no item that is definitely the first to fail it, and no sharp boundary between the items that definitely satisfy it and those that are merely borderline. Fourth, the precision in version A must be semantic or institutional rather than epistemic; it must not be that the rule is vague while our instruments are coarse, and the series must remain a genuine continuum of the underlying quantity, so that no item is separated from its neighbours by a natural joint. Fifth, no item may be singled out by the story itself as the cutoff; the case has to be one the theories answer, not one the author answers by fiat.

The case exists to show that these four positions disagree about the source of vagueness and not only about its extent, so a construction that simply announces that item twelve is the borderline case defeats the purpose, and so does a construction whose two versions differ in anything other than the presence of a stipulation. With that in mind: give me the scenario; then, for each of epistemicism, supervaluationism with truth-value gaps, degree theory, and contextualism, state the verdict in version A and the verdict in version B — at minimum for the two extremes, the item nearest the middle, and the items immediately on either side of it — with one line of justification for each verdict. Then tell me which single feature of the construction is doing the work, what each theory's verdicts collapse to once that feature is removed, which of the four theories the case treats most unkindly and how a defender of that theory would try to defuse the case without abandoning the position, and finally where the construction is rigged or question-begging. Be explicit wherever a theory is being asked to say something its proponents would rather not say, and do not give me references or a reading list — the case has to stand on its own.
