I am preparing for a small joint seminar next month, organized between my department's statistical mechanics group and the philosophy department's philosophy-of-physics contingent, and I would like you to draft my Q&A prep sheet. I am presenting a deflationary thesis about Maxwell's demon, and I need to know what this particular room will throw at me.

The thesis, compressed: the demon is not defeated by the cost of erasing its memory, which is how the standard erasure-resolution runs, but by something logically prior to it. Any finite demon must carry a memory that is itself a physical system with free energy of its own, and you cannot spend that free energy and simultaneously use the very same degrees of freedom as an unbiased record of which molecules went where. The so-called information cost, on my account, is not an extra physical ingredient that arrives just in time to rescue the second law; it is a redescription of the free-energy cost of building, maintaining, and resetting the demon. My slogan is that the demon's memory is a battery, not a ledger. The paper's central move is to separate two things the literature runs together: thermodynamic information, meaning correlations that carry free energy and can be spent as work, and bookkeeping information, meaning Shannon information, which by itself does no work at all. I claim that the demon tradition equivocates between these, and that once you pull them apart the demon falls to ordinary thermodynamics, with no information-theoretic novelty doing the work.

The audience, in the order they will speak:

- The statistical mechanics group, who for the most part regard the demon as a closed case. For them the erasure cost is experimentally settled — a single bit of erasure costs kT ln 2 and no less — and my paper will sound like someone reopening a textbook exercise for the pleasure of it.
- One quantum information theorist, who will insist that the only interesting demon is the quantum one, where the demon's memory can be entangled with the system, correlations themselves become a fuel, and my neat ledger/battery distinction may be exactly what quantum thermodynamics is designed to blur.
- Two philosophers of physics: one who thinks "information" in physics is a metaphor that has been quietly reified into an entity, and one who is a committed Landauer partisan and will hear my thesis as a denial of something she takes to be established.
- The group's historian of science, who knows Maxwell's original setup and its intended moral and will be annoyed if I "solve" a problem Maxwell was not posing.

Please give me the prep sheet. I want the roughly eight objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones I would get at a general physics colloquium, ordered by how likely each is combined with how much damage it would do. Phrase each objection in the idiom of the person who will raise it, since the same point lands differently coming from the quantum information theorist than from the Landauer partisan. For each, give me a short reply I can deliver in two or three minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft — the place a sharp questioner will press. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than defend, and which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the hour. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
