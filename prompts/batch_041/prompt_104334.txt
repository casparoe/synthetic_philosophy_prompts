I'm a second-year PhD student working at the border of statistics and philosophy of science, and I've been having a running argument with a friend in my department about what Simpson's paradox actually shows. We agree on the textbook line, and neither of us needs it rehearsed: a reversal can be produced by a lurking variable, association is not causation, and once you pose a well-posed causal question the reversal usually stops looking paradoxical. The argument begins exactly where that line stops. I have been pressing the claim that the paradox exposes a real defect in the dominance-style reasoning we all rely on, the inference from better in every subgroup to better on the whole, because that inference is not invariant under changes of partition; and that every repair I have seen, including the one my friend favours, does not remove the defect but relocates it into a question the data cannot settle, namely which partition is the right one. My friend holds that the principle was never meant to survive a change of partition, that the correct partition is fixed by causal structure rather than by the numbers in the table, and that this supplies an independent criterion rather than a restatement of the conclusion. I would like you to write out the dialogue between two characters who hold these two positions, as a fair-minded exchange rather than a demolition of one side.

To keep it concrete, we have been working with a toy table: two treatments, A and B, and a binary pre-treatment covariate splitting patients into two groups. In group 1, A succeeds in 90 of 100 cases and B in 170 of 200. In group 2, A succeeds in 40 of 100 and B in 35 of 100. A therefore wins in both groups, while B wins overall, 205 of 300 against A's 130 of 200. Either character may use this table, or construct a sharper one if the argument needs it.

Both speakers should be competent and neither should be a strawman; each should answer the other's actual last point rather than restating a position, and each should get to press at least two distinct lines. I would especially like to see whether the defender of the causal criterion can state a test for the right partition that does not presuppose the conclusion, and whether the critic can produce a case in which two partitions are equally well grounded but yield opposite recommendations. My hunch is that the sharpest version of that case involves a covariate that is itself affected by the treatment, so that adjusting and not adjusting are both defensible; if the characters can reach that thought by their own route, so much the better, but they should not simply assert it and move on. The exchange should keep a concrete decision in view, which treatment to give, so that the disagreement about partitions surfaces as a disagreement about what to do and not merely about what to say. It need not end in agreement or in a verdict, and it should not strain for one. Neither character should assume the reader knows the technical vocabulary of causal inference; terms should be introduced in passing as they become necessary. Aim for something substantial, long enough to allow three or four genuine exchanges on each side rather than a pair of position statements, and do not worry about citations or about who has said what in the literature, since I know the literature and will add references myself.
