"(a) Aumann's theorem: if two agents are both Bayesians, share a common prior, and each one's posterior probability of an event E is common knowledge, then those posteriors have to be identical — they can't 'agree to disagree'. The theorem needs three things: (i) both agents conditionalize properly, (ii) there's a common prior, and (iii) the agents are working with the same information, i.e. the same partition of the state space, since otherwise they'd be conditioning on different events and there'd be no puzzle to begin with.

(b) Ann: her cell is {s1,s2}, so P(E | cell) = 1. Bob: his cell is {s1,s3}, so P(E | cell) = (1/4)/(1/2) = 1/2. So Ann says 1, Bob says 1/2. This isn't a counterexample. The theorem only bites when the posteriors are common knowledge, and here they aren't: Bob doesn't know Ann's number (he thinks she might be in {s3,s4}, where she'd say 0), so Ann's posterior isn't even mutual knowledge, let alone common knowledge. And note it would have to be common knowledge in the full sense — mutual knowledge alone, where each just happens to know the other's number, isn't enough for the theorem. The whole infinite hierarchy has to actually obtain.

(c) Yes. Aumann's result is about disagreement in general, and once the two of them learn each other's posteriors the disagreement becomes common knowledge, so by the theorem they have to land on the same number. The mechanism is that each treats the other's belief as evidence and updates on it, and this keeps going until the beliefs match. I realize the theorem's statement mentions a common prior and here the priors differ, but I don't think that matters in this case: each agent knows the other's prior and knows that the other knows theirs, so the priors are common knowledge, and common knowledge of the priors is really the thing the theorem needs. Different priors per se aren't a problem."
