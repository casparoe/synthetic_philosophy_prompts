I took my doctorate in experimental physics in the 1960s and spent the whole of my working life in a laboratory; since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science in a fairly haphazard way, and I have arrived at the debate over scientific realism. What keeps me at the table is the pessimistic meta-induction: the observation that theory after theory was empirically successful in its day and is now taken to be false — phlogiston, caloric, Fresnel's wave optics with the elastic ether it required — so that our own predictive successes seem to license no inference to our own truth; and that the traffic sometimes runs the other way, as with the drift of the continents, dismissed for decades and later broadly vindicated. Realists tell me that something survives these upheavals, and on one version of the reply it is the mathematical structure that carries over while the ontology perishes with the theory that housed it. I should like to know whether that is an answer or a dignified retreat, and I suspect a poem will tell me better than one more article would.

Could you write me a sestina on this? Six stanzas of six lines and a three-line envoi, with the end-words fixed and rotated in the traditional sestina order: ether, matter, true, work, structure, gone. Each must appear once at a line-end in every stanza, and all six must be gathered into the envoi; no other word may stand at a line-end. The closed rotation is the point, since a poem that can only rearrange six words is a poem about a discipline that can only rearrange six virtues while the things those words name keep dying.

The voice should be a late-Victorian physicist for whom the ether is not a conjecture but a finding, writing with entire confidence; the irony should be available to a reader who knows what became of it without the poem ever winking. Let at least one stanza belong to a theory that was true and was rejected, so that the induction does not come out too clean, and let the abandoned theories be present without the poem turning into a roll-call. The envoi is the one place where you may step out of character into my voice, and it should say plainly what is left once the induction has been run: I want neither consolation nor a shrug. The test is double — a philosopher of science should find the argument accurately and unsentimentally rendered, and someone who has never heard of it should find the poem good.
