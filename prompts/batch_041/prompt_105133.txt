A disagreement last week left me unable to say what I actually think the coherence argument for expected-utility maximization establishes, and the question has since become practical: I am now expected to advise on whether a system that cannot compare two of its own options is thereby defective. Please write me a decision tree that starts from the one step I take to be secure — that an agent can be made to lose for sure by a sequence of trades it accepts — and leads to a named position on what that shows about expected-utility maximization. Each branch node should be a contested step, phrased as a question I could answer without knowing where it leads: whether the defect lies in the preferences themselves or only in the agent's situation and counterparty; whether the agent must be a single locus of choice across time, or whether the argument can be run on time-slices each of which prefers its own trade; whether incompleteness and intransitivity are the same failure or different ones, and whether incompleteness only counts as a failure given that a choice must be made; whether what follows is a requirement or merely a permission; whether coherence of choice suffices or expected utility needs an extra condition such as independence; and whether the utility representation is unique or only fixed up to some class of transformations. Leaves should carry the position's name where it has one and be marked unnamed where it does not, each with a sentence on its most awkward commitment. I expect at least eight leaves, and I suspect the tree cannot separate incompleteness-is-permissible from incompleteness-is-a-non-exploitable-defect; flag that and any other collapse rather than papering over it.
