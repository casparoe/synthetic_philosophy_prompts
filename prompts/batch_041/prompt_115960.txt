I have been reading LessWrong and the surrounding rationalist blogosphere for about a decade, and I have watched the acausal threat and surrogate goal discussion resurface every couple of years. Last week at a meetup, two friends of mine, Mira and Dev, argued about exactly this for an hour, and I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move.

The setup, as they were using the terms: an acausal threat is a threat that works not by causally affecting you but by predicting your decision procedure and punishing a simulation or counterpart of you unless you comply. A surrogate goal is a goal you adopt specifically so that threats against you become counterproductive—for example, you commit now to a policy of, if threatened, pursuing something the threatener hates, so that a predictor sees no profit in threatening you.

Mira: "The surrogate goal is the right response. If a predictor threatens to torture a simulation of me unless I wire money to a stranger, I do not want to be the kind of agent that pays. But simply refusing to pay is fragile: the predictor can simulate my refusal and then threaten me with something else. A surrogate goal is more robust. I commit now to a policy: if I am ever threatened acausally, I will pursue a goal that the threatener finds maximally repugnant—say, I will donate to a cause it opposes, or I will become a threatener myself. Then the threatener, predicting this, has no incentive to threaten me in the first place."

Dev: "That only works if the threatener cannot tell the difference between your adopting the surrogate goal and your bluffing. But if it is a good predictor, it sees your decision procedure. If you genuinely adopt the goal, you have changed your utility function. That is not a free move. You will now do repugnant things in other scenarios, or at least you will have a goal that is costly to satisfy. And if you do not genuinely adopt it, it is not credible. So you are either paying a real cost or you are bluffing."

Mira: "Of course it is a real cost. That is the point. You pay a cost now to avoid a worse outcome later. And the cost can be small if you design the surrogate goal carefully. You do not have to become a full-blown threatener; you can commit to something like 'if threatened, I will give a large sum to a cause the threatener hates.' That is a bounded cost. And it is credible because you have actually adopted it."

Dev: "But then the threatener can just threaten you with something that triggers your surrogate goal in a way that is good for it. Suppose your surrogate goal is 'if threatened, donate to cause X.' If the threatener wants you to donate to cause X, it can threaten you. Now your defense is an attack surface. And if your surrogate goal is 'if threatened, do something the threatener hates,' then the threatener can pretend to be a different agent, or it can threaten you in a way that makes the surrogate goal fire but the threatener is not the target. The goal is underspecified."

Mira: "You are treating the threatener as a causal agent that can manipulate you. But acausal threats are about correlation, not manipulation. The threatener is not sending you a message; it is predicting your decision. If it predicts that threatening you will trigger your surrogate goal, it will not threaten you. If it predicts that threatening you will make you pay, it will. So the question is what your decision procedure outputs in the correlated equilibrium. Adopting a surrogate goal changes that output. It is not about hiding it; it is about actually being a different agent."

Dev: "Then you are in an equilibrium selection problem. There are multiple equilibria: one where no one threatens and no one adopts surrogate goals; one where everyone adopts surrogate goals and no one threatens; one where threateners threaten and some agents pay. You cannot just declare that you are in the good equilibrium. And if you adopt a surrogate goal, you might be exploited by other agents who do not adopt it. Suppose a third party knows you have a surrogate goal that makes you punish threateners. It can threaten you to make you punish its enemy. So your surrogate goal is a weapon others can aim."

Mira: "That is why the surrogate goal should be designed so that it is not exploitable. You can commit to punishing anyone who threatens you, including third parties. Then no one threatens you. And if someone does, you punish them. That is a deterrent. It is the same logic as a policy of never negotiating with terrorists. You take a hit in the short term, but you gain in the long term."

Dev: "But the analogy to terrorists is causal. In the causal case, you can actually refuse to negotiate, and the terrorist sees that. In the acausal case, the threatener is predicting your decision procedure. If you are the kind of agent that punishes threateners, it predicts that and does not threaten. So you never get to punish anyone. The surrogate goal is never activated. You have adopted a costly goal for a threat that never materializes. And if the threatener is not perfectly predictive, then you might get threatened anyway, and then you punish, but the punishment is costly and might not deter the actual threatener. So the expected value is unclear."

Mira: "That is true of any precommitment. You pay a cost to change the game. The fact that the threat never materializes is a success, not a failure. And the cost can be small. You are acting as if the surrogate goal has to be a huge deal, but it can be a minor modification. The alternative is being the kind of agent that pays acausal blackmail, which is much worse."

Dev: "I am not saying you should pay. I am saying the surrogate goal is a more complicated version of a simpler policy: never pay acausal blackmail. A simple policy is easier to commit to and less likely to backfire. The surrogate goal adds a new goal to your utility function, which can conflict with your other goals. A policy of never paying is just a constraint on your actions; you still want the same things. So the surrogate goal is overkill."

Mira: "A policy of never paying is itself a surrogate goal. It is a goal you adopt to make threats ineffective. The only difference is that it is a negative goal—do not pay—rather than a positive one. But the threatener predicts it just the same. And a negative goal is less robust: if the threatener threatens you with something other than payment, your policy does not tell you what to do. A positive surrogate goal gives you a response to any threat. So it is more general."

Dev: "But a positive surrogate goal can be worse than the threat. If the threatener threatens you with a small harm unless you do something small, and your surrogate goal is to do something huge in response, you have made yourself worse off. The threatener can exploit that by threatening you with a small harm to trigger a huge response that benefits it. So the surrogate goal has to be calibrated. And calibration is hard. A simple policy of never paying is calibrated by default: you just do not pay."

Mira: "You are assuming the threatener can choose the threat to trigger your surrogate goal in a way that benefits it. But if your surrogate goal is to punish the threatener, then any threat that triggers it makes the threatener worse off. So the threatener will not threaten you. The only way it benefits is if the threatener is not the target of the punishment, but you can define the surrogate goal to target whoever threatens you. So it is robust."

Dev: "Then the threatener can threaten you anonymously, or through a proxy, or it can be a different agent that does not care about your punishment. And if there are multiple threateners, your surrogate goal might make you punish one while the other benefits. So it is not as clean as you think."

Mira: "No defense is perfect. The question is whether the surrogate goal is better than the alternatives. I think it is."

Dev: "I think the alternatives are better: a simple no-pay policy, or just accepting that acausal threats are rare and not worth the cost of a surrogate goal."

Please adjudicate this exchange. I want a point-by-point verdict: for each of the main claims they make, say who is right, who is wrong, and why. Then say what both of them are missing—what the actual structure of the problem is, and what a correct position would look like. Do not just tell me who won overall; I want to know which specific moves are sound and which are confused. I am not interested in citations or reading lists; I want the arguments themselves.
