After forty years in a chemistry lab I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and last week's session was on the absent-minded driver — you're driving home, there are two turn-offs that look identical, you can't remember whether you've already passed the first one, you get nothing if you turn off too early, the jackpot if you turn off at the right one, and a consolation prize if you overshoot both. Our discussion went round in circles, so I'd like your take. Could you put the following responses into tiers, top tier = actually solves it and bottom tier = doesn't get off the ground, with a sentence or two on each saying why it lands where it does?

1. Pick a single probability of turning off and use it at both turn-offs, choosing whatever probability maximizes your expected payoff.
2. Don't decide at the turn-off at all; ask which rule you'd have wanted to install in yourself before setting out, and follow that.
3. Always keep going, since turning off at the first turn-off is the worst outcome while carrying on at least keeps the jackpot in play.
4. Flip a fair coin at each turn-off, since from behind the wheel the two turn-offs are indistinguishable and there's no reason to favor either.
5. Treat your choice now as evidence about what you'd choose at the other turn-off, and pick whichever option looks best once you've allowed for that correlation.
6. Call the whole thing a modelling artefact — a driver with no memory of having passed a turn-off isn't the kind of thing you can give advice to, and once you let him use a landmark or the odometer the puzzle evaporates.

I'm definately not confident about my list, so if something important is missing, say so.
