I'm a third-year cognitive science student, and I'm taking a seminar on animal minds that has a lab component, which is how I ended up spending most of my summer filming solitary digger wasps in a sandbox in the basement of the psych building. The routine goes like this: the female digs a burrow, flies off and stings a cricket so it's paralyzed but still alive, drags it back by the antennae, drops it right at the mouth of the burrow, goes down inside to check the tunnel, comes back up, and only then pulls the cricket down and seals everything. The grad student I work for showed me the party trick: wait until she's inside on the check, then nudge the cricket a few inches away. She pops out, finds it, drags it back to the mouth, and — instead of just hauling it in — goes back down for another check. We did this to the same wasp for like an hour and she never once skipped the check. My supervisor says there's a whole fight in the literature about this, though, and that if you keep at it long enough the wasp apparently does start adapting, which she thinks is the interesting part and not a footnote.

My roommate is a philosophy major and he talks about it alot. He says the wasp is a machine and that's the end of it, and then he said the thing that's been eating at me: "and you're no different, you just have more loops." I laughed it off, but then I noticed that I open the fridge, stare into it, close it, and walk away, and that when someone says something I say "what?" and then answer them before they even repeat themselves, and now I can't stop thinking about it, which is a wierd thing to be stuck on at 2am. My instinct is to say "but I can notice my loops and break them," but I suspect that move is doing way less work than I want it to, and I have to write a three-page response paper on this anyway, so I'd rather not hand in something that just says humans are special.

So what's the actual difference supposed to be here, if there is one?
