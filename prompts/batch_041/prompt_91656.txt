Longtime LW reader here, been lurking since the old days, and I have a question I can't seem to get a straight answer on from the Sequences or from anyone in the comments.

I keep going back to Dennett's Quining Qualia paper and I genuinely can't tell whether I understand the argument or whether I've just absorbed the vibe of it. The vibe is "qualia are an incoherent posit, stop talking about them." But every time I try to reconstruct the actual argument I end up either (a) agreeing with something trivial, or (b) agreeing with something that seems to just deny that experience exists, which can't be what he means.

So please walk me through the real structure of it, not the summary. The part I most want is the four-features thing. As I remember, the four are: ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly/immediately apprehensible (sometimes glossed as incorrigible). Each one, taken alone, sounds like it's just a plain description of what it's like to taste coffee. So where's the alleged conflict? Is it that intrinsic and private can't both be established by introspection? Is it that direct apprehension makes ineffability redundant? Or that if something is directly apprehensible then it can't be private in the relevant sense? Lay out which pairs actually clash, which of the four are load-bearing for the pro-qualia side, and which ones you could drop without losing the core intuition that there's something it's like.

Then the Chase and Sanborn coffee case, which I think is the actual heart of the paper. My memory: two longtime coffee drinkers, one says the taste is the same as always but he's lost his taste for it, the other says the taste itself changed and now it's bitter to him. Dennett's move is supposed to be that there's no fact of the matter about whose qualia changed. But I've never understood why that's an argument against qualia rather than an argument that we can't tell from the outside. Help me see the actual move. Is he claiming the question is indeterminate? That it's meaningless? That the reports are theory-laden and there's nothing underneath to be right or wrong about? Those are seperate claims and I think I've been conflating them, which is probably why I keep going in circles.

Here's the thing I actually want to get at, and I haven't seen anyone spell it out. The incoherence charge seems to treat qualia like a theoretical posit — the way phlogiston or caloric was a posit, where the entity has a job description and the job description turns out to be self-contradictory, so we should stop positing it. But most people who believe in qualia aren't positing them to explain anything. They're pointing at the most obvious thing in the world and saying "this." So does the incoherence argument even land on that target? And if the answer is "the concept is incoherent but the phenomenon is real, we just need a better concept," then what is the better concept supposed to look like, and how is that different from just being a functionalist who admits he has experiences? That last question is definately the one that bugs me most, because every time I try to answer it I end up saying something that sounds like I've given up and become a zombie.

Also, where does the argument actually stand now? I know there's an illusionist crew that picked up the ball and ran with it, and I know there are people who say Dennett basically begs the question by building incorrigibility and privacy into the definition and then showing those are hard to square with the rest. Which of those criticisms has teeth and which are just refusing to engage? And is there a version of the argument that survives even if you stipulate that there's something it's like, or does the whole thing only work if you're already a functionalist?

Please don't just summarize the paper. I want the joints of the argument, where the load-bearing steps are, and where a sympathetic but unconvinced reader should push back.
