I belong to a small reading group on welfare, autonomy and paternalism, and we have spent most of a term failing to settle a question that looked trivial in the first week. The question is when an attitude that someone sincerely reports — an aversion, a taste, an attachment, a settled contentment with the shape of their life — is to be counted as a preference that we have reason to respect, and when it is to be counted as a bias that we have reason to discount, correct, or keep out of the social calculus altogether. We agreed early on to hold the psychology fixed: nobody in the group disputes what the person wants, how strongly, how stably, or, so far as we can tell, how the attitude came about. The cases that divide us are of a familiar kind — a preference for a painful death over a medically managed one, a contentment with a life that looks to the rest of us deprived, a wish for a body that conforms to a norm we find objectionable — and the pattern of discussion is always the same. One of us calls the attitude a preference; another calls it a bias and offers a reason of the form 'it is a bias because it was produced by deprivation', or 'because it rests on an emotional reaction that is irrelevant to what is actually at stake', or 'because the person would disown it if they saw the matter clearly'; someone then asks how we could know which of those descriptions fits, given that we have already agreed on the psychology; and we are back where we started.

We tried a procedural test at one point, and it has made matters worse rather than better. The test was to present the same attitude twice, once wrapped in a flattering causal story and once in an unflattering one, and to see whether our verdicts tracked the attitude or the story. They tracked the story almost every time. Some of us take this to show that the distinction really does turn on causal history, and that our judgements are merely unreliable instruments for reading it off; others take it to show that the distinction is doing no descriptive work at all, and that what we are really doing when we call something a bias is announcing that it ought not to count — in which case the label comes after the moral theory rather than before it, and our apparent disagreement about the mind is a disagreement about what makes a life go well, wearing a psychological costume.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about the mind, a merely verbal one about the word 'preference', two groups answering different questions under a single heading, or a covert normative disagreement of the kind the third of us suspects? My own hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and not as a settled view, is that the distinction is downstream of the theory of welfare rather than upstream of it, so that no further psychology could settle it; if you think that is wrong, say where it goes wrong and what the disagreement really is instead. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction we have not drawn, a criterion we could apply, a change in the question we are asking, an empirical result that would matter — or, if you think the impasse is not the sort of thing that admits of progress, argue for that and say what we should do with the question instead. Please write in British English.
