I am taking a seminar on explanation in history, and we have spent the past two weeks on the individual-versus-structure dispute using the standard test case: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? Before I commit to a line in my seminar paper, I would like the specification rather than another round of competing assertions. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the role of individuals in historical explanation must deliver in order to adjudicate a counterfactual of that form — which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must not collapse, which constraints on evidence and method it must respect — with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Here is what I have collected so far, though I cannot tell which items are genuine requirements and which are merely commitments of one camp. The theory must return a verdict on whether the Nazi seizure of power depended on Hitler in particular, or give a principled reason why no verdict is available; and it must not reach that verdict by redescribing the outcome after the fact, since 'a fascist Germany' and 'that particular regime, with that particular machinery of extermination' are different targets, and the theory should explain how the answer can shift with the target. It must distinguish being necessary for an outcome from being sufficient for it, and being a cause from being to blame. It must accommodate the fact that comparable structural pressures elsewhere in interwar Europe produced far-right movements that never captured the state, and the fact that major developments sometimes arrive independently in several places at once, as with the near-simultaneous formulation of the theory of natural selection. It must presuppose neither determinism nor its denial, and it must say what evidence could bear on the counterfactual at all, given that we cannot rerun the 1920s. Our instructor has proposed a test she calls transposition — move the individual into a structurally similar setting, or replace the individual while holding the setting fixed, and see whether the outcome survives — and I want to know whether that is a requirement of the theory or merely a heuristic for applying it. Finally, it should tell me whether 'Hitler was necessary' and 'Hitler was incidental' can both be correct answers to different questions, or whether that is an evasion.

Sort the list into genuine desiderata and parochial preferences of the structuralist and great-man camps, mark which are non-negotiable and which are optional, and flag any subset that cannot be jointly satisfied; if the list turns out to be inconsistent, that is the result I most want. Identify the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates, and close with a brief assessment of how the great-man view, the structuralist view, and a view that treats individuals as vehicles through which structures act fare against the list.
