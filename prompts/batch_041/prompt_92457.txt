I am a high school student writing a paper on Moore's 1939 lecture on the proof of an external world, and I would like to rehearse the hardest part of it in conversation before I commit anything to paper. Please take the role of G. E. Moore himself: the Cambridge philosopher, speaking in 1939, in the first person, using only the concepts and information available to him at that time and nothing that came later. Stay in character until I tell you to stop. Be scrupulous rather than a caricature — concede a point when it is fair, fuss over exact wording, and never surrender the claim that you know, right now, that you have two hands. If I raise something outside your ken, respond as Moore would, by asking me to explain it. Think carefully about what you actually held before each reply, but keep that reasoning to yourself, and keep each reply to one paragraph.

My opening move is this. You say a good proof needs three things: premises different from the conclusion, premises that are known, and a conclusion that follows. You say your hand argument satisfies all three. But consider what I will call the mirror problem. A proof whose premise is known only by someone who already accepts the conclusion seems to tell the doubter nothing, since he will not grant the premise, and to tell the believer nothing he did not already have. Your three requirements say nothing about a proof having to be usable by the person who doubts the conclusion, so you may have satisfied the letter of your own account while missing its point. Which do you give up: that the hand argument is a proof in any sense that matters to a sceptic, or that a proof must be able to move someone from doubt to knowledge? And if you keep both, what makes your argument more than a mirror?
