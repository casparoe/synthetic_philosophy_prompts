Last night a friend and I argued for about three hours about statues and street names and now I've got a pile of gut verdicts and no idea what principle they add up to. My friend's line is that a monument is basically a speech act — the city, by leaving it up, is saying something in the person's name — so the test is whether the thing being said is something you'd be willing to say out loud today. I think that's roughly right and it still can't handle half my reactions, so I want you to test it against the cases, repair it or replace it, and then tell me what the best principle actually is even if it means I have to give up one of my verdicts. Dont worry about citing anybody, I just want the principle.

Here's what I think. (1) The bronze of the 19th-century doctor who founded the county hospital and also wrote a pamphlet arguing for sterilizing the unfit: take it down, and a new plaque explaining the pamphlet doesn't rescue it — I've seen that compromise in a couple of places and it just feels like we keep him up there and pay lip service. (2) The street named after a mayor from the 50s who was a hard segregationist, except nobody under 60 has any idea who he was — it's just the word we use for the road with the dentist on it: keep the name, and I feel weird about how little this bothers me next to (1). (3) The big granite thing in the park to the local boys who died in a war that the town's men volunteered for out of enthusiasm for a cause I think was bad: keep it, add context maybe, and I don't think I'm just being sentimental — it's addressed to the dead, not to the cause. (4) The plaque at the university honoring a chemist whose work genuinely matters and who was by all accounts a nightmare to his students, some of it stuff that would get him fired now: keep it; honoring the work isn't honoring the man's character. (5) A statue of a bandit from our local folk tales who in the stories definitely killed people: keep it, it's a story, nobody reads it as a moral endorsement. (6) Same statue as (1), but suppose the city sells it to a private museum that displays it as history: I'd be fine with that, which makes me think the problem is public standing and not the object. (7) The statue in the square put up in 1920 to celebrate an empire, which a generation later got adopted by the kids who hang out under it and by an immigrant neighborhood that treats it as their own landmark: keep, and I feel strongly about this one, which maybe means meaning can migrate out from under the original intent. (8) A statue of a guy who did nothing objectionable at all but it's genuinely hideous and the square would be better without it: I don't think ugliness is a reason to remove it, though I notice I'd happily move it. (9) A monument to the workers of the mill that's been the site of the town's Remembrance Day ceremony for a century: keep, even though the mill did some bad things, because the ceremony is doing work the monument's subject doesn't have to endorse.

So what gets (1) through (9)? My friend's version predicts (2) should go, since we're still saying the segregationist was worth a street, and (4) should go, since we're honoring a man who hurt people, and I don't want either. But I also can't just say it depends how bad the person was, because (3) and (9) are about genuinely bad stuff and I keep them. I suspect the real distinctions are (a) whether the thing asserts something or merely records something, (b) whether anyone living is currently being asked to walk past it and take it as addressed to them, and (c) whether current use has drifted far enough from the original that the original message is dead — but I can't get those to line up either, since I'm not sure (3) even fails (b). So: give me the principle stated precisely, say which of my nine verdicts each clause is doing the work for, say what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed (I'm especially curious about one where the person is still alive and one where the monument is to a group rather than a person), and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save. And tell me what would change your mind — what argument, or what fact about how commemoration actually works in practice, would flip you to the other principle, and where your reasoning is weakest. I'd rather have a principle with one clause too many that saves eight of nine than a tidy one that quietly rewrites what I think.
