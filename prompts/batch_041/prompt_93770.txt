I'm a journalist who writes a column about science and ideas, so I end up at alot of panels and dinners where people say things that sound deep and turn out to be doing three jobs at once. Last week I was on one about political scandals and I got into it with a guy from a think tank over a senator who's been loudly condemning a lobbying scandal while having taken money from the same industry years back. I said the condemnation still stands and should be taken seriously regardless of who's making it; he cut me off with 'people in glass houses shouldn't throw stones', delivered it like a mic drop, and half the room nodded. I'm fairly sure that proverb is doing about four different things at once and that at least one of them is nonsense, but I couldn't untangle them on my feet, and I'd like to before I file the piece.

So could you lay out the distinct precise claims 'people in glass houses shouldn't throw stones' could be making when someone trots it out in an argument about blame? I want a menu rather than a lecture. There's the flatly prudential reading (you'll just get hit back), the strong moral reading that the person has no standing to blame at all, the weaker one that their blame is insincere or self-serving, the evidential one that their blame gives the rest of us less reason to change anything, the idea that consistency between word and deed is a condition on the speech act itself rather than on the person, and whatever else is hiding in there that I've definately missed. For each, say whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or just false -- and crucially which of them would actually license what my guy did: ending the argument, treating the condemnation as void, treating it as void but only in this particular forum, or just refusing to hear it from him.

One more thing I'd like in there: tell me which reading people usually mean when they deploy it, because my suspicion is the rhetoric only works by sliding between a couple of them mid-sentence, and I want to be able to say which slide is happening. And if you can, note which readings leave the senator's actual conduct as wrong as it ever was, versus which ones quietly let him off the hook -- that distinction feels like the whole ballgame and I keep losing track of it.
