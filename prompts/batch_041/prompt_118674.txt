I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, and I keep running into a rhetorical move that I have never been able to translate into the world I actually inhabit. Someone will invoke Chesterton's fence to argue against deleting an old rule or institution, or will gesture at Burkean conservatism to defend incremental reform over root-and-branch redesign, and the conversation usually ends there. I work as a senior software engineer on a large, old codebase—a monolith with layers of undocumented behavior, feature flags, migration scripts, and a dependency graph that no single person understands. I am fluent in the vocabulary of version control, code review, regression tests, backwards compatibility, load-bearing hacks, incident postmortems, and the general experience of discovering that a piece of code that looks pointless is in fact keeping three other systems from falling over. I have no formal philosophy training, and I do not want a literature review, a reading list, or citations; I want the ideas restated in the terms I use every day.

Please restate the core of Burkean conservatism and the fence parable as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of maintaining a large legacy system. Treat Burke's picture of society as a partnership across generations as a kind of versioning contract, the conservative regard for prejudice and inherited practice as a respect for undocumented invariants and cached priors, and the preference for reform that conserves as refactoring under a strict behavior-preservation constraint. Treat the fence as an undocumented dependency: the reformer who wants to remove it is the engineer who wants to delete code whose purpose is not obvious. I am especially interested in two wrinkles that I think the standard telling glosses over. First, what happens when the fence was originally erected for a bad reason but has since become load-bearing because other systems adapted to its presence? Second, what happens when the fence is not a physical obstacle but a coordination equilibrium—everyone's expectations are calibrated to it, and removing it changes behavior even if no single component strictly requires it? Include these wrinkles if they fit the translation, and if they do not, say so in the residue report.

Then give me the residue report. Structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: The translation (500 to 700 words), in which you render the Burkean position and the fence parable in legacy-code terms as concretely as you can; What survives cleanly (150 to 250 words), listing the claims that map onto software maintenance without loss; What survives only with distortion (250 to 350 words), explaining what has to be bent or fudged to make the mapping work and what the distortion hides; The residue (250 to 350 words), identifying anything in the original conservative position or the fence parable that has no counterpart at all in the vocabulary of legacy code; and Verdict on the residue (150 to 250 words), saying whether the residue looks like a defect of the software vocabulary—something it cannot express—or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it appeared. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle in the assumption that every existing institution is an efficient solution to a real problem and then call that a translation. The interesting part is what does not carry over.
