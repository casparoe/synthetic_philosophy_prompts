After a career in experimental physics I have taken up philosophy in retirement, and I have lately been reading Augustine on lying. His position, as I understand it, is far stricter than anything I was trained to accept: the essence of a lie is the intention to deceive, so that a man may utter what is false and yet not lie, provided he believes it true, and may utter what is true and yet lie, if he takes it to be false; jokes and figurative speech escape the charge only because no deception is meant; and the well-meant lie — the falsehood that spares someone pain, or saves a life — is not for that reason licensed. I should like this framework brought to bear on Ian McEwan's Atonement. Briony's false identification of Robbie at thirteen is the obvious case, and I take it that Augustine would call it a lie notwithstanding her confusion and her youth; what puzzles me is the elderly Briony, who has spent a lifetime in fiction and who discloses at the end that the reunion she has narrated never took place. Is her invented ending a lie in Augustine's sense, or is it precisely the thing he exempts, since a novel does not set out to deceive? And if it is not a lie, does it follow that it wrongs nobody — or does it wrong the reader, in a way that Augustine's account, framed as it is around the neighbour one speaks to, has no means of expressing? I want a reading that is faithful both to McEwan's book and to Augustine's distinctions, including the places where the novel resists the verdict I am half inclined to draw.
