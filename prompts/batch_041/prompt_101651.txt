For most of my adult life I have assumed that pleasure is one thing—that there is some single quality, however hard to describe, present whenever someone is enjoying something and absent otherwise. The more I have turned the matter over, and the more I have read of what people who study it say, the less I believe it; and yet I cannot shake the feeling that the question ought to have a definite answer. What I want from you is not a verdict on which account of pleasure is correct, but a diagnosis of why the question keeps refusing to settle.

Here is what I have noticed on my own. The taste of something sweet I am craving is a pleasure, and it seems to be a sensation in my mouth. The relief when a headache finally lifts is a pleasure, but it seems to consist in the absence of a sensation rather than in any positive feeling—there is nothing it is like to be relieved in the way there is something it is like to taste sugar. The pleasure I take in a friend's good news is a pleasure, and it involves no sensation at all; it is a way of being pleased about something. Listening to a piece of music that moves me to sadness is a pleasure, though the very thing I am enjoying is the sadness. Scratching an itch is a pleasure, and there is the stranger case of pain that one has come to want and would not be without. If I look for a single feeling running through all of these, I cannot find one. If I say instead that what unites them is being pleased or liking, I seem to lose the bodily cases, where the pleasure does not seem to be about anything, and I worry that being pleased simply renames the phenomenon rather than explaining it.

I gather that something like this standoff is familiar: some people insist that pleasure must be a distinctive experience or feeling, others that it is an attitude of taking pleasure in something, and a third group that there is no common thread at all and that pleasure is a loose, disjunctive label we apply for practical purposes—rather as fruit picks out things with nothing in common but a role in our eating habits. Each position seems to handle some of my cases and to strain on others, and I have not seen anyone move the others an inch.

My hypothesis—offered as a hypothesis—is that the disagreement is not really about pleasure at all, but about what sort of question we are asking. Perhaps one side is asking for the essence of a natural kind while the other is asking what a word is for; perhaps one side wants an account that explains why pleasure is worth having while the other wants an account that classifies experiences; or perhaps the demand for a single common feature is itself the mistake, and the question of what pleasures have in common is malformed in something like the way the corresponding question about games is. But I may be wrong, and it may be a straightforward disagreement about the phenomena.

So: diagnose the impasse. Is this a verbal dispute about the word, two groups answering different questions under one heading, a substantive disagreement about experience, or a demand for an essence the phenomena simply do not have? Tell me what each side would have to concede for the debate to move, whether any single case could break the tie, and whether progress here would look like a distinction, a discovery, or the dissolution of the question. And if my hypothesis is wrong, say where it goes wrong.
