I am running a session of a philosophy reading group for advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students. They have had one course's worth of modal logic, and none of them has seen Fitch's paradox of knowability. I do not want the argument explained to me; I want a teaching plan for how I should explain it. The session is ninety minutes, and my aim is that the contradiction should strike them as something they could have found themselves rather than as a rabbit pulled from a hat.

Begin with the order of ideas. Tell me what each piece depends on, so that by the time the substitution goes through, every premise it needs is already in their hands and uncontroversial: what they must grant about the factivity of knowledge, about whether knowledge distributes over conjunction, about which sense of possibility the knowability principle is trading in, and about the bare existence of presently unknown truths. Say exactly where in the sequence each of these has to have been secured for the final move to land as a proof rather than a trick.

Then give me the carrying example. I want the whole session built on a single unknown truth, something mundane, decidable in principle, and as yet unknown, and I want you to say what such an example must have and must lack for the paradox to bite. In particular I want the plan to make clear why the example has to be a truth that is merely unknown rather than one that is unknowable, and what goes wrong if a student offers an example of the wrong kind.

Then the misconception map. I expect at least the following, and for each I want to know the point in the sequence where it should be headed off and how: that the argument refutes verificationism or antirealism outright, rather than a specific unrestricted principle; that the mischief lies in the self-referential-looking conjunct; that the argument equivocates on possibility; that the conclusion is that some truth is unknowable rather than that the unrestricted principle is false; and that the distribution of knowledge over conjunction is the obvious culprit. Add any I have not anticipated.

Two further things. First, I would like the session to end on a repair I have been mulling: keeping the knowability principle unrestricted, but reading the possibility it invokes as physical rather than logical possibility. Arrange the plan so this arrives as the natural final step, and tell me honestly whether it is a coherent repair or a non-starter. Second, tell me what I should openly decline to settle within the ninety minutes and say as much to the group.

Finish with the check for understanding: a single question I can put to them at the end that separates someone who has grasped why the unrestricted principle has to go from someone who has merely memorized the derivation.

If the way I have framed this is confused, if factivity and distribution are not really where the action is, or the repair I want them to reach is incoherent, say so plainly rather than building the plan around my framing.
