I run a small reading group on mechanism, agency, and responsibility, and we are closing the term with a session on sphexishness. Everyone will have read the anecdote in its usual form: the digger wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it by the antennae to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it there while she goes inside to inspect the nest, and if the cricket is nudged a few inches away during that inspection, she retrieves it, drags it back, and inspects again — a reset that can be triggered repeatedly, though the real insects do eventually vary what they do. The term itself comes from Hofstadter's essay on mechanizing creativity, the anecdote is one Dennett leaned on in an early paper about mechanism and responsibility, and Keijzer has since argued that the literature has repeated the story more rigidly than the wasps ever repeated themselves. What I want for the last ten minutes of the session is a poem that does this work rather than mentions it: a villanelle in iambic pentameter, nineteen lines, in which the two refrains are the two halves of the loop — one line for the leaving of the prey at the threshold, one for the going in to check — so that the form's own compulsory returns enact the reset rather than describing it. Let the refrains converge in the closing quatrain as villanelles do, but change a single word in the last iteration of the first refrain, so that the loop is broken by the smallest possible margin and the reader cannot tell whether what happened was insight, adaptation, or drift. Follow it with a coda of five to seven lines in a form that does not repeat itself at all — blank verse or loose free verse, your choice — which is where the experimenter's own repetition has to surface: she is the one who keeps moving the cricket back, she has done this before, she will do it again, and the poem should let that symmetry land without smugness. Constraints: do not use instinct or program as explanations anywhere in the poem, since those are the words the poem is supposed to be interrogating; do not decide whether there is anything it is like to be the wasp, and do not let the human's flexibility be asserted as a settled fact; avoid the robot-with-no-inside cliché and any line that could be swapped into a nature documentary without loss; and no archaic diction, no apostrophes to abstractions. Someone who knows the Hofstadter, Dennett, and Keijzer material should find the poem accurate to the dispute, including its uncertainty about the anecdote itself, and someone who has never heard of any of it should still find a good poem about a small creature at a door.

Begin your response with the poem itself. A title is fine if you want one, but there should be no preface, no framing sentence, no explanation of the form or of the references, and no note or commentary after the coda.
