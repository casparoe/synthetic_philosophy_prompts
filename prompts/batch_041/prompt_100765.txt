A colleague in economic history — someone whose judgment I generally trust — gave our reading group the following account of Max Weber's argument in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. I am reproducing it from my notes, so the wording is mine but the substance is hers.

Her summary: Weber's thesis is that the Protestant Reformation caused the rise of capitalism. Calvinism taught that God had already decided who was saved, and since no one could change that verdict, believers threw themselves into disciplined work as a way of proving they belonged among the elect. Worldly asceticism — denying oneself pleasure and ploughing the profits back into the enterprise — is what accumulated capital, and it produced the modern attitude in which profit is pursued rationally as an end in itself. Once capitalism was underway it no longer needed religion; Weber closes the book with a gloomy prediction that the rationalized work order will hold us captive permanently. The whole thing is a causal claim of the form culture causes economic structure, and in that respect it is the exact opposite of Marx, for whom the economy causes culture. Weber then tried to test the thesis by studying other religions and demonstrated that only Protestantism ever produced capitalism.

I want an accuracy audit, not a restatement of Weber. Go through the characterization claim by claim and separate three things: what a careful reader of Weber would accept as correct; what is a defensible popularization that a specialist would merely wince at; and what Weber would explicitly reject or what misstates his position. Please attend in particular to the following, and say plainly if you think the summary is right about any of them:

- whether the essay's central claim is a causal thesis about the rise of capitalism or something narrower about the spirit of capitalism and its affinity with a particular ethic;
- whether the vocabulary of a straightforward causal claim — culture causes economy — does justice to how Weber thought such explanations work, given his use of ideal types, elective affinity, and singular causal analysis;
- whether the description of predestination and the role of work in the believer's life is faithful, including whether work functioned as a cause of salvation or as a sign of it;
- whether the closing image of the rationalized order is fairly described as a prediction;
- whether the contrast with Marx is as clean as the summary suggests;
- and whether the comparative studies of other religions really functioned as a test that established Protestantism's uniqueness.

Finally, tell me whether the summary's concluding inference — that Weber offers a cultural explanation standing opposed to economic explanation — survives your corrections, or whether the corrections undermine it. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want the reasoning, and wherever the summary is wrong, an explicit statement of what the accurate version would be.
