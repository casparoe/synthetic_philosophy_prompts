I sit in the compliance function of a company that sells into both rich and poor countries, and I have been trying to work out what our anti-corruption apparatus actually believes. The pattern that puzzles me is this: whether a payment is called corruption seems to depend on who receives it and on what we say its purpose was, not on what it does. A bottle of whisky for the customs clerk who clears our shipments is a dismissable offence, even when the clerk is obliged to clear the shipment anyway, the whisky buys us nothing but courtesy, and no decision is changed; the same bottle for the procurement manager at a private customer, who genuinely does choose between us and a competitor, counts as hospitality and appears in no policy at all. Our staff in one country point out that a gift to an official's family is a social obligation there and is offered with no expectation of return, and we tell them it is a bribe regardless. In some of the places we operate, small payments to move along routine actions the official already owes are tolerated by the letter of the law, while in others they are criminal. At home we make large, lawful donations to the campaigns of politicians, including the minister who appoints the customs chief, and we hire former regulators as consultants; nobody in the industry regards either as corruption. We export this rulebook, along with the international indices and conventions that now measure countries by it, and I am no longer sure whether we are describing corruption or describing our own arrangements.

What theory of corruption does this apparatus presuppose, and how much of it is actually forced by the theory? Reconstruct the implicit view, say which rival accounts of corruption it quietly rules out, and tell me whether it is internally consistent or whether the treatment of campaign donations, lobbying and private-sector gifts shows that a second, unstated view is doing the work. Say whether the public-private line is principled or merely administrative, and whether the theory is parochial in treating particularistic gift exchange as a deviation from a norm rather than as a different way of constituting social relations, or whether a defensible account of corruption must be universal. Then tell me concretely what would have to change in our rulebook if we replaced the implicit view with the alternative you think is strongest.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Take a definite position throughout: tell me which theory the practice presupposes and whether it hangs together, rather than laying out the candidates and leaving the choice to me.
