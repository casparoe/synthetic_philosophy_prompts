I'm a novelist, and for the better part of a year I've been researching a book set among a Liverpool merchant family during the abolition campaigns. I mention that only so you understand the shape of my ignorance: my characters have to argue about these things over dinner, and I want to know what they would actually be saying. I've been reading popular history rather than monographs, and the book I have just finished offers what it presents as the modern consensus on why moral revolutions happen, in roughly these words: 'Historians used to think slavery and dueling ended because people were finally persuaded by moral argument — the pamphlets, the speeches, the philosophy of natural rights. Two lines of work have demolished that story. An economic historian showed that British abolition was really driven by the declining profitability of the plantation economy and the rise of free-trade interests, which is why the book calls it the Williams thesis (I assume the historian, not the philosopher of moral luck); on that account the moral arguments were essentially window dressing. And a philosopher, Kwame Anthony Appiah, has shown that dueling in England and footbinding in China were ended not by argument but by honor: people stopped because the practice became shameful, and shame is a matter of social standing rather than moral truth. Taken together, this shows that moral revolutions are not rational responses to better arguments at all: they occur when interests shift or when the code of honor happens to move, and the moral justifications people offer afterwards are stories they tell themselves.'

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on how moral norms actually change, and give me a careful audit rather than a verdict. I'd like you to think first about what distinguishes a causal explanation of a change from an assessment of the reasons for it, and about which of these three explanations — argument, interest, honor — is even answering the same question as the others; then work through the summary more or less sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a specialist would accept, which are common simplifications they'd wince at, and which are simply wrong, being explicit about what each original view does and does not claim. I am especially interested in whether the book's concluding claim follows from anything true in the earlier sentences, and in what a defender of the older 'argument' story could still say on his own behalf. Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I want the analysis, not the bibliography — and if any sentence in the summary is ambiguous in a way that would change your verdict, say so and tell me which reading you are adopting.
