I spent the better part of thirty-five years as a physiologist, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy with the enthusiasm of a convert and the competence of an autodidact. One dispute I keep circling back to is whether there is a moral difference between doing harm and allowing harm, and I have come to suspect that my old discipline makes trouble for the way the question is usually posed. The trouble is that living things have no untouched baseline: an organism is held away from equilibrium by a great many processes that must be actively maintained, so the phrase 'letting nature take its course' does not describe the absence of a contribution — it describes one particular contribution to a process that is already heading somewhere. I have started calling this the maintenance asymmetry, and I cannot decide whether it dissolves the distinction between doing and allowing, or merely relocates where the real work is done.

Please write an essay on the following controversy: does the distinction between doing harm and allowing harm mark a morally fundamental difference, or is it better understood as a rough proxy for something else — the agent's intentions, the evidence her conduct gives about what she cares about, or the causal shape of what she did? Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides and reach a verdict rather than ending on a shrug. If you think the distinction is fundamental in some cases but not others, say which, and give me the criterion that marks them off.

I should like the argument tested against four cases, all of which I have invented and none of which I take to have an obvious answer:

1. Withdrawal of maintenance. A patient will die within the hour unless a carer performs a small task — cranking a pump, say. The carer has been doing this every hour for months, and now stops. The patient dies.

2. Failure to initiate maintenance. The same patient and the same task, except that this carer has never once performed it; the patient's life has so far depended on another carer who has just left the room. The remaining carer does nothing. The patient dies.

3. Sustained harm. A patient will die unless a carer performs that same small task, because the task keeps a poison circulating. The carer performs it, as she has every hour for months, and the patient dies.

4. The inherited default. A patient will die at midnight because an automatic device, designed and switched on years earlier by a particular carer, will then administer a lethal dose. The device can be switched off by a single action. The carer does nothing.

What, if anything, does the doing/allowing distinction track across these four cases? Is it a difference in causal contribution, in what the agent has taken on, in what her conduct reveals about her, or in nothing beyond the direction in which the process was already running? Does it matter that in the first and third the sustaining activity is itself artificial, and that in the fourth the lethal process is the agent's own artefact — or is the 'natural' baseline simply whatever would have happened had nobody ever intervened, so that the distinction survives only where such a baseline is well defined? Consider also whether the distinction might do useful moral work without being fundamental: for instance, by marking the cases in which an agent's conduct is especially telling evidence about her commitments, in a way that could in principle be replaced by direct evidence about those commitments.

I have no library to hand and do not want a reading list or citations; I want your own reasoning. Please write in British English.
