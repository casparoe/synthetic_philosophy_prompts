The context: we were reading about the decline of historical empires for a class project, and the conversation turned to whether anything about a present-day power can be forecast from how long past empires lasted.

Maya: You cannot forecast from past empires at all. "Empire" is a historian's convenience, and it is defined partly by eventually losing dominance, so "all empires fall" is closer to a definition than to a finding. The sample is tiny -- a dozen or two cases at most. The outcome is fuzzy: historians disagree about when Rome fell by more than a millennium. And the records we have are the ones left by states big enough to leave records, so the sample is selected on the very outcome we are trying to study. Worst of all, if I can always pick a different reference class and get a different base rate, then the base rate is not information -- it is a mirror of whatever the analyst already believed.

Theo: Refusing the outside view is not neutral. If a friend tells me he is going to quit school to trade currencies, the fact that most people who try this lose money is genuinely informative about him, even though he is a unique individual in a unique situation. That is what a base rate is: the information you have before you know the details of the case. If you insist on knowing the details before you will accept any base rate, you have guaranteed that the base rate can never correct you, and "this time is different" becomes unfalsifiable.

Maya: The day-trading case works because there are thousands of near-identical cases with a clear outcome. Empires are not like that. And even granting the class, age alone is not evidence of anything unless the risk of collapse actually rises with age. If the yearly risk of collapse were roughly constant, an old empire would be no more likely to fall in the next decade than a young one, and "we are due" would be the gambler's fallacy with centuries in place of coins.

Theo: Nobody serious claims that age itself causes collapse. The claim is that these systems accumulate specific liabilities -- fiscal overstretch, elite competition, loss of legitimacy -- and the base rate is a reality check on our story about which liabilities matter and how fast they bite. The outside view is a debiasing device: it is what stops us from treating our own vivid narrative as if it were evidence.

Maya: Then we agree that the base rate does no work on its own. It is only as good as the reference class, and the reference class is chosen by exactly the kind of judgment the outside view was supposed to be checking. That is not a small concession; it means the outside view sits inside the inside view, one level up. There is a further problem too. If a forecast of decline becomes widely believed, it changes what people do -- investors move money, elites hedge, voters turn on a government -- so the forecast can help bring about or help prevent the thing it predicts. A base rate computed over cases that do not react to being studied is one thing; this is more like a self-fulfilling or self-defeating prophecy.

Theo: Reflexivity makes the base rate a moving target; it does not make it a mirror of the analyst. Weather forecasts change what people do, and we do not conclude that meteorology is a Rorschach test. The choice of reference class can be criticised, but the alternative -- a single case, reasoned about entirely from the inside -- has no error-correction in it at all.
