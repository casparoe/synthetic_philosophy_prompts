I'm about to start a three-year postdoc with almost no teaching load and I've decided to spend it on vagueness — specifically the long-running standoff between the epistemicists (vague predicates have sharp boundaries, we just can't know where they fall) and the supervaluationists (truth is supertruth, borderline cases are ones where no way of precisifying the predicate settles things, and there's a determinacy operator to say so). I know all the standard moves — the sorites, higher-order vagueness, whether supervaluationism can hang onto truth-functionality, whether epistemicism can explain our ignorance without sliding into wholesale scepticism about meaning — and that's exactly the problem: the standard moves are what everyone recites, and I want to get past them. A colleague keeps insisting the real action isn't the sorites at all but cases like the problem of the many, where there seem to be a dozen equally good candidates for what an object is, and that vagueness in predicates is downstream of that; I'm not sure I buy it, but that's the kind of reframing I'm after. So what I'd like from you is an agenda, not a survey: given three years and licence to be ambitious, which of the genuinely open questions here is most worth attacking first, in the sense that settling it would make the rest of the landscape look different?

The candidates I've got in mind are things like: whether the whole dispute is really about where the indeterminacy lives (in language, in the world, in our concepts) rather than about truth-values; whether the determinacy operator needs a logic of its own and what that logic has to be; whether higher-order vagueness is a real phenomenon or an artefact of how we try to state the positions; and whether either camp can actually account for the felt datum that in borderline cases there's no fact of the matter, rather than merely a fact we're barred from knowing. But please add anything you think is upstream of those, including connections I haven't thought of — to measurement, to the semantics of comparatives like 'redder than', to how vagueness behaves in legal predicates — as long as it genuinely is upstream rather than just adjacent. For each thing you rank, I want the reasoning: why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone working alone with a logic background and no empirical data to lean on, and what result or argument over the next couple of years would move it up or down the list.

Please lay it out like this: start with a section called 'The shortlist', where you just rank the questions in order, one line each; then 'The top three, in detail', roughly a paragraph per question covering why it's upstream, tractability, and what would change the ranking; then 'What I'd leave alone', on the tempting but low-value options and why they don't pay off; and finish with 'What would move things', a short list of results or arguments that would reorder the shortlist. Keep the whole thing under about 1,300 words, and don't pad it with definitions of epistemicism and supervaluationism — I could write those sections myself.
