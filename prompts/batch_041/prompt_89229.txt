In Germany it is a criminal offence to deny the Nazi genocide, and the law is defended on the ground that denial is not merely false but an assault on the dignity of the victims and on the civic order that acknowledges them. Turkey, by contrast, has prosecuted journalists and writers for describing the 1915 massacres of Armenians as a genocide, under a provision that makes it an offence to denigrate the Turkish nation; there the wrong is said to lie in the insult to the nation and its founding story. Several Southern American states now require local governments to obtain state permission before removing or renaming a monument, a rule whose effect, and in some cases stated purpose, is to keep Confederate memorials standing — here the state is not punishing a claim about the past so much as freezing an object in place. All three enlist the law on the side of a memory, and all three are defended in the language of dignity and respect, yet one punishes the denial of a nation's own crimes, another punishes their assertion, and the third protects a monument to the perpetrators. What view of the state's relation to historical truth, and of whose dignity is at stake, does each presuppose? Are these one kind of practice under three descriptions, or do they rest on incompatible theories of what a state owes the past? And which features of each are strictly required by its professed rationale rather than merely compatible with it?
