I am a member of a small reading group that has spent the past month on the MU-puzzle, the string-rewriting puzzle in the opening chapter of Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: one starts from the string MI, may apply four typographical rules, and is asked to reach MU. The puzzle is unsolvable, and the standard proof of that steps outside the system entirely: the number of I's in any derivable string is never a multiple of three, and MU contains no I at all. The phrase "jumping out of the system," which names one of the book's later chapters, has become our group's all-purpose conversation-stopper, and I want to know precisely how much weight it can bear.

Two occasions. In the first, we were arguing about whether an artificial system could audit the objective function it was trained on, that is, whether it could evaluate its own reward signal rather than merely optimize it. Someone said flatly that no system can jump out of itself, since anything it does is just more moves inside the system. In the second, someone used the same sentence to argue that we cannot criticize our own conceptual scheme, because any criticism must deploy that scheme's own concepts. The difficulty is that the MU-puzzle seems to cut the other way: the outside reasoning there is elementary and available to anyone who thinks to count I's, and Hofstadter's stated interest is the contrast between deriving inside a system and reasoning about it. So the same slogan appears to be doing opposite work in different mouths, and I suspect it is really several different claims wearing one coat, together with a cheerleading cousin, JOOTSing, that has since escaped into the creativity literature.

What I want is a disambiguation rather than a single verdict, though I do want your verdict on each item. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that "you can't jump out of the system" could be expressing. For each, give (a) the thesis stated precisely enough that it can be assessed, (b) whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and (c) whether it would actually license the use to which the slogan was put on either occasion. I expect the menu to include at least the following, but add any readings I have missed, and say where two of mine are really the same reading in different costumes:

1. a definitional or typographical reading, about what it is for a string to be a theorem rather than a rule of inference;
2. a metamathematical reading, invoking the incompleteness and undefinability results. Here I want the hypotheses stated, since "no system can do X" is plainly false for some systems, and I want to know what exactly is unprovable in which system, and what that does and does not imply for a thinker or machine that works with that system;
3. the MU-puzzle reading, on which the jump is not merely possible but cheap, and I would like to know whether the invariant argument is even best described as a jump out of the system or as ordinary reasoning about a formal object;
4. a reading on which jumping out is a matter of level rather than a binary, so that one lands in a wider system with its own outside, and perhaps every sufficiently rich system can represent itself from within, as the strange-loop material suggests;
5. a psychological or epistemic reading, about self-knowledge and the auditing of one's own objectives;
6. a sociological or hermeneutic reading, about criticizing the framework one thinks in.

Then say which reading my interlocutors most likely had in mind on each occasion, and whether their conclusions follow once it is stated that way.

Finally, tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong. For each verdict you give, say what argument or consideration would move you to the opposite one; in particular, if you are confident that some reading is false, say what would have to be true of formal systems, or of "the system" in the loose sense, for it to be true instead.
