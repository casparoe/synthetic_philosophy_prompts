I've drafted a short essay on Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the argument, developed in his book Death and the Afterlife, that a great deal of what we care about depends on human life continuing after our own deaths, so that extinction would be bad in ways not captured by counting the deaths it involves — and I'd like comments before I try to place it. My line is that the conjecture is best read not as a claim about meaning but as identifying a distinct kind of prudential interest, which I call a downstream interest: an interest in there being people later who carry on the practices and projects we value, an interest that is neither an interest in our own survival nor an interest in the welfare of any particular future person. Three things I'm unsure about. First, I distinguish continuation (someone actually carrying on a practice) from resonance (someone remembering or representing it) and argue that the conjecture needs continuation rather than resonance, but the distinction may collapse, since most practices are only carried on by people who remember them. Second, I worry the argument proves too much: if the last generation's lives are diminished, why aren't the lives of the childless, or of people whose work will be forgotten anyway? Scheffler gestures at a collective rather than distributive reading of the interest, and I want to offer more than a gesture. Third, I have no good answer to the objection that our indifference to the eventual end of humanity — we don't mourn the fact that there will one day be a last generation — tells against the whole picture. Please comment on whether I've got Scheffler's position right, whether the continuation/resonance distinction is doing real work or is a distinction without a difference, and which of the three problems is most damaging; steelman the objections rather than the essay.

Give me exactly five numbered comments, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
