I keep running into a pattern in debates about how much we owe the distant future, and I have no name for it. The setup is familiar: because the far future could contain astronomically many people, even a minute chance of improving it is said to outweigh any present sacrifice. Then a critic objects that we are probably living in a computer simulation, so our efforts never reach the real future, and the stakes collapse. Then a defender turns the same premise around: if simulations are common, then most of the value there will ever be sits inside simulations, so our being simulated multiplies the stakes instead of dividing them. I know this is in the neighborhood of the doomsday argument, but that concerns how long our species lasts, not whether the future counts for more or less. What I want named is the shape of the argument itself: the same fact — that we are one unremarkable instance of a far larger population of observers, most of whom are unlike us — is deployed in one breath to deflate the value of the future and in the next to inflate it, and nobody reconciles the two. My own guess is that the two moves differ only in whether value is counted per world or per observer, and that this is why the verdict flips, but I don't know whether that diagnosis is right, whether anyone has drawn the distinction, or what to call the phenomenon if they have. Tell me: is there an established concept here, and if so, exactly where does it fall short of what I've described? If not, coin a term, define it, and mark its edges — in particular, how it differs from the doomsday argument and from ordinary probability discounting. Give a definite answer, with your reasons, rather than a survey of the options, in no more than 350 words.
