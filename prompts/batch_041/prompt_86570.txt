A lecturer I heard summarized Maimonides' treatment of animal sacrifice in the Guide of the Perplexed roughly as follows: Maimonides held that animal sacrifice was a concession to the Israelites' primitive mentality. God never wanted sacrifices; the laws were a temporary accommodation, a kind of divine condescension, because the people were too attached to the bloody rituals of their neighbors. The true purpose of the Law is prayer and contemplation, so Maimonides believed the sacrificial system would eventually be abolished, and he would have welcomed its replacement by synagogue worship.

I suspect the lecturer is right that Maimonides was uncomfortable with sacrifice, but I don't know enough to say where the summary goes wrong. How much of this is fair? Please go claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of the Guide would accept, what they would wince at, and what is just wrong. In particular, distinguish between the claim that the sacrificial commandments were historically conditioned and pedagogically motivated, the claim that they were not commanded by God or were merely tolerated, the claim that they are inherently inferior to prayer, and the claim that they are temporary and will be abolished. How does Maimonides' insistence on the permanence of the commandments, and his detailed account of the reasons for the specific sacrificial laws, bear on the summary? Does the conclusion that he would favor abolishing sacrifice today follow from the accurate parts? Where does the summary conflate a historical explanation with a normative judgment?
