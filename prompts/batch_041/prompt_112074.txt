I run communications for a mid-sized state health department — it's me plus two writers, and basically everything from outbreak notices to the commissioner's op-eds crosses my desk before it goes out, usually on a tight clock. I've been lurking on rationalist blogs and forums for, God, a decade now, and the thing that keeps nagging me is the lying-vs-misleading question. Everyone agrees lying is bad, but honestly half our job is choosing which true things to say and which true things to sit on, and I genuinely can't tell anymore where "framing" ends and "deceiving people" begins. The commissioner's whole guidance is "just don't lie," which is useless, because the drafts that scare me most are ones where every single sentence is literally true. I want a decision procedure my team can actually run on a draft before it ships — grounded in what the philosophy of language actually knows about the gap between what a sentence says and what it gets people to believe (implicature, presupposition, what's asserted vs. what's merely suggested, the difference between "causing someone to have a false belief" and "intentionally deceiving them"), not just vibes.

So: give me the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand the call to a human because the question is really one of values and not facts. It has to be runnable by a smart writer who's never read any philosophy, in maybe fifteen minutes, and it has to be honest about which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as tests — I'd much rather have a procedure with clearly marked holes than one that pretends to settle everything. Then run it on three drafts of ours. (1) A water-quality notice where we lead with "the water is within legal limits" while burying the fact that the legal limit is way above what our own toxicologist thinks is safe — my instinct is that this is the bad one but I want the procedure to tell me why, if it's really bad. (2) A statement that a clinic closure is "part of a routine review" when the real story is the lease fell through and we're embarrassed. (3) One I think we're actually fine on: a release that says an outbreak is "under investigation" and leaves off "and it's spreading," because at the time we genuinely didn't know which way it was going. I especially want you to flag any case where the procedure says a statement I'm quietly proud of is actually over the line. Also, my two writers are going to ask "what if we can't tell whether the audience will draw the false inference" — build in an answer to that instead of hand-waving.
