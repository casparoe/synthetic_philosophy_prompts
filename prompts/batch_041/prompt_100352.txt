A friend of mine and I have now spent the better part of three evenings arguing about the liberal paradox, and we have reached the uncomfortable point where we no longer disagree about the theorem but about what the theorem is a theorem about. I set it up the standard way: a single copy of a book, two people who want it in incompatible ways, one of them with strong preferences about who ends up reading it and the other indifferent on that score, and then two seemingly innocuous commitments -- that each person should be decisive over the assignment in some pair of alternatives that is specially their own business, and that if everyone prefers one outcome to another then the group should prefer it too. The two commitments cannot both hold in general, and I take the upshot to be that we have to say which one is doing the illicit work and why. My friend, who is more theoretically ambitious than I am, insists that we have both been reading the "personal sphere" premise as a claim about outcomes when it is really a claim about who controls the composition of the menu in the first place -- that a right is better modeled as authority over which alternatives get placed before the group at all, and that once you relocate the right there, the conflict either dissolves or mutates into a different and possibly more tractable question about how menus are formed, who may revise them, and what constrains that revision. I am not sure whether this is a genuine advance or a relabeling that merely hides the original difficulty, and I would rather find out by working through the terrain than by trading intuitions at midnight.

Here is what I actually want from you. I am putting together a semester-long reading group -- six or seven people, mostly graduate students in political philosophy and social choice, plus one person who is comfortable with the formal machinery -- and I need to decide what the group should spend its limited attention on, so I need a defended agenda rather than a survey. Which open questions in this area most deserve sustained work, and in what order? For each, tell me why it is upstream of the others (what other questions would be clarified or rendered moot if it were settled), how tractable it is for a group of this composition rather than for someone who can invent new formal machinery on demand, and what kind of progress over a semester would move it up or down my list. I am especially interested in whether the reframing my friend proposed -- rights as authority over menu-formation rather than as decisiveness over outcomes -- is a live research line worth building the group around or a dead end that will collapse back into the original puzzle, and in what would have to be true for the reframing to count as a solution rather than a restatement. Where two of the candidate questions trade off against each other, say so explicitly and tell me which you would sacrifice.
