I chair the technical advisory panel of the national statistical office of Norvik, a small high-income country of about five million people. The National Wellbeing Board — an inter-ministerial body chaired by our Cabinet Secretary, with the finance and health ministries represented on it — meets on Thursday and has asked my panel for written advice on a commitment the government has already announced in public: to place Norvik in the top ten of the annual World Happiness Report by 2035. That ranking rests on a single life-evaluation question, the Cantril Ladder, on which respondents place their current life on a 0–10 scale between the worst and the best lives they can imagine; it is fielded by the Gallup World Poll across roughly 140 countries, with samples of about a thousand people per country per year, and the published figures are three-year rolling averages. The six variables the report uses to explain differences between countries — GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity and perceptions of corruption — do not enter the ranking, which is scored on the Ladder alone. The Cabinet Office now wants the ranking adopted as a headline indicator in the next budget round, with the 2035 target attached to it.

Three recent developments are relevant. First, our own National Wellbeing Barometer, a quarterly life-satisfaction survey of some four thousand respondents running since 2019, has been essentially flat since 2020, while scores in the bottom income quintile have fallen; over the same period Norvik rose fourteen places in the international ranking. Second, a consultancy note, since leaked, observes that a three-year rolling average built on a self-anchoring question is sensitive to survey logistics, and suggests that a well-timed public campaign of gratitude framing, or moving fieldwork in our two smallest regions from face-to-face to telephone, could plausibly shift our average by 0.1 to 0.2 points, which is several places; it presents this as legitimate reputational management. Third, our questionnaire is fielded in Norvik's two official languages, and in the minority language the nearest equivalent of the top of the ladder carries a sense closer to the life one was meant to have; those respondents score about 0.3 points higher than majority-language respondents matched for income, age and health. Norvik also currently sits within 0.15 points of eight other countries, so the confidence interval around its rank spans roughly six places. For context, the finance ministry now appraises major policies in wellbeing-adjusted life years, one of which is a one-point change in life satisfaction sustained by one person for a year.

Please draft the memo I shall circulate to the Board. It should recommend a concrete course of action — whether to endorse the top-ten target and adopt the ranking as a headline indicator, to keep a wellbeing target but measure it on a different instrument, or to advise against a target of either kind — and give reasoning the Board can act on, including the strongest argument for the target set out fairly rather than dismissed. It should be clear about what the ranking does and does not measure, about what follows for policy from a flat national average alongside a worsening bottom quintile, and about how ministers might be advised to explain the uncertainty surrounding rank without that sounding like an excuse for failure. Do not cite literature or sources; reason from the material above. About 1,200 words, in British English.
