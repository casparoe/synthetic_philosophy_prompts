The question:

"Three judges have to decide whether a company is liable for breach of contract. The rule is: the company is liable (L) just in case it breached the contract (B) and the breach caused the loss (C). Each judge votes on each of the three propositions separately. The votes come out like this:

Judge 1: B yes, C yes, L yes
Judge 2: B yes, C no, L no
Judge 3: B no, C yes, L no

(a) Aggregate the judges proposition by proposition, by simple majority. Give the collective set of judgments. Is it consistent? Is it complete?
(b) Some textbooks say what goes wrong here is that the group's judgments are 'incomplete'. Is that the right diagnosis in this case? Explain what actually fails.
(c) Two familiar fixes. Premise-based: take a majority vote on B and on C, then read L off the rule. Conclusion-based: take a majority vote on L alone, leaving B and C undecided. What does each procedure give in this example?
(d) A third proposal that came up in section: settle the propositions in a fixed order of priority — B first, then C, then L — and at each step, if the proposition's value is already forced by the ones you've settled, take the forced value instead of voting on it. Does the priority order make a difference to the result? Does the proposal always deliver a consistent collective set?
(e) Does the example show that majority rule is irrational, or that we just can't have all the properties we'd like in one procedure? If the latter, say which properties come into conflict, and finish with a sentence on which of the two procedures in (c) you'd want a real tribunal to use, and why."

The student's answer:

"(a) Every judge is internally consistent — nobody holds a contradictory set, since anyone who accepts both B and C also accepts L, and everyone who rejects B or C rejects L. So the trouble isn't coming from the individuals.

Aggregating by majority: B gets two votes (judges 1 and 2), so B is accepted. C gets two votes (judges 1 and 3), so C is accepted. L gets one vote (judge 1), so L is rejected. The collective set is {B, C, not-L}. That set is inconsistent with the rule, since B and C together entail L.

(b) Yes, I'd say incompleteness is the right description. The group never really lands on an answer to the liability question — the majority has endorsed the two premises separately, but there's no majority judgment that puts them together, so the tribunal ends up not having decided the very thing it was convened to decide. That's what makes it a problem: a court has to return a verdict, and here there's a gap where the verdict should be.

(c) Premise-based gives B yes and C yes, so by the rule L yes — liable. Conclusion-based ignores the premises and just takes the majority on L, which is no — not liable. So the two procedures disagree, which is the point of the example: the same votes can yield either verdict depending on which level you aggregate at.

The premise-based route is the one that keeps faith with the majority, though. Since B and C both carry a majority, the verdict it produces is the one a majority of the tribunal actually endorses; the conclusion-based route gets its answer only by throwing away the information in the premises. So if you want the group's verdict to reflect what its members think, premise-based is what you want.

(d) In this example the priority procedure gives the same result as the premise-based one: B is voted yes, C is voted yes, and then L is forced, so L comes out yes. The order of priority doesn't make a difference, because at each step a proposition is either voted on or forced, and a forced value can't conflict with the votes that force it — the procedure is just deriving the consequences of what's already settled, so the set it produces can't come out inconsistent. And since every proposition either gets a vote or gets a value from the rule, the set is complete as well.

(e) I think it shows the second thing. Majority rule isn't irrational here: every judge is perfectly rational, and the majority is right about each proposition taken on its own. What we can't have is both responsiveness to the majority on the conclusion and logical consistency of the collective set. Aggregate the premises and you get consistency; aggregate the conclusion and you get responsiveness; you can't have both once the majority is on opposite sides of an entailment. So a group can be rational or democratic, but not both. (Worth noting this isn't a Condorcet cycle — nobody's judgments are intransitive and there's no preference ordering anywhere in the example, so it's a different animal from the voting paradox.)

For a real tribunal I'd take the premise-based route: consistency matters more for a court than matching the majority's instinct about the final verdict, and it has the side benefit of deciding the questions the parties actually argued about."
