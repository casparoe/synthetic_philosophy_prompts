I've just joined the research-ethics committee at a neuroscience institute, and the first proposal on my desk is divisive enough that the chair has asked each member to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's vote. A lab proposes to engineer a line of mice in which the affective dimension of pain is selectively blunted: the animals would still detect injury and withdraw from it, but would not find the state aversive or distressing. The applicants call this a proof of concept for suffering-free livestock, and they add a further claim — that once the technique works, declining to develop it would make us complicit in a vast quantity of avoidable animal suffering. Our remit, however, permits approval only of work that can be expected to benefit the animals used or their kind, and several members doubt that a creature stripped of the capacity to suffer can be said to benefit at all, or even to remain the sort of being whose interests we can weigh. Draft the opinion I will circulate: give me a definite recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons the researchers could genuinely respond to, and a fair statement of the strongest case against my recommendation. I am new to this committee and would rather not decide it on instinct.
