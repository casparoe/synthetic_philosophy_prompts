Ok so I've been chewing on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, the version where there's some line of suffering such that anything at or worse than the line can't be outweighed by any amount of pleasure, while suffering milder than the line just trades off against pleasure at a normal fixed rate. I want a thought experiment that makes the threshold do real work, and I want to see the thing actually crack.

Here's the spec. Build me a case with exactly two options. The total amount of suffering across the two options should be the same, but distributed differently relative to the line, and the total pleasure should differ by a hair, a negligible sliver, so that a plain additive view (pleasure and pain on one common scale, just sum them) weakly prefers one option while the threshold view strictly prefers the other. The divergence has to come only from where the line sits relative to how the suffering is spread, not from different totals of suffering, not from rights or consent or side constraints, nothing deontological. Keep it realistic: a hospital, a workplace, a family, that kind of thing. No sci-fi, no trolleys, no utility monsters, no brains in vats.

Then the analysis. (1) What the threshold view says, and exactly which feature of the distribution is triggering the lexical priority. (2) What the additive view says and why, and note how thin the margin is. (3) Now take one person's suffering and move it by a tiny amount, just enough to shove them across the line, and show how the threshold view's verdict flips or collapses while the additive view's ranking dont budge at all. That flip is the point, so make it vivid. (4) Tell me honestly whether the case only works because I stipulated where the line is, and if the threshold view is just smuggling in an arbitrary cutoff. Then finish with how a defender of the additive/commensurable view would attack the case to defuse it.

One paragraph for the scenario itself, then the analysis after. Keep the numbers simple enough that I can add them up in my head.
