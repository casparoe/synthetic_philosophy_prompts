Write a deadpan parody, roughly 600 words, in the form of a formal claims-denial letter from a staid insurance firm of your own invention to a policyholder who has filed for a serious but mundane personal misfortune — a house fire, a fatal illness, a child's accident. The adjuster must deny the claim, and the denial must be argued entirely in the logic of Leibniz's theodicy, so that a reader who knows the Theodicy laughs at the accuracy rather than wincing: the firm did not will the loss but only permitted it (the distinction between an ordaining and a permissive will); a world in which this particular loss did not occur would be incompossible with greater goods the policy already underwrites, so that removing the loss would mean issuing a different world rather than a better one; the principle of sufficient reason guarantees the loss has a reason even though the adjuster declines to disclose it; the claimant's own free agency is a standard policy feature and not a defect; and the refusal is an act of justice in Leibniz's sense — the charity of the wise, which is under no obligation to be mere pity. The comedy must come from the theodicy being internally correct, never from the adjuster being a cartoon villain, and the letter should stay legible to a reader who has never heard of Leibniz. End with one line of fine print that quietly concedes the crux: that this is the best of all possible policies, and that no better one can be issued. Quote nothing from Leibniz or any real text, and never name him.
