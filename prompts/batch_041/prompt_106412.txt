I hold a view about interpersonal utility comparisons and want a map of its exposure.

The view: comparisons of welfare between persons are meaningful — there is a fact of the matter about whether A is worse off than B — but their meaningfulness does not consist in the existence of a shared psychological scale of intensity. Rather, a comparative claim is meaningful to the extent that it does work in a decision. It is meaningful when adopting it changes which option some specified decision rule selects; it is idle, and neither true nor false in any sense worth caring about, when every admissible decision rule would select the same option however the comparison came out. On this account the familiar apparatus of cardinal scales, unit comparability, level comparability, and the rest is bookkeeping: its job is to keep track of which comparisons are load-bearing, not to tell us how much comparability we actually have. The long-running dispute over whether we possess more or less than ordinal comparability is, on this view, largely a dispute about notation.

What I want from you:

1. What would count against this? Separate what would be decisive from what would be merely awkward, and be explicit about the difference.

2. Which objections would a defender of the view concede as counting against it, as opposed to those only critics press? I care about the gap between concessions the view is forced to make and ones it merely invites.

3. Does the view collapse into a rival? In particular, say whether the criterion can be run without already presupposing the comparisons it claims to vindicate. If the decision rule must itself be specified in welfare terms, it begins to look as though the account is circular, or else reduces to the view that comparisons are conventions adopted for the sake of a chosen aggregation rule. If that is what it comes to, say so plainly rather than softening it.

4. Is the view falsifiable, or has it been insulated? Identify any qualifications that would immunize it, and say whether they are legitimate or evasive.

5. Close with what would change your own verdict: which argument or consideration would move you from your assessment, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

Argue from the structure of the problem and from cases rather than from the literature; I do not need citations. The argument I would personally find most persuasive is one that identifies a concrete decision problem in which a comparison is ineliminable — where no paraphrase avoids it and no admissible rule is indifferent to how it comes out.
