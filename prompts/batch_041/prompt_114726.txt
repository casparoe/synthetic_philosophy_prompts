my reading group just finished the last chapter of Moore's Principia Ethica — the part where he lays out his ideal and says the two greatest goods we can conceive are the enjoyment of beautiful things and the pleasures of human company, and suggests the best of all is when the two come together in the same object, i.e. loving a person who's also beautiful. The bit I keep chewing on is his claim that the value of a whole isn't just the sum of the values of its parts — so a whole can be better or worse than its parts added up, and the very same part, unchanged, can contribute something different depending on what it's sitting next to. Our group got into a fight about weather that's a genuine insight or just a fancy way of saying context matters.

Anyway, I want to close the semester with a poem, and I want the form to do the arguing for me. Write me a villanelle — proper form, 19 lines, rhyme scheme and all. The two refrains should be roughly: one line about the enjoyment of beautiful things, and one line about the love of persons / human company. Both refrains have to come back verbatim every time, not a word changed, and the poem should make the reader feel that the identical line is doing a different job each time it returns, so the thing enacts Moore's point about organic wholes instead of just stating it. In the closing quatrain the two refrains should land side by side, and that should be where his ideal — beauty and affection together, worth more than either alone — becomes visible, without going soft or greeting-card on me; Moore's own tone is dry and a little cold and I want some of that in there. Somewhere in the middle, give me one stanza that imagines a world exactly like a beautiful world except that something small and ugly has been added to it, and make it clear the second world is worse — worse not because of the little ugly thing's own value but because of what its addition does to the whole. Someone who's read the book should recognize the moves; someone who hasn't should still get a good poem out of it. Don't name Moore or the book inside the poem itself.
