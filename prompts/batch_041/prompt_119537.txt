The question was: Wittgenstein says some propositions are exempt from doubt and that doubt presupposes certainty. Explain what he means and why he thinks it, and assess whether this commits him to foundationalism.

The student wrote:

Wittgenstein's target in On Certainty is Moore's attempt to answer the skeptic by claiming to know things like that he has two hands. Wittgenstein agrees with Moore that these are things we know, he just thinks Moore picked a bad context to say it in. For Wittgenstein these propositions aren't known in the ordinary evidential sense anyway; they're the background against which knowing and doubting happen. He calls them hinges — propositions that stand fast and that our language-games turn on. If you tried to doubt everything at once you couldn't even get the practice of doubting started, since doubting requires taking alot for granted. So global skepticism isn't exactly false, it's more like incoherent.

He also uses the river image: some propositions are like the bed and banks that channel the flow of inquiry, while others are the water moving along. The bed isn't what's being tested when we test things downstream. Over time, what counts as bed can change.

Does this make him a foundationalist? Sort of. He does think there are basic certainties that don't rest on further grounds, and that justification has to come to an end somewhere. But it's not classical foundationalism, since the hinges aren't basic beliefs in the Cartesian sense — they aren't indubitable inner items we introspect and then use as premises. They're more like rules or a frame we absorb through training. And they're not universal or ahistorical; different language-games have different hinges. So he's closer to a contextualist about justification than a traditional foundationalist. And because the hinges are fixed, they can never be given up, which is why the skeptic's challenge can be dismissed instead of answered.
