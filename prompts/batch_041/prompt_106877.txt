I've been covering the character debate in moral psychology for a feature, and after a dozen interviews I still can't tell whether the two camps disagree or are talking past each other. The situationists hold that the experimental record — the seminary students who stepped over a slumped man because they were late, the people who helped more readily after finding a dime in a payphone slot, the obedience studies — shows that behaviour is driven by circumstance rather than by stable traits, and they conclude that virtue ethics rests on an empirically false psychology. The virtue ethicists have largely stopped defending global traits as behaviour-frequency dispositions: some now say a virtue is a reason-responsive disposition, others that the situationist data at most refute a folk notion of character no virtue ethicist needs, and that local, domain-specific traits are all virtue ethics ever required. The situationists reply that this is a change of subject. Meanwhile the replication crisis has put some of the situationists' own classic studies under a cloud, so both sides now contest which evidence counts and what the modest cross-situational correlations that personality psychology reports actually show.

My own hypothesis — which I'd like you to test rather than adopt — is that the impasse is neither straightforwardly empirical nor merely verbal, but turns on how a "situation" is individuated: if situations are picked out by how the agent construes them, then construal is itself a person-variable, and the person/situation partition on which the whole question of how much of behaviour is situational depends has never been specified, so the two sides may be measuring different things under one name. Is that right? Or is the real split about explanatory aims — predicting behaviour versus appraising action — or about what an empirically adequate virtue would have to look like, or about whether any behavioural finding could bear on a normative theory at all? Diagnose the disagreement as precisely as you can, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a study design, a formal result — and say plainly if the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard. I don't need citations or a survey of the literature; I need the diagnosis and your reasons for it.
