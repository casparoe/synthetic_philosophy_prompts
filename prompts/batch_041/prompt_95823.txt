Some months ago a critic, reviewing my fifth novel, observed that the book shows the self to be nothing but a story the brain keeps telling itself, and went on to say — meaning it as praise — that fiction of this kind does the work of neuroscience by other means. I have since heard a version of the same sentence from a neuroscientist on a festival panel, where it was used to close down a question of mine about whether the inner lives I invent are answerable to anything at all. He added, as if it settled the matter, that there is no little theatre in the head where it all comes together, only drafts that never convene.

I would like the sentence taken apart rather than defended.

Here is why I care. The novel in question was written backwards: I composed the final chapter first, and everything before it is provisional. The device — I have only a workshop name for it, the backward draft — means that a decision my protagonist makes in chapter two is a decision, or a rationalisation, or an accident, depending on what the ending requires. I have written the same scene three ways and cannot say which is true of him. The panelist's remark, if it means anything, tells me that this is not a defect of my method but a fact about minds. The critic's remark, if it means anything, tells me that my method is a discovery. I suspect both are trading on a sentence that could mean five or six quite different things, and I would like to know which of them the sentence can honestly bear.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making. For each, state the claim as exactly as you can; say what it commits you to, and in particular whether it implies a teller, an audience, or a place where the telling occurs — since I notice that the word story smuggles in someone to tell it, and that looks uncomfortably like the little theatre the panelist was denying. Distinguish, where the distinction bites, between a claim about what exists and a claim about what can be known; between a story that is told and a pattern that need never be told to anyone; between a story the brain tells and a story a culture tells through a person. Then give a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible, dubious, or false — and say where two of the readings collapse into one another or where a reading is really two claims wearing a single coat.

Finish with three short things. Which reading, if any, would actually license the critic's compliment, and which would license the panelist's dismissal. Whether any reading entails that there is no fact of the matter about what my protagonist was thinking in chapter two, as opposed to there being a fact he cannot read. And three questions I could put to the critic that would force him to say which reading he had in mind.

I do not need a reading list or citations; I need the positions separated and adjudicated.
