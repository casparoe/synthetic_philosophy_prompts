I am trying to pin down what a theory of corruption would have to get right before I take sides between any of the accounts on offer. The ordinary cases are too easy and the hard cases pull in opposite directions, so I want the specification drawn up first, on its own, before any candidate is graded against it.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of corruption: the full list of what any account must deliver if it is to say which practices are corrupt and which are not, in a way that is neither parochially Western nor so permissive that nothing is corrupt. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it and say what would be lost if the requirement were dropped. Then divide the list into the requirements that are non-negotiable and those that are merely the preferences of a particular camp. Finally — and this is the part I most want — say whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so, which subset. A real contradiction among the desiderata would be worth more to me than a tidy list that conceals one.

The cases that generate the requirements, each of which should be made to do real work; add any you think I have missed:

1. A municipal clerk in a society where anyone whose paperwork is expedited is expected to make a public, recorded offering to the clerk's household at the next harvest; the offering is customary, its size is roughly fixed, everyone knows the clerk accepts them, and declining to give one is read as a snub that costs you your place in the queue. There is no secrecy, no haggling, and no differential price. A visiting consultant calls it a bribe; the clerk's neighbours call it manners.

2. A payment that is secret, negotiated case by case, priced to what the payer can bear, and made precisely because it changes an official decision. Any theory, I take it, must count this as corrupt; the question is what it shares with case 1 that case 1 lacks, and whether the theory can isolate that feature without smuggling in the outsider's disgust.

3. A society with no word corresponding to corruption that nevertheless punishes certain betrayals of office, set against a donor country that treats its own statutory definition as the measure of the rest of the world. Say whether the outsider is right, wrong, or making a category mistake, and what follows for the theory either way.

4. A payment to a low-level official that secures a service the state is failing to provide, in a bureaucracy whose formal procedures are unusable. I want to know whether the theory is entitled to call this corrupt even while conceding that the alternative is worse.

5. Patronage that functions as a channel of political representation for people whom formal institutions ignore. Whether this is corruption or a different thing wearing its surface seems to me the sharpest test of where a theory draws its lines.

Two distinctions I want the list to force a verdict on rather than blur: between a gift that creates an obligation and a bribe that buys a decision, and between corruption and clientelism. Say also whether the theory must be able to define corruption without leaning on the local word for it or the local sense of shame, and what it costs the theory if it cannot.

Then name the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise popular theories, and say what it excludes.
