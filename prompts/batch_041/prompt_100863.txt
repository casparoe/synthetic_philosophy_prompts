I have been trying to get clear on the difference between knowing how to do something and knowing that something is the case, and I keep returning to a pile of cases where my gut verdicts refuse to settle. My uncle rides a bicycle beautifully and cannot tell you, even roughly, what keeps him upright; I want to say he knows how to ride and does not know that you stay up by steering into a fall. A friend read a swimming manual cover to cover and can recite the stroke and the breathing pattern without a flaw, but put him in a pool and he sinks; he has the facts and does not know how to swim. A former competitive swimmer I know is now paralyzed and will never swim again, yet I still want to say she knows how to swim, that the knowing did not leave with the ability. A man who has not driven in forty years and would now be a menace on the road can still narrate a three-point turn step by step; there I want to say he has the facts and has lost the how. A chess player who finds the right move at a glance but can only gesture at why, and a musician who plays entirely by ear, both seem to me to know how while barely knowing that. And a woman I met who acquired a difficult manual skill under hypnosis performs it flawlessly but insists she has no idea how she does it and that it feels like it happens to her, which leaves me unsure whether to credit her with the knowing at all. The case that most unsettles me is the ear-wiggler: someone who could always wiggle her ears, never learned, and has no idea how she does it — I find I want to say she knows how, and I cannot tell whether that verdict belongs with the bike rider or is simply a mistake on my part.

I keep reaching for a principle and it keeps breaking. The tempting first one is that knowing how simply is the ability to do the thing; but the paralyzed swimmer has the knowing without the ability, so that cannot be right as stated. The tempting second one is that knowing how is just knowing a true proposition of the right sort — knowing a way of doing it — but the bike rider seems to have no such proposition, and the ex-driver seems to have one without the knowing how, so that breaks too. My best attempt so far is something like: knowing how to do X is the ability to do X when that ability is guided by the agent's own grasp of how it is done — but I cannot say whether the "grasp" here is a proposition, a disposition, or something else, and the ear-wiggler and the hypnotized performer both make me suspect the whole approach is misdirected. Two further cases I would add to the pile and would want your verdict on, since I cannot settle them myself: a chess engine that plays at superhuman level and stores no description of its own method, and a person whose only way to get anywhere is by following a navigation device, who could not retrace a route she has driven a hundred times if the device were taken away.

What I want is the principle, or a small set of competing principles, that best captures this pattern: stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is answering for which case; tested against the cases I have listed and the two I have added; and accompanied by an honest account of which of my verdicts the winning principle has to overturn, or of what further case would decide between the survivors. If my tentative "guided ability" principle can be repaired rather than abandoned, tell me how, and tell me what it then predicts for a case I have not mentioned — say, a person who can perform a skill only while reciting a nonsense phrase, and who fails the moment the phrase is taken from her.
