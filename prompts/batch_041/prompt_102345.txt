Last night a friend and I spent two hours arguing about whether the meaning of a word can be simply mistaken, and he kept ending every exchange with the same move: language is just a convention, so if enough people start using a word a certain way, that is what it means, and anyone who insists otherwise -- a dictionary, a teacher, a pedant online -- is just enforcing a preference. He went further and claimed that this shows we could, in principle, decide by collective fiat to change what our words mean, the way we might all agree to drive on the other side of the road, and that there is therefore nothing for a dispute about usage to be about except power. I am fairly sure that several quite different claims are packed into that one sentence, that some of them are near-trivial, some are serious and defensible philosophical theses, and some are probably just false, but I could not separate them fast enough to say which of them his argument actually needed. I would like you to act as a philosopher of language and social ontology with a firm command of the tradition running from David Lewis's work on convention through the more recent game-theoretic and evolutionary treatments of norms, and to help me take the slogan apart. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming the candidate readings before you commit to anything; I want the analysis itself, not a reading list or citations.

Lay the readings out for me as a numbered menu rather than choosing one and defending it. For each reading, give the precise thesis it amounts to, an example that would make the difference between it and its neighbors vivid, a verdict on whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and an explicit note on whether it would actually license the specific moves my friend made -- the claim that no one can be wrong about what a word means, and the claim that a community can change its language by decision. I want the boundaries marked: where 'convention' is being used to mean merely 'not natural' or 'could have been otherwise,' where it is being used in something like the Lewisian sense of a regularity sustained by mutual expectation and common knowledge with a live alternative, and where it is being used to mean a socially enforced norm backed by sanctions. Related distinctions that I suspect are doing hidden work and that you should address head on: the several senses of 'arbitrary' (indifferent, unjustified, non-natural, revisable at will); whether a convention requires more than convergent behavior; whether conventions must be shared knowledge or can be merely statistical; whether a single speaker can have a private convention; and whether meaning is conventional in the same sense that the side of the road we drive on is conventional, or whether the analogy breaks because there is no pre-existing fact for a meaning-convention to coordinate us onto.

Finally, since my friend also likes to say that conventions evolve and were never handed down, tell me how the evolutionary and game-theoretic picture bears on his fiat claim -- whether an account of conventions as things that emerge and drift through repeated interaction supports his conclusion or quietly undercuts it, and whether it makes any difference that a convention can be described as arbitrary while still being the one thing it would be irrational for any individual to deviate from unilaterally. Close by saying which single reading you think he most likely had in mind, which one he would need for his argument to go through, and what further question I could ask him to find out which he actually holds. Please write your answer in American English.
