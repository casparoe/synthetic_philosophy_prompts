For a couple of years now I've kept a note on my phone of my own gut reactions to situations where someone has to choose between options they say are equally good, and I've reached the point where I can't tell whether the note records a consistent view or just a pile of moods. I'm not a philosopher; I do accounts payable for a hospital group, and this started when a friend who teaches philosophy at the local college made a joke about the starving donkey between two identical bales of hay (I've seen the story called Buridan's ass) and I said I'd have eaten one by now, and he said that wasn't the point. Since then I've been collecting cases. What I want from you is not a lecture on free will or on decision theory but a principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures the pattern in my reactions, together with an honest account of which reactions the principle forces me to abandon. If no principle gets them all, I would rather hear that than be handed one padded to fit.

Here are the reactions as plainly as I can put them. Two identical tins of tomatoes, same brand, same price, and I take one without a thought: entirely fine, nothing to answer for. Two cafés I like equally, one to my left and one to my right, and I stand there dithering: not fine, it feels like a malfunction, and I would tell a friend to just walk into one. Tossing a coin to choose between two films I am equally keen on: fine, and it does not feel like a cop-out. Tossing a coin to settle whether to take a job I am genuinely torn over: not fine, it feels like an abdication, even though I have just told you the two offers are equally good. A friend who is genuinely indifferent between two flats asking me to name one: fine, I don't think they are shirking. An employer genuinely indifferent between two equally qualified candidates who picks the one whose name comes first alphabetically: not fine, and I think it is the other people affected, not the employer's own reasoning, that makes the difference. Two options I value equally, one reversible and one not — which sandwich versus where to live for the next decade — and only the reversible one feels like something I can leave to chance. And a case where I say I am indifferent but really I just haven't bothered to think it through: picking arbitrarily there is not fine, and I suspect that is because my indifference is fake.

The pair I cannot square is the coin toss for the films and the coin toss for the job, and I have a feeling the donkey is where I have been cheating: I keep saying the donkey is not irrational, only that the story is rigged because nothing is ever exactly equal, but that cannot be right if the tomatoes and the flats are exactly equal. There is also one case I have not made my mind up about: identical twins, both drowning, and I can only reach one. A coin toss there does not feel like a cop-out to me, but I cannot say what makes it different from the job. Start with the principle in a sentence or two, then the cases, then what I have to give up. Keep it under six hundred words.
