I'm a backend engineer — distributed systems, mostly Go and Rust — with no formal training in philosophy beyond a single undergraduate logic course and a long-running argument with a friend who has a doctorate in it. I've written up my side of the argument as an essay and I'd like detailed comments. Please be blunt about the errors; I'd rather be told the whole thing is confused than that it's interesting.

Here's the essay.

**Do I outrun my own compiler? Gödel, machines, and the consistency ladder**

Suppose mechanism is true: my beliefs are the theorems of some formal system F, recursively axiomatizable, so a machine can enumerate them. Take F to be consistent. By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem there is a sentence G_F that says, in effect, that no proof of it exists in F. F does not prove G_F. If F is consistent, then G_F is true. So there is a truth I can see and F cannot prove; F is therefore not me; and since F was arbitrary, no machine is me. That is the Lucas–Penrose argument in a paragraph, and for about a year I thought it was decisive.

Then I noticed how cheap the first step is. F doesn't prove G_F, but F' = F + Con(F) proves G_F immediately, because G_F follows from the consistency of F by elementary reasoning. And Con(F) is a computable function of a description of F, so a machine can be built that simply keeps doing this: F_0 is arithmetic, F_{n+1} = F_n + Con(F_n). So the anti-mechanist's observation isn't that no machine can believe G_F; it's that no machine believes G_F while staying at the same level. Nobody ever accepted a constraint that a mind has to stay at the same level. The ladder starts climbing and F_1, one rung up, now proves its own consistency, which is the thing F_0 could not prove about itself.

The ladder also doesn't terminate. Let F_ω be the union of all the F_n: still a recursively axiomatizable theory, since I can enumerate its axioms by enumerating n and then the axioms of F_n. Gödel applies to F_ω as well. And here is the part I find genuinely striking. F_ω proves Con(F_n) for every n — each rung certifies every rung beneath it — but F_ω does not prove the single statement that every rung is consistent. If it did, it would prove Con(F_ω), because if F_ω were inconsistent then some finite subset of it would be, and that finite subset sits inside some F_n. So the ladder is consistent in every part and cannot see its own consistency as a whole. Local yes, uniform no.

Now the strongest version of the anti-mechanist case, which is the part I can't settle. I don't merely see the consistency of each rung in isolation; I seem to see, in one act, that every rung is consistent and that the construction preserves soundness all the way up. That is precisely the uniform statement that no rung proves and that F_ω doesn't prove either. If that act is real, then it is a capacity not captured by any system in the ladder — including the system a machine would produce by climbing the ladder as far as it likes. So mechanism dies not at the first rung but at the limit, and what kills it is my grasp of one universally quantified fact.

Two things keep me from concluding that. First, the grasp might be an illusion. I can say the words "every rung is consistent," but can I justify them without assuming something at least as strong — that my mathematical insight is sound, or that my beliefs are closed under reflection, meaning that whenever I accept a system I also accept its consistency? That assumption is exactly what is in dispute, and a machine can adopt it too: hand the machine the reflection rule and it becomes a stronger machine with a fresh Gödel sentence of its own. Second, the entire setup assumes my beliefs are closed under deduction, so that if I believe the axioms I believe every consequence of them. I don't think I am closed under deduction; I have never derived the ten-millionth consequence of anything. So F, understood as the system whose theorems are my beliefs, is probably not a system I have at all. It's an idealization, and the argument may be quietly comparing an idealized machine against an unidealized me.

My question is whether the ladder plus the uniform-statement observation really is the strong form of the anti-mechanist case, or whether I have dressed up an equivocation in the notation of a proof.
