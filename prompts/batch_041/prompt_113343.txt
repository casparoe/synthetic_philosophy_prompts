I have been studying the units-of-selection debate and keep returning to an argument that appears to settle it. The gene's-eye view and multilevel selection are mathematically equivalent: any evolutionary change can be partitioned into within- and between-group components, or written entirely in terms of gene frequencies. If that equivalence holds, does it not show that group selection is never a distinct evolutionary cause? After all, if every group-level effect can be re-expressed as a gene-level effect, then groups cannot be units of selection in any robust sense—they are merely a convenient bookkeeping device. I have also encountered a distinction between selection of a group-level trait and selection for that trait, but I do not see how it can do any work if the gene-level accounting is complete. And yet I read about a trait that is costly within groups and spreads because groups with more of it produce more migrants, which I am told is genuine group selection. Which is it, and where does my inference go wrong?
