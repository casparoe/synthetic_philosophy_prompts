The organizers of a joint session between our pain research group and the philosophy of mind reading group have put me on the program, and what I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense. The thesis I am defending is that the badness of pain is carried entirely by its affective-motivational component — the hurt — and not at all by its sensory-discriminative component. Nociception, on my view, is a signaling quantity with no intrinsic disvalue: a being could register tissue damage as crisply as we do and still have nothing to complain about, no more than a thermostat does.

My argument runs roughly as follows. The clinical dissociations show the two components can come apart — pains that do not hurt, and, more controversially, hurts that are not pains — so a state can be a full pain by every sensory criterion and yet carry no disvalue at all. If the disvalue were a function of the sensory quality, that would be impossible. So the disvalue must be a function of the affect. I want to distinguish three things the literature tends to run together: nociception, the sensory signaling; hurt, the affective quality that makes a pain bad; and minding, the higher-order attitude of caring that it is happening. The standard objection to my view — that the asymbolic patient still hurts and merely does not mind it — conflates the second and third. I claim the best reading of the asymbolia cases is that the hurt itself is absent, and that this is what does the load-bearing work. I also want to run one thought experiment past the room: a species whose members have our nociceptors and our pain reports but none of the aversion — congenital asymbolics — and I claim we would owe them no analgesia whatsoever. I expect that to be the flashpoint.

The room is genuinely mixed, which is the problem. From the pain group: a rodent electrophysiologist who treats affect as a placeholder for what we have not yet measured; a human psychophysicist who runs quantitative sensory testing and will want to know how I operationalize hurt in a nonverbal subject; and a chronic-pain clinician who will ask, with some heat, what I would do differently on Monday morning. From the philosophy side: a representationalist who will want to know why I am not simply an imperativist or an evaluativist about pain's content; a functionalist who will ask whether hurt names a role or a feeling; and someone who works on phenomenal concepts and will ask what any of this has to do with the hard problem. Two people in the room have read the asymbolia literature more carefully than I have, and one of them believes the patients' reports are confabulated.

Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get at a philosophy conference: ordered by expected damage — likelihood times severity — and attributed to the objector who will raise each, phrased in that person's vocabulary and steelmanned before you give me the reply. For each, a short spoken answer of about ninety seconds and an honest note on where the answer is soft. Tell me what to concede up front to buy credibility and what to hold, and give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Mark which objections are noise and which, if they land, force me to revise the thesis rather than merely sharpen it; if there is a single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the room, say which it is. No reading list — the room knows the literature better than I do, and I would rather have the objections than the citations.

Make the document self-critical rather than reassuring. At the end, name the single consideration — argument, case, or finding — that, if it held up, should change my mind about the thesis rather than being something to defuse, and say where your own assessment is most likely to be wrong.
