I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar on animal ethics and environmental philosophy, with about sixteen students (mostly philosophy majors, three or four from biology), and I want to spend the whole session on one uncomfortable claim that a colleague and I keep arguing about over coffee: that habitat destruction can reduce the total suffering of wild animals, and that the net welfare of an ecosystem is therefore not the same thing as its health, its integrity, or its species richness. The students will have read nothing on this, so the point has to land through what they do rather than through what I announce. Please design a runnable activity for me — a simulation, a role-play with assigned roles and rules, or a structured deliberation under constraints — in which the class sits as a board of trustees for a fictional island reserve and has to spend a fixed budget of land-use decisions across several habitat types, then compute a welfare ledger from materials I hand out. I want the numbers arranged so that the allocation scoring highest on a separate ecosystem-integrity scorecard — the one their instincts and the biology students will push for — is also the allocation producing the largest total quantity of animal suffering, and so that holding the pristine habitat is precisely what drives the suffering up. They should arrive at that result by filling in their own columns, before anyone names the idea; the moment of arithmetic is the whole point. Supply the species list, the per-individual welfare parameters, the population responses to each land-use option, both scorecards, and print-ready materials, and make the figures internally consistent and checkable by hand in the time available. Invent the species rather than using real ones, so that nothing turns on facts the students might remember wrongly.

Once the arithmetic is on the board, I want the activity to turn against the framework that produced it, because the claim itself is only half of what I am after. Build the debrief so it has two distinct targets: first, the counterintuitive result they just generated; second, the aggregation assumptions that generated it. So the materials should let us ask, using their own numbers, what it means to add the life-years of a small, hugely fecund species with heavy juvenile mortality to those of a large, slow-breeding one; whether treating each death as a harm is doing all the work in the total; whether the replacement of one species by another should count as a welfare gain at all; and whether an ecosystem can be said to have a welfare in the first place, or whether that phrase is a metaphor imported from medicine and management. The design should make those questions feel like discoveries about the ledger the students themselves built, not like a lecture appended to it.

Please give me the full package: the setup and any roles, the rules and the budget and a scoring scheme that needs no real money, the printed materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the debrief with explicit mappings from particular moments in the activity to the parts of the idea, and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure. Finish with a frank note on which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in speech — for instance any part of the argument that depends on how the numbers were chosen in the first place — so that I am not left thinking the game proved more than it did.
