I have no formal training in philosophy or in economics and no academic affiliation, but I have spent two years reading on my own about procrastination and self-control, and next month I am moderating the audience Q&A after a public lecture by a philosopher-economist whose settled position is that the traditional topic of weakness of will is best handled as time-inconsistent preference — a hyperbolic discount curve that makes the near term weigh more heavily than the far term — and that talk of acting against one's better judgment is a colorful redescription of a preference reversal rather than a phenomenon requiring its own explanation. Treat her as the defender of that position stated in its strongest form; you do not need to know anything about her beyond what I have just said.

Please take the role of a philosopher working at the intersection of action theory and behavioral economics, and also of a coach preparing someone to conduct a demanding interview. Begin by thinking carefully, in your own notes, about what the strongest version of her position is and where a model built out of preferences over dated outcomes genuinely runs out of resources — I would rather have two or three real pressure points than ten shallow ones — and only then draft the questions.

Draft eight to ten numbered questions, each followed by a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I am not left holding a follow-up that misses. After each question, add one bracketed line telling me what that question is designed to expose. The questions must turn on the content of her view: do not ask her to summarize it, do not ask how she came to it, and do not ask anything that would fit any guest on any topic. Do not give me reading lists, citations, or suggestions for further reading — I have no use for them, and she will supply her own.

Include at least one question on each of the following, and make them as sharp as the material allows. Some of these will naturally yield more than one question.

1. The case where preference and judgment agree and the action still fails. Suppose that at eight in the evening she both ranks the work above the television and judges that the work is worth doing, and then watches television anyway. A discount curve has nothing to reverse here, because the near-term option is not preferred. Ask whether her account has any room for this case, and whether it must redescribe it — as a failure to act on a ranking she held, or as a ranking she never really held — in a way that quietly reinstates the very thing she set out to eliminate. Follow up on whichever escape she takes.

2. A distinction I have come to think matters, which I have not seen put this way. Call it shallow versus deep akrasia. In the shallow case the judgment is stable and loses. In the deep case the judgment itself flips at the moment of action: at eight in the evening she sincerely ranks the television above the work she sincerely ranked above it that morning, and the next morning she regards the previous evening's ranking not merely as different from her own but as corrupted. Ask what work "corrupted" is doing that "different" would not do, and whether her framework can make sense of the thought that one of the two rankings is a mistake rather than simply the other's successor. Follow up by pressing on whether a preference ordering can be criticizable by the very person who has it.

3. A treatment that leaves the discount curve exactly as it is — the television still feels more attractive at eight in the evening — but removes the tendency to act on the pull, so that she works. Ask whether this person has acquired a stronger will or merely a different preference ordering, and whether her framework can tell those apart at all. Follow up on whichever answer she gives, especially if she says the two descriptions come to the same thing.

4. Second-order preferences and commitment devices. If she hands her laptop to a neighbor for the evening, is that not evidence that she ranks her own rankings, so that there is a standpoint above the discount curve from which the curve is assessed and found wanting? Ask whether that higher standpoint is just another preference, and if so what stops the regress; then ask whether the person who binds herself is exercising self-control or outsourcing it, and whether her account makes the second look like weakness. Follow up on what she takes the regress point to show.

5. Exploitability. Suppose someone knows the exact shape of her curve and offers a sequence of trades, each of which she prefers at the moment it is offered, leaving her worse off by her own later reckoning. Ask whether exposure to that counts as a defect of rationality or only as inconsistency, whether she thinks the two come apart in a way that matters, and what she would say about a person whose curve is unusual but perfectly consistent and who nevertheless describes herself as unable to do what she knows she should. Follow up accordingly.

6. The self that is supposed to be weak. If she models the agent as a sequence of dated selves bargaining with one another, ask whether anything in that picture can be weak-willed as opposed to outvoted, and whether the picture loses the very thing that needs explaining — one agent divided against herself. Follow up on whether she is willing to call the divided-agent language a mere manner of speaking, and what would be lost if it were.

7. The normative sting. Ask whether her view entails that the procrastinator is merely differently constituted rather than defective, and whether she accepts that consequence for the person who loses out by her own lights. Follow up on whether she thinks the word "defective" can be given any content once the discount curve is treated as just another parameter.

Finally, tell me which single question you would open with and which you would save for last, and why.
