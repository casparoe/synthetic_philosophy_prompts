I'm a software engineer on a team that ships machine-learning systems, and I have no formal training in philosophy. In our internal design and ethics reviews, the sentence "you can't get an ought from an is" keeps surfacing, and it seems to be doing a different job every time. In the most recent review, a colleague used it to argue that our evaluation results — all descriptive claims about how the model behaves under various inputs and conditions — cannot support any conclusion about what we ought to build or ship, so the ethics reviewer's recommendations do not follow from the technical findings. I have also heard the same sentence used to mean that moral claims are merely expressions of preference, that science can never tell us what to value, and that anyone who draws a policy recommendation from a study has committed a fallacy. I cannot tell whether these are one claim in different clothes or several unrelated claims sharing a single sentence.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that "you can't get an ought from an is" could be standing for, as a menu I can bring back to the team. State each reading as sharply as you can, and for each one say whether it is a trivial consequence of how terms and entailment work, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false — and what kind of "ought" and what kind of "is" it is about. I would like the menu to separate at least: (a) a claim about deductive entailment, including the point that a normative sentence can be derived from descriptive premises by adding a disjunct or by starting from contradictory premises, so that the interesting thesis must be about relevance or about detaching a normative conclusion rather than derivability as such; (b) a claim about the meanings or definitions of normative versus descriptive terms; (c) a claim about how normative conclusions can be justified rather than deduced; (d) a claim about whether facts alone can motivate action; and (e) a claim about what kind of properties moral properties are, including whether it is compatible with the thought that there can be no normative difference between two situations without some descriptive difference between them. It would also help to say how the slogan relates to and differs from the naturalistic fallacy and the fact/value distinction, since people around me treat all three as interchangeable.

Two things I keep getting stuck on and would like the menu to address. First, our premises are not purely descriptive: they include commitments like "the model should not be deployed if it fails this test," institutional facts like "this feature is required by the contract," and statements like "the model is unsafe in this domain" that seem descriptive and evaluative at once. Does the slogan still do work when the descriptive premises are entangled with goals, rules, and evaluative descriptions, and if so, what exactly is left over? Second, even if no descriptive premises deductively entail a normative conclusion, descriptive premises might still support one — an empirical finding might make a policy unreasonable, or might combine with a general norm to yield a specific recommendation. Is there a reading of the slogan that rules that out, or only readings that leave it untouched?

Finally, please say which readings would actually support my colleague's move in the design review — from "these are the evaluation results" to "therefore no recommendation about what to ship follows" — and which reading people most likely have in mind when they repeat the sentence. I want to know whether the slogan, on its best reading, is a serious objection to what the ethics reviewer was doing, or whether it is being made to carry more weight than it can bear.
