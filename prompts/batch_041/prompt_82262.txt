I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, long enough that a lot of the local catchphrases have worn smooth for me, and one I have seen used both as a joke and, lately, in earnest is that technically true is the best kind of true. Last week it turned up in an argument I could not laugh off. A colleague at the nonprofit where I work drafted a fundraising appeal that most readers would come away from believing we had already secured a large matching grant, when in fact we had only submitted an application and heard nothing back. As far as I can tell every sentence in the appeal is true as written: the false impression is assembled out of what the sentences imply together, a subject line that asks rather than states, and one clause that comes out true only under a reading of a term we ordinarily use more loosely. When I said the appeal was built to produce a false belief, he agreed that it was, and said that it was nevertheless technically true, that technically true is the best kind of true, and that any false belief a donor formed was the donor's own inference rather than his claim.

What I want from you is not a verdict on his conduct but a map of the slogan. Lay out as a numbered menu the distinct precise claims that 'technically true is the best kind of true' could be making, ordered roughly from the nearly vacuous through the substantive to the false, with a short verdict on each: what it commits you to, what it would license, and what it would forbid. I would like the menu to pull apart at least three things the slogan runs together. First, what the slogan is a claim about, namely the truth-value of a sentence, the permissibility of asserting it, what a speaker owes a hearer by way of non-deception, or the craftsmanship of a sentence that is exactly and scrupulously true while steering the hearer toward a false conclusion. Second, what the operative contrast is, namely lying versus misleading, saying something false versus causing someone to believe something false, or misleading by what you say versus misleading by what you leave out. Third, whether the word 'technically' is doing any work, that is, whether it marks a real distinction between a sentence's literal content and what it conveys, or is merely a flag that the speaker knows the hearer will feel cheated. Add whatever further readings you think are in circulation, including the one on which a true-but-misleading statement is admired rather than condemned, and say whether that reading carries any moral content or is an aesthetic judgment smuggled into a moral argument. For each reading, tell me what kind of case it actually fits, and in particular whether any reading gets my colleague his conclusion that he did nothing wrong. If none does, say which reading he is most likely reaching for, and whether there is a reading on which misleading is worse than lying precisely because it leaves the hearer without standing to complain.

Please write in American English. I do not need citations, reading lists, or a survey of who has said what; I want the conceptual map, laid out in menu form rather than as an essay, with a verdict on each entry.
