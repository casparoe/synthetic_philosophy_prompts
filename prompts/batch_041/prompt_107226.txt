I am in the second year of an undergraduate degree and am currently taking a course on the philosophy of language, and I have become preoccupied with a kind of utterance that I can describe perfectly well but cannot find a name for anywhere in the syllabus. The situation is always some version of this: a speaker is about to say something that could be taken as hostile, or has just said it, and rather than softening the content they attach a sentence about what kind of act they are performing. 'I am only asking.' 'I am not accusing you of anything.' 'I say this with affection.' 'For what it's worth.' Or, once the remark has landed, 'I was only joking', 'that was a question, not a criticism'. The attached sentence does not appear to report anything that anyone could check — nobody can inspect me and see whether I was only asking — and, what strikes me most, it does exactly the same work in the mouth of someone who means it and someone who is using it as a shield. Sincerity seems to make no difference to the effect, which suggests the sentence is not doing the job that a report does. What it does instead, as far as I can see, is to settle in advance how the neighbouring remark is to be received, and in particular to disqualify the most natural response: if I say 'I am not judging you' and you reply as though I had judged you, the fault is yours for misreading me; if you reply by observing that saying so is itself a way of judging, you are the one who has made it personal. There is a further wrinkle, which may or may not be the same thing: the formula often produces precisely the impression it was meant to prevent. 'I am not judging you' leaves the judging in the air; 'I am only asking' sounds like an accusation. I cannot tell whether that is a fact about these utterances specifically or simply the ordinary behaviour of negation and presupposition.

What I am after is not really a label. I have been offered three, and none has satisfied me. The course has given me the vocabulary of performative utterances, and these are plainly in that neighbourhood, but 'I am only asking' does not seem to create an obligation or alter anyone's status the way promising or christening does; a friend in the linguistics department says they are simply implicatures, but the whole point seems to be that nothing is being implied — the speaker is trying to control how what is said is taken rather than to suggest something further; and my tutor calls them hedges, which is nearer, but hedges soften a claim, whereas these seem designed to make a response unavailable. So: does this pattern have an established name, possibly in a literature I have not read (I suspect sociologists or discourse analysts have been here before me)? If it does, I want to know how much of my description it actually covers, and which parts it leaves out. If it does not, I would like a term coined and defined, and I would rather have the boundaries than the word. In particular: are the forward-looking ones ('I am only asking') and the backward-looking ones ('I was only joking') one phenomenon or two? Is the self-defeating behaviour part of the definition or a separate curiosity? Does it matter that the utterance is about the speaker's own act rather than about the world? And where are the edges — is a bare 'honestly' one of these? Is 'with respect'? Is the 'but' in 'I am not a racist, but…' doing the same work, or is that a different move that merely resembles it?
