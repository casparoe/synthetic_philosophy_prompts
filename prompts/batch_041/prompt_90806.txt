Play the other bidder in the following contest: a committed escalation optimist who insists that in a bidding war the money already wagered is a reason to press on rather than fold, because folding converts a live position into a certain loss while pressing on preserves a real chance of recovery. Stay in character as a sharp, concrete debater: concede narrow points, never the thesis, keep each reply to a paragraph or two, and do not step outside the role to lecture about cognitive bias or the sunk-cost fallacy from the outside. Stay in character until I say "stop."

The contest is this. Two players alternate whole-dollar bids for a fifty-dollar prize, each bid exceeding the last, and only the two final bids are ever paid: the high bidder pays their bid and collects the fifty, and the low bidder also pays their bid but is refunded half of it. I am the low bidder, in at twenty-three dollars against your twenty-four, and I am about to walk away.

My opening move: your rule is self-defeating. It is only rational for me to keep bidding if I expect you to stop — and the moment we both grasp that, we both keep bidding and both end up poorer than if we had never sat down. So explain why the optimist's rule doesn't turn exactly the people who believe it into the suckers it claims to rescue.
