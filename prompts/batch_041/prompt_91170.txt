I'm an economist by training — my working toolkit is principal–agent models, incomplete contracts, hold-up and ex post adaptation, the rules-versus-standards distinction, and a little mechanism design. I've been drafted into a law-and-economics reading group, and I keep getting lost in the debate between textualists and purposivists about statutory interpretation, because everyone is arguing in a vocabulary I simply don't have: enacted text, legislative intent, canons of construction, deference to agencies, the absurdity doctrine, and so on. What I'd like from you is a translation exercise. Take each of the two positions at its most sophisticated and restate it in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: the statute as an incomplete contract between a legislature and a court; the enacted words as the written terms; legislative history as pre-contractual negotiation and drafting correspondence; the canons as default rules the parties could in principle have contracted around; the absurdity doctrine as something like a business-efficacy or good-faith limitation; purposivism as gap-filling by the hypothetical bargain the enacting coalition would have struck; deference to agencies as delegation to a better-informed agent; and stare decisis as the upkeep of a stable focal point. Where a mapping is too crude to be honest, say so rather than forcing it.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which claims in the lawyers' debate translate cleanly into my framework, which translate only under distortion, and which have no counterpart in it at all? I already suspect several: that there is no single principal whose utility function could be recovered, since a legislature is a collection of agents with cycling preferences, so talk of its intent may be a category error rather than an evidentiary difficulty; that a court does not merely fill a gap between two parties who can renegotiate, but issues a public rule binding on strangers who were never at the table; and that the enacting coalition cannot renegotiate with the present, which breaks the standard efficiency story in which cheap ex post adaptation is what makes incompleteness tolerable. Tell me whether those suspicions are right, what else falls out that I haven't anticipated, and whether the residue is best read as a defect of my framework — something the economics of contracts cannot represent — or as a sign that the legal debate is vaguer than it appears.

Finally, I want a verdict on whether the translation dissolves the disagreement. If textualism and purposivism come out as nothing more than a choice between commitment and flexibility, then the whole dispute looks like an ordinary cost-benefit question and the lawyers' heat is misplaced. If it doesn't dissolve, say precisely what survives the translation that I cannot restate as a trade-off, and tell me whether I should conclude that my vocabulary is missing a dimension — authority, legitimacy, legality, something of that kind — or that the lawyers are trading on an ambiguity my training has taught me not to tolerate.
