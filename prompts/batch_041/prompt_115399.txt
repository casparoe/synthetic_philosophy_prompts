I chair the bylaws committee of a forty-household housing cooperative, and we are rewriting the single clause that decides what belongs to the individual member and what belongs to the community. Our present clause is a list of topics — what a member eats, what they keep in their own unit, what they hang in their window — and it keeps failing on cases the list never anticipated: a member burning wood in a flue shared with three neighbours, a member who wants the co-op to ban meat from the common kitchen, a member who has hung a flag in the window that faces the courtyard. I have read that Sen's impossibility result for a Paretian liberal is the right frame for precisely this problem, and I believe it is, but I need it turned into something a committee of six non-economists can run in one two-hour meeting and then defend at a general meeting where a two-thirds majority has to adopt the outcome.

As I understand the result, it concerns three conditions that cannot all hold at once: (i) unrestricted domain — any consistent ranking of the alternatives by any member is admissible; (ii) weak Pareto — if every member strictly prefers one outcome to another, the community's ranking must agree; (iii) minimal liberalism — at least two members are each decisive over at least one pair of outcomes, where "decisive" means the community's ranking of that pair must follow that member's ranking even when everyone else disagrees. Please work from that statement rather than from the literature. Two features of our situation matter here. First, we never face a mere two-outcome choice: we always have at least two live binary questions, and two binary questions generate four combinations, so the three-alternative precondition for the impossibility is always met and cannot be dodged by observing that any single vote is yes-or-no. Second, the theorem is about what a consistent community ranking can look like; it does not by itself tell a drafting committee what to give up.

I want the procedure built on the following vocabulary, which I would like used exactly as given, extended only where I have left a gap:

- personal coordinate: the component of an outcome that concerns a given member's own body, conduct, or possessions.
- domain: a pair of outcomes over which a member is to be decisive, i.e. the community adopts that member's ranking of the pair.
- pair test: a pair of outcomes lies inside a member's domain only if the two outcomes differ in that member's coordinate and in no other member's coordinate.
- coordinate-altering effect: an effect of one member's coordinate on another member's coordinate — sensory, physical, or resource — as opposed to an effect that runs only through the second member's opinions or knowledge of the first.
- meddlesome preference: a preference that ranks outcomes by another member's coordinate while the holder's own coordinate is unchanged, e.g. disapproval of what a neighbour eats.
- unanimity trap: the situation in which a meddlesome preference, counted in the weak-Pareto step, together with a member's domain, forces the community to rank one pair of outcomes both ways.
- the three levers: (a) narrow domains until no domain pair interacts with any other member's coordinate; (b) restrict the preference base by excluding meddlesome preferences from the Pareto step; (c) retain both conditions and tolerate an inconsistent community ranking, resolved by a pre-announced tie-break.

What I need from you:

1. A procedure, as a table with exactly these columns: Step | The question to ask | What counts as a sufficient answer | Where this step can end the process, and with what verdict | Forced by the impossibility result, or a design choice? Order the steps so the committee asks the cheap, mechanical questions before the expensive ones, and mark every row in which the committee is choosing among the three levers rather than discovering an answer.

2. Three worked applications, one per case above, each as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "case"; "outcome_space" (at least three alternatives, showing the combinations of live questions); "coordinates" (each member's personal coordinate in each alternative); "pair_tests" (list of objects with "pair", "differs_only_in", "verdict", the last one of inside_domain, outside_domain, line_call); "interactions" (list of objects with "member_affected", "trigger", "effect_type", the last one of sensory, physical, resource, symbolic, knowledge_only); "meddlesome_preferences" (list of objects with "holder", "ranks", "why_meddlesome"); "unanimity_trap" (object with "present" and "derivation", the derivation showing each ranking step that produces the conflicting pair); "lever_used" (one of a, b, c, or a combination, with what each costs); "verdict"; "who_decides"; "stopped_at_step"; "unresolvable_by_procedure" (true or false); "judgment_calls" (list); "what_would_change_the_verdict".

3. The meat case is the one I expect to dislike; give me that verdict straight rather than softening it. The flag case I expect the procedure to be unable to settle; if that is right, say so in "unresolvable_by_procedure", identify the row of the table that hands the decision to a person, and say who that person should be.

4. A closing list, no longer than six items, of what this procedure cannot do — including whatever it cannot detect about how members will actually behave once they know the rule.

I do not need citations or a reading list. I need the procedure, the reasoning, and a plain statement of where your procedure is a design choice rather than a consequence of the theorem.
