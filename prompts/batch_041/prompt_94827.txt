Alright, I need a hand thinking something through. I've recently gotten involved in the organizing committee at my job (warehouse-adjacent, I won't say which) and at the same time I finally sat down and read Marx properly instead of absorbing him secondhand, so now I have Opinions and I want to know if they actually hold together. Please take the role of a political philosopher who works on Marx and on the analytical-Marxist literature. Think it through carefully before you answer, and start by spelling out what each of my commitments actually commits me to before you start judging them — I don't want to be told that each item is "contested by someone"; I want to know which ones pull on each other.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) Exploitation is a technical, non-moral notion. A exploits B when A appropriates surplus labor that B performs, against a background where B has no reasonable alternative. Nothing about that is automatically a wrong; if you want to call it wrong you need a seperate story about entitlement.

(2) Alienation isn't a feeling. It's a structural fact about the labor process: someone else sets the purposes, the pace and the design of the work, and the worker's activity is a means to ends she doesn't own. People can be thoroughly alienated and perfectly content, and can feel estranged in jobs where they actually have a lot of control.

(3) The labor theory of value is false — magnitudes of value just aren't proportional to socially necessary labor time — and I don't think anything I care about needs it. I'm happy to give it up.

(4) You can have exploitation without private capitalists. A state enterprise squeezing surplus out of its workforce for the sake of a plan is doing the same thing; so is a worker-owned firm that takes on seasonal hires and keeps the surplus for the owner-members. If exploitation is about who ends up with the surplus product and on what terms, then abolishing private ownership doesn't abolish it.

(5) The moral case against capitalism should run through alienation, not exploitation. What's actually objectionable is unaccountable power over the labor process — someone else's ends organizing my hours. Call it the "whose ends" point. Exploitation as I defined it in (1) is a diagnostic, not an indictment.

(6) A concrete verdict I'm attached to: a shareholder firm that pays seasonal temps the going rate is exploiting them; a worker co-op that hires the same temps at the same rate is not. Same wage, same people, same market — but one of them is doing something the other isn't.

Two smaller things that are part of the picture: I think losing control of the product and losing control of the process come apart, and the second is worse (think platform gig work, where you might nominally own what you produce while every minute of the process is dictated); and I support co-determination and worker ownership as a serious fix, though given (4) I don't expect it to end exploitation.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and which one has to go if I want a package I can defend at the next meeting? Rank the real tensions by how bad they are, give me the cheapest repair for each and what that repair costs me, and tell me straight whether the whole thing is less coherent than it looks.
