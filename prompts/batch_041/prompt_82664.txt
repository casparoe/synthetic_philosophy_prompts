I have been arguing with a colleague in a seminar on comparative collapse, and I would like you to work through the substance of the dispute rather than the rhetoric of it. Her proposal is that we should stop treating collapse as a single predicate and instead separate three things that the word usually runs together: first, the loss of the capacity to coordinate action at scale — the state, the tribute apparatus, the long-distance exchange network; second, the loss of accumulated stock — built infrastructure, stored administrative and technical knowledge, craft repertoires; and third, the loss of a shared frame of meaning, the ritual and cosmological order that made the first two seem worth reproducing. Her further claim, the one I find interesting and suspicious in equal measure, is that these three come apart systematically, and that when they do, the diagnostically important question is which of them failed first, because that answer decides whether we are looking at a failure at all or merely at a reorganisation under a new description.

I would like this tested on the Late Bronze Age collapse, which is the case I know best. Take the following as given, and do not spend your effort adjudicating causes, which are contested: the Hittite state disappears from the record; the Mycenaean palace centres are destroyed or abandoned; the Levantine port cities, Ugarit among them, are destroyed; long-distance exchange contracts sharply; the Linear B script falls out of use; Egypt survives in a reduced form. What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a narrative. Which of the three failures is best evidenced in each of those developments, which of them the surviving evidence can actually speak to at all as opposed to merely being consistent with, and which one, on my colleague's own account, has to have gone first — and whether that last claim is in fact testable against the archaeological and textual record, or whether it is the kind of claim that any sufficiently flexible sequence can be made to fit. Then I want the reverse direction, case against framework: does the Bronze Age material confirm the three-way split, does it show that the three are not separable in practice, or does it expose a fourth kind of failure the framework has no room for?

Finally, and more briefly: say whether the same three-way split earns its keep on one further case, and choose whichever of the Maya Terminal Classic or Rapa Nui you think makes the harder test, given that in both the relevant evidence is thinner and more contested than in the Bronze Age. I am less interested in a verdict on whether those societies failed than in whether the framework's central claim — that the order of the three failures is what matters — survives contact with a case whose chronology we cannot pin down, and what a defender of the framework would have to give up in order to keep it.
