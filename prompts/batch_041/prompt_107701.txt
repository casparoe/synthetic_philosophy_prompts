In most jurisdictions the approval of an agricultural insecticide rests on a balancing test: the benefits of use — yield, cost, food supply — must outweigh the harms, where the harms counted are human poisoning, residues, and damage to vertebrates and to a short list of valued insects such as honeybees, pollinators, and natural enemies. The pest species the product exists to kill is not counted at all, however many die or however they die, and neither are the non-pest insects killed alongside them. Yet the same framework demands humane endpoints for the vertebrates used in the toxicity testing that generates this evidence, and integrated pest management instructs growers to spray only when pest density crosses an economic threshold — that is, only when the value of the crop at risk exceeds the cost of the spray. What theory of moral status and of value does this arrangement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can: anthropocentrism, a welfarism that assigns insect suffering a weight near zero, a contractarian view on which only those who can reciprocate or complain have standing, or something else. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, say whether its protection of vertebrates and honeybees is consistent with the rest of the framework or quietly undermines it, and identify which rival theories would license exactly these same rules. Then say what would have to change in the approval process itself — which tests, which evidence, which numbers — if insect sentience were admitted as morally relevant.
