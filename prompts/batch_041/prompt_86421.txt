Two colleagues of mine, both competent philosophers and neither playing devil's advocate, spent most of an afternoon last week arguing about the puzzle usually called Buridan's ass: the donkey placed exactly midway between two identical bales of hay, equally hungry and equally far from each, which starves because nothing tips the balance toward one rather than the other. Several of us stayed to listen, and none of us could say afterwards who had the better of it. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange for me, move by move.

What follows is my reconstruction of what was said, in the order it was said. Call the speakers A and B.

1. A: "The donkey is a fiction, and the fiction is doing no work. No creature has ever been in a state of exact indifference between two options equal in every respect; there is always an asymmetry — you stand a millimetre closer to one bale, one smells faintly sweeter, you happened to look left first. The case describes a situation that cannot arise, so it cannot tell us anything about how a rational agent should act. And even if the options really were tied, there would be nothing to get right: any choice would be as good as any other, which is not a paradox, it is a decision."

2. B: "You are treating a thought experiment as a prediction. The bales are stipulated equal; that is a premise in an argument, not a description of a farmyard. The argument runs: an agent acts only for a reason, and the will follows the greater apparent good. If that principle is true, the donkey has no sufficient reason to go left and none to go right, so it cannot act at all — which is absurd. So either the principle is false or rational action is not always possible. That is a real result, and it does not depend on any donkey having ever existed."

3. A: "Then the result is empty, because it holds only in a situation that cannot arise. A principle about rational action can hold in every situation that actually occurs and still fail in your stipulated one; that is not a refutation of the principle, it is a demonstration that you have applied it outside its domain. And suppose I grant you the tie: the agent picks. That is not a failure of the will and not an irrational act. Choosing requires a reason to prefer one option to the other; picking requires only a reason to stop deliberating. They are different activities, and only the first one needs a preference."

4. B: "Your distinction cuts against you. If picking is something the agent does on purpose — if it picks in order to get on with its life — then it acts for a reason after all, namely that further deliberation costs more than it can buy. But that reason is a reason to pick something, not a reason to pick this bale rather than that one. So either the pick has no reason behind it, in which case it is not an intentional action at all and your theory of action has no room for it; or it has a reason that is indifferent between the options, in which case the will does not always follow the greater apparent good, and the donkey was never paralyzed in the first place. You have either described an action your theory cannot contain, or handed me a counterexample to the principle I was using the donkey to attack."

5. A: "Then notice where we have arrived. The reason you just gave — that further deliberation costs more than it can buy — is a reason to take either bale rather than neither, and the donkey has it too. Once you count the cost of indecision, the two bales are not the only options on the table: starving is a third option with a strictly worse payoff. A donkey that sees that does not face a tie. Genuine indifference is unstable; specify what happens if you do not choose, and the options stop being equal."

6. B: "That relocates the tie rather than removing it. Give the donkey a third option — left bale, right bale, or a slightly worse bale off to the side — and the two good bales remain exactly equal to each other; the presence of a worse third option does not make the first two unequal. What you have shown is that a rational donkey will not starve. You have not shown what makes it go left rather than right, and that was the question."

Please answer in four parts.

First, go through the six moves in order and rate each on this five-point scale: clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, clearly right. Give two or three sentences of justification per rating, and say what the move gets right even when the rating is negative. Rate the moves individually rather than averaging the two speakers against each other; a speaker can be right in one move and wrong in the next. Be willing to say that one of them is simply wrong at a particular step — a verdict that splits every difference is not an adjudication.

Second, answer the three questions the exchange actually turns on, separately, since they can come out differently: (a) does the donkey starve, and is its starvation irrational? (b) does the case refute the principle that the will follows the greater apparent good, or only refute it for situations that cannot occur? (c) can a reason to pick something be a reason to pick this rather than that?

Third, name the point both of them are missing, if there is one, and say whether it dissolves the dispute or merely sharpens it.

Fourth, end with a single final line in the form \answer{...}, where the braces contain a short phrase of your own wording naming which of the two is closer to right overall and on what. That line must be the last thing in your reply.
