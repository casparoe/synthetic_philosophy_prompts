Two weeks ago I got into a long thing at a cookout with my old college roommate — he's a staff attorney at an immigration legal-aid outfit now — about a case that's coming to our panel next month, and I still can't figure out how to write it up. I sit on the credibility review panel for our refugee protection board; we're the body that hears challenges when a first-instance officer denies a claim mainly on the ground that the applicant's own account "doesn't hang together." The file in front of us: the applicant narrates being at a demonstration and also being home with his brother that same afternoon, his dates for two other events are off by about a year, and he says he was "arrested" but describes something that reads more like a summons he blew off. The officer treats all three as lies. My roommate says you can't interpret another person at all unless you assume their beliefs are mostly true and mostly consistent with each other, so the officer's reasoning is incoherent on its face and we should remand with instructions; I said that sounds like a license to believe everyone, and besides, who made our logic the yardstick for his testimony; he said I was confusing a constraint on what counts as interpretation with a moral virtue, and that if I drop the constraint I have to drop the idea that we're interpreting him rather than just scoring noise. He also kept insisting there's a difference between maximizing how much of what someone says comes out true and maximizing how intelligible they are to us, and that the case turns on which one you're after — I nodded like I got it.

Draft the panel's written decision memo, the thing I circulate before we vote: a concrete disposition (remand with a specific instruction, affirm, or affirm in part) plus the reasoning, and a rule the officers below could actually apply to the next file. Please give the officer's side its strongest honest hearing rather than a strawman — I've got two colleagues who'll vote against anything that smells like "credit every inconsistency," and I don't want to lose them on a technicality. One of them wants to bolt on a two-part test: first ask whether any reasonable assignment of beliefs and desires would make the account mostly true and internally coherent, and only then ask whether that assignment is independently plausible given what else we know about the region and the timeline. Tell me whether that test actually captures the principle or whether it smuggles in something extra — because honestly I suspect it does, but I can't say what. And if you think the whole "you have to assume they're mostly right" line is overstated as a general rule, say where exactly it breaks down; I'd rather the memo be correct than have it vindicate my friend.
