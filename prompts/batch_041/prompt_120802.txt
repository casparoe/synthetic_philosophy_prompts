A long-running dispute in the study of nationalism has stalled because its two camps talk past each other. The modernists hold that nations are recent: products of state centralization, print capitalism, mass schooling, conscription, and the deliberate manufacture of traditions, whose claimed continuity with a pre-modern past is largely retrospective. The ethno-symbolists hold that nations are not created ex nihilo: they are built out of pre-modern ethnic communities -- ethnies -- with their own names, myths of descent, memories, symbols, and boundaries, and that an account which makes nations appear only in the last two centuries will fail to explain why the nations that did form formed where, when, and as they did.

Assume the two camps have agreed to a genuine adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement they could both sign. It must be a document a modernist and an ethno-symbolist would each recognize as a fair rendering of their own position -- no false compromise in which both are made to say something neither believes. It should contain, in order:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides accept: about what the evidence shows, about what any theory of nationalism must explain, and about what each side concedes the other gets right. Each claim should cost each side something; none may be hedged so thinly that either side could have written it alone.

2. A jointly acceptable procedure for dating a nation -- what would have to be true, of whom, and by when, for it to be correct to say that a given nation existed at a given time. The two sides need not expect the procedure to yield the same verdicts in the same cases; they must both accept it as fair.

3. A translation table for the terms doing the most work -- nation, ethnic community, tradition, invention, consciousness, continuity, modernity. For each term, give the modernists' reading, the ethno-symbolists' reading, and what, if anything, turns on the difference. Mark each surviving disagreement as substantive or verbal.

4. The residual disagreements, each stated in the wording its own side would accept, and each made as sharp as possible without caricature.

5. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, state the test, case, or kind of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance -- including what each would count as disconfirming its own view.

Constraints: work from general knowledge. Include no citations, no bibliography, and no attributed quotations, and do not lean on the contents of any particular text. Keep the historical cases you invoke to ones you can characterize accurately from general knowledge, and phrase each side's prediction so that it does not depend on contested details. Do not let either side win by definitional fiat: where a point turns on how a term is defined, the definitional work belongs in the translation table, and the statement should say plainly what turns on it.

Close with a short paragraph identifying which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal rather than substantive, and why.
