I'm on a committee redesigning my department's course offerings, and I keep bumping into something wierd about how we slice up the past. History has medievalists who stop around 1500 and early modernists who start there. Art history has a Renaissance running through the 1500s and sometimes past it. The history of science people have a Scientific Revolution sitting on top of both. So someone who works on, say, the 1550s is a late medievalist in one building, a Renaissance person in another, and an early modernist in a third. Same decades, three different periods. And the course catalog, the library database's time-period filters, and the way museums hang their galleries in date order all inherit the same labels.

What does that whole arrangement presuppose? Is it committed to periods being real joints in history, or can it get by with them being useful administrative fictions? Does being an expert in 'the Middle Ages' presuppose that those centuries are more alike each other than they are like what comes after? The Renaissance label seems to smuggle in a claim about a sharp break, 'Middle Ages' seems to smuggle in a story of decline and recovery, and 'early modern' seems to assume that we're the sequel. Which of those are actually required by the practice, and which just happen to fit it? And is the practice quietly inconsistent, given that the same decade counts as three different periods, or can it be patched up by saying each label is tracking a different thing -- politics vs. art vs. method?

A colleague wants to throw the labels out and re-sort everything by plain centuries, or by slow variables like climate, trade, and demography. Tell me what would really change if we did, and where the current setup is loose enough that several incompatible views about periods would all license it. I dont need cites or a reading list, just the argument.
