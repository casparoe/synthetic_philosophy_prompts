I'm a TA for a graduate seminar in normative ethics, and the final session is on the aggregation of small harms: cases where a very large number of individually trivial harms is set against a single severe one, and the verdict that favours the trivial harms seems to fall out of principles nobody in the room wants to abandon. I'd like to close the term with a poem rather than a summary, and I'd like the poem to do something the summary can't.

Write it in the voice of one bearer of the trivial harm — a single person in the crowd, not a spokesperson for it — who has been told the terms of the comparison, has been told the size of the crowd in a notation they cannot read, and is now being asked, in effect, to accept their share of it. Nothing in the poem may name a number, a quantity, or a rate, and the words "utility," "aggregate," "infinity," "threshold," and "trade-off" are off limits. The scale of the case has to be present as something the speaker cannot get into the poem, rather than as a figure the poem can state.

For the form: exactly five numbered sections. Each of the first four ends on the same short line — no more than six words, stating something plainly and uncontroversially true of one person's afternoon — and the fifth must bring that line back with its truth no longer available, without the poem explaining what has changed or why. Let the line accumulate weight by returning rather than by being elaborated, and let the ending leave the speaker where they stand rather than deliver a verdict. The closing section should be the shortest.

Someone who has taught this problem should recognise the case from the poem alone: the speck, the eye, the stretch of years, the one other person, and the speaker's failure to locate, in their own experience, the weight that is supposed to settle the argument either for them or against them. Someone who hasn't should still meet a poem rather than a diagram, so keep the diction plain and the images physical. A philosopher of the relevant sort shouldn't wince, and a reader who dislikes the arithmetic shouldn't feel that the poem is on anyone's side.

Return only the poem, as a numbered list of exactly five items, one stanza per number, each stanza of six lines or fewer: no title, no epigraph, no dedication, and no note after it.
