In the legal systems of most wealthy countries, two bodies of doctrine sit side by side.

The ordinary law of contract holds a promise binding on the promisor's future self. A thirty-year mortgage signed at twenty-five is enforced at fifty-five, however much the promisor's circumstances, preferences, and considered judgment have changed in the interval; there is no general cooling-off period, no requirement that the promise be periodically renewed, and no official inquiry into whether it still serves her interests. The same law permits far more open-ended bindings than a mortgage: covenants not to compete, long-term supply contracts, loans secured against future wages.

Personal bankruptcy law in the same systems releases the debtor from precisely those obligations. The release is ordinarily available as a matter of course, subject to exceptions such as taxes, certain student loans, and family support obligations, and it is structured asymmetrically. A debtor who wants to keep paying a debt that has just been discharged must re-assume it in a fresh act made at the moment of discharge, subject to court approval or to an attorney's certification that it will not impose an undue hardship, and revocable for sixty days after it is filed with the court or until discharge, whichever is later. So the promise made at the beginning is enforced with no safeguards at all, while the promise made at the end, at the very moment of release, is wrapped in cooling-off, supervision, and a right of rescission. The same law refuses certain self-bindings outright: one cannot lawfully promise perpetual personal service, and the right to quit cannot in general be given away in advance. And the release cuts along a line of its own: it extinguishes the creditor's personal claim against the debtor but leaves any lien on her property standing, so a single debt can die as an obligation and survive as a property interest.

I want a reconstruction of what this cluster of practices presupposes about the person across time and about diachronic rationality. Address the following.

First, which conception of the subject does the arrangement require? One continuous agent who owns her own future and may therefore pledge it; a succession of temporally distinct selves, each with claims against the others; or something that distinguishes sharply between alienating one's labor and alienating one's will? Note that the practice treats a personal claim and a property claim on the same asset differently, and say what that distinction presupposes about what a person can and cannot put up as collateral.

Second, bring in the money-pump tradition. The standard arguments there hold that an agent whose preferences are cyclic, or whose preferences change over time, can be walked through a sequence of transactions that she welcomes at each stage and that leaves her worse off — and that the exploiter need not be a separate person, since a creditor who understands the pattern, or the debtor's own later self, will do. Does the mandatory discharge presuppose that debtors are actually vulnerable to such pumps, or only that they are poor at foreseeing them? The literature usually treats vulnerability to a pump as evidence that a preference ordering is defective; if the discharge is a legal remedy for a pump, does the law thereby treat ordinary preference change as a defect rather than as a fact to be planned around? And does the structure of reaffirmation — an act that must be contemporaneous with the release, supervised, and revocable — presuppose that the debtor is not competent to bind herself even when she is fully informed?

Third, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits. Several theories would license it: a consequentialist story about insurance and the ex ante cost of credit, a republican story about domination by one's own past, a Kantian story about the inalienability of the will, a straightforwardly paternalist story about consumer protection. Say where the practice is underdetermined among these, and identify which rival views it rules out.

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. Work out the two limiting cases: a legal order that treated the future self as a distinct person whose interests the present self has no standing to pledge, and one that treated the present self as sovereign over the future self, enforcing advance waivers of the discharge so that the twenty-five-year-old could sell the fifty-five-year-old's release at the moment of borrowing. In each case, say which doctrines would have to go, and whether the resulting order would still be recognizable as a system of contract and credit at all.
