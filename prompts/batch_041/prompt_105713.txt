I've been reading about a move in game theory that I think is underused, and I want a short story built around it. Here's the move: instead of trying to predict what the other party will do, you redesign the situation itself so that no matter how they would have played by default, everyone ends up better off — better off in every scenario, not on average, and without you needing any beliefs about their reasoning. The only thing you get to assume is something thin and structural, like: renaming the options doesn't change how a person chooses betweeen them.

Write me a short story (around 1500 words) with two characters who manage this, in an ordinary setting — roommates, a co-op, two departments fighting over a budget, that kind of thing. Not a lab game, not a heist, not a genie. Then, late in the story, one of the thin assumptions they leaned on quietly fails — the relabeling turns out to carry information, say — and the arrangement that was supposed to be safe for both of them goes wrong in a way that isn't really anyone's fault. I want the reader to feel the gap between "better for us in every scenario" and "better for us given how we each read the other." Nobody should lecture about game theory; let the mechanics of the situation do it. Don't name the concept and don't end with a moral.
