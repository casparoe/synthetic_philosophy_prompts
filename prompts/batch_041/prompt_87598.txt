I retired some years ago from an experimental-science career and have spent much of the time since teaching myself philosophy; for the past year my reading has been concentrated on logical uncertainty, the problem of how a rational agent should assign degrees of belief to mathematical and logical statements it cannot yet prove or refute. Before I present a position paper to a small reading group, I want an audit of the position itself, because I suspect I have imported assumptions from my old habits of thought without noticing where they pull against one another.

Here is where I have landed.

(1) Degrees of belief are subjective probabilities, and I take the sure-loss argument to be the fundamental vindication of this: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, a bookmaker can construct a set of bets that guarantees you a loss whatever happens, and no rational agent can be exposed to that. Conditionalization is the corresponding update rule.

(2) A rational agent is not logically omniscient. No actual agent assigns probability 1 to every logical truth, and I do not think a normative theory framed for an agent that could compute all the consequences of its beliefs is a theory of rationality for creatures like us.

(3) There is a fact of the matter about every well-formed mathematical statement, including statements no one will ever prove or refute, and it is settled independently of us.

(4) Rational degrees of belief should be computable, or at least computably approximable: it should be possible in principle to specify a procedure that, given time, issues the credences a rational agent ought to have. I read the logical induction program — a computable procedure that assigns probabilities to logical statements and refines them over time, whose central guarantee is that no efficiently specifiable betting strategy can extract an unbounded profit — as evidence that this is the right shape for a theory of logical uncertainty.

(5) For a fixed body of evidence and a fixed mathematical background there is a unique correct credence function; two agents with the same evidence who disagree about an open conjecture, one at 0.3 and one at 0.6, cannot both be rational.

(6) Mathematical inquiry is rational inquiry. A mathematician who has not yet proved a theorem is doing something rational when partial results, analogies, and the failure of attempted counterexamples move her credence, and if the theorem is provable her credence ought to climb toward 1 as she works.

What I want is a consistency audit of this package. Which of these are genuinely in tension with which, and how badly — rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list. Which pairs merely appear to conflict, and why is the appearance misleading. For each genuine tension, give me the cheapest way to restore consistency and say what that repair costs me elsewhere, since I would rather know the price than be told the package is fine. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I would still be left owing after making it.

Two constraints on the answer. First, do not tell me what is in the literature to read; I can find that myself, and I want the audit, not a bibliography. Second, be careful to separate what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus — where an answer to one of my questions depends on a live dispute, say so and give me the shape of the dispute rather than a verdict dressed up as settled.
