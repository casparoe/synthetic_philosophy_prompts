Write a poem spoken by the Tortoise to Achilles, on the question their exchange leaves hanging: whether a deductive step can be justified without circularity, or whether the Tortoise's refusal to reach the conclusion is a confusion rather than a discovery. Use the triangle from Carroll's dialogue — the two sides each equal to the same thing, and the conclusion they are supposed to yield — as the poem's one concrete image, and let the form do the philosophical work. Build it as a cascade: each stanza grants everything the previous stanza granted and adds exactly one further conditional premise, so that stanza one contains one such premise, stanza two contains two, and so on, and the reader feels the argument accumulating premises it can never spend. Aim for roughly twenty to twenty-eight lines. Do not end on the conclusion and do not end on another conditional; end on a line of a different grammatical kind — an instruction, a gesture, a refusal, an admission — and let that choice of ending carry your verdict. If you take the Tortoise to be right that no stock of premises ever compels the step, the cascade should stop because there is genuinely nothing left to add; if you take him to have mistaken a rule of inference for an additional premise, the ending should be the moment someone stops asking for a premise and simply infers. Commit to one of these rather than staging the standoff. After the poem, add three or four sentences stating which reading you committed to and where the poem leans on a contested interpretation of the dialogue; I would rather have a poem with a clear position than one that hedges.
