I'm writing a novel about the naming and memorial board of a mid-sized English city — the people who decide which statues stay up and which streets get renamed — and I've wrote myself into a corner with the board's seventh decision. They've ruled six times so far, and each one is defensible on its own and maddening when you try to state the through-line: (1) a statue of a general who sent troops into a crowd of strikers and had eleven of them shot — it stood on the corner of the works where it happened — came off its plinth and went into the city museum with a room of context, after the families of the dead campaigned for years. (2) A street named after a novelist who preyed on the young women in his circle kept its name, with a small plate at each end explaining the record, after the families kicked up a fuss. (3) A school named after a poet who walked out on his wife and children was left completely alone; the report said they'd rather not turn schools into courts. (4) The fountain in the park was left alone, no plate, even though the mill owner who gave it ran his mills on child labor — the inscription honors a gift to the city, not the giver's life. (5) The war memorial kept every name, including two men who came home and did things that got them locked up, one of them for what he did to his own family; a memorial to the dead isn't a certificate of good character. (6) A square named after a doctor who campaigned in public for the compulsory sterilization of people he called the unfit, and who sat on the board that carried it out, got a new name, the old sign into the archive, and a plate explaining why — and that square happens to be the one outside the building where the sterilization board actually sat, which I think I did by accident and which might be doing all the work.

Now the seventh: a health center across town, named after the man who was the city's Medical Officer of Health in the 1930s, who got the TB sanatorium built and who also ran the same sterilization program out of that office, public lectures and all. The building isn't on the site of the old institution — that's a retail park now. The survivors' group is still alive and asking. The staff say the name has just become the name of the place where they work. And the family trust pays for a research post there and has started making noises about the money.

So what does my board do, and what should it do if it wants to be consistent with itself? Give me the rules that fit all six earlier decisions, what each one predicts about the health center, which of the six is the closest analog, which rule you'd go with and why, and which case I should write next if I want to force the board to choose between them. If the six decisions genuinely underdetermine the seventh, tell me what principle the board would be adopting either way. No reading list please — I've got Margalit and Rieff on the nightstand and neither of them has ever had to vote on a plaque. Two things worth knowing: one character keeps saying that the plinth forgets and the vitrine remembers, and another wants every naming to work like a leasehold, expiring after fifty years unless the city votes it back; if either of those is the real rule my board is running, say so.

And could you write it up in British English? The book's set over here and my copyeditor keeps correcting me, so I'd like to see it in her dialect before she gets her hands on it.
