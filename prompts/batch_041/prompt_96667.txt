My game theory reading group has spent the past few weeks on the strategy-stealing argument, and I keep noticing that a single one-liner is being made to do an enormous amount of work: an extra move can never hurt you. It surfaced again last night, when a colleague used it to explain why the first player must win Hex, and then dismissed my objection about chess — where the obligation to move is frequently what loses the game — as my "confusing a game-theoretic fact with a chess fact." I am not convinced the slogan is simply false, but I am fairly sure it is not a single claim either, and I would like to see the space of readings laid out before I decide whether his move was legitimate.

So please treat an extra move can never hurt you as a slogan whose content has to be recovered, and set out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, as a menu rather than a single preferred reading. Vary the readings along the dimensions that actually matter here: what is being added (an extra legal move, an extra turn, a strictly larger action set, a pass option, a second chance after an error), what "hurt" is measured against (the same player without the option, the opponent's counterfactual position, the game's outcome value, expected payoff under a specified belief), and whether the claim concerns a single position, a game, or a whole family of games. Distinguish claims about the existence of a winning strategy from claims about equilibrium payoffs and from claims about a player's information or commitments; separate the pure monotonicity claim (adding options never lowers the value of a decision problem) from the stronger first-mover claim that stealing the opponent's strategy yields a win; and say exactly which structural conditions each reading requires — symmetry between the players, no draws, a no-zugzwang or free-pass property, determinacy, finiteness, perfect information. Some readings should come out as near-tautologies, some as substantive and true only within a restricted class of games, and at least one as flatly false in general; I want each labelled accordingly, and I want to be told, for each reading, whether it actually licenses the inference my colleague drew in Hex and whether it survives the chess case or must be explicitly restricted to games that exclude zugzwang. Where a reading is true only under a condition, state the condition; where a single phrase hides two formalizations, split them into two entries.

Return the result as a JSON object I can paste into my notes, with exactly these fields: {"slogan": the slogan under analysis, as a string; "readings": an array of objects, each with "label" (a short name for the reading), "precise_claim" (the reading stated as a claim that could be true or false), "conditions" (the structural conditions under which it holds, or "none"), "verdict" (one of "trivial", "defensible", "restricted-truth", "false", "ambiguous"), "supports_hex_inference" (one of "yes", "no", "partly"), "supports_chess_objection" (one of "yes", "no", "partly", meaning: does the reading leave room for the chess counterexample), and "comment" (one sentence); "most_likely_intended_reading" (which reading the colleague probably meant, as a string); "bottom_line" (two or three sentences on whether the slogan can bear the weight it was carrying)}. Keep the prose inside the fields tight; the menu is the point.
