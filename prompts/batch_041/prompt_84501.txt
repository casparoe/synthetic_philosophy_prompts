I am a fifth-year PhD student in intellectual history, finishing a chapter on early modern regimens of self-cultivation, and I have run into a passage in Franklin's Autobiography that I cannot get to hold still. I would like help working out what the passage is actually claiming, because the more closely I look at it the more its parts seem to pull in different directions. The passage is the account of his project of moral perfection, together with the method he contrives for it:

> It was about this time I conceived the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection. I wished to live without committing any fault at any time; I would conquer all that either natural inclination, custom, or company might lead me into. As I knew, or thought I knew, what was right and wrong, I did not see why I might not always do the one and avoid the other. But I soon found I had undertaken a task of more difficulty than I had imagined. While my care was employed in guarding against one fault, I was often surprised by another; habit took the advantage of inattention; inclination was sometimes too strong for reason. I concluded, at length, that the mere speculative conviction that it was our interest to be completely virtuous was not sufficient to prevent our slipping, and that the contrary habits must be broken, and good ones acquired and established, before we can have any dependence on a steady, uniform rectitude of conduct. For this purpose I therefore contrived the following method.

The method is the familiar one. He draws up thirteen names of virtues with a short precept annexed to each:

- Temperance: Eat not to dullness; drink not to elevation.
- Silence: Speak not but what may benefit others or yourself; avoid trifling conversation.
- Order: Let all your things have their places; let each part of your business have its time.
- Resolution: Resolve to perform what you ought; perform without fail what you resolve.
- Frugality: Make no expense but to do good to others or yourself, i.e., waste nothing.
- Industry: Lose no time; be always employed in something useful; cut off all unnecessary actions.
- Sincerity: Use no hurtful deceit; think innocently and justly, and, if you speak, speak accordingly.
- Justice: Wrong none by doing injuries or omitting the benefits that are your duty.
- Moderation: Avoid extremes; forbear resenting injuries so much as you think they deserve.
- Cleanliness: Tolerate no uncleanliness in body, clothes, or habitation.
- Tranquillity: Be not disturbed at trifles, or at accidents common or unavoidable.
- Chastity: Rarely use venery but for health or offspring, never to dullness, weakness, or the injury of your own or another's peace or reputation.
- Humility: Imitate Jesus and Socrates.

He then keeps a little book, a page per virtue, ruled into seven columns for the days of the week, in which he marks a black spot for every fault committed respecting that virtue on that day. He gives a week's strict attention to each virtue successively, leaving the others to their ordinary chance and marking faults each evening; he proceeds through all thirteen, four courses in a year, and likens the procedure to a man with a garden to weed, who does not attempt to eradicate all the bad herbs at once but works one bed at a time, hoping in the end to view a clean book after a thirteen weeks' daily examination.

My difficulty is not with the vocabulary or the historical context. It is that the passage appears to contain two different accounts of how moral improvement happens, and I cannot tell whether Franklin is aware of the difference or whether the second is meant as an implementation of the first. The first is the diagnosis at the close of the long paragraph above: speculative conviction is insufficient, so contrary habits must be broken and good ones acquired and established. That is a causal-psychological claim about what changes conduct. The second is the apparatus: the enumerated virtues, the precepts, the ruled book, the black spots, the weekly rotation, the garden analogy. That apparatus looks like a technology of attention and record-keeping. But I cannot see how recording a spot breaks a habit. The precepts are rules for acts, whereas the weekly unit is a virtue, and the spots are entered for faults committed respecting that virtue upon that day; so the object being trained seems to shift between act, virtue, and record, and I do not know which of these the method is supposed to be operating on.

Two further features I cannot place. First, the ordering of the virtues is presented as if each prepares the next, and Temperance and Silence are plausible prerequisites for the rest. But Resolution, which tells him to perform without fail what he resolves, seems to be presupposed by the very decision to spend a week on Temperance, so placing it fourth looks circular rather than strategic. Second, Humility is last, and its precept is the only one that does not specify an action; it says to imitate Jesus and Socrates. That is an exemplar instruction rather than a rule, and it appears to reintroduce precisely the kind of model-based moral cognition that the habit-diagnosis had displaced.

In my chapter I have been using a distinction between two ways a regimen can target a bad trait: extirpation, where the trait is starved by disuse, and substitution, where an incompatible good is installed in its place. Franklin's sentence names both, habits broken and habits acquired, but the method as described seems to accomplish only the second, and even then by monitoring rather than by installing. I also have a working distinction between a practice, whose repetition is constitutive of the activity, and a habit, whose repetition is merely causal, and I suspect Franklin slides between the two, but I do not want to attribute a confusion to him until I have seen whether the text can be read more charitably.

What I would like is a reading of the passage that tells me: first, what work the book and the spots are supposed to do, given the habit-diagnosis that precedes them; second, whether the ordering of the virtues is meant as a dependency claim or only as a pedagogical convenience, and what in the text supports one reading over the other; and third, how to take the Humility precept, since it is formally unlike the other twelve and occupies the terminal position. If my extirpation/substitution distinction is anachronistic here, say so and tell me what distinction the text is actually operating with. I would rather have a reconstruction that holds the passage together than a verdict that Franklin is simply inconsistent, if a reconstruction is available.
