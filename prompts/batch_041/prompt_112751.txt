Ok so I'm TAing an intro ethics unit and the nonidentity problem is the part everyone finds hardest, because the standard Parfit examples (the 14 year old girl, the "worse off but still fine" cases) are so abstract that students just nod along and dont feel the pull. So I want to teach it through a film instead. I keep coming back to Back to the Future. Marty goes back to 1955, screws up how his parents meet, and his brother and sister literally fade out of the family photo, then he fixes it, comes home, and now the family is rich and everyone's got a nice car and the siblings are basically different people. Nobody in the movie treats this as a tragedy for the original brother and sister, because they never existed in that timeline, and the ending is played as an unambiguous win. So read the film as a dramatization of the nonidentity problem. Is the movie quietly committed to the no-difference view (Parfit's idea that in a same-number choice it doesn't matter which particular people exist, only how well off they are), or does the sentimental slow fade of the photo smuggle in a person-affecting intuition that someone is being harmed? What does it do with the same-number vs different-number distinction, given that Marty somehow survives both timelines even though his parents met differently? And here's the bit I actually care about: does the fade-out imply that existence comes in degrees, and if the film lets you be half-erased, does that break the problem as usually set up, or does it accidentally capture something the philosophy misses? I want a reading that's faithful in both directions, where the film really does illuminate the debate and where it distorts it or trades on a confusion, and ends by saying whether it's actually a good teaching tool or whether it makes the problem worse. No reading lists or citations needed, just the reading itself.
