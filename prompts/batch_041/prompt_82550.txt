For the past several months I have chaired the Harm Appeals Panel of a mid-sized social virtual-reality platform; we are the last internal stop before the company's outside ombudsman, and at our next sitting we must dispose of three consolidated appeals at once. That has forced the panel to decide, in effect, a question I have spent the last two weeks arguing about with a close friend until neither of us could stand it: whether anything that happens to an avatar can be a harm of the kind our charter cares about. The charter is short, and it was written for a text-and-image platform. It authorizes us to act on "harm to persons," defines harm as "a setback to a person's interests," and then adds two riders we cannot ignore: the panel "shall not treat a representation as harmful merely because it is offensive," and, following the Vantor appeal, losses confined to in-world currency or in-world property are contractual matters for the terms of service rather than dignitary matters for us. My friend's position, which I find harder to answer than I would like, is that all of this is derivative — that virtual harms are real only as causes of real states in real bodies: dread, shame, the memory of violation — and that once you say that, the avatar drops out of the analysis and what we are really adjudicating is distress. My position is that something is lost in that reduction, though I cannot yet say precisely what. One thing we did agree on is that our disagreement is testable in a small way: his view orders the three cases below by how much real distress can be shown, mine orders them by whether the harmed party's own point of view was occupied.

The appeals are these. First, a user whose avatar was subjected to sexual contact through a haptic full-body suit by another user's avatar; the respondent insists nothing was touched, that no body was involved, and that our jurisdiction over "persons" therefore never attaches. Second, a user who spent two years building a memorial garden for a dead parent in-world; another user tore it down for entertainment and streamed it, and the builder claims something closer to desecration than to property damage, though under Vantor the garden is in-world property. Third, and the one that worries me most, a user who built a private "arena" containing photorealistic avatars of real, identifiable people who are not users of the platform at all — one is a schoolteacher in a town of nine hundred — and subjected them to simulated violence. The schoolteacher learned of it when her students found it, and has written to us. She has no account, has consented to nothing, and holds no in-world property. All three of us on the panel suspect the answers differ across these cases, and suspect the difference has something to do with embodiment, presence, and whose perspective was occupied — but we cannot yet get a rule out of that.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's sitting. I need a concrete disposition for each appeal — uphold, overturn, or remand with instructions — with a time frame for whatever we order, and, more importantly, a general rule stated so that we can apply it to the cases already queued behind these. Engage the derivative-harm position at its strongest rather than refuting a caricature of it; if your recommendation concedes something to my friend, say what and why, and say which of our two orderings the rule you adopt commits us to. I have no interest in citations or in what the literature says — the charter and its two riders are the only texts that bind us — and I would rather have a memo that admits where it is stipulating than one that pretends the charter already answers this.
