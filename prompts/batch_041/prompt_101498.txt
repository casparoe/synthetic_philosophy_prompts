I have been thinking about self-amending rule systems — constitutions, club bylaws, the rulebooks of games in which the players are permitted to change the rules — and I keep running into a structure for which I have no name. I would like to know whether one exists; if it does not, I would like the nearest named ideas and a precise account of how the thing I am describing differs from each of them.

The structure appears as soon as I try to protect a rule. Suppose a rule can be changed by a two-thirds vote. To protect it, I add a second rule: this rule may be changed only unanimously. But the second rule is itself a rule, and it is governed by the first: two-thirds of us can lower the threshold for amending the first rule, and then use the lowered threshold to repeal the protection. So a rule is only ever as strong as the rule that governs its amendment, and that rule is only as strong as the rule governing its amendment, and so on. Protection never attaches to the thing I wanted to protect; it always sits one level up.

The tower can end in only three ways, and none of them is comfortable. The regress can continue without end, in which case nothing is finally secured. It can stop at a rule that is itself amendable, in which case everything above it is only as firm as that rule. Or it can stop at a rule that forbids its own amendment — and then we must ask whether that prohibition can be repealed, since it was presumably enacted under some procedure. If it can be repealed, the protection was never real. If it cannot, a rule adopted once, perhaps carelessly, governs every later generation with no way out.

What should this be called? It is not quite the puzzle of the single rule that forbids its own amendment, because my worry is not confined to that rule; it concerns the whole shape of the tower. It is not quite the problem of one generation binding another, because that problem concerns whether the binding is wise, whereas mine concerns whether binding is even available. And it is not quite the thought that a legal order must rest on some rule that is accepted rather than enacted, because I am not asking what founds the system from outside; I am asking what becomes of protection inside it.

If there is an established name, give me the name together with an account of which nearby ideas it must not be confused with, and why. If there is none, coin a term, and define it sharply enough that I can sort the borderline cases: in particular, whether a system whose amendment thresholds are equal all the way down — a simple majority at every level — exhibits the same structure or only a degenerate case of it, and whether what I have described is a paradox at all or merely a fact about levels.
