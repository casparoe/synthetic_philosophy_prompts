I spent thirty years as an analytical chemist and the last few years reading moral and political philosophy, mostly on corruption. I keep meeting a pattern in the ethnographic writing that I cannot find a name for, and I would rather have the analysis than the label. In one case a customs post posts its charges on a board by the door: the payment is open and fixed, and goes to the office, which uses it to top up salaries that everyone, ministry included, knows are nominal. An external audit calls this extortion; the clerks and the traders who pay call it the price of getting goods through; nobody disputes what changed hands. In another case a family keeps a ledger of gifts given and received at weddings and funerals, and the returns are deliberately never exact, since an exact return would be read as ending the relationship. A consultant's report calls this gift exchange shading into bribery; the families think the report has misunderstood what a gift is. In both cases the facts are agreed and only the identity of the act is in dispute. Is there an established name for acts of this kind, whose identity is fixed not by their content but by the normative order they sit inside, so that one and the same transfer is a different act under two orders? I assume this is somewhere near the gift-and-commodity literature, legal pluralism, and the notion of institutional facts, but none of those seems to be quite it. What I want is the carving, not the term: what the phenomenon is, how it differs from ordinary moral disagreement and from relativism about moral truth, and where its edges lie — in particular whether corruption names a property of acts or a relation between an act and the order that judges it, since that seems to be what is really at stake when people argue about whether anti-corruption standards are universal.
