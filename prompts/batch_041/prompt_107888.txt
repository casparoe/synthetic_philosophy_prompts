For thirty years I designed controlled experiments on physiological systems, and the hardest part of the work was never the theory but the artifact: deciding whether a signal I had recorded belonged to the organism or to my apparatus. Having retired into philosophy, I have come to think that the long quarrel over moral preferences and moral biases is the same problem wearing different clothes, and I have adopted a view about it that I would like you to try to break. The view is this: an attitude is a moral bias, and not a moral preference, exactly when it can be shown to be causally sensitive to factors that have no bearing on whether the action or practice it concerns is right or wrong — the moral analogue of a measurement artifact. If I disapprove of something less when it is described to me in a foreign language, or more after I have smelled something foul, or differently depending on whether the person affected is one of my own, and if any competent account of what makes the conduct wrong in the first place would exclude those influences from the reckoning, then what I have is a bias; if the attitude survives all such manipulation, it is at least a candidate for a preference. On this view the distinction is neither metaphysical nor merely conventional but empirical, and it is to be established the way I used to establish that a signal was real: by controls.

Now tell me what would count against it, with the defeaters sorted by how much damage they actually do — from the ones that ought to make me abandon the view to the ones that would merely oblige me to qualify it. Some of the pressure I already anticipate, and would like you to assess rather than simply repeat: that the criterion must know which factors are irrelevant to moral truth before it can run at all, and that this is precisely the thing in dispute; that framing, order, and mood effects perturb every judgment, including ones I would never dream of calling biases, so the test seems either to condemn everything or to condemn nothing; that the attitudes we are most confident are biases are frequently the most robust and would survive my manipulations untouched, while fragile but genuine moral insight might not survive them; and that what I am really measuring is entrenchment rather than correctness. What I am less sure of is whether the view can survive the observation that an attitude's causal history can discredit it even when no irrelevant factor is at work — whether unreliability and manipulability come apart, and if they do, which of the two is doing the real work.

I would also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not. Does it make any novel claim about which manipulations should move which attitudes, or about where people should and should not converge, such that the claim could turn out false? Or have I built a device that can classify any attitude as a preference or a bias after the fact, and therefore risks nothing? If it does risk something, say what result would embarrass it; if it does not, tell me whether that is a fatal defect in a view of this kind or simply the price of insisting on an operational criterion. And tell me, finally, whether the view collapses into one of its rivals — into the idealization picture, on which the distinction lives at the level of the fully informed and coherent attitude rather than the actual causal one, or into the suspicion that if the moral truth of the matter is what does the excluding, then the whole scheme is only as secure as some substantive moral realism. Throughout, I want the distinction between what a defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it and what only a critic would press.
