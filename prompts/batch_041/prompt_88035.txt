So I'm a few years into a PhD doing ecological and epidemiological modeling, and I've noticed I'm totaly inconsistent about which false assumptions in a model bother me. I've been keeping a list to try to figure out if there's a rule, or if I'm just protective of the idealizations my own subfield happens to use. Here's where my gut lands.

- No friction and no air resistance in a ballistics problem: fine, obviously.
- Ideal gas, point particles that don't interact: fine as long as you stay at low pressure.
- Infinite population size in a model of genetic drift: fine.
- Infinite population size when what I actually want is the extinction probability of a bird down to 40 individuals: not fine. That's the entire ballgame.
- Well-mixed population, everyone contacts everyone at the same rate, for a fast respiratory bug in a dense city: fine.
- Same well-mixed assumption for something spreading down a river network: not fine.
- No mutation over 20 years in a model of a species' range shift: fine.
- No mutation over 200,000 years: not fine, come on.
- No gene flow between two populations when I'm dating their split: not fine. That's exactly the quantity I'm trying to zero out.
- Ignoring air resistance when I want to know how far a cannonball flies: fine, same as the first one.
- Replacing a whole heterogeneous population with one representative individual in an econ model of a carbon tax: not fine, and this one actually annoys me.
- Replacing a whole heterogeneous population with one average individual when I want the average efficacy of a vaccine: fine. I don't see the problem.
- A linear model over a narrow range where the truth is curved: fine.
- A linear model over a huge range where the truth clearly saturates: not fine.
- A closed population, no immigration and no emigration, where in reality both are large but roughly cancel: I want to say fine, but it makes me nervous, like I'd be getting the right answer for the wrong reason.

And the one I flip-flop on: assuming gene flow is zero when I know it's small but have no clue how small. My instinct says not fine, same as the dating case. But a collegue says it's different because at least I know which direction the bias goes, and now I don't trust my instinct.

Two people have offered me rules and I don't think either works. One says it's Jensen's inequality: if the quantity you care about is a nonlinear function of whatever you're averaging over, averaging is a mistake, otherwise it's harmless. But that seems to condemn the vaccine case, which I'm sure is fine, and it doesn't explain the friction case at all. The other says it's just about size, that an idealization is OK when the neglected effect is small relative to what you're measuring. But friction on a cannonball is not small and I still say fine, and gene flow between diverging populations is usually tiny and I still say not fine. So I'm the wrong way around on both, and that rule is dead.

My own suspicion is that the real cut is between assumptions that are scaffolding for the question I'm asking and assumptions that are load-bearing for the answer, where load-bearing means that if you relaxed it, the conclusion would flip, or shrink to nothing, or stop being about the thing I claimed it was about. But I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just a dressed-up way of saying 'it depends,' and I don't know what it predicts for the well-mixed case or the cancelling-migration case.

So what I want: the principle, or the two or three competing principles, that best capture these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself. For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Tell me what each principle says about a case I haven't listed. And be honest about which of my verdicts it can't keep, because I'd rather give up a verdict than keep a principle that's doing backflips to save it.
