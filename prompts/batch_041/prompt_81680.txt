You are a philosopher of history and social ontology, and I would like you to think carefully about what the thesis actually commits one to before you design anything. I spent forty years in a laboratory and have only lately taken up philosophy, and I now convene a 90-minute seminar for about a dozen other retired scientists and professionals, none of whom has read Hobsbawm or Anderson. Design a session that makes them feel the force of the claim that practices they take to be ancient are frequently recent inventions, and that the authority a practice wears tells one nothing about its pedigree.

Constraints. No lecture at the outset, and the names Hobsbawm and Anderson must not be mentioned until the debrief. The group should first make something and then find itself bound by what it has made; the inventedness has to be theirs rather than mine. I want a blind element with the flavour of a controlled trial: among a handful of ceremonial practices I present, at least one is genuine and documented and at least one I fabricated that morning, unlabelled, and the group must sort them by age, so that their confidence visibly fails to track the truth and I can demonstrate this afterwards without humiliating anyone.

Give me the rules, materials, roles and a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps each phase onto the distinction between an invented tradition and one that is merely adapted or reinterpreted, and onto the difference between a community's being imagined and its being fabricated; and an honest account of what a single room of volunteers cannot reproduce — chiefly the forgetting across generations, and the scale of print, census and commemoration. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it.
