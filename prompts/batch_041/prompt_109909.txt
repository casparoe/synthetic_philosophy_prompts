A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the no-trade theorem, and I came away defending a position I would now like you to stress-test. The position is this: once we assume rational agents, a common prior, and a market in which every relevant contingency can be traded, the mere fact that two agents disagree — or that one of them holds private information — cannot by itself generate trade. Any trade we actually observe must therefore be traced to something outside that picture: heterogeneous priors, hedging and liquidity needs, or constraints that stop the parties from simply reallocating their initial endowments. Tell me what would count against this. Which assumptions are doing the real work, and what would the world have to look like for each of them to fail? Are there cases of trade that cannot be assimilated to one of the outside-the-picture factors without draining the theorem of content? And if the view turns out to be nearly unfalsifiable — because every apparent counterexample gets redescribed as a failure of common priors or of market completeness — is that a defect, or exactly what we should expect from a result of this kind? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender of the position would concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss as misunderstandings.
