I've spent the last two months on a hiring committee and have just come out the other side of a promotion round that came down to a genuinely close call between me and a colleague I like and respect, so questions about who deserves what have stopped being abstract for me. I'm due to present my position at a departmental reading group on distributive justice next week, and I'd like a consistency check before I do. Here is roughly where I've landed.

1. Desert tracks agency. What a person deserves is fixed by what she chooses and strives for. Where she could genuinely have done otherwise and chose not to, she has no complaint about the resulting distribution.
2. But I've never been able to shake the thought that the capacity to strive — the discipline, the ambition, the willingness to defer gratification — is itself something no one selected. If that is right, I cannot see how anything is deserved all the way down.
3. I am nonetheless a strong defender of meritocratic selection. Positions should go to the demonstrably most competent candidate, and the reason is not that she deserves the post, but that this is the allocation which produces the best results and which treats applicants as people whose prospects should not be settled by irrelevant facts about them — family, gender, accent, who they know. I am reasonably confident that our existing instruments measure what they claim to measure.
4. I also think the distinctive damage of a meritocracy is the moral story it tells: that the winners earned their winnings and that the losers are therefore failures. I think that story is false, and I think it is corrosive.
5. On my own case, though, I have to report the following. I do not think I owe my colleague anything, I would have felt cheated if the decision had gone the other way, and I have been quietly pleased to be told I deserved it.

I have been assured that the distinction between deserving a reward and being entitled to it under a fair procedure is supposed to rescue a package like this one, and I want to know whether that distinction is doing real work here or is merely a relabeling of the thing it is meant to explain.

What I'd like from you is an audit. Which of these five pull against each other, and how badly — rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. Tell me which pairs only appear to conflict and why the appearance is misleading, and for each genuine tension say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I would lose by making it.
