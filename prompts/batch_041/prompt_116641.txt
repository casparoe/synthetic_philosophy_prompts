I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the old question of why knowledge is supposed to be worth more than mere true belief — the thing people now call the Meno problem. I've been staring at the bit in the Meno where Socrates finally answers it, and I keep feeling like the standard gloss I remember from college is smoothing over a lot. Here's the passage, which I grabbed off Perseus:

It is because you have not observed with attention the images of Daedalus. But perhaps there are none in your country. ... That if they are not fastened up they play truant and run away; but, if fastened, they stay where they are.

To possess one of his works which is let loose does not count for much in value; it will not stay with you any more than a runaway slave: but when fastened up it is worth a great deal, for his productions are very fine things. And to what am I referring in all this? To true opinion. For these, so long as they stay with us, are a fine possession, and effect all that is good; but they do not care to stay for long, and run away out of the human soul, and thus are of no great value until one makes them fast with causal reasoning. And this process, friend Meno, is recollection, as in our previous talk we have agreed. But when once they are fastened, in the first place they turn into knowledge, and in the second, are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more prized than right opinion: the one transcends the other by its trammels.

What I want from you is basically a reading guide plus an argument helper. Walk me through what's actually going on in the Daedalus statue image and in the claim that knowledge differs from true opinion by its trammels — the fastening or tethering thing. A few specific things I keep tripping on:

The statue metaphor makes it sound like a stickiness thing, but then he says the tethering is causal reasoning, i.e. working out the reason why, which sounds like it's adding something rather than just making the same thing last longer. Are those two readings in tension, and which do people who actually know this text think is right?

2. How much of this is even meant to answer the question we now call the Meno problem? Where does the text genuinely underdetermine the modern readings, and where am I just importing them?
