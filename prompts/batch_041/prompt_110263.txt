Ok so a friend and I have been arguing for like a year about what Aumann's agreement theorem actually shows and we keep going in circles, and I want a diagnosis of the fight itself rather than another round of it. The theorem I understand: if two agents start from a common prior and are rational, and it becomes common knowledge between them what their posterior credences are, then those posteriors have to coincide — you literally can't agree to disagree while staying inside the model. What I cant figure out is why the philosophical discussion around it has been stuck for decades, split into camps that don't engage each other. Camp 1 treats it as a genuine result about rational belief, so whenever real people who seem rational keep disagreeing, something hidden must explain it — different priors, a failure of common knowledge, some bound on rationality — and the whole game is working out which. Camp 2 treats it as a reductio of the common-prior assumption: the prior is doing 100% of the work, real people never share priors, so the theorem tells us nothing about real disagreement and we should stop acting like it does. And camp 3 says it's just an artifact of modeling belief as probability and knowledge as common knowledge, so it's a fact about a model, not about us.

Heres my hypothesis and I want you to actually stress-test it rather than nod along. I think these arent three positions on one question but three answers to three different questions that got crammed under a single heading: (a) is the theorem true of the formalism, (b) does the formalism describe actual rational agents, and (c) what should you do when you actually disagree with someone who looks as smart and well-informed as you. The third is the one I care about, and it seems like it has to connect to the peer-disagreement stuff — the conciliationist idea that finding out someone disagrees with you is itself higher-order evidence that should shift your credence — but almost nobody in the Aumann debate makes that link, and when they gesture at it it's clearly some other problem wearing the same words. There's also the market cousin of all this, where the same logic says informed traders shouldn't be able to speculate against each other, which obviously they do constantly, and that debate seems to stall in exactly the same way.

So diagnose it. Is this a verbal dispute, a bundle of distinct disagreements sharing a name, or is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work that everyone's pretending is philosophical? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as actual progress — a distinction, a theorem, abandoning one of the three questions — or whether this is a case where no progress should be expected and why. Don't survey the literature at me; tell me what kind of disagreement this is.
