My two flatmates got into it last night over Thomson's lamp — the one where a lamp gets flicked on and off, each flick taking half as long as the last, so infinitely many flicks are done by the two-minute mark — and what state it's in then. I stayed out of it, but I've been chewing on it since and I'd rather have a proper referee than pick a side.

Roughly how it went:

Sam: the puzzle shows the setup is incoherent. "On" and "off" are the only states, every flick flips it, and there's no last flick — so the description never pins down a state at 2:00. People who say "it's neither on nor off" are just dressing up an incomplete description as a mystery.

Priya: no — the interesting bit is *why* it's unpinned. A bulb's state is a discontinuous function of the toggle history; flip the switch and the state jumps. So a sequence of states with no limit is exactly what you'd expect when you feed a convergent sequence of times through a discontinuous map. Make the lamp continuous — a bulb that gets brighter by half the remaining amount each flick — and the brightness at 2:00 is perfectly well-defined. The lesson isn't "infinity breaks the scenario," it's "the limit of the states needn't be the state at the limit."

Sam: that just relabels it. The limit of your brightnesses is a fact about your sequence, not a fact about the lamp at 2:00. Nothing in the physical description forces a real bulb to actually *be* at that value at 2:00 — any lag, any hysteresis, and it sits wherever it likes. You've swapped the puzzle for an easier one by smuggling in continuity the original lamp never had.

Priya: I'll take that, but it cuts both ways. If the verdict depends on assumptions about the physics that we never stated, then the original lamp's answer depends on assumptions we never stated — which is a point *about the description*, not a proof that supertasks are impossible. So Sam and I actually agree the original is under-specified; we disagree about whether that's the end of the story or the start of it.
