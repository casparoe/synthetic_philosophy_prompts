I write the science-and-ideas pages, and I'm at work on a long feature about the strange machinery by which economists decide what is good for people. The centrepiece is to be a two-hander, and I'd like you to draft it.

The participants are Dr Ayesha Karim, an economist at a regulator who has spent her career building welfare estimates out of observed choices, and who defends without embarrassment the working assumption that the best evidence of what someone prefers is what they do when the alternatives are in front of them; and Dr Martin Blythe, a philosopher of economics who holds that this assumption is either empty or false — empty when it is treated as a definition of preference, false when it is treated as a claim about what people actually want. They are friends, they have had this argument many times, and neither is a fool. I want them equally competent and equally sharp, each answering the other's actual point rather than a convenient version of it.

Keep them to the material I give you here rather than ranging over the literature; the empirical content of the dialogue should be these two cases and nothing else.

The first is a workplace-pension trial I have been reporting on and have agreed to describe without naming the employer. Staff were assigned to one of three arrangements. In the first, everyone was automatically enrolled at 8 per cent of salary and could opt out at any time; 9 per cent did. In the second, there was no default at all — employees had to pick a contribution rate of 3, 5, 8 or 12 per cent before a payroll deadline, or nothing was paid in. The average rate chosen was 5.6 per cent. A year on, the active choosers reported higher satisfaction with their retirement saving than the defaulted group, and were far more likely to say they had thought about it properly. In the third, everyone was defaulted in at 8 per cent, but with an option to lock the rate in for five years, with a £30 fee for any change; 40 per cent took the lock, and of those who later paid to change, most moved down to 5 per cent. There is one further wrinkle I want them to grapple with: when the defaulted group were asked what they would have chosen had they been made to choose, a majority said a lower rate than 8 per cent — while also saying they were glad to be in at 8.

The second case is the standard illustration of pricing safety. A job carries an extra one-in-ten-thousand annual risk of a fatal accident and pays £2,000 a year more. A regulator reads off a value of a statistical life of £20 million and feeds that figure into a cost-benefit analysis. Both of them know that workers differ, that many do not know the risk, and that the number will help decide whether a safety rule is worth its cost.

What I want the dialogue to reach, in the end, is the question underneath both cases: whether preference is a summary of what people choose, in which case the framework cannot be wrong, only more or less useful; or a psychological fact that explains choice and can come apart from it, in which case the framework can be badly wrong, and the regulator is inferring well-being from evidence that does not track it. I would like the adaptation point to surface — that people can come to want what they have, or what they can get — along with the question of whose preferences count, the person at the desk or the same person across a lifetime; and I want them to press each other on whether any neutral alternative exists. If stated attitudes and hypothetical answers are unreliable, as they plainly are, what is left?

House rules for the piece. No citations, no named studies, no reading lists, no appeals to what the literature shows — the argument has to be made inside the dialogue. Neither of them may settle a point by invoking a famous name; a well-known label is allowed, its authority is not. British spelling and idiom throughout. And the exchange should have real movement: at least one concession on each side, and at least one moment where one of them changes the subject of the argument rather than the terms of it, which the other should notice.

Please give me:

- "The scene" — roughly 100 words, putting them somewhere ordinary (a canteen, a train) and telling me only what I need in order to hear them.
- "The dialogue" — 1,200 to 1,500 words, with at least four substantive turns for each of them.
- "Where it leaves the argument" — 200 to 300 words, in my voice, saying which of the two came out better on the pension case and which on the safety case, and what the exchange revealed that argument alone cannot settle. Do not split the difference for the sake of balance.
