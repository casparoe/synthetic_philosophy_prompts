I'm writing a feature on why economists and philosophers keep reaching for one analogy when they argue about discounting the future: they say that distance in time is like distance in space, so that just as it is not permissible to care less about a person merely because she lives far away, it is not permissible to care less about a person merely because she lives later. The move is deployed against pure time preference, meaning the discounting of a benefit or harm solely because it arrives later, and it is used to push social discount rates toward zero, leaving uncertainty and growth as the only legitimate reasons to weight the future less. I want an audit of how far the analogy actually holds. Give me the correspondence table first, saying what plays the role of distance, of transport cost, of communication, and of the common present we are imagined to share; then lay out where the mapping breaks, including the causal asymmetry between us and them, the absence of trade or reciprocity with future people, the uncertainty about whether they will exist at all, and the fact that temporal discounting also applies to my own later self, for which spatial distance has no analogue. Tell me which of those breaks matter for the conclusion the analogy is being asked to support and which are idle, then whether the near-zero-discount conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table or whether the analogy was carrying it all along, and whether some better analogy is available. Keep it under 600 words.
