I'm putting together a proposal for a small six-month pilot grant (basically buys my own time plus a bit of travel money), and I want to pitch something in the value-alignment / political-philosophy overlap, but I'm worried the obvious framing is played out. The obvious framing goes: AI should "serve human values," but whose? Then you get the social-choice move that aggregating preferences runs into impossibility (Arrow-ish), and the liberal move that the state shouldn't take sides among conceptions of the good, and everyone nods and moves on. I want to get underneath that instead of restating it.

My half-formed idea, and I'd like you to build the proposal around it: the liberal-neutrality analogy is seductive but I think it's actually broken, and broken in an interesting way. Neutrality as a political ideal is a constraint on what the state may do — and crucially the state can often just not act, or act thinly. An AI assistant can't not act: it has to emit some token, pick some default, resolve some ambiguity, and every one of those is already a substantive choice among ways of living. So "neutrality" for a system that is always-already acting might be an incoherent target, and the real question is what should replace it. My hunch is the replacement is justification-based rather than aggregation-based: the outputs should be ones the affected users could each endorse from their own point of view, even where they flatly disagree. But I don't know if that's right, or even well-defined, and I don't know how to make it more than a slogan. There's a wrinkle I can't get past either: users have preferences about how the AI should weigh their preferences (the blunt roommate wants it to stay blunt even when outvoted), so the thing being aggregated is partly about the aggregation rule itself, and I suspect that's where the real puzzle lives. A concrete case I keep coming back to: a shared-household assistant where one user wants everything delivered bluntly and the other wants everything softened — no amount of "just maximize satisfaction" or "just stay neutral" seems to tell it what to say.

Turn this into the proposal. I need: a question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the existing answers (straight aggregation, liberal neutrality, the justification / public-reason tradition) don't settle it — and be honest if some of them just dissolve my worry rather than leaving it open; the method, i.e. what I'd actually spend six months doing (I'm a philosopher so it's conceptual, but I'd like at least one piece of it to be something you could call a result and not just another essay); what a real result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a scope that honestly fits six months, including telling me if the truthful answer is that this is two projects.
