Ok I need a disambiguation here, not a ruling. Dinner-party situation: I'm American, I was in Lisbon with my partner's family, the bill comes, I leave 15% out of habit, the cousin laughs and says 'when in Rome, do as the Romans do.' Fine, whatever. But later in the same meal, when I said the whole US 20%-or-you're-exploiting-the-server thing is basically a shakedown, someone used the exact same phrase on me in the other direction -- that since I actually live in the US, I have to follow the local convention whether I like it or not. Same proverb, two opposite jobs, and I don't think its doing either one properly.

So take 'when in Rome, do as the Romans do' and lay out the distinct things it could actually be claiming, as a menu, from the boring-true to the genuinely contested, with a verdict on each -- trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false. Here's what I've come up with, tell me what I'm missing: (1) plain prudence, you'll get side-eye, so comply if you want a quiet life; (2) a claim about what the act means locally, so that not tipping in the US isn't 'keeping my money' in the local grammar of actions, it's 'stiffing the server', and I can't just carry my home meaning around with me; (3) fair play or reciprocity, I take the benefits of the local arrangement (menu prices set on the assumption of tips) so I owe my bit; (4) flat relativism, whatever the locals do is right for them and for me while I'm there; (5) respect or gratitude toward my hosts, which I suspect isn't really about the convention at all; (6) etiquette isn't morality, so the proverb governs manners and has no business settling a moral question.

For each reading I want to know what it has to assume -- whose expectations count, what 'everyone' is quantifying over, what it implies for someone who genuinely can't afford the local convention, and for a resident who wants to change the norm rather than just go along with it. And say which readings actually deliver the conclusion in the two cases I care about: (a) the tourist who thinks tipping is demeaning should still tip here, and (b) the resident or visitor who wants to opt out of a local convention they think is harmful.

One thing I keep circling: does any of this depend on the practice being a real coordination problem, like queuing, where there's an actual conflict about who goes next and everyone is better off with one shared answer -- as opposed to something like tipping, which seems to be held up by nothing but expectation plus disapproval? If the queue and the tip come out differently, that seems like it should matter for whether 'when in Rome' has any moral force at all, and I'd like to know if it does.

And if it doesn't blow up the answer, one more case: jaywalking, where all the locals do it and the law says don't. Is the visitor who waits being a sucker, and is the local who crosses a free rider on the pedestrians who follow the rule?

Finish by telling me which of the readings people usually mean when they use the phrase to end a conversation, and whether in that use the proverb is doing any work or just acting as a period at the end of the sentence.
