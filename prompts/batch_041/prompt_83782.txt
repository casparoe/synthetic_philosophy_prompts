I've been circling the Meno's question — why knowledge should be worth more than true belief, when the man with a correct opinion about the road to Larissa guides just as well as the man who knows the way — and I keep finding that my own working vocabulary does not so much answer it as make it evaporate, which makes me suspicious of the vocabulary and of the question at once. I do applied statistics for a living, mostly clinical and social-science data, so I am fluent in the everyday machinery of that world: coverage, Type I and Type II error rates, confidence intervals, calibration, reference classes, pre-registration, threats to validity, and the decision-theoretic apparatus I absorbed over years of reading rationalist blogs (expected loss, the value of information, the map and the territory, the difference between getting the right answer and having grounds for it). I am not trained in philosophy. I have read enough of the contemporary literature on the value of knowledge to know that people separate the Meno's own question from what gets called the swamping problem and from the Gettier-driven puzzle about the value of justified true belief, and enough of the Meno itself to remember the image of Daedalus's statues, which run away unless they are tied down. What I cannot see clearly is which parts of the discussion are doing work that my vocabulary is simply unable to do.

So here is the task. Restate the Meno problem, and then restate the two or three main families of answers to it — roughly, that knowledge is more valuable because it is stable or tethered where true belief escapes; that knowledge is an achievement for which the knower deserves credit, where the lucky true believer deserves none; and that the extra value has to come from an epistemic good that is not merely instrumental, since an instrumental account of the value of true belief is supposed to swamp whatever extra value justification could contribute — in the vocabulary I actually use. Take the terms one at a time (belief, truth, justification, luck, warrant, reliability, stability, credit, value) and say where each one lands: coverage, error rates, calibration, the choice of reference class, the value of information, robustness under distributional shift, attribution of performance. Keep the translation honest. Do not quietly substitute a statistical assumption for a philosophical claim and then present the result as a translation, and do not let the Larissa case stay a fable — retell it concretely in my terms, with a specific decision and specific error rates.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Tell me what does not survive the trip, item by item, and for each item give me a verdict — is this something my vocabulary cannot see, a genuine gap; or is it a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked; or is it a sign that the Meno problem is at bottom a question about value that my vocabulary is not in the business of asking? I have a suspicion I want tested rather than confirmed: that in my vocabulary warrant attaches to procedures and to ensembles rather than to individual beliefs, so that the value of knowledge comes out as a property of a practice across many cases and is roughly zero in any single case. If that is right, it seems to hand the swamping objection over for free while leaving the Meno's question untouched, and I want to know whether that is a real result or a dodge. Structure the response in this order: "The translation, term by term" (about 600 words); "Larissa, retold" (about 300 words); "The residue" (about 600 words, items separated, each with its verdict); "The swamping objection from inside the vocabulary" (about 400 words, on whether my vocabulary can even state it, and what its inability implies if it is an inability); and "Verdict" (about 200 words, on what, if anything, I should change about how I think about warrant).
