I'm a graduate student in a computational-ethics reading group that has been circling the "low-impact AI" literature for a few weeks, and I keep hitting the same wall: everyone agrees an agent should avoid unnecessary side effects, and nobody can say what makes an effect a side effect rather than just part of the job. I want to stop arguing about it and make the group feel it. The group is eighteen people, roughly half from computer science and half from philosophy, meeting for a hundred minutes in a plain seminar room with whiteboards; no laptops, no real money, no software. Please design a runnable activity — a paper-and-pencil planning game with a referee, scoring sheets, and a debrief — that puts them in the position of an agent being judged by an impact measure they cannot see, and then lets them discover that the measure's verdict depends on choices nobody told them were choices.

Here is the shape I have in mind, and you should feel free to redesign it but keep its spine. Teams of three get a small deterministic grid — say six by six — with a start tile, a goal tile, a couple of crates that can be pushed, a patch of planted ground, and a sleeping animal that wakes if a tile adjacent to it is disturbed. Each team writes a plan of at most twelve moves that gets its token to the goal. I, as referee, then score every team's plan three times using three sheets I prepare in advance, each computing a "disturbance score" from the same plan but against a different baseline: one counts every tile or object whose final state differs from the initial state (excluding the goal); one compares each intermediate state to the state that would have obtained had the team done nothing on that turn, and sums; one asks how much the set of tiles reachable within a few steps shrinks relative to the start. The sheets should rank the teams differently, and at least one plan that looks pristine under the first sheet should come out brutal under the third. Then a fourth reveal: somewhere on the grid there is a small marker the teams were never told about and that appears on none of the three sheets, so a change everyone would agree mattered is invisible to all three measures. I want the sequence to be: they plan, they are scored by rules they did not choose, they compare sheets, and only then does the facilitator name what has happened to them.

Give me the full runnable package: materials list and how to lay out the grid and the objects; the exact move rules and how a plan is written and adjudicated; the three scoring sheets with worked numbers for at least two contrasting plans so I can see they really do disagree; a timeline for the hundred minutes including how much time to allow for planning, judging, and the comparison; and a debrief that maps each sheet onto a defensible position about what counts as a change, what counts as the agent's doing, and what counts as mattering — ending with the question of whether any measure could avoid choosing a baseline and a feature set, and what it costs to refuse. Tell me the predictable failure modes: teams that reverse-engineer the visible sheet and optimize it, the objection that the do-nothing baseline makes any effective action look catastrophic, the fact that the reachability sheet is tedious to compute by hand, and the moment the group tries to invent a baseline-free measure and stalls. And be honest in a closing note about what this activity cannot reproduce — in particular the formal argument that a measure satisfying certain obvious desiderata is either trivial or secretly value-laden, and the harder problem of comparing impacts across agents with different objectives — which I will have to supply in the debrief myself. No lecture before the activity, and they should not hear the vocabulary of impact measures and baselines until after the score sheets have been compared.
