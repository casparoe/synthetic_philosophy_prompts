for the last session of a reading group on the identity of indiscernibles — we just did Black's two spheres, the universe with nothing in it but two exactly similar spheres and nowhere for a difference to live except position — i want to close with a poem instead of a summary. Write it as a pantoum, 16 lines, four quatrains. The form should do the arguing: a pantoum keeps hauling the same lines back in new positions, which is more or less the situation the two spheres are in, so the poem should read as if the same content keeps reappearing and the only thing that ever changes is where it sits. Get the two anchor lines as close to identical as you can, one word apart if possible — the whole fight is over whether a difference that small is a difference at all. One of the recurring lines should carry the Leibnizian demand (no discernible difference, so no second thing), the other should carry the counterexample, and they should keep crossing each other in a way the poem never settles. It should also be genuinely undecidable whether one voice is speaking or two, since thats the question. No technical vocabulary, no philosophers names, dont explain the puzzle — someone who's read the paper should recognize the world, someone who hasnt should just find a poem about twinning, and if it lands on a verdict it has failed.
