You are a philosopher of law with specialist knowledge of twentieth-century Anglo-American jurisprudence and the legal-moralism debate, and I would like you to reason carefully and to brainstorm the candidate readings before you evaluate any of them. I am a PhD student in legal theory, and I have a question about a slogan that keeps ending arguments I care about.

In a seminar last week a visiting speaker closed off a discussion of whether the criminal law may be used to sustain a shared sexual morality by saying that you can't legislate morality, as though the point were both obvious and decisive. I have heard the same line used by people who want to decriminalize nearly everything, and by people who insist that every criminal statute enacts somebody's morality, so I no longer know what work it is doing. What I want is a menu rather than a verdict: lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making — descriptive, conceptual, normative, and the flatly trivial — and for each say whether it is true, whether it is contested, and whether it would actually have supported the speaker's use of it to close the argument. Then say which of the readings, if any, give the legal-moralist side something to worry about — the side that holds that society may enforce its shared morality because the erosion of shared moral bonds threatens social cohesion, and that the test for criminalization is whether the ordinary reasonable person would react to the conduct with intolerance, indignation and disgust — and which readings leave that position untouched.

Test the readings against two statutes, so that they do not float free of cases: one criminalizing conduct that many find disgusting but that harms no one identifiable, prefaced by a preamble declaring the conduct contrary to the community's shared morality; and one requiring schools to teach a curriculum in civic virtue. Say which readings license, forbid, or are silent about each.

End by stating what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would move you to a different assessment of the readings — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
