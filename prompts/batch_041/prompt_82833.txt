A friend of mine teaches moral philosophy, and for some months I have been collecting her verdicts on cases of supererogation — acts that go beyond what morality requires of the agent, and that are admirable precisely because they were not required. I am trying to work out the rule she is actually running. Her verdicts so far:

- A stranger donates a kidney to someone she has never met, at real risk to her own health, out of ordinary concern for the recipient: supererogatory.
- A passer-by wades into a canal to pull two children out of a sinking car and is badly injured doing it: supererogatory.
- A woman with no dependents gives away her entire savings to endow a scholarship for refugees, leaving herself with nothing: supererogatory.
- A parent pays for her own child's life-saving surgery: required, not supererogatory.
- A lifeguard pulls a struggling swimmer out of the water while on duty: required, not supererogatory.
- A man who embezzles from his employer in order to fund an orphanage: impermissible, not supererogatory.
- Someone who returns a lost wallet with the cash still in it: not supererogatory; on her view that is simply what honesty demands.
- A comfortably-off man who gives nothing at all to charity: she says no one could demand it of him, though she is quite willing to think worse of him for it.

The new case she has been asked to judge is this. A man volunteers to donate part of his liver to a colleague he barely knows. The surgery carries real risk, he will lose six weeks of unpaid leave, and his partner, who is against the whole business, will be left carrying all the childcare alone for two months. He is moved partly by genuine concern for the colleague and partly by the fact that he is up for promotion and the donation programme is heavily publicised; he has told friends that he expects it to look good on his record. Nothing in particular obliges him to this colleague.

Tell me what she will say. I would like the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts — including the ones that fit them just as well as whatever rule she seems to be running — together with what each of those rules predicts about the new case, your best guess between them and the reason for it, and the further case that would most cleanly separate the rules still in play. What her track record leaves genuinely open, as far as I can see, is how much the act has to cost the agent, whether the agent's motives bear on whether it counts, and whether an act that leaves someone else worse off can be supererogatory at all.
