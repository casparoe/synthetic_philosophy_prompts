My flatmate and I have been circling the question of whether taxation is theft, and the argument keeps landing on an analogy of his that I find harder to answer than I expected. He is not a libertarian — he is about as far from one as it gets — and his move is to say that the state is simply a very large firm: it supplies us with roads, courts, schools, a currency, a defence, we pay for them, and no one describes the money handed over at a supermarket till as stolen. On his picture the tax bill is a price, the citizen is a customer, and the theft charge is a category error, a confusion between a price one resents and an expropriation. My instinct is that the analogy is carrying the entire conclusion and that the conclusion collapses the moment the analogy is set down, but I cannot say precisely where it fails, and I would rather not simply assert that governments are not businesses. We were arguing over dinner and my brother, who works for a revenue agency, was no help at all.

So please audit the analogy. I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the firm, the customer, the price, the goods on the shelf, the competitors, the contract, and the exit door — and I want you to note where the mapping is underdetermined, because it seems to me the analogy can be read in two ways that pull apart: are we customers buying a service, or are we owners with a residual claim and a vote? I suspect those two readings license opposite verdicts on the theft charge, and I would like that suspicion tested. Then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that matter for what the analogy is being used to establish and the ones that are merely decorative. My guess is that the load-bearing ones concern exit (whether there is anywhere to take one's custom, and whether leaving is possible at all), excludability (a firm cannot charge for what it cannot withhold), and enforcement (a firm's contracts are enforced by the state, so using the firm to explain the state's authority looks circular). The test I would most like you to run is this: identify the closest thing in ordinary commercial life to the state under this analogy — I suspect it is not a shop but something more like a homeowners' association, or a gym membership with automatic renewal and no cancellation — and then say whether we regard the transfers those institutions extract as theft-like, since whatever we say there is what the analogy actually delivers.

Finally, be committed rather than even-handed: tell me whether the analogy can support his conclusion that taxation is not theft, and tell me whether taxation is theft, giving your reasons for both, instead of laying out the positions and leaving me to choose. If you think the analogy should be replaced by a better one, say what it is and what it would and would not carry.
