You are a philosopher specializing in moral epistemology and the history of biography. I am preparing a seminar on moral education, and I want to interrogate a slogan that a colleague used to shut down a discussion. She said: "We learn virtue from lives, not from rules." She meant it as a decisive point against a proposal to teach ethics through explicit principles and case studies, and she seemed to think it vindicated the tradition of moral biography from Plutarch to modern hagiography. I am not sure what the slogan actually claims. Can you lay out the different precise theses it could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and say whether it would actually support her dismissal of the principles-and-cases approach. I would also like you to consider readings that turn on distinctions like learning that versus learning how, emulation versus admiration, the particular versus the general, the motivational versus the epistemic role of narratives, and the difference between Plutarch's parallel lives and later hagiography. Think carefully, brainstorm first, and then present your answer as a menu of readings rather than picking one and defending it. Conclude by saying which reading you think she probably had in mind and what follows for the use of biography in moral learning.
