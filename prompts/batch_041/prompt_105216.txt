John Stuart Mill died in 1873, before politics became a mass profession and before lottery selection was proposed as a remedy for it. Suppose he were shown a concrete modern scheme: replace the upper chamber with 300 citizens drawn by lot from the electoral register, serving a single non-renewable four-year term on a full salary, with no party affiliation, empowered to amend but not initiate legislation — the rationale being that this would break the grip of career politicians. Give Mill's most likely verdict and the reasoning behind it. Then locate the points where his own commitments pull against one another — his proposal to give the educated extra votes, his view that the proper function of a representative assembly is to scrutinize and control the government rather than to administer it, and his conviction that taking part in public affairs is itself an education in civic character — and say which of these he would let go, and which single feature of the scheme would decide the matter for him.
