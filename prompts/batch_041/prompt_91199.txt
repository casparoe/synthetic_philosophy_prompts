I'm writing a seminar paper on the Gödelian core of Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I want to get the central inference exactly right before I build anything on it. My reconstruction runs as follows. Gödel shows that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains a sentence it cannot prove but which we can recognize as true. Hofstadter then identifies the mind with a "strange loop" of precisely this self-referential shape, so that the mind, in recognizing the truth of its own unprovable sentence, steps outside any formal system that might model it. The conclusion I take him to be driving at is that the self-aware "I" just is the system's Gödel sentence — the point at which a mechanism becomes aware of itself, and therefore the point at which no mechanistic account can be complete. Let me call the move that licenses this the loop-transfer principle: wherever a formal system and a mind share a self-referential structure of the Gödelian kind, the limits proved for the system transfer to the mind.

Two things I cannot settle. First, in locating the self-model within the loop, should the conscious "I" be identified with the Gödel sentence G itself, with the provability predicate, or with the meta-system that proves G? Second, if the entire force of the analogy is that G is "about itself," does that not already concede that the system possesses a minimal self-awareness — and if so, is that not exactly the property the analogy was supposed to explain rather than assume?

Please lay out the argument in its strongest form, step by step, so that I can check each move: I want to know which premise carries the weight, whether the loop-transfer principle is sound as stated or needs an additional premise, and what exactly the Gödel sentence contributes to the picture of self-awareness.
