Sat through a planning consultation last week about a proposed four-storey block of flats on a corner plot, and the loudest objector wrapped up with 'a man's home is his castle' and sat down to applause. Nobody in the room, me included, could have said exactly what that sentence was a claim about. I want to use it in my seminar on property, planning and the city, so: seperate out the precise claims it could be making, from the trivially descriptive to the very strong, and say for each whether it's true, substantive-but-defensible, or false, and which one the objector has to be making for it to do any work against the block. Three things I want to be exact about. First, the maxim comes out of a 1604 case whose actual holding was that the sheriff may break in and do the King's business if you wont open the door, and that the householder's privilege doesn't extend to strangers or their goods — so how much absolutism was ever in it, and does the privilege attach to the threshold of the dwelling or to what happens around it? Second, whether 'castle' is better read as a single right to exclude or as a bundle of sticks (occupation, use, development, transfer) that can come apart, given that zoning splits them anyway. Third, whether the reading that would actually stop the block — a right to decide the character of the neighbourhood — is a castle claim at all or its opposite, since it asks the state to restrict somebody else's castle. And say what becomes of zoning as such on the strong reading. Give it to me as a menu I can photocopy for the handout, not an essay. British English please.
