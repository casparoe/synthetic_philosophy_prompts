For the past several months I have been driving the same stretch of divided highway twice a day, and it contains two junctions that are, from the driver's seat, indistinguishable: the same blue sign for the same small town, the same slip road peeling away to the right, the same mile of nothing before the next one. Twice now I have meant to take the second and sailed past it, having lost track somewhere in between of which junction I was at; the first time I only understood what had happened forty miles later, when the road I was on had quietly become the wrong one. Our reading group has spent the term on puzzles of decision under imperfect recall, and the session where this one came up is the only one I have not been able to stop thinking about since. The setup is simple enough to state: two junctions I cannot tell apart, and no memory of whether I have already passed the first. Turning off at the first is worth nothing to me; turning off at the second is worth a great deal (call it four); driving on past both leaves me on the same road, further from home, worth something small and merely bearable (call it one). The arithmetic, I gather, says that someone who will forget should keep going about two times in three — but what I actually find strange is not the arithmetic, it is the way the best plan and the best choice come apart: the best policy for a driver who forgets is to keep going and hope, while a driver who could be certain he was standing at the second junction should simply take it, and I am never that driver, and never not that driver, and cannot tell which sentence I am in.

I would like to close the group's final session with a poem about this, and I am asking you to write it. I want a pantoum of four quatrains, so that lines 2 and 4 of each stanza return as lines 1 and 3 of the next and the poem's last line is its first line — the form doing the work, because a pantoum is a loop that must end where it began and has no way to take the exit. Write it in the first person, from the seat of the car, and let the two junctions be genuinely indistinguishable: no landmarks, no note taped to the dashboard, no clever way out. The moment the poem allows the driver to tell which junction he is at, it has cheated and the puzzle is gone. Make the middle two stanzas as nearly interchangeable as you can, so that a reader loses their place the way the driver does, and let at least one recurring line be the kind that is true at either junction — the sort of sentence that means one thing the first time it is said and another thing the second time, without a word changing. I would also like the payoffs to be legible without being lectured at: nothing for turning off too early, the good thing for the second turn, something merely endurable for driving past both — enough that a reader feels why the sensible thing at a single junction and the sensible thing to have settled on beforehand pull in opposite directions. Someone in the group who knows the puzzle should be able to point at the two junctions and at the two conflicting recommendations; someone who does not should still finish the poem thinking about a road. Please do not resolve it. I do not want a last line in which the driver finally gets it right, and I do not want the poem to console me about forgetting; I want it to end the way the drive does.
