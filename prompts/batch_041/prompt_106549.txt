I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I'm trying to build a short story around a puzzle I can't stop thinking about. Suppose there are two objects that are alike in every respect we could ever measure — same mass, same shape, same internal structure down to the last atom, placed at equal distances to your left and to your right — so that no test, however precise, could ever tell you which one you were looking at. Is that two objects, or one object that we are simply describing twice? I gather this is roughly the puzzle philosophers discuss under the name "the identity of indiscernibles," and that one common line is that there really are two, because being two does not require differing in any qualitative way; the fact that there are two is just a brute, further fact about the world. The opposing line I keep running into is that if nothing observable whatsoever distinguishes them, then the claim that there are two asserts something no evidence could ever touch. I'd like a story that keeps this tension alive rather than dissolving it in an argument.

Please write a short story, roughly 1,000–1,400 words, built around a specific scene: a technician at an isolated facility is ordered to destroy "the original" of a pair of objects that the facility's duplication machine has rendered perfectly indiscernible — and there is, by construction, no fact of the matter about which one is the original. The story should honor the constraint that the two objects remain genuinely indiscernible: don't sneak in a serial number, a scratch, a positional difference the technician can exploit, or an asymmetry introduced by the observer. The drama has to come from people trying to act when the distinction they need simply isn't there. It should read as a story rather than a staged debate — give the technician a life, a reason for being there, some sensory texture — with the philosophy as pressure rather than exposition. It's fine, and in fact encouraged, for the story to end without resolving whether there were ever two things, or whether "which is the original" was a question with an answer. I'd like at least one character to press the view that there's a brute difference between the two that no measurement could ever reveal, and at least one to press the view that talk of "two," or of "the original," has quietly stopped meaning anything here.

Then, in a separate section titled "Commentary on the Choices Made," roughly 300–450 words, explain what you were doing: which positions in the puzzle the story dramatizes, how you kept the objects indiscernible without letting the scene go inert, and why you ended it the way you did.
