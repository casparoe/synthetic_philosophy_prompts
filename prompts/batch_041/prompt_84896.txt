I work at the seam between philosophy of science and interpretability, and there's a dispute in that seam that I keep running into and can't get past. On one side: mechanism identity is determinate. Two systems run the same mechanism just in case their causal structure can be mapped onto a common model of the right grain — corresponding parts, corresponding interventions, corresponding effects — so questions like "did this trained network and that rodent brain implement one mechanism or two that merely resemble each other?" have answers, and when people disagree, someone has the mapping wrong. On the other side: mechanisms are individuated relative to a phenomenon, a level of description, and a research interest, so "same mechanism" is not an identity predicate at all. It's shorthand for the usefulness of a model, and there is no fact of the matter to get wrong. Neither camp has given ground in twenty years, and the practical stakes have risen: in mechanistic anomaly detection we want to license the claim "the model is still using the same mechanism, just on an unfamiliar input," and use it to license a safety conclusion. If the second camp is right, that conclusion is relative to an interest nobody has stated.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on which camp is correct. Is this a substantive disagreement about mechanisms, a verbal one about the word 'same', or two questions sharing one heading — a metaphysical question about identity and a methodological question about when a generalization claim is warranted? Is some hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, for instance whether an intervention-based abstraction has a unique minimal level, so that identity claims are only well-defined once the level is fixed? My own hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than adopt, is that the impasse is an equivocation: 'same mechanism' does identity work in the first camp and similarity work in the second, and the disagreement is manufactured by the shared phrase. If that's the right diagnosis, tell me where the cut should fall and what each side keeps; if it's wrong, tell me what the real disagreement is and why my hypothesis fails. Either way, say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a criterion, a formal result, an empirical finding, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected here and why.

Give me a committed answer: choose a diagnosis, defend it, and say which of my framing choices you think is doing illicit work. Don't lay out the options and leave the pick to me.
