I'm making a handout for a graduate reading group on idealized-preference accounts of what is good for a person — full-information theories, ideal-advisor views, coherent extrapolated volition, and the critics who hold that the idealized agent is a stranger whose verdicts cannot bind you. I want a decision tree that sorts a reader by what they say about cases rather than by the label they would choose. Give it as exactly five numbered branch questions, each one or two sentences and answerable without knowing where it leads, with no introduction or conclusion. Build the tree from the logical space before locating anyone in it: separate idealization that supplies missing information from idealization that repairs inconsistency, and separate views on whether the idealized verdict corrects the actual agent's preference or replaces it. Each numbered item should say what its answers split off and name the leaves they reach, including at least actual-preference subjectivism, the full-information and ideal-advisor views, coherent extrapolated volition, the replacement or stranger objection, and an objectivist who treats idealization as a route to value that was never preference-based. Put both of these cases to work somewhere in the branches: the person whose preferences have adapted to deprivation (the sour-grapes case), and the person whose preferences were deliberately engineered. Flag any combination of answers that no position occupies and say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate.
