I have no training in philosophy — I read it on my own — but for a while now I have been stuck on the argument over Gottlob Frege's private political notebook, the one he kept in his final years and which was published long after his death, full of virulent antisemitism and nationalist fantasy. What puzzles me is not the content but the shape of the dispute about it, which has not budged in decades. Nobody denies what the notebook says. The question is whether it bears on the philosophy.

As I understand the two sides: one holds that arguments are valid or invalid regardless of who wrote them, that nothing in the notebook appears as a premise anywhere in the logical works, and that the project of grounding arithmetic in logic can be assessed entirely on its own terms — at most the notebook tells us about the man, not the doctrine. The other holds that this is a naive picture of how a philosophical system hangs together: that a doctrine which places logic and arithmetic in an objective realm of thoughts shared by all thinkers has already taken a stand on who counts as a thinker, and that the politics is not an embarrassing appendage but the visible edge of the same picture of the world. I have a half-formed version of that second argument of my own: that his horror of cultural mixing is the political shadow of a requirement that thinkers share a common stock of thoughts, though I cannot tell whether that is a real connection or a pun on the word 'shared'.

My hypothesis about the impasse is that the two sides are running different tests for what it is for a fact to bear on a doctrine. Call the first the premise test: a fact about the author bears on the doctrine only if some argument in the doctrine would lose its support if the fact were otherwise, or if the fact explains a choice the doctrine did not have to make. Call the second the kinship test: it bears on the doctrine if the same underlying image of the world is doing work in both, whether or not any argument depends on it. If that is right, part of the dispute is really about which test is the correct one, and possibly not a dispute about Frege at all.

Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive disagreement left over once the tests are made explicit — a hidden historical question about whether the antisemitism actually shaped any doctrinal choice, or a bedrock disagreement about what a philosophical work is, a body of arguments or the expression of a life? I am also suspicious that the kinship test is unfalsifiable, since any two bodies of writing share vocabulary, and that the premise test is too narrow, since the connections that matter in philosophy are rarely premises. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a historical demonstration of a doctrinal choice that only the politics explains, or the conclusion that the question is ill-posed. No reading list, please; I want the diagnosis.
