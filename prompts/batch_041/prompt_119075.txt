I've been in a reading group on the socialist calculation debate for the better part of a year, and I've come to suspect that the participants — then and now — are not disagreeing about what they take themselves to be disagreeing about. I'd like you to diagnose the disagreement rather than settle it, and to tell me what would count as progress.

The shape of the original exchange, as I understand it. Mises argued that without market prices for the means of production a socialist economy has no way of rationally allocating resources; his target was the moneyless, in-kind accounting proposals of his contemporaries, not market-using schemes. Lange and Lerner replied with what looked like a counterexample: a central planning board posts prices, instructs enterprise managers to equate marginal cost to price and to expand or contract accordingly, and then adjusts posted prices in response to observed shortages and surpluses until equilibrium is reached; the informational burden, they argued, is no greater than that of a competitive market. Hayek's rejoinder was that this misidentifies the problem: the relevant knowledge is dispersed, local, and often tacit, and much of it does not exist until the process of competition elicits it, so prices are not a computational device for solving a known system of equations but a procedure by which agents discover what to do.

What I cannot get past is that the debate keeps returning in exactly the same shape. In its current form the computational objection is generally treated as dead — the equation-solving is trivial for modern hardware, and machine learning can estimate demand elasticities from transaction data at a scale no planning board of the 1930s could have imagined — and yet the two camps restate their positions almost verbatim and nobody moves. The "Lange won" side says the planner's problem was always an information-processing problem, and that we have now solved it; the "Hayek won" side says the objection was never about processing power, so nothing has changed.

My hypothesis is that this is neither a factual dispute nor a merely verbal one, but a dispute about what counts as a solution to the economic problem. The first side treats a solution as an existence proof — there is a price vector, and here is a procedure that converges on it — while the second treats a solution as a claim about a process actually available to agents who do not already possess the relevant knowledge, so that a scheme whose convergence presupposes the knowledge has already been centralized has assumed away the problem rather than solved it. On that reading the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, and each answer is true of its own question. I also notice that the modern formal literature seems to have split the difference in a way that pleases neither camp: mechanism design takes the informational asymmetry between planner and agents as the binding constraint, which is Hayek's point made rigorous, and yet the same literature's impossibility results suggest that efficiency, incentive compatibility, and the other desiderata cannot generally be had together — a vindication of the sceptics for reasons that have nothing to do with computation.

So: is my hypothesis the right diagnosis, or is the impasse something else — a substantive disagreement I have mis-described, a difference over what an economic theory is supposed to deliver, or two literatures talking past one another under one label? If the sides are answering different questions, is the choice between those questions itself arguable, or does it come down to what one wants a theory for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result, a distinction, an empirical finding, or nothing, because the two sides answer to different criteria and there is no shared tribunal? Be honest about whether the computational developments change anything for the second camp or are simply beside the point for it.

I don't want a survey of the debate or a chronology of who said what when; I want the diagnosis and your reasons for it.
