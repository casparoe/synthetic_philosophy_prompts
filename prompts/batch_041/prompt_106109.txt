For the better part of this year I have been convening a fortnightly discussion group that has no university affiliation — most of us simply read on our own and argue about it afterwards — and we have spent the last several meetings on vagueness, the one topic on which every one of us has changed our mind at least twice. We have now reached the point where the group can recite the standard treatments without hesitation: the view that there really is a hidden boundary and we are simply ignorant of where it falls; the view that there is no boundary because our words go indeterminate at the edges; the view that any boundary is drawn afresh by the conversational setting; and the view that truth itself admits of degrees. That fluency is precisely what I want to defeat, because none of it tells me whether these treatments are answers to the same question. So I would like you to draft a single essay question, for a ninety-minute written exercise marked out of twenty, that forces the group to work on the phenomenal rather than the linguistic version of the puzzle, and to build it around the following setup, which I would rather you take as given than source: a long row of colour patches, the first unmistakably red and the last unmistakably green, arranged so that no patch can be told apart from the one beside it when the two are held up together; a subject walks the row and, at each patch, reports on what she sees. She cannot mark the step at which red gave way, because there is no visible step, and yet she ends at a green patch — and the argument appears to show something about experience rather than about words.

I want the question to press on four things, and I want the marking scheme built so that a student who merely names the four positions and declares a winner cannot pass. First, I want them to say whether the hidden-boundary view is even available here, given that it seems to require a person to be ignorant, or mistaken, about how things presently look to her; I suspect this is a much harder sell when the subject matter is one's own experience than when it is a heap of sand, and I want to see whether they see that. Second, I want them to ask what the context-shifting view can say about a subject who is walking — whether the setting can shift from step to step, and, if it can, whether the paradox has been explained or merely moved. Third, I want them to say what the degrees-of-truth view buys and what it costs, since it seems to purchase a smooth transition at the price of some of the logic the rest of the argument leans on. And fourth, I want a variant folded into the question in which the subject has no colour vocabulary at all, or no language whatever, and I want the students to say what each of the four treatments can even be about in that case — that, to my mind, is where the group's fluency will finally be worth something or nothing.

Please give me the question itself, in no more than about two hundred words so that it can be read aloud and still leave time to write, then a model answer of roughly six hundred words, and then a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer must contain, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on the mistake that most often costs a candidate that mark. Where two of the positions turn out to agree on some sub-point, I would like the scheme to say so, so that a student who notices the agreement is credited rather than penalised for failing to pick a side. And if you think one of my four press-points is malformed — that the question it asks is not really open, or not really distinct from another — say so in a short note at the end rather than quietly repairing it, because I would rather set the group a question I have got wrong on purpose than one I have got wrong by accident.
