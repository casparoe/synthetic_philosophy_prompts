I'm the staff researcher for the foreign affairs select comittee in our parliament, the one that writes the non-binding "should we back this" recommendation before the chamber votes on a deployment. We've built up a track record and next week we have to take a position on a live case, and I want to know what we should recommend if we're going to be consistent with ourselves rather than with whoever shouted loudest in the last debate. You're a just-war theorist who's spent a career on the ethics of intervention. Please think this through carefully before you commit to anything, and start by brainstorming the full space of rules, don't just grab the first one.

Our calls so far:
- Kosovo, 1999: recommended backing the NATO air campaign, even though the Security Council never authorised it. We told ourselves NATO's sign-off counted as the multilateral cover.
- Iraq, 2003: recommended against. Our reasoning was that the case being made was about disarmament and replacing the government, not about anyone who was actually being killed at the time.
- Libya, 2011: recommended backing the no-fly zone and the strikes. Security Council authorised, and we liked two things: the resolution ruled out any foreign occupying force, and it stayed an air campaign rather than a ground invasion.
- East Timor, 1999: recommended backing the Australian-led force. Council-authorised, humanitarian in aim, and pointedly not aimed at toppling Indonesia's government.

So we seem to be running some rule like: (a) it's about whether intervening will save more lives than it costs, authorisation be damned; or (b) it's about who signed off, with a regional body counting as enough; or (c) it's about means, no invasion, no occupation, no regime change. Maybe some mix of the three.

Now the new case. Nara, a country whose government is backed by a permanent member of the Security Council, is killing a minority population in its northern provinces. That permanent member will veto anything that comes to the Council, and has said so. The regional body, the Coastal Union, has voted to endorse an intervention and formally asked for a coalition. Two of Nara's neighbours have offered troops, and both of them happen to covet Nara's oilfields. Our defence analysts say the only way to actually stop the killing is to take the capital and remove the government, then stay for years to keep order afterwards; there is no air-only option, the terrain and the dense cities rule it out. The killing is ongoing, though slower right now than Kosovo's was.

What I need: which rule or rules our four precedents are actually consistent with, what each of them predicts for Nara, your best guess at what we should recommend and the reasons for it, and the single earlier case, real or hypothetical, that would settle which rule we're really running. If choosing either way means we're quietly adopting a new principle, spell out plainly what that principle is. And if our precedents genuinely don't decide this one, say so rather than pretending they do.
