In April 1994, a subcommittee of the U.S. House of Representatives, chaired by Henry Waxman, subpoenaed the chief executives of the largest American cigarette companies and put them under oath. One after another, the executives testified that they did not believe nicotine was addictive. The commissioner of the Food and Drug Administration testified at the same hearing that the companies manipulated nicotine levels to sustain smokers' dependence, and internal industry documents released in the following years showed that the executives had strong reason to believe the opposite of what they were saying. What the hearing displayed was not a single dramatic falsehood but a pattern: carefully qualified statements of personal belief, stipulative redefinitions of the word addiction, technically accurate answers to narrowly construed questions, and true statements offered in contexts that invited a false inference.

Analyze that episode through the philosophical distinction between lying and misleading. Begin by setting out, as a menu, the competing accounts of where the distinction lies and what moral weight it carries. One account locates the wrong of lying in the intention to deceive, so that a speaker who asserts what is true in order to produce a false belief is a liar in everything but form. Another locates it in the violation of a commitment undertaken specifically by assertion, namely the commitment to assert only what one believes, so that a misleading truth is a different act with a different, and perhaps lesser, wrong. A third locates it in the difference between what is said and what is implicated, and asks whether the exploitation of implicature is as grave as the assertion of a falsehood. A fourth treats the distinction as, at bottom, a matter of detection: a lie is falsifiable by checking the world, whereas a misleading statement is falsifiable only by reconstructing the speaker's intentions, so that the misleading speaker obtains the benefits of deception while retaining the deniability of truthfulness. State the precise thesis each account commits to, and note where the accounts agree and where they are genuinely incompatible.

Then do the mapping. For each characteristic move by the executives, namely the statement of personal belief, the stipulative redefinition of addiction, the technically accurate but unresponsive answer, and the true statement that invites a false inference, say what each account classifies the move as, what would have to be true of the speaker's mental state for that classification to hold, and whether the classification survives the fact that the speakers were under oath and were being questioned by a body with a right to the information. Distinguish carefully among asserting a false proposition, asserting a true proposition that one believes to be false, implicating a false proposition, and merely failing to correct a false belief. Consider also whether the oath adds a promissory obligation that a misleading truth can violate even when no falsehood is asserted, and whether the casuistical tradition of reserving a private sense for one's words offers the executives any defense.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction. The hearing is often treated either as a showcase for the view that misleading is a distinct and lesser wrong or as a showcase for the view that the distinction is a loophole the powerful exploit. Which reading does the episode actually support? Does the executives' reliance on definitional gerrymandering and hedged belief-claims show that the line between lying and misleading is stable enough to bear moral weight, or that it is a legalistic artifact any motivated speaker can manipulate? Does the institutional setting change the moral status of a misleading truth, and if so, how? Conclude with a verdict: is there a moral difference in kind between lying and misleading, or only a difference in means, detectability, and deniability, and does the tobacco hearing give you any reason to prefer one verdict over the other?
