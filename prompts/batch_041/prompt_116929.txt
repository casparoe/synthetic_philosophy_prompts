I am in a reading group on ethics, and we have been circling the figure people call the moral saint, the person whose life is maximally devoted to moral ends. I built the following argument to try to capture what seems troubling about that ideal, but the conclusion is too strong and I cannot decide which step to give up. (1) A life is admirable to the degree that it realizes the good. (2) The only good relevant to a person's moral worth is moral good, understood as acting from duty or for the sake of others. (3) Nonmoral excellences, such as artistic creation, athletic achievement, intellectual curiosity pursued for its own sake, and deep personal attachments, are not moral goods. (4) Therefore a life that maximizes moral good is more admirable than any life that trades some moral good for nonmoral excellence. (5) The moral saint is by definition the life that maximizes moral good. (6) So the saint's life is the most admirable life. (7) But when I imagine the saint, someone whose projects, relationships, and pleasures are all justified by moral reasons, I do not find that life desirable, and I do not think my reluctance is merely weakness. (8) If (6) is true, my reluctance is a failure to appreciate what is admirable. (9) But my reluctance seems appropriate. (10) So something has gone wrong.

I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, because I do not want to say that morality is just one value among others or that moral worth is irrelevant to admiration. A friend suggested that the problem is that the argument treats moral good as a quantity to be maximized, whereas the saint's life is really characterized by a certain quality of attention or love, not by a sum of moral units. I do not see how that helps, because if the quality of attention is itself a moral good, the saint still wins, and if it is not, the saint's life is not morally best. Another friend said the real mistake is treating a moral ideal as a human ideal, but I cannot tell whether that is a diagnosis of the argument or just a restatement of the problem. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? In particular, if I give up (4), do I have to say that admirability is pluralistic and has no common measure, and if I give up (7), what would it take to make the saint's life genuinely desirable rather than merely admirable? I want a diagnosis that prices the alternatives, not just a list of positions.
