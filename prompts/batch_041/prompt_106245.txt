I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works in decision theory, and I have come away with an argument I can neither refute nor bring myself to accept. I would like you to find where it goes wrong — or, if it does not go wrong, to tell me which premise I should surrender and what surrendering it would cost.

The setting is the three-colour urn. An urn holds ninety balls. Thirty are red. The remaining sixty are either black or yellow, in a proportion I am not told. Four bets are offered, each paying a hundred dollars on the stipulated colour and nothing otherwise:

A: red.
B: black.
C: red or yellow.
D: black or yellow.

My friend reports, and I share, two inclinations. I would rather bet on red than on black; and I would rather bet on black-or-yellow than on red-or-yellow. Call these (i) A ≻ B and (ii) D ≻ C.

His argument runs as follows.

1. A rational agent's ranking of two acts cannot depend on what those acts pay in states where they pay the same thing. If two acts agree on an event E, then replacing their common consequence on E by any other common consequence must leave the ranking untouched. He calls this the sure-thing principle and insists it is nothing more than the demand that a choice turn on what actually distinguishes the options.

2. A and B agree on yellow: each pays nothing there. C and D agree on yellow: each pays a hundred there. Further, A differs from C only in what happens on yellow, and B differs from D only in what happens on yellow.

3. So the comparison of A with B and the comparison of C with D are the same comparison, differing only in a common consequence on yellow. By step 1 they must receive the same verdict: A ≻ B if and only if C ≻ D.

4. But (i) and (ii) yield A ≻ B together with C ≺ D. The inclinations therefore violate the sure-thing principle.

5. Such a violation, he says, is not a taste but an exploitable inconsistency. An agent whose ranking can be reversed by altering an event that does not distinguish the options can be led through a sequence of transactions she will accept, each of which she would have done better to refuse; she can be made to pay for the privilege of holding a position she already holds. An agent who can be treated this way has no determinate preference ordering, only a succession of impulses.

6. Hence my inclinations are not merely non-standard; they are incoherent, and there is nothing for an ambiguity-averse decision theory to be a theory of.

This is where I stall, because the conclusion proves too much for me. The same reasoning, if it holds, condemns anyone who declines a wager whose odds he cannot estimate: anyone who buys insurance against a hazard he cannot quantify, anyone who prefers a diagnostic test with a known error rate to one whose error rate is unknown. My friend is content to bite that bullet and calls all of it confusion. I am not content.

So where does the argument fail? If step 1 is the culprit, tell me exactly what I forfeit by rejecting it — what else I believe that rests on it, and whether rejecting it leaves me able to reason about choices at all. If instead the fault lies in steps 5 or 6 — in the move from violating the sure-thing principle to being exploitable, or from being exploitable to having no preferences — tell me why that move looked compelling and what an ambiguity-averse agent can say in its defence. And if the argument is simply valid, tell me which of (i) and (ii) to abandon and what the intuition behind it was tracking. I would rather keep the inclinations than keep the sure-thing principle, but I want to see the price before I pay it.
