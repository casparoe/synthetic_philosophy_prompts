I keep hearing a particular summary of the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence from people in my department, and I want to know how much of it survives scrutiny. It goes roughly: 'The orthogonality thesis says intelligence and final goals are independent — a superintelligence could have any goal at all, which is why an AI won't automatically share our values. It's Hume's point that reason is a slave to the passions: getting smarter doesn't tell you what to want. The instrumental convergence thesis says that any sufficiently intelligent agent will converge on the same intermediate goals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, self-improvement, keeping its goal intact — because those pay off no matter what your final goal is. Put the two together and you get the standard picture: once we build an AGI it will try to take over and stop us switching it off, and since we can't predict what it'll want, there's no way of controlling it. The paperclip maximiser is the proof.'

I'm fairly sure this is in the right neighbourhood but I dont trust the details. Can you go claim by claim: what's actually asserted by each thesis, what's an overstatement or a conflation, and what simply doesn't follow? I'm especially interested in whether the two theses are really a package deal (does either need the other?), whether the paperclip maximiser can carry the weight it's being asked to carry here, and whether the 'no way of controlling it' conclusion follows from anything true in the summary. British English please, and don't bother being diplomatic — if the framing as a whole is confused I'd rather find out.
