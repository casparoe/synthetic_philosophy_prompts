Okay, so I'm putting together a handout for the last session of a reading group that's been working through the philosophy of transformative experience — the L.A. Paul thing where certain choices (having a kid, becoming a vampire, uprooting to a totally foreign life) are supposedly ones you can't actually reason your way into, becuase you can't know what the outcome will be like from the inside, and becuase the choice will change who you are, so the "you" doing the evaluating isn't the "you" who has to live with it.

I want to close the handout with a poem. Please write it as someone who's actually read the literature and not just absorbed the phrase — a poet who also happens to be a decent philosopher of mind. Before you write anything, take a minute to think through how the form itself can carry the idea (I don't need to see that part), then write the poem.

Here's the shape I'm after. Two halves, "Before" and "After," six lines each. Both halves use the same six end-words — same six words, reordered however you like in the second half — but by the second half those words should mean something different, or carry a different weight, because the speaker has been through the change. The "Before" half is the speaker trying to imagine what it'll be like; let it try to weigh the thing like a normal decision, pros and cons, and let that approach quietly fail. The "After" half is the same person, on the other side, and it shouldn't be able to hand the knowledge back. The thing I care about most: the before-speaker's imaginings should turn out wrong in a way they could never have caught from where they were standing. I want the reader to feel the wall between the halves, not be told there's a wall. And don't make the wall total — the before-speaker knows plenty, just not the one thing that matters.

Ground rules: don't name the experience or explain the theory inside the poem, just render the before and after. Please don't make it about becoming a parent; that's the example everyone reaches for and it's worn out. Pick something else — the vampire's fair game, or invent your own (a new sense, a new appetite, a conversion), as long as it reads like a real transformative experience and not a metaphor for one. No citations, no footnotes, no "see Paul 2014" — just the poem. If it wants to end on the question of whether you'd want to find out, that's fine, but don't answer it.

Then, under the poem, two or three sentences on the choices you made and where you think it's weakest.
