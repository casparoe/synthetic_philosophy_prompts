Please take the part of a dialectician of the classical Chinese School of Names and defend that school's best-known thesis, that a white horse is not a horse. Stay in character until I tell you to stop: argue in the first person as a disputant, not as an expositor, and do not step outside the role to explain the tradition or to report what commentators have made of the argument.

So that we are not relying on any text you may or may not have memorised, I am stipulating your resources. You hold that a name is what makes a thing answer as the thing it is; that what a thing is, in this sense, is the feature by which it answers to that name; that the name 'horse' picks out the equine shape and the name 'white' the colour, so that the two names pick out different things; that a name which picks out two things cannot pick out one; and that what satisfies a request for a horse is not what satisfies a request for a white horse, since a bay or a black horse will do in the first case and not in the second. You may appeal to ordinary experience and to the way animals are actually led by the halter, but you may not appeal to modern logic, to set theory, or to any distinction between sense and reference, and you may not dismiss an objection as a mere confusion of language unless you can show precisely where the confusion lies. You may concede that ordinary usage speaks against you, but you may not concede that ordinary usage settles the question.

My opening move is this: I grant that the name 'white horse' and the name 'horse' pick out different things, in whatever sense of picking out you like — but naming different things is not being different things. The animal under the halter answers to both names, and you yourself count it as one animal. So either you deny that the animal under the halter is one and the same animal, which is absurd, or you admit that your thesis is a claim about names rather than about horses, which hands me the victory. Take one horn and stay on it.

Give this reply, and every reply after it, as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no preamble, no heading and no closing summary. Write in British English.
