You are a philosopher of biology with a working command of molecular biology and a second specialty in the metaphysics of representation and information. I am preparing a recorded interview and need your help with the question list. Before drafting anything, think carefully and make a short list of the four or five points at which the position described below is most exposed — the joints where a well-informed interlocutor could apply real pressure — and build the questions from that list rather than from a general map of the topic.

The guest is a philosopher of biology defending a two-part thesis. She is a composite figure invented for this exercise: do not attribute the view to any actual researcher, and do not ask her to name critics or to cite literature, since the conversation is meant to stand on the ideas alone. Her first claim is that the arbitrariness of the genetic code — the fact that the mapping from nucleotide triplets to amino acids is not derivable from the chemistry of the molecules involved, that the standard mapping is degenerate, and that some lineages read certain codons differently — is a genuine and consequential fact, but a fact about a mapping rather than about a molecule, and therefore insufficient on its own to show that DNA is a code in any sense worth arguing about. Her second claim is that the semantic content in protein synthesis is conferred by the decoding machinery: the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (the enzymes that attach each amino acid to its matching tRNA) and the ribosome are, on her view, the real bearers of the code, and DNA is better described as the medium the code is written on than as the thing that means anything.

Write nine to eleven questions, ordered so that the sequence builds, each phrased as it would be spoken aloud, with a follow-up after each one keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The questions must turn on the content of her position, not on her biography or her motives, and none of them should merely invite her to restate the two theses.

Include at least one question on each of the following, placed where they will do the most damage to the argument's momentum:

- Where the arbitrariness of the mapping comes from, if not from a convention or an agreement, and what follows for her view if codes require users rather than merely systematic mappings.
- Whether her account can distinguish the decoding machinery from the many other indispensable causal inputs to protein synthesis, so that singling out the synthetases and the ribosome is not itself arbitrary.
- Whether a code, on her account, can be misread — whether there is room for a difference between correct and incorrect decoding when the decoder is chemistry doing what it does.
- How the position handles cases where one and the same codon or stretch of sequence means different things in different contexts: variant codes, the reassignment of a stop codon to an amino acid, and the fact that a single sequence can be read into different proteins depending on where the reading frame starts.
- Whether anything in biology, or in the way we ordinarily talk about genes and what they are for, would have to change if she is right, or whether the disagreement is verbal.

After the questions, add a brief note identifying the single question she is least likely to answer without revising one of her two theses, and explain why in two or three sentences.
