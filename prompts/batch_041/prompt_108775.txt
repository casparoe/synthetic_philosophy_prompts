I am a third-year doctoral candidate in political theory, and in a fortnight I shall present the central chapter of my thesis at a departmental workshop. The chapter argues that the republican ideal of freedom as non-domination cannot be extended to what I call infrastructural dependence: the condition of agents whose feasible options are fixed by privately held systems — payment rails, ranking algorithms, identity providers, logistics networks — that they can neither exit, contest, nor materially influence. I claim that any attempt to extend republicanism to this case either reduces non-domination to a species of negative liberty, once we drop the assumption that domination requires a dominator possessed of a will and a capacity to interfere, or else inflates the concept until every unchosen constraint counts as domination, at which point it ceases to be distinguishable from a socialist account of exploitation. My positive proposal is a two-tier account that keeps interference and dependence analytically separate and treats contestability as a standing relation rather than an episodic remedy.

The audience is small and I know it precisely: one Pettit-trained republican who has written on domination in the workplace and will defend the modal character of the capacity to interfere; one Cambridge-school historian of political thought who suspects that the digital economy is being read through eighteenth-century categories that do not fit it; one Kantian legal philosopher who will ask what juridical form my contestability relation could possibly take; one economist who will want to know what work "dependence" does that "market power" does not already do; and one critical theorist who regards the entire republican vocabulary as a liberal alibi for capital.
