Here's the case. A family in the next county says their ancestor's farm was sold off in 1843 and that the money went into the endowment that set up our university's predecessor academy. What we have:

- A photocopy, held by the family, of one page that looks like it comes from the county court's record book for 1843-44, showing a sale of their ancestor's 240 acres to "the trustees of the academy." The family does not have the original. The volume in the county archive is missing that page — you can see where it was cut out, and the archive's own index lists a page that isn't there anymore.
- Family oral history, passed down, that the land was taken.
- A short newspaper item from 1847 about a "suit over the academy lands" that never names the family.
- Our trustee minutes for 1843-44, which are complete and never mention the sale or the family. But the university adopted a records policy in 1931 that let it routinely destroy financial papers and accounts older than sixty years, and alot of the 19th-century financial material went that way.
- Our lawyer's counter: the county had more than one man with that surname in 1840, so the name in the ledger page might not be the family's ancestor at all. The family says that's a coincidence we invented.

The group is split. Some want to formally acknowledge it and offer something concrete (a scholarship, a marker on the quad, a payment). Others say you can't act on a photocopy of a missing page plus a family story, and want to keep researching indefinitely. I have to write the memo that recommends a decision.

What I need: a clear recommendation with a timeframe attached, and the reasoning. Tell me if the standard of proof should be different for "acknowledge it in our official history" than for "hand over money," and say what new evidence we should commission and by when.
