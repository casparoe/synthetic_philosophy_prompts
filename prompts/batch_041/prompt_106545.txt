Thirty-five years as a physiologist taught me a great deal about how bodies keep themselves going and nothing at all about when one of them stops being a person. Since retiring I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about the moment of death, and I have a set of verdicts that will not add up.

A patient with locked-in syndrome — fully conscious, able to answer by blinking, paralysed from the eyes down: alive. A patient in a persistent vegetative state, cerebral hemispheres destroyed, brainstem intact, breathing without a ventilator, no evidence of consciousness and none expected: alive, though I would say the person is gone. A patient whose entire brain, brainstem included, has been destroyed, who makes no respiratory effort, whose blood pressure and temperature are held up by machine and drug, and whose body nonetheless closes its wounds and resists infection: dead — and I would say so even though the body is warm, and even if it were a pregnant woman maintained long enough to deliver a child. A donor after circulatory death, heart stopped, the team having decided not to resuscitate, whose heart is then cut out and restarted in a recipient: I want to say she was already dead when the heart was taken. A hypothermic patient pulled from a lake with no heartbeat and no brain activity, rewarmed in the emergency department and discharged walking: not dead, and not dead for a moment.

The second and third verdicts are the ones I cannot hold together. Both patients are permanently unconscious; the only difference between them is that one body breathes on its own and the other does not. If the vegetative patient is alive and the brain-destroyed patient is dead, then consciousness is not what is doing the work — something about the body's capacity to run itself is. So let me offer a principle for testing: death is the irreversible cessation of an organism's capacity to maintain itself as an integrated whole. That principle seems to give me the hypothermia verdict, since the capacity was never lost, and the vegetative-state verdict, since the brainstem is still integrating. I am less confident about the rest. If integration supplied by machine does not count, why does the ventilator-dependent quadriplegic, whose integration is also partly machine-supplied, count as alive? And irreversibility appears to mean two different things across my own verdicts: in the hypothermia case, that no one could reverse it; in the circulatory-death case, that no one will, because we have decided not to. I would like to know whether this principle can be repaired — perhaps by granting the brain a special standing as the integrator of the organism — or whether it should be traded for something nearer to irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, which I suspect would force me to abandon one of the two verdicts above. If so, which one, and why that one rather than its partner? I have gathered that philosophers separate the definition of death from the criterion used to apply it and the test used to apply the criterion; if some of my verdicts are really about criteria while pretending to be about death, I would like that said plainly.

Finally, tell me what your preferred principle implies about cases I have not put to it, and be candid about where it embarrasses you. A planarian cut in half, each half regenerating into a complete worm: has the original died? A tardigrade dried to a husk, metabolically silent, revivable after years — and does your answer change if it is never revived? A human embryo in liquid nitrogen, metabolism effectively stopped. A brain kept alive and functioning in a perfusion chamber with no body at all: is the body dead and the brain alive, or is the person alive? And the Portuguese man-of-war, which I am told is not a single animal but a colony of specialised polyps that cannot survive apart — what would its death even be? I would rather have a principle that deals honestly with these than one that tidies up my original five.
