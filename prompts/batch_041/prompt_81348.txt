I am a novelist, and I have been trying to discover, by my own reactions, what I actually believe about the standpoint from which one judges oneself. My verdicts: I cut a friend's finest scene from her manuscript and felt entirely clean about it, though she did not speak to me for a year. I wrote a blurb I did not believe and felt soiled for a fortnight, though the book was harmless and no one ever knew. I still wince, twenty years on, at a name I mispronounced at a dinner party, and almost never at the lie I told the person who mattered most. When a room laughs at a joke I think cruel, I feel the cruelty as mine for having laughed. When I hold my tongue under a public insult, I feel better than if I had answered, even when answering would have been just.

Is there a single account of the standpoint of self-judgment that yields all of these? If so, state it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and tell me what it predicts for a case I have not mentioned: an act no one could ever have witnessed, done in a locked room. If no single principle will do, give me the smallest set that will, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I should give that one up rather than complicate the principle to save it. A friend who reads philosophy says I am only redescribing Adam Smith's impartial spectator; I would like to know whether that is true, or whether these cases force a different or a repaired principle.
