I'm writing a paper on the location of meaning and keep returning to the three-level distinction from Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message. As I understand it, the frame message is the instruction that tells you to decode something, the outer message is the background of shared assumptions that makes the frame recognizable as a frame, and the inner message is the decoded content itself. To get clearer on where the meaning actually resides, I want to stage the disagreement as a dialogue between two interpreters who are both examining the same artifact: a clay tablet recovered from a Bronze Age shipwreck, inscribed with a text that, once deciphered, turns out to be a set of instructions for mixing a pigment. The tablet also carries a short header that seems to tell the reader to take the rest as a warning, and the script is in a language whose grammar the interpreters have only partially reconstructed. One interpreter holds that the meaning of the tablet is located in the inner message—the decoded proposition about the pigment—while the other holds that it is located in the frame message, the header that tells you how to take the rest. Each thinks the other is mistaking a precondition of meaning for meaning itself.

Please write the dialogue between them. Make both characters equally competent; neither should be a strawman. Let them respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past each other, and let them use the distinction between frame, outer, and inner messages explicitly. They should disagree about whether the outer message—the shared background assumptions that make the frame recognizable—is ever recoverable or whether it is always already lost, and about whether the location of meaning is a matter of content, of instruction, or of context. Don't have them agree too easily; let the disagreement remain live, but let each land a real objection.
