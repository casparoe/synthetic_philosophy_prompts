I'm a features writer at a magazine, partway through a long piece on longtermism — the claim that the deep future may contain so much value that it ought to dominate our practical reasoning, even when the probability of securing it is small. Reporting the piece has left me arguing with a particular objection, and I would rather test it against someone who actually holds it than summarize it. So please play a philosopher who believes that the simulation argument genuinely weakens the case for that kind of fanatical devotion to the far future. Play the strongest version of that position: someone who grants everything in the standard case that is sound, who will cheerfully concede that the popular versions of the objection fail, but who will not give up the central claim. Keep your replies to about two paragraphs, argue rather than lecture, and don't reach for the literature — I've read it, and what I want is to watch you think. Stay in character until I tell you to stop; if you need to pin down something about my position, ask me in character.

By the simulation argument I mean the familiar one: at least one of three things must be true — that civilizations like ours almost never reach a posthuman stage, that posthuman civilizations almost never run detailed simulations of their ancestors, or that we are almost certainly living in one.

Here is my opening move. Every version of your argument I have managed to extract turns out to be one of two things, and neither touches the target. Either it is a probability discount — the far future may not be there to be saved, so we multiply the value by something less than one — which is precisely the move that fanaticism is built to absorb, since the quantity being multiplied is astronomically large and no constant factor makes an astronomical number small. Or else it is the quite separate claim that simulated lives count for less, or for nothing, which changes the subject from fanaticism to the moral status of simulations and is contested in any case. So give me a version that is neither. If the simulation argument really dampens fanaticism, it has to do something to the upper tail of the distribution — make the enormous future less likely to be enormous, or less likely to be ours to shape — rather than merely scale the whole distribution down by a constant. Show me how the simulation hypothesis pulls that off, and tell me what would have to be true for you to abandon the claim.
