Okay so background: I'm a backend engineer, compilers and static analysis mostly, zero formal philosophy training past one ethics class in undergrad, and I got dragged into an argument at a birthday party over the weekend that I still can't get out of my head. The host's brother-in-law coaches math olympiad kids and is exactly the kind of guy who's read just enough Godel to be dangerous. We ended up argueing about a little proof checker I've been building at home. It's a total, terminating toy language with a Godel numbering for its own syntax, so in principle it can talk about its own proofs, and the checker itself is written in the same language it checks. I said the long-term goal is a version that can check its own soundness. He said that's provably impossible, and then he laid this out, which I've written up the way he said it:

(1) Any consistent formal system strong enough to express basic arithmetic has a sentence it can neither prove nor disprove, its own Godel sentence.
(2) That sentence is true anyway, and we can see it's true by stepping outside the system and reasoning about it.
(3) So for any such system there's a truth it can't reach, but a being who can reason about the system can reach it.
(4) A computer program is a formal system, so if a program could do everything a human mathematician does, then the mathematician's reasoning would also be a formal system.
(5) Therefore the human mind is not a formal system, and no program can keep up with a human mathematician, because the machine is always one Godel sentence behind.
(6) Which means no AI can be trusted to verify its own code, and my checker can never certify its own soundness.
(7) It doesn't get better with a bigger system either: the new system has its own blind spot, so the blind spot just moves, it never closes.

The part that gets me is (7). If it's right, my checker is worthless, but so is my whole team, because three engineers reviewing each other's proofs is just a bigger formal-ish system, and by (2) there's some truth about their own reasoning they can't see. And if I run (3) on you, the person reading this, then you can't see the truth about your own reasoning either, and yet here you are reasoning. So it feels like it's proving way more than Godel actually gives you. But every line by itself looks fine to me. I'd like to keep (1) and (2), I'm fairly sure those are just the theorem, and if I have to lose something I'd rather lose (5) than (6), mostly because (6) is the one that changes what I do on Monday. So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up actually cost me? And if the answer is that the regress is real but harmless, tell me why it's harmless for the machine and not for me. I don't want a reading list, I want the diagnosis.
