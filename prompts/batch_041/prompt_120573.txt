I'm a novelist — three books, the last one about a Victorian balloonist who can't afford to actually fly so he spends the whole novel reasoning about air he'll never test — and through some chain of favors I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on thought experiments in science. Sixty people in the room: a solid block of philosophers of science up front, a scattering of working physicists and a couple of biologists, and then grad students, donors, and spouses who came for the free dinner and the wine. Fifteen minutes, standing up, no slides, and I'm the warm-up act, so half the room is still taking off its coat.

Here's what I want the talk to do, and I'd like you to write it. I want to build the whole thing around one thought experiment, actually perform it on the audience — make them do the imagining with me, in real time, and then turn around and ask why that just worked, which is, I take it, the question the workshop is really about: how can a little movie that plays entirely inside your head, about a situation that never happened and maybe couldn't happen, tell you anything true about the world? I keep circling two answers I've read somewhere and can't choose between. One says a thought experiment is really just an argument with the argument left out — the pictures are packaging, the force is all deduction, which is why the good ones feel like proofs. The other says no, it's closer to seeing: Galileo with his two balls, Einstein in the elevator feeling his stomach drop, hands you something no list of premises could. I don't need you to settle it, I couldn't survive settling it in fifteen minutes, but the room should feel both pulls by the end — including the woman in the third row who took the free dinner and has never read a word of philosophy. And the balloonist is in there somewhere, a man who can't run the experiment so he runs it in his head and then has to decide whether to trust the result with his life, so use him if he's useful.

Couple of things it has to do. It should use a case where the intuition is contested, where two competent people look at the same imaginary setup and one sees a proof and the other sees a trick — the one I keep circling is the marble balanced exactly on top of a frictionless dome that, according to some, starts sliding off at a moment nothing chose, which either shows Newtonian physics doesn't determine the future or shows somebody cheated with the math (use that or a better one, I dont need to be right, I need the room to feel the disagreement). And it has to stay a speech. No names, no dates, no 'as the literature shows,' nothing that smells like a bibliography — the philosophy has to arrive through the examples and the jokes, not through citations. Also their's a dinner afterward and I'd like to be invited back, so leave them liking each other and liking me. Aim for something I can read aloud in about fifteen minutes.
