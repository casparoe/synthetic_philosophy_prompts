I'm a second-year law student, and I've spent the past few months arguing about transitional justice with a friend who does documentation work for a human rights group. The thing I keep running into, and can't find a proper name for, is a structural effect of conditional amnesty — the amnesty-for-full-disclosure bargain that South Africa's post-apartheid settlement is the best-known example of. On paper the condition is the whole truth. In practice, an applicant knows the state already holds a file on him: the names, the dates, the incidents that some other witness happened to record. So the confession that buys him immunity is the one that covers the file and then stops. The incident he committed that nobody photographed, that no one survived to describe, that never made it into any report — that is precisely the incident he has no reason to raise, since raising it only adds exposure. The upshot is that an instrument built to widen the record ends up reproducing the outline of the state's own prior knowledge, and it does so exactly because the record is what is being traded. I've been calling this "confessing to the file" in my own notes, but I doubt that's a real term. What I want to know is whether an established concept covers it — and I don't mean the general truth-versus-impunity trade-off, which I've read about; I mean the narrower feedback loop in which the baseline of already-documented facts sets the ceiling on what the process can ever learn.

There is a second half to this that troubles me more. Because the early confessions become the public reference point, later applicants calibrate against them: a version of events already said aloud and accepted is cheaper to confirm than a new one, so successive accounts drift toward the earliest documented version rather than correcting it. The record therefore doesn't merely stop at the edge of the file; it hardens along that edge and then presents the result as a consensus about what happened. I've tried to fit this to things I half-know. The streetlight effect is close — searching where the light is rather than where the keys fell — but that concerns a searcher's laziness, not an incentive that makes the witness censor himself along the same line, and it says nothing about the second-order convergence. Goodhart's law is close too, in that a measure deforms to become the target, but here nobody is gaming a score; the applicant is telling the truth, just a truth whose shape was fixed before he opened his mouth. Nor is it survivorship bias, because the missing incidents weren't merely unobserved by the analyst — they were never available to anyone, including the person who committed them and chose to stay silent.

What I'm after is the carving, not the label: what exactly this structure is, which of the neighboring ideas it is genuinely distinct from and why, and where its edges lie — does it survive if the state's file is destroyed, or if a second institution holding independent records enters the picture? Take a definite position. If there is a name and a body of work behind it, tell me which concept fits and where the fit breaks down; if nothing fits, say so plainly, coin something, and then defend the coinage against the two or three nearest rivals, stating what it does and does not cover. Please answer in American English, and resist hedging into a survey of everything adjacent — I would much rather have one argued verdict than five candidates laid side by side.
