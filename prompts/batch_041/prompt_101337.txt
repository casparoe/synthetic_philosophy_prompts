I run a small reading group for psychiatry residents and philosophy grad students, and next month it's my turn to lead on the whole "what even is a disease" question. The thing I keep noticing is that people argue past each other because they're quietly working with different views about whether calling something a disease is a descriptive, scientific matter or an evaluative one — and neither side realizes the other is on a different branch.

So I'd like you to act as a philosopher of medicine who's spent real time on the naturalism/normativism debate — the biostatistical-style accounts, harmful-dysfunction analyses, the thoroughgoing normativist and social-constructionist lines, phenomenological accounts that separate "disease" from "illness," welfare or capability views where health is about achieving your vital goals, and the deflationary family-resemblance option where there's no single essence at all. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming out loud: what are the actual axes of disagreement here (descriptive vs evaluative, does dysfunction need species-typicality or evolutionary history, is harm necessary or just typical, what's the reference class, first-person vs third-person, etc.), and which combinations of answers are even coherent. Then build me the thing.

The deliverable: a decision tree / flowchart that takes a person from a starting commitment to a named position on what makes something a disease. Each branch node should be a question phrased as a concrete case they can actually answer, not "are you a normativist?" — they shouldn't be able to see which leaf they're heading for. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody really defends it) plus one sentence on that view's most awkward commitment. 8 to 10 branch nodes, and give me a leaf for each of the views above; the family-resemblance and pure-normativist ones have to be in there even if they feel like cop-outs.

Please use these cases somewhere on the tree, since these are the ones my group keeps getting stuck on: myopia, now that most of a generation has it — is a trait that's statistically normal still a dysfunction?; a harmless anatomical or functional variant that hurts no one and that the person wouldn't change; a condition whose biological side is clearly real but that only counts as a "disease" in certain environments (something like the malaria-protective blood trait); aging and ordinary decline; a risk state like prediabetes where nothing is wrong yet; a diagnosis that seems to exist mainly to police a social norm rather than to describe a malfunction; and chronic fatigue / chronic pain, where there's real suffering but the measurable dysfunction is contested. I made up a couple of these framings on the spot so don't feel you have to match them to anything in the literature — I care about what they do to the tree.

Two extra things: wherever the logical space has a combination nobody occupies, mark it and say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two positions your tree genuinely can't tell apart — don't paper over it by adding a fake question.
