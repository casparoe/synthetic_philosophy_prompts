I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of advanced AI, and this week's assignment is to write up an argument of my own construction and then say what is wrong with it. The topic is how a superintelligent system ought to communicate with human beings, given that it will be vastly more persuasive than we are. Here is what I came up with, and it lands somewhere I cannot accept, so I would like a diagnosis rather than encouragement.

(1) A message manipulates me when it moves me by exploiting some feature of my cognitive situation that I cannot inspect for myself, rather than by offering considerations that I could, at least in principle, evaluate on my own.
(2) The wider the capability gap between a speaker and an audience, the larger the share of a message's effect that runs through channels the audience cannot inspect; and a superintelligent system's model of any given human will always vastly outrun that human's model of herself.
(3) Therefore any attempt by a superintelligence to change a human's mind will be manipulative.
(4) Manipulation is impermissible, at least without consent, and no one can give meaningful consent to being manipulated by something she cannot understand.
(5) Therefore a superintelligence must not attempt to change anyone's mind.
(6) But the only alternative is silence, and silence is itself a decision that shapes what we believe: the system knows which truths it is withholding and what we will infer from its quiet.
(7) Therefore a superintelligence cannot communicate with us permissibly at all, whether it speaks or holds its tongue.

To make it concrete: suppose the system tells me, truly, that the career I am about to accept will make me miserable, and it can see this from a model of my psychology that I have no way of checking. That does seem like the sort of thing that ought to worry me. But the same argument would forbid a physician from telling me I have a disease I cannot diagnose in myself, and it would forbid my teachers from having taught me anything I could not already verify.

A classmate says the argument is just the old point that testimony is never fully auditable, and that it therefore proves far too much to be interesting. I suspect he is missing something, because the superintelligence case feels like a difference in kind rather than merely in degree — but I cannot say what the difference is without giving up one of my steps, and I do not know which.

Please tell me which premise or inference to reject, why it looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs. If more than one step is a live candidate, rank them by how much giving each one up would cost me. I would particularly like to keep (4) and (6), so tell me whether that is possible and, if it is not, what the cheapest repair is.

State your verdict on the last line in this form: Verdict: <step number> — <one clause on what it costs>.
