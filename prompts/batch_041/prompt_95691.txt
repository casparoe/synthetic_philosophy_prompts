I write about philosophy and the social sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I've been poking at a piece about the drowning-child argument — the one people call Singer's — and how far it actually gets you. Last month I was at a dinner after a public lecture and two people I'm friendly with went at each other for about forty minutes over it: Ines, who's deep in the effective-altruism world, and Marcus, who teaches political philosophy. I took notes on the back of a menu and I've been trying to figure out ever since who actually had the better of it. I'm a journalist, not a philosopher, so I'd definately rather get this from someone who really knows the terrain than guess at it myself.

Here's what I want from you. Act like a philosopher who works on normative ethics and has spent years on the ethics of rescue and the demandingness debate — the kind of person who has strong views about which objections to the rescue principle are real and which are just discomfort wearing a suit. Take your time and don't just react. Start by brainstorming: write out the distinct claims in the exchange below, seperate the ones that are conceptual from the ones that are empirical, and mark which moves are load-bearing for each person. Then give me your actual verdicts. Please don't hedge into "both make valid points" — I need to know which specific moves hold up and which don't, and where something is genuinely unresolved, say so and say why.

The gist, in order:

1. Ines: You'd wade into a shallow pond and ruin your shoes to pull out a drowning toddler. Kids are dying right now of stuff that a few hundred dollars fixes, and you spend that on shoes without thinking twice. The rule she's invoking is roughly: if you can prevent something bad without giving up anything comparably important, you ought to. So either you give up the pond intuition or you start handing over a serious share of your income. There's no principled place to stop in between.

2. Marcus: The pond case hides most of its work. At the pond you're the only person there, you can see the kid, and it's a one-time thing — you act and the situation is over. In the real case there are millions of possible donors, hundreds of millions of people in need, and the need never gets discharged. Take the pond logic literally and you're on the hook forever, up to giving until you're nearly as badly off as the person you're helping. A principle like that doesn't guide anyone, it just makes ordinary life a permanent moral failure.

3. Ines: Two replies. The scale doesn't change the principle, it multiplies it — more drowning kids, more obligation, it doesn't get diluted. And "I'm not the only one" cuts her way, not his: if ten of us are at the pond and we can all swim, it doesn't become fine for all ten to watch because someone else will handle it. Each of us has a duty, unless someone's already in the water.

4. Marcus: That's the murky part. Each has a duty to do what? If all ten of us think we personally have to be the one who jumps in, nine of us wasted the effort, and nobody can coordinate who goes. What's true at the ten-person pond is that each of us owes our fair share of the rescue — and if it's a one-person job and there are ten of us, the fair share is small: shout for a lifeguard, throw a rope. But in the global case there's no fair share to compute, because the job is unbounded. The difference between the pond and the world isn't scale, it's that the pond is something a person can actually resolve.

5. Ines: He's smuggling in other people's behaviour. "My fair share" is defined by what everyone else would do if they were doing their bit, so he's using the fact that they aren't as a discount on what she owes. That's exactly the excuse the pond case exists to block — "I don't have to jump in, someone else probably will." And the unbounded-need thing is just the demandingness objection in a costume. Too demanding isn't an argument that a moral claim is false.

6. Marcus: It's not squeamishness. A morality nobody can live up to, that treats your own projects, your family, your health and your future capacity to help as carrying no independent weight, and that gives you no way to choose among the millions you could help — that morality just gets ignored, and he's not even sure "ought" applies to something like that. Also, it can't be right that pulling the kid out of the pond and wiring money to a charity with a 90% chance of saving a kid are morally identical. One is a certainty about a person you can see; the other is a bet on a statistic.

7. Ines: The principle doesn't say your own life counts for nothing — it says you can't weigh a pair of shoes against a kid's life. And "I probably won't follow it" has never been a reason to think a moral claim is false.

OK. Four things I need, and keep it concrete because it has to survive an editor:

(a) Go round by round. For each numbered move, tell me whether it's sound, whether it's a real argument or a restatement of the other side's view in new words, and whether the next person's reply actually answers it. Rate each move from "clearly mistaken" to "clearly right" and justify the rating in a couple of sentences.

(b) Zoom out: what's the strongest version of each position, and as the exchange actually went, who had the better of it?

(c) Are they talking past each other anywhere? Is there a distinction one of them is missing that would dissolve part of the fight? In particular I want your read on whether Marcus's fair-share move is a real argument or a rationalization, and whether his "the need never gets discharged" point does any independent moral work or is just the scale point again.

(d) Finally, what is each of them missing that neither of them said? And if you think the whole framing is off — say, that the pond case was never meant to be cashed out as a general principle about giving — tell me that too.
