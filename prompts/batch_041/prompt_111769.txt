I'm not a philosopher by training, but over the past few months I've become personally entangled with an argument that a close friend keeps pressing on me, and it has started to affect a real decision I have to make — how much of my income to commit, year after year, to a fund whose only payoffs would land unimaginably far away in space and time, well outside any causal reach I will ever have. The argument, as I understand it, is roughly this. My choice is evidence about how agents relevantly like me choose. If there are other agents elsewhere in a very large universe whose choices are correlated with mine because they implement the same decision procedure, then choosing the cooperative option makes it more likely that they choose it too, and their choosing it makes it more likely that I do; the arrangement in which everyone cooperates is better for each of us in expectation than the arrangement in which everyone defects. There is no causal channel between us, but the evidential correlation is supposed to do all the work, and so my sacrifice is meant to be justified by agents I could never meet, affect, or confirm the existence of. I find this both compelling and suspicious, and I've written a short draft of an essay defending it so that someone can tell me exactly where it fails.

Here is the step I cannot get comfortable with, and the reason I'm asking for comments rather than for a verdict on the whole idea. The argument seems to depend entirely on how I individuate the decision procedure that I and my supposed counterpart are said to share, and every choice of grain seems to break it in a different direction. Individuate coarsely — say, agents who accept this very argument — and the correlation is robust, but the counterparts become a heterogeneous crowd whose cooperative acts may have nothing to do with the specific cause I am funding, so my choice tells me nothing about whether they do the particular thing that would benefit me. Individuate finely — agents with my values, my information, and my situation — and the counterpart's act is exactly the one I care about, but the class is so narrow that the correlation is either negligible or, in the limit, collapses into my simply treating myself as my own counterpart. Somewhere between these poles there must be a principled stopping point, or the argument is either vacuous or empty. In the draft I try three repairs and am dissatisfied with all of them: appealing to the level of abstraction at which the decision procedure is specified rather than the physical system that runs it; appealing to a notion of the same decision problem that abstracts away from the details of the situation; and treating the correlation as a matter of degree that trades off against how relevant the counterpart's act is to my interests. I suspect I am conflating two distinctions that people who work on this keep separate, but I can't see which.

I'd like you to comment on the draft as a critical reader, not a cheerleader. Specifically: (i) is the individuation worry a genuine objection or an artifact of how I've set the problem up, and if it is genuine, does it show the argument is empty, vacuous, or merely incomplete? (ii) Is any of my three repairs the right kind of move, and if so, which, and what would it need to say to actually work? (iii) Where am I conflating things — in particular, is there a distinction between the correlation being real and the correlation being something I can reason from, or between how a procedure is specified and which procedure I in fact implement, that I'm blurring? I'd rather have two sharp criticisms that bite than a list of vague suggestions, and please don't tell me the question is interesting — assume I know that and tell me where the argument breaks. End your reply with a final line beginning "Verdict:" and containing exactly one of the labels survives, fails, or survives-in-part, followed by a single sentence naming the one change that would most improve the draft.
