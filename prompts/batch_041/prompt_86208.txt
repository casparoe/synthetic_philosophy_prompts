I am preparing a defense of a rigorist position on veracity for a symposium, and before I commit to it in print I would like an audit of whether the commitments below form a tenable package. Here is where I stand.

(1) Veracity is a strict duty and admits of no exceptions. I do not lie, and I would not lie to the murderer at the door.

(2) Lying, strictly speaking, is a declaration contrary to my own thought made to someone who has a right to the truth. Withholding something from a person who has no such right is not lying, however much it may mislead him.

(3) The ground of the duty of veracity is not any particular person's claim on me but what a lie does to humanity in general and to my own dignity: a lie damages the standing of speech as such, and I may no more make an exception of myself here than anywhere else.

(4) The murderer who comes to my door has, by the maxim on which he acts, put himself outside the class of those who can demand truth from me. The duty of veracity is owed to those who can claim it.

(5) Genuine conflicts of duty do not occur. When two duties appear to collide, what has really happened is that one of the candidate duties was never in force in that situation; the appearance of an insoluble dilemma is always a sign that I have described the case wrongly.

(6) Beneficence toward my endangered friend is not a discretionary extra. I owe him protection, and I may not purchase the tidiness of my own record with his life.

(7) A lie is wrong in the first place because it treats the person deceived merely as a means to my ends.

(8) I take the Formula of Universal Law to be the fundamental formulation of the categorical imperative, with the Formula of Humanity as a restatement of it rather than an independent test.

Two clarifications about the case as I intend it, since the standard version makes matters too easy. First, when the murderer asks whether his intended victim is under my roof, I take it that veracity does not command disclosure: I may decline to answer, and on (2) declining is not lying. Second, I am considering the variant in which he adds that he will take my silence as a yes, so that saying nothing is itself the conveying of a falsehood and the option of simply not answering is closed. That variant is the one a commentator has put to me, and I would rather not be surprised by it.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these commitments actually pull against one another, and which pairs only look as though they do? For each real tension, say which item would have to give and what it would cost me to give it. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and be explicit about which of them are exposed only by the second version of the case. I do not know where the trouble is, and I would rather hear it from you than from a referee, so please do not stop at telling me that the package is rigorist. Tell me which item is load-bearing, which single commitment could be abandoned at the least cost to repair the most, and what verdicts in each version of the case I would then have to accept.
