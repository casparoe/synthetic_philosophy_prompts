I write about science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and I've got a column due Friday — before I file it I want your read on it. It's about escalation: two parties raising the stakes long past the point where winning is worth anything. My peg is the dollar auction. Setup: you auction off a $1 bill, the highest bidder pays what they bid and gets the dollar, and — this is the sting — the second-highest bidder also pays what they bid and gets nothing. I ran it at a dinner party last month. Bidding crawled up in nickels, got to 60 cents, and then something flipped and the two guys out front just kept going, past a dollar, past three, and I collected $9 for a dollar I'd borrowed off the host. It was funny for about ninety seconds and then it was genuinely awful. Nobody would quit.

The thesis of the piece is that this is the master key to a whole family of things we call irrational — the streaming wars, the AI capex race, two neighbours in a boundry dispute, the last two bidders in a takeover fight. And the mechanism I claim is the sunk cost fallacy: once you've got money on the table you can't walk away from it, so you keep throwing good money after bad. Shubik — that's who came up with the dollar auction, back in '71 — used it to show a short-sighted rational choice adding up to a long-run irrational one, and I lean on Staw's escalation-of-commitment work from the 70s to say the people in these situations are basically the dinner party.

I also float a distinction I'm rather pleased with: "locked-in" escalation, where the loser has to pay (the dollar auction, where you're on the hook for your last bid wether you quit or not), versus "free-exit" escalation, where the loser just eats a sunk cost and walks (a bidding war for a house, a failed format war). I claim only the first kind is genuinely self-propelling, and that in the second kind a clear-eyed bidder can simply stop.

What I want from you: (1) is the sunk-cost diagnosis right, or have I got the engine wrong — I've got a nagging feeling the mechanism is doing something other than what I say it is; (2) is the locked-in/free-exit distinction sound, and is "only the first kind is self-propelling" too strong; (3) does the AI-race example survive contact, given that nobody there is collecting from the loser — I worry the analogy leaks; and (4) honestly, is this interesting, or is it "sunk costs, again" with a dinner-party story stapled to it. I don't need a reading list, I need to know where the thing is soft.
