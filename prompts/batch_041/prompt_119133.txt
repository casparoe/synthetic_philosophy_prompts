I retired a few years ago from a career in the natural sciences and have since spent much of my time on philosophy of mind, largely by way of a small reading group. There is one sentence I cannot get past, because I have now heard it from three people doing three quite different jobs with it.

A philosopher I respect closed off a discussion of whether a sufficiently faithful simulation of a brain would be a mind by saying that the brain is a computer, and therefore of course it would be; the remark was meant to end the inquiry, not open it. A second member of the group uses the same sentence to deflate the problem of consciousness: it is information processing, nothing more. A third, otherwise sympathetic to the computational picture, uses it against that picture — the brain, he says, is not a computer at all, and our computational models of it are instruments we mistake for the thing modeled.

I suspect all three are trading on an ambiguity, but I cannot state the alternatives cleanly. Please separate the precise claims that the sentence could be making, laid out as a menu ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each: which are platitudes that no one denies, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which are too vague to be assessed at all.

One distinction I would like you to keep in view, since it was raised by the formal-minded member of the group and I suspect it does most of the work: take any physical system with finitely many distinguishable states, and you can find some abstract machine whose transitions track it. If being a computer means only being describable as implementing a computation, then thermostats, rivers, and the solar system are computers, and the slogan says nothing. He concluded that the interesting claim must be that the brain implements the right computation, and asked which one; nobody answered.

Finally, please say for each of the three uses above which reading would actually license it, and whether that reading is one you have judged defensible. If any of my interlocutors is using the sentence to do work that no defensible reading supports, I would rather hear it plainly. One of them also insists that the sentence is really about the mind rather than the brain, and that the substitution of one noun for the other is not innocent; tell me whether it changes what is being claimed.
