I run a tiny podcast, basically me and a microphone in my kitchen, and next month I've got on this writer who's spent years arguing that Malthus was never refuted, just early. I've read his book twice and I still cant tell if he's got something real or if he's just very good at wriggling, so I want to come at him properly. Put yourself in the shoes of someone who knows the falsification and ad hoc rescue literature inside out, a philosopher of science who's spent their life on the question of when a theory is actually dead, and help me build the interview.

Before you write anything, think it through on your own: what are the three or four strongest lines you could take against his view, and what is he most likely to say back to each? Then turn that into eight to ten questions.

His position, as I understand it: he says there are two different ways a prediction can fail. One is that the thing he predicted didn't happen on time, call that a timing failure, and the other is that the mechanism he named turned out not to be running at all. He thinks only the second one is a real refutation. A timing failure just means the mechanism is slower than expected, or that something else is temporarily holding it off, and he has a rule for that too: whatever is holding it off has to be something his theory didn't already rule out. That's the whole move and everything hangs off it.

I want at least one question on each of the following, and for each one a follow-up keyed to whatever he is most likely to say, so I'm not left standing there when he gives the standard reply:

how you tell a genuine case of something else holding it off from a fudge invented after the fact. He'll say his offset is independent and testable, so push him on who decides that, and when they get to decide it.

the symmetry test. If his patience is good for Malthus it should be good for theories he doesn't like, predictions that we'd have run out of oil by now, that automation would have ended most work, that some economy was going to collapse. Does he grant those the same grace? If not, what's the principled difference rather than just the political one?

the finite-offset problem, which is the one that bugs me most. If the reason food hasn't run out is fossil fuels and mined fertiliser, then the mechanism isn't late, it's being paid for out of something that itself runs down. So is his theory unfalsifiable until that runs out? And if a theory can only be tested after the thing postponing it is gone, is it still an empirical claim, or is it a forecast about a world that doesn't exist yet?

whether right but early is actually any different from right about a different world, and how we'd ever tell those apart.

what would actually make him drop it. I want a real answer, not well, if the mechanism stopped.

the fact that being early isn't free. If you act on a prediction that's early, real people pay for it now, and I've read that Malthus-ish thinking got used to justify being pretty brutal to poor people. Does he think a theory that's right but early carries any responsibility for the harm done in its name while it waits?

And one more on how he'd test the timing clause itself: can you ever get evidence about when a mechanism should have shown up, or is the timing bit always the part that can't be checked?

Don't ask him the usual what do you say to critics stuff, and don't let any question be answerable by him just restating the book.

For the format: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Write it as continuous prose, the way you'd explain it to me over coffee, one paragraph per question with the follow-up worked in. And tell me where you think he's actually got a point, not only where he's vulnerable.
