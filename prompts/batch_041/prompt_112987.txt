One of the engineers on my team has been reading one of those big popular books about East Asian culture and keeps summarizing it at lunch, and I honestly can't tell how much of it to believe. His version goes like this: Western philosophy starts with the lone individual — Locke, social contract, rights you have before you ever join a society — and treats the group as something you opt into. Confucianism starts at the other end, where the self is built out of its relationships, so you're a son and a colleague before you're a person, and the group's interests come first. That's why East Asia values harmony, which basically means not disagreeing with the boss and not making waves, and why they're collectivist and we're individualist. He also says the Confucians made being a good son and a good brother the root of the whole ethics, and that their sequence runs family, then state, then world, so the family is the basic unit of the whole system and the individual barely gets a look in. His conclusion is that the model cant be right for us, because if the group comes first there's no room for rights or for criticising your own side.

I'm not equipped to argue with him, and I half buy it, which is sort of the problem. Which parts would someone who actually studies this accept, which would they wince at, and does his conclusion follow from whatever survives? He's not dumb, so I want to know where exactly it goes wrong rather than just that it's more complicated than that.
