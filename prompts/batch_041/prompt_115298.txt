My friend Priya and I burned almost two hours last night arguing about whether you can ever point to *the* cause of a big historical event, and now I want to turn the fight into something I can actually run with students instead of just rehashing it. The thing we couldn't get past goes like this: take any revolution or war or crash, and you can list six or seven factors, and for each one there's a perfectly good story where if you'd changed just that factor the whole thing doesn't happen — so every factor looks necessary. But they can't all be necessary in that sense, because the event did happen, and the world didn't have to independently cooperate in seven different ways to head it off. Flip it around and you get the opposite headache: if each factor was by itself sufficient, then any one of them would've brought the event about regardless of the others, so none of them was actually needed, and the historian who says "the real cause was X" is just picking a favorite. Priya says the honest move is to list everything and shut up; I say the picking is the whole job, and I couldn't defend that for the life of me. So what I want is an activity where people feel that squeeze in their gut rather than hearing me lecture about it.

I'm running the Thursday meeting of a philosophy of history seminar — 12 grad students, 90 minutes, sharp but mostly trained in history rather than the metaphysics of causation, so please don't lean on any jargon and don't assume they've heard of any of the standard puzzles. I want them to build the case themselves and only notice where they've landed at the end. Here's the setup I have in mind, and keep the event invented rather than real so nobody can shortcut it with trivia they already know: the outbreak of the Meridian riots, a made-up port city, spring of 1911. Hand each student a factor card — a wage cut at the docks, a newspaper editor who stoked it, a police chief who pulled his men back, a bad harvest that spiked bread prices, a rumor that the governor was sending troops, a rival union spoiling for a fight. Round one, each person has to argue that their factor was necessary, i.e. that without it the riots don't happen. Round two, the group has to fill in a ledger of causal shares that sums to 100%, which is where I expect it to get ugly. Then spring two twists on them: one card is a backup that was armed but never fired — the militia that got the order and turned back at the bridge — so the counterfactual test gives a crisp answer for that one and goes mushy for the simultaneous ones; and at some point make them answer not "why did the riots happen" but "why did they happen that week and not three months earlier," and let them discover their whole ledger reorders itself.

Give me the runnable version: roles or rules, materials, a timeline that fits 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps the specific moments in the activity onto the pieces of the puzzle (necessity vs. sufficiency, the difference between a cause that never fired and two that fired at once, and why changing the question changes which factor wins). Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be straight with me about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. Also, if you think the ledger round is a mistake, say so and give me something better.
