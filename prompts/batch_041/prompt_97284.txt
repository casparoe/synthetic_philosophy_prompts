I'm in sixth form and I run a sort of unofficial philosophy club with a teacher, Mrs H, who's definately got a bee in her bonnet about guilt and shame. She keeps giving verdicts on cases and I want to know what she'll say about the one we're actually dealing with at school.

Her verdicts so far, as best I can write them down:

- A kid who cheats in a mock exam, feels guilty, confesses, and asks to resit: she says guilt is doing its job here.
- A kid who cheats, feels ashamed, and starts avoiding the subject and lying about it: she says shame is doing damage here.
- A girl who feels ashamed that her family can't afford the school trip, so she doesn't go and doesn't tell anyone: she says that's shame being dumped on her by the school's culture, not a useful moral emotion.
- A boy who feels guilty about something he couldn't control, like his parents splitting up, and can't stop apologising: she says that's guilt gone wrong, not guilt doing its job.
- A workplace where people feel shame about missing targets and then work harder: she says shame is doing its job there, even though she doesn't like it.
- A restorative justice meeting where the person who did the harm feels guilt, hears from the victim, and repairs what they can: she says that's the gold standard.
- A public shaming campaign on social media where someone is humiliated and then apologises: she says that's shame being used as punishment, and it doesn't count as moral repair.

Then she says something I can't fully get my head round. She thinks cultures can choose which emotion they major in, but not in the way people usually mean. She says institutions don't just punish behaviour; they build the emotional plumbing. She calls this an emotion regime. But she also says there's a difference between an emotion being available in a culture and a culture actually selecting it. She thinks guilt and shame aren't rivals exactly; they're two different ways of attaching a person to a norm, and the real question is what the norm is attached to — the act or the self — and whether the person can do anything about it. She also thinks you can't always seperate the emotion from the audience: shame needs a real or imagined watcher, guilt needs an internalised standard, and a culture can supply one without the other.

Now the actual case. Our school is bringing in a new policy for vaping. Option G is the guilt-ish one: private reflection with a tutor, a letter of apology to someone affected (maybe the cleaner who has to pick up the pods), and a repair task. Option S is the shame-ish one: a small meeting where the kid has to admit what they did in front of a few affected people, hear how it landed, and then do a private repair task. The aim of S is to make them feel ashamed of being the kind of person who vapes in the toilets, not just guilty about the act. The head wants to know which will work better, and also whether we can deliberately make the school a guilt culture rather than a shame culture. She says she wants a decision that's consistent with what Mrs H has already said.

What would Mrs H say about Option G vs Option S? What rule is she actually running? What other rules fit her earlier verdicts just as well? Which of those rules would come apart on this case, and what further case would tell them apart? I'm less bothered about whether she's right than about what she's committed to.
