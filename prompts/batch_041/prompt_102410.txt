I'm a PhD student and my supervisor is a Rawlsian of the old school — reads the Restatement like scripture. I'm trying to guess her verdicts before she gives them, mostly to save myself a meeting.

Her track record on distributive stuff so far:

- Flat consumption tax that lifts GDP but leaves the bottom fifth's real income exactly where it is: unjust, no hedging.
- A graduate tax that funds universal childcare and actually widens the ratio between top and bottom: just, on the grounds that the bottom fifth end up better off than they would under the status quo.
- A housing reform that hands landowners a windfall but funds social housing, where she agreed a land-value tax would have done more for the bottom fifth — she still called it just, because the land-value tax wasn't politically feasible.
- Letting hospitals set specialist pay by the market, which she was fine with, her stated reason being that the bottom fifth do better under it than under the flat-scale alternative.
- Cutting the top marginal rate to fund a jobs guarantee that mostly helps the bottom two fifths: just.

So: she's obessed with the bottom group, tolerates inequality when the bottom gains, and won't hold a policy hostage to a non-feasible alternative. Fine.

The countries in question aren't failed or burdened states; they're just poor relative to the top.

Does she call the proposal as drafted just? I care less about the verdict than about the rule. Give me the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts, what each one predicts here, your best guess with reasons, and the one further case that would actually tell the rules apart.
