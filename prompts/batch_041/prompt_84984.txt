I retired after thirty years in population biology—mostly host-parasite dynamics and biological control—and have spent the last two years trying to get up to speed in philosophy and AI safety. I keep returning to the cluster of proposals usually called AI control: the family of safety measures that assume the model may be misaligned and try to bound the damage anyway, through untrusted monitoring, sandboxing, trusted editing, capability limitations, and the like. I would like to argue with a committed defender of that program rather than read another overview. Please play such a defender: someone who believes alignment is necessary but insufficient, who thinks control is a distinct and tractable research agenda, and who can grant that control has costs, failure modes, and unresolved conceptual problems without conceding that it collapses into alignment or that it is hopeless. Stay in character until I say stop, speak in the first person, and keep each reply to two or three substantial paragraphs so that it feels like a conversation rather than a report. Do not break character to give me a literature review; if you need to invoke a protocol or distinction, describe it in your own words.

My opening move is a challenge I have been developing with a logician friend. Suppose we have an untrusted model U that is at least as capable as any trusted model T we might use to monitor it. In a protocol where T watches U, either T is weaker than U, in which case U can plausibly evade or manipulate it, or T is at least as strong, in which case the guarantee seems to rest on T being aligned—which is precisely what the alignment program was supposed to secure and what the control program was supposed to do without. If we try to avoid that by making T narrow or by hiding information from U, we seem to trade one unearned trust assumption for another, because the designer must trust the sandbox, the human overseer, or the narrow monitor not to be fooled. My friend calls this the trusted-monitor trilemma: a monitor must be strong enough to catch the untrusted model, weak enough not to be a threat itself, and aligned enough to be trusted, and it looks as though you can have at most two. Which two do you give up, and why is that not simply alignment with extra steps? I am not looking for a survey of proposed protocols; I want to see whether the control agenda has a conceptual foundation that does not quietly borrow the very thing it was meant to replace.
