Something's been bugging me and I cant tell if it's a real tension or I'm just confused. We take advance directives really seriously. If you sign a living will saying no feeding tube if you're permanently unconscious, hospitals will follow it even when the later you — demented, cheerful, asking for lunch — seems to be enjoying life and wants to keep going. We let the earlier you boss around the later you. But we don't do that anywhere else: you can't sign a contract binding yourself to work for free forever, a court won't enforce a promise to stay married, and if I change my mind before surgery the old consent form doesn't override me. So what does honoring advance directives actually presuppose?

I want an essay that reconstructs the theory of personal identity and authority the practice rests on. Does it need the view that the earlier, competent self is the real self and the later one is basically a stranger? Or a distinction between a person's deep critical interests — what they cared about for their life as a whole — and their moment-to-moment experiential interests? Or is it just generic respect for autonomy, deferring to whatever the person said while competent? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? And is it even consistent — does the same logic commit us to honoring a Ulysses contract to be lobotomized, or to refusing to let a still-competent person revoke their own directive?

Be careful to seperate what the practice strictly requires from what it just happens to fit with, and flag where it's underdetermined — where several different theories would license the exact same behavior. Then tell me what would actually have to change if we dropped the idea that the earlier self has authority over the later one. Would advance directives even make sense anymore, or would we just be doing something else and calling it the same thing?
