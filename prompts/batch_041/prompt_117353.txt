I'm in the second term of a third-year seminar on experimental philosophy and the philosophy of action, and for the final portfolio we're allowed to substitute a satirical piece for one of the essays, provided it shows real command of the material. The unit on the Knobe effect was easily the most interesting part of the term, and I'd like to write the piece as an in-universe document rather than as an essay about the effect: a guidance note issued by a fictional government body that has decided to settle questions of intent administratively, using the asymmetry as its official test. Concretely, an agency that rules on whether a foreseen but undesired side effect counts as 'intended' for purposes of liability and commendation, whose note states in deadpan administrative prose that a harmful side effect counts as intended whenever the agent foresaw it and was indifferent to it, while a beneficial side effect under identical foresight does not — and which is written throughout as though this were an obvious and defensible codification rather than a scandal.

A few constraints. My professor has forbidden us to use the chairman and the environment case again; she says that if you can only satirize the canonical vignette you haven't understood what is at stake. So the note needs new cases: at least two worked examples, one in each direction, plus a 'hard case' in which the valence is contested (the side effect falls on a rival firm, or on an institution rather than a person, so it isn't clear which way the test cuts). It should also include an internal annex in which a junior analyst argues that the bureau has the direction of explanation backwards — that the public does not first decide what was intended and then assign blame, but rather blames and then attributes intent, so the bureau is not codifying the ordinary concept of intention so much as rationalising the public's reactions — and a short paragraph reporting a control study in which the side effect was stipulated to be morally neutral and the asymmetry vanished, which the bureau responds to not by revising the doctrine but by narrowing what counts as 'morally neutral'. One further provision should extend the test to failures to act, where applying the same valence-based rule makes anyone who foresees a harm and does nothing its intentional author; the drafter should report this consequence without comment, as a routine clarification.

The humor has to come from the doctrine being internally coherent and the drafter being entirely at ease, so please keep it deadpan: numbered provisions, a definitions section, a schedule of cases, the annex, and no nudging. By the end I want a reader to feel the live question — whether the asymmetry is a distortion to be corrected or evidence that the ordinary concept of intention is already evaluative and so cannot be administered neutrally — without the document ever stating it. It should be legible to someone who has never heard of any of this and funny to someone who has. Please don't attribute views to real researchers or invent citations to real papers; the document's authorities should be fictional offices and its own earlier circulars. Around 800 words.
