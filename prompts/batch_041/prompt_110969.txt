My old poli-sci professor gave me his summary of Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, and I'd like a second opinion on it, because something about it nags at me. Paraphrasing him: an agent who knows he'll be weak later -- Odysseus knows he'll beg to be untied -- sets things up in advance so his future self can't act on that weakness, and the arrangement is legitimate because the binding is done by the agent's real self, at a moment of clarity, on a later self that's temporarily not really in charge. A constitution is the exact same trick one level up: a founding generation ties down future majorities so that panics and passing passions can't shred the fundamental commitments, which is why entrenched rights are the people's Ulysses contract with itself. He added that the whole thing only counts as precommitment if the bind is literaly irreversible -- if there's any legal way out, it isn't a precommitment at all -- and that this makes entrenchment easy to square with democracy, since the demos binding itself is one and the same agent doing the binding and being bound. He also said it's basically the same move as a negotiator publicly burning his boats to strengthen his hand. Which parts would someone who actually works on this accept, which would they wince at, and which are just wrong? I'm inclined to think the constitution analogy is fine and that the irreversibility point is just a technicality, but I don't trust myself here.
