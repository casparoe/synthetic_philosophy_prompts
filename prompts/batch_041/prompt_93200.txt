For a graduate seminar in applied ethics I've been asked to circulate a short position paper and collect written comments on it, and I'd like yours. The paper is my own, and I'm less interested in being told the topic matters than in being told where it is empirically shaky, where it is internally inconsistent, and whether its central move is genuinely new or a repackaging of something familiar. Here is the argument.

I used to think the ethics of eating animals came down to a simple comparison: how many animals, and how much suffering, does a given diet cause per unit of nutrition? Then I read around in the literature on crop cultivation and wild animal suffering and lost my footing. Three claims do the work. First, plant agriculture is not harm-free: harvesting grain kills a great many small vertebrates — mice, voles, birds, rabbits — through machinery and habitat destruction, and the death toll scales with the area cultivated. Second, this makes the beef-versus-chicken comparison less obvious than it looks. Beef is far more land- and crop-intensive per calorie than chicken, so a kilogram of beef arguably carries a larger "harvest shadow" of field-animal deaths, while a kilogram of chicken directly kills more sentient individuals per calorie but is grown on less land. The usual verdict that chicken is better because it is less resource-intensive may therefore invert once the harvest shadow is counted.

The third claim is the one I actually care about, and I suspect it is either the interesting part or the crack that breaks the paper. I want to add a term the standard accounting omits: foregone wild animals. When land is converted to agriculture, it stops supporting whatever wild population it would otherwise have carried. If the average wild animal life is net negative — full of hunger, disease, predation, and early death — then preventing those lives from being lived is a benefit rather than a cost, and it should enter the ledger with a negative sign; call this the sterilization offset. The uncomfortable result is that the most land-hungry foods might be the most morally defensible, because they suppress the most wild suffering, and that a low-footprint plant diet might fare worse than beef. I've tried to state this so that it depends only on the sign of average wild-animal welfare and not on any particular estimate of it, but I can't tell whether I've succeeded or whether I've just smuggled in a controversial premise under a technical name. I'd like your comments on three things: whether the empirical claims in the first two paragraphs are as solid as I've made them sound; whether the sterilization offset is coherent or double-counts (does it conflict with the harvest-shadow term, or are the two independent?); and whether the whole thing is actually novel or just a dressed-up version of arguments that already exist, in which case I'd rather find out now than in seminar.
