Next month I am presenting at a small interdisciplinary workshop on pain, with a sixty-minute slot of which I expect at least half to be discussion. What I need from you is a question-period prep sheet, not a critique of the argument itself.

The audience divides almost evenly, and the two halves will not press me in the same way. One half are philosophers of mind and ethicists: several work on the phenomenal character of bodily experience, a couple on the metaphysics of experiential properties, and one or two on the ethics of animal pain. They are fluent in a priori taxonomy and wary of inferences drawn from lesion cases. The other half are clinician-researchers in pain medicine who work within the sensory-discriminative/affective-motivational framework, together with two imaging people who study the insula and anterior cingulate and have little patience for distinctions they cannot operationalize.

My thesis concerns pain asymbolia, the condition in which patients report and can localize pain yet are untroubled by it, sometimes meeting a noxious stimulus with indifference or amusement. The standard gloss is that such patients have lost the unpleasantness of pain while retaining its sensory character. I argue that this gloss is wrong. I distinguish three things that can come apart in these patients: (i) the intensity and bodily location of the sensation; (ii) its felt aversiveness, in the thin sense of its being a state the subject would, other things equal, rather not be in; and (iii) what I call the demand of pain, the way an ordinary pain recruits the subject into wanting it to stop and presents the cessation as a reason to act. My claim is that the loss in asymbolia is the loss of (iii); that the empirical dispute over whether (ii) survives is unresolvable until (ii) is pried apart from (iii); that the received notion of unpleasantness is a mongrel that conflates the two and should be retired; and that the badness of pain is accordingly not a property of the sensation but a relation between the sensation and the subject's will. I close by drawing consequences for how we should weigh animal pain and for the value of relieving pain in patients who do not mind it.

Give me the objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way each half of the room will phrase them, the clinicians' in their vocabulary and the philosophers' in theirs, and ranked by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it. For each objection, tell me what to concede up front, what to hold, a two-minute reply, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Indicate which single objection, if it lands, should make me abandon part of the thesis rather than defend it. End with the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
