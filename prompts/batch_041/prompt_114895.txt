AI developers now routinely report capability and behaviour on separate axes: a model's scores on coding, mathematics or cyber-offence benchmarks are presented as facts about what it can do, while its tendency to lie, manipulate, seek power or resist shutdown is presented as a separate fact about what it is disposed to do, and power-seeking and self-preservation are red-teamed as recurring failure modes rather than accidents of one training run. What view of the relation between intelligence and motivation does this practice presuppose? Does it commit its users to the orthogonality thesis, on which competence and final goals vary independently, or only to the weaker claim that the two can be measured and reported separately, and which rival views — that training on human text instils human-like values, or that values and capabilities are entangled and develop together — does it quietly rule out? The same institutions argue that greater capability makes systems more dangerous partly because capable agents tend to converge on instrumentally useful subgoals such as power-seeking and self-preservation. Say whether that commitment sits consistently with the first, where the reporting practice is philosophically underdetermined such that several theories of the intelligence–motivation relation would license it, and what would have to change in how results are reported and acted on if the orthogonality thesis turned out to be false.
