The deliverable is a dialogue, not an essay. Two researchers, H and I, are both expert in the biology and the economics of signalling, and neither is to be a strawman. H defends the handicap principle: honest signalling is explained by cost, and more precisely by the fact that a signal's marginal cost is heavier for low-quality signallers, so that only high-quality signallers can profitably bear it; H treats cost as the general engine of reliability and regards apparently cost-free signals as either misdescribed or as limiting cases of extreme cost. I defends an index-based account: reliability can be secured without any handicap, either because the signal is physically constrained by the signaller's underlying quality, so that a low-quality signaller cannot produce it at all regardless of what it is willing to spend, or because receivers enforce honesty through repeated interaction and punishment; on this view cost is neither necessary nor sufficient, and the handicap principle mistakes a special case for the rule.

Write their conversation so that it satisfies the following.

1. Both characters argue at full strength and answer each other's actual points; they do not talk past each other, and neither is permitted a cheap victory.

2. Have them work through two contrasting cases, which they should test rather than merely mention: a display that is cheap for every signaller yet reliably honest because a low-quality signaller is physically incapable of performing it; and a display that is expensive for every signaller, including low-quality ones, and is therefore uninformative. For each case, have each character say what his account predicts and how he reconciles the case with his position.

3. Let H advance the move that any honest signalling equilibrium can be redescribed as a handicap, and let I press whether that rescues the principle or empties it of content.

4. Let each character name at least one observation that would distinguish the two accounts, and let them discuss whether such observations are available in practice.

5. Close with one point on which they genuinely agree and one disagreement they leave unresolved. No narrator is to adjudicate, and no character is to win by fiat.

The dialogue should be self-contained: it may not presuppose that the reader knows any particular paper, and it should not present either position as the obvious default.
