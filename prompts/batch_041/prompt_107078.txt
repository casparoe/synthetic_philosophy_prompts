I'm putting together a proposal for a small internal research grant — enough for a focused summer, one person, no lab and no co-authors — and I'd like you to help me turn a half-formed idea into the document. The idea concerns Kavka's toxin puzzle. Nearly everyone who writes about it treats the puzzle as a question about the rationality of forming an intention to do something one will not in fact do: you are offered a large reward for intending tonight to drink a foul but harmless toxin tomorrow, and the standard question is whether it can be rational to form that intention when you already know you will not carry it out. I have come to suspect that this framing conceals the interesting problem. The interesting problem, as I see it, is that the puzzle quietly presupposes an account of what an intention is such that it can be deliberately formed in advance, be read off reliably by whoever is paying, and yet not commit the agent to the act. Very few theories of intention are asked to do all three of those things at once, and I want to know which ones can. My provisional thought is that the puzzle is best read not as a test case for decision theory but as a stress test for the metaphysics and the norms of intention — and that the variants of the puzzle, not the original, are where the real work lies.

Turn this into a proposal of roughly 1500 to 2500 words, structured as a plan for work not yet done rather than a defense of a thesis. State the research question so that it has a determinate answer, and explain why the existing treatments leave it open. I want a method, and I want the method to be specific. In particular, I want the project to generate a small family of variants that each vary one structural feature at a time: for instance, a variant in which the payoff depends on the intention with some probability less than one; a variant in which the agent can take a pill that mechanically induces the intention, so that having it is no rational achievement at all; and a variant in which the reward is paid on the basis of a reliable external predictor of the intention rather than on the intention itself. The project should then test candidate theories of intention against the whole family — intention as a plan held to norms of consistency, intention as a commitment or self-binding device, and intention as a self-fulfilling expectation — saying for each theory which variants it handles, which it cannot, and what that pattern of success and failure would show. Include a short argument for why this approach, rather than the more usual route of adding a fourth principle to the decision-theoretic menu, is the one likely to make progress. Give me a month-by-month schedule for a summer's work, and be honest about scope: which parts are genuinely tractable for one person in three months and which would have to be cut. Finally, tell me what would count as the project failing — the result that would show my framing was wrong rather than merely incomplete. Don't worry about citations or a literature review; I'll add the references myself.
