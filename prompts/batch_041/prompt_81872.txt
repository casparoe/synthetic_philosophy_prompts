I'm in this reading group on consciousness and philosophy of mind and I somehow volunteered to kick off our session on introspection next week, which means I have to circulate a short position piece beforehand and then defend it for an hour. I'd love your comments on it before I send it around — mainly on whether the central distinction I'm leaning on actually holds up, because if it doesn't I'd rather find out now than in front of everyone.

Here's roughly what I've got. Everyone knows the experiments where people confidently explain a choice they never made — the face-picking one, where you say which of two photos you find more attractive, the experimenter quietly hands back the *other* one and asks why you chose it, and most people don't notice the switch and give you a fluent little story about the smile or the eyes. Or the ones where something is planted in you and you then "recall" reasons you already held it. My move is that these only ever knock down one kind of introspective report, and everybody treats them as knocking down introspection as a whole. So I want to split introspective reports into two jobs: reporting *what* is going on with you (this is cold, I want the cake, I'm annoyed) and reporting *why* it's going on (I'm annoyed because of that email). The debunking stuff is all about the why-job, and the why-job is causal explanation, which we already knew is a reconstruction — you don't get to watch the causes of your states any more than you get to watch the causes of your perceptions. But the what-job is different: it isn't an inference to a hidden cause, it's the state presenting itself, so "I'm annoyed" can't come apart from the annoyance the way "I'm annoyed because of that email" can come apart from the actual cause. Conclusion I want: the experiments are real but their scope is much narrower than advertised, and first-person methods in philosophy aren't nearly as dead as the debunkers claim.

So — is that split real or am I cheating? Push on it. Where does the what/why line actually break down? I already feel like implicit bias cases are a problem, since someone can sincerely report they aren't nervous around a certain group and be wrong, and that looks like a what-report rather than a why-report. Is there a better way to draw the line than what vs. why — something about the kind of error each report can make, or about what the report is *for*? And am I overreading the experiments: is there a version of the debunking argument that bites the what-reports too, and that I'm quietly ignoring? Also tell me straight if any of this is just old news dressed up, or if I'm smuggling in an assumption that introspection is "direct" in a way a materialist ought to be embarassed about. I don't need reading suggestions — just whether the argument holds, where it's weakest, and what I should shore up.
