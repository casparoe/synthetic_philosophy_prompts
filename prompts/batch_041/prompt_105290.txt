I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, preparing a seminar contribution on biological function, and I have a reasonably firm grip on the textbook contrast itself: on the etiological or selected-effects account, a trait's function is (roughly) what it was selected for, whereas on the causal-role or systemic account a function is simply the contribution a part makes to some capacity of the containing system, with no evolutionary history required. What I need is not another rehearsal of that contrast but its downstream consequences, and I want to work them out through a concrete case rather than in generalities. My partner works in a synthetic-biology lab that has built a bacterial strain carrying a metabolic cycle which exists nowhere in nature and was assembled by rational design rather than by selection; the cycle reliably keeps the cells alive under the lab's conditions, and the lab routinely says things like "the cycle's function is to regenerate NADH." The wrinkle that makes this interesting to me is that the cycle as a whole is novel, but its component enzymes were repurposed from ordinary, ancestrally selected pathways, so the parts may have functions on the etiological account even if the cycle they now compose does not. On a strict etiological view, then, the assembled cycle has no function at all — it merely does what it does — while the causal-role view has no trouble calling its contribution a function.

Please lay out the most important implications of having to choose between the two accounts, using this strain as the running example. In particular, I'd like to know what follows for (a) whether the cycle can properly be said to malfunction, and what malfunction could even mean for a designed-but-unevolved part whose components nonetheless have evolutionary pedigrees; (b) whether the lab's ordinary causal-role ascriptions are legitimate, or a category error that a careful philosopher should correct; and (c) whether the choice between the accounts should turn on anything more than which of our existing practices we are unwilling to give up. I'd also like you to say which account a working synthetic biologist is implicitly committed to, and whether that commitment is defensible, since my suspicion is that the answer is "whichever one lets them keep talking the way they already do," and I genuinely don't know whether that observation should count as a criticism or is simply how concept-choice always goes.
