Last night a friend and I argued for about three hours about whether inverse reinforcement learning can ever be a route to learning what people actually value, and we ended somewhere I think is a genuine research question rather than a rehash of the usual optimism-versus-skepticism. His case was the identifiability one: when the dynamics are held fixed, any two reward functions that differ by a potential function over states — the shaping transformation — induce exactly the same optimal behavior, so demonstrations cannot distinguish them; and once you stop assuming the dynamics are known, the set of reward-and-transition pairs consistent with the data balloons further. The standard responses (a maximum-entropy prior over reward functions, a Bayesian posterior, restricting attention to shaping-invariant formulations) all concede his point and then quietly pick a representative of the equivalence class, which is harmless if all you want is a policy but looks like sleight of hand if what you claim to be doing is recovering preferences. My counter was that this only shows we have been asking the learner to output the wrong kind of object, and that I would rather it output the ambiguity — but when he pressed me on what that would even mean formally, I had nothing. What I want to propose is a project that makes the ambiguity the object of inference rather than an obstacle to it.

The half-formed idea, which I would like you to turn into something defensible: separate two things the literature tends to run together, namely recovering a point-valued preference ranking over trajectories and reporting which further observations or interventions would discriminate among the reward functions still consistent with the data. Concretely, the learner should return, alongside whatever point estimate it produces, (i) the partial order on trajectories that every member of the consistent set agrees on, (ii) the set of unresolved comparisons, and (iii) for each unresolved comparison, an account of what query or intervention would resolve it and how much of the residual ambiguity is attributable to the demonstrator's own bounded rationality rather than to the data — since if the demonstrator is modeled as only approximately optimal, the rationality parameter and the preferences are inferred jointly and can trade off against each other, so the demonstrator's preferences and the demonstrator's competence are not separately identifiable either. I suspect this third point is the load-bearing one and that the first two are mostly bookkeeping, and I would be happy to be shown wrong about that, but the proposal should be written so that a reader can see which way the argument would have to go.
