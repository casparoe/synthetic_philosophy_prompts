I have been working through the technical literature on AI safety and keep colliding with the phrase worst-case guarantee. One camp insists that anything worth calling alignment must come with worst-case guarantees about a system's behavior; another camp replies that worst-case guarantees are unattainable and that we should settle for good behavior on average over the distribution of inputs the system will actually encounter; and a third camp mutters that the whole contrast is confused. I suspect the disagreement is at least partly conceptual rather than empirical, and I would like you to untangle it. Explain what it means, precisely, to have a worst-case guarantee about an AI system and how that differs from an average-case guarantee, and then say which of the two notions does real work in the alignment context and why.

The point I most want pressed is that a worst-case guarantee is only as meaningful as the set of possibilities the worst case is taken over, and in alignment discussions that set is usually left unstated. If the set is any environment whatsoever, including one containing an adversarial superintelligence, the worst case is catastrophic by construction and the guarantee is vacuous; if the set is the training or deployment distribution, then the worst case is really a tail bound on that distribution, and the guarantee is an average-case claim in disguise. So is there a principled middle ground, an adversary class that is neither everything nor nothing, and what would it take for a guarantee over such a class to transfer to a system in the wild? To make the discussion concrete, I have found it useful to distinguish three readings, though I am not sure the labels are standard: an adversarial worst case (worst over a specified set of environments or inputs), a distributional worst case (the tail of a known or assumed distribution), and an epistemic or Knightian worst case (worst over models of the world that the designer has not even conceived). Tell me which of these the alignment debate is actually invoking at each turn, where the slides between them happen, and whether the demand for worst-case guarantees is best understood as a demand for a particular kind of evidence rather than a particular kind of bound. I would also like the analogy to robust optimization and control theory made explicit, since I have the impression that the alignment discussion is reinventing that machinery, including its notion of an uncertainty set, without always noticing that the formal version is only as good as the uncertainty set one feeds it. Finally, if the distinction does turn out to be partly terminological, say what substantive disagreement remains once the terms are fixed.
