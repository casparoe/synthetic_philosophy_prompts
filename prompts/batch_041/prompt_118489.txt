My flatmate and I spent most of last night arguing about the Principal Principle, and I came away more confused than when we started. As I understand it, the principle says that a rational agent's credence in an event, conditional on that event's objective chance being some value x, ought itself to be x; that rationality requires us to defer to objective chance. That is the gloss I cannot make sense of, because objective chance seems to me to have to be one of two things, and the principle comes out either empty or false on both of them. If objective chance simply is the credence that a rational agent ought to have, then the principle instructs me to hold the credence I ought to hold, which is not a constraint on anything; it merely restates what it was supposed to explain. If instead objective chance is the relative frequency with which the event occurs in the long run, and that, so far as I can tell, is the only reading on which chance is not some mysterious extra ingredient in the world over and above what actually happens, then the principle looks plainly false: a fair coin's chance of landing heads is one-half even though the relative frequency of heads in any finite run of tosses need not be one-half, and my credence in heads surely should not be pinned to a frequency I may never get to observe. My flatmate kept insisting that the principle only governs credences conditional on admissible evidence, but she could not tell me what makes evidence admissible, and it sounded to me like a stipulation designed to exempt the principle from exactly the cases that would test it.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a textbook recitation. Is the Principal Principle a substantive constraint on rational belief at all, and if so, what does it actually claim and what does it rule out? More pointedly: have I got the relationship between chance and credence backwards; is deference doing something other than identifying objective chance with what a rational agent should believe? Is there a respectable understanding of objective chance on which the principle is neither trivial nor false, and does accepting that understanding commit me to chances being something more than long-run frequencies? And what work is the admissibility condition really doing; does it rescue the principle from the kinds of counterexamples I have described, or is it, as I suspect, just a way of never letting the principle be refuted? If there is a formulation that answers all of this, I want to see why it is not circular.
