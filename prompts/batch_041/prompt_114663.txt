I teach a grad seminar on philosophy of science and we just finished the Lakatos unit — the big paper in the Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge volume, hard core versus protective belt, negative and positive heuristic, progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts. One of my students pushed back in a way I havent been able to answer to my own satisfaction, and I'd like an essay that actually takes a side rather than doing the both-sides-then-shrug thing. Her objection is that Lakatos's central evaluative notion is retrospective through and through: you can only say a programme was progressive once the novel predictions have already panned out, and by then the live question — should I keep working on this, or jump ship? — has been settled by history rather than by methodology. So the whole apparatus looks like it sorts research programmes after the fact but can't guide anyone in the moment, which would make it a historiography with normative pretensions rather than a normative methodology. I know the standard reply is that rational reconstruction is addressed to the appraiser of a completed episode, not to the working scientist, and that even in real time you can ask whether a programme is heuristically progressive, meaning it keeps generating new problems instead of patching holes. I want you to press harder than that and decide whether the reply works.

Here's the twist I actually care about, and it's where I think the standard treatments get thin. Consider a thought experiment: a research programme in some branch of biology whose hard core says populations are held near equilibrium by density-dependent regulation. Over forty years the slogan stays on the letterhead and in the introductions, but every model that does real predictive work has quietly become a disturbance-driven nonequilibrium model, with the equilibrium claim retained as a limiting case nobody uses. The programme keeps generating novel predictions, and plenty of them get corroborated, so by the excess-content criterion it looks progressive. But the core is doing no work — the auxiliary belt has eaten it. Call this the hollow core case. So: does Lakatos have to call this one programme, and a progressive one? Or does abandoning the load-bearing role of the core while keeping its name amount to starting a new programme, with the continuity being sociological rather than methodological? And if he can call it progressive, does that show the progressive/degenerating distinction is tracking something other than what we want it to track — maybe something like whether the core is doing explanatory work, which his framework has no clean way to express? I'd like you to weigh the best arguments on both sides of both questions, the retrospective objection and the hollow core case, and then land somewhere. Say what you think Lakatos should say, whether that's a repair or an admission that the framework needs supplementing, and what follows for how we should teach the progressive/degenerating distinction to grad students. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just paragraphs, the way you'd write a long email. And don't bother with citations or a reading list, I know the literature; I want the argument.
