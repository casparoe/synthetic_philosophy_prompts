So my little brother's getting married in three weeks and I have to give the best man speech, and I've decided to make it way harder on myself than it needs to be.

Context: this wedding is basically two rooms stapled together. My brother met his fiancee in a perception lab — she was running it, he was a postdoc in it, that whole thing — so a good chunk of the guests are vision scientists and grad students, and the rest are our family, who are lovely and will have no idea what anyone is talking about if I go technical. I need it to land for both, and I need it to land as warm, not as a seminar.

Here's what I actually want to build it around. There's this old demo in perception, the color phi thing. You flash a dot in one spot in one color, then a moment later flash a dot somewhere else in a different color, and people report seeing one dot travel between the two spots and change color halfway across. The weird part is they report the color change happening before the second dot even showed up, which shouldn't be possible if experience just unfolds in real time. Two ways you can explain it. Either the brain delays the whole thing until it's got all the info, so by the time you consciously see dot one you've already secretly processed dot two and your experience gets assembled backwards (somebody called this the Stalinesque version, like a show trial where the verdict's decided first and the evidence is arranged to match). Or you really do experience it live, dot one as plain old dot one, no delay, and then your memory gets edited after the fact so you remember a color change that never happened in the experience itself (the Orwellian version — the records get rewritten later). And the kicker is nobody can tell which one it was, because both give the exact same report. There's no experiment that seperates them. Some people think that means the whole question is empty — there's just no fact of the matter about which one happened.

What I want is a speech that uses this for real, not as a bit of decoration. The idea I'm chasing: the story of how they met, the story I'm about to tell a room full of people, isn't a recording, it's a reconstruction — and whether we "lived it live and edited the memory later" or "had it built for us before we ever noticed" might not have an answer, and maybe that's not a tragedy, maybe that's just what memory and love are. But it has to be funny and warm and it absolutely cannot turn into a lecture. The scientists will wince if I get the science wrong or oversell it (I can already hear one of them telling me phi is more complicated than that), and my mom's side will tune out if it sounds like a journal club. Five minutes, ends on an actual toast.

Also please don't make the science wrong in a way a perception person would flinch at, and keep the personal details generic — you can invent small neutral stuff (the lab, a conference, a broken centrifuge) but nothing that reads like a real embarrassing story about real people.
