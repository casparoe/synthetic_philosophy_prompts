Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about moral trade — the idea that two people with different moral priorities can both come out ahead if each does something that advances the other's priorities. I ended up defending a conclusion I stated badly, and I'd like you to make the case for it properly: that moral trade is self-limiting, in that its gains shrink the more legible and the more scalable the practice becomes. Please press hard on three things. First, why trading conditional commitments differs in kind from trading one-off actions. Second, whether making commitments publicly observable gives each party an incentive to cultivate moral positions that are expensive for others to satisfy, so that the practice rewards escalation rather than exchange. Third, whether it can be stable only while it remains bilateral and opaque, and what that would imply about the value of the mutual gains it is supposed to deliver. I am not asking about legal enforceability or about how often such trades occur in practice; I want the conceptual argument about whether this mechanism can be a general and stable source of mutual benefit.
