I run grantmaking at a small foundation — about four million dollars a year, two program officers (myself and one colleague, neither of us with any philosophy training), a board of seven that signs off twice a year, and a published rationale for every grant we make. Two years ago the board adopted, without quite intending to, a position that one trustee calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and I have been asked to turn it into something we can actually run on the forty to sixty proposals we see each cycle. As the board states it: there is some severity line such that suffering above it has absolute priority, so that no quantity of pleasure, flourishing, or life satisfaction bought with the same money can justify leaving it in place; below the line, pain and pleasure are weighed against each other in the ordinary way, so a dollar is a dollar and we may buy either. The board has never said whether the priority is a rule about the next dollar or about the portfolio as a whole, and I suspect no procedure can avoid forcing them to pick. I need a decision procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which steps are gates that can stop a proposal with a no, and which steps must end in a referral to the board because the question is one of values rather than facts — where exactly the line falls, or whether two things are comparable at all. It has to be runnable in about two hours per proposal by two people without training in the theory, it has to yield a rationale we would be willing to publish, and it has to be candid about the places where it will misfire or be gamed by applicants who learn the criteria. I do not need a literature review or citations; assume I know the standard arguments and want the operational version of them.

Then run the procedure on three proposals from last cycle: (1) a proven but expensive therapy and peer-support program for people with severe, long-standing depression in two provinces, reaching roughly three hundred people at a cost that would consume our entire community portfolio for a year; (2) a cheap and well-evidenced social-prescribing scheme — choirs, walking groups, communal meals — that reliably lifts mood and life satisfaction for several thousand isolated older adults but has no measurable effect on clinical depression; and (3) a program that relieves severe chronic pain for a small group only by putting participants through a physically unpleasant and demoralizing regimen, that is, it removes above-the-line suffering at the cost of imposing a substantial quantity of below-the-line suffering. I will tell you plainly that I am attached to (2) and expect the procedure to tell me to cut it; if instead the procedure lets it through, I want to see precisely which step did that, and whether the board would recognize the move as theirs.

Give me the procedure as a table with the columns Step number, Question to ask, What counts as a sufficient answer, If the answer is no or unclear, and Who decides, on what basis — and use the last column to flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as measurement. Keep the prose explanation of each step to two or three sentences, and say what the procedure cannot do. Then give me a second table with the columns Proposal, Where the procedure lands, The step that decides it, and What the procedure cannot settle here. Close with no more than five sentences on the failure modes I should watch for.
