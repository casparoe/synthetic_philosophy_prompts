I am a novelist, and I have reached the scene in my manuscript where a character — a woman who has spent twenty years writing public apologies for corporations and who now hears a confession inside every subordinate clause — is required to tell a joke at a dinner party. I want that joke to be, in its bones, a piece of philosophy of language: something that turns on the machinery of speaker meaning rather than on a philosopher's name.

The machinery I have in mind is Grice's. What interests me is the gap between what a sentence says and what a speaker means by it, and the difference between quietly violating a conversational maxim, which misleads, and openly flouting one, which invites the hearer to draw an inference the speaker never quite stated. I would like the joke to work by cancellation: the setup should make some implicature feel practically unavoidable, and the punchline should withdraw it in a way that leaves the hearer having inferred something the speaker was never committed to. Somewhere in the joke, the reflexive condition on meaning — that a speaker means something only if she intends the hearer to be affected by recognizing that very intention — should be doing real work rather than merely being gestured at.

Please make it speakable aloud in under a minute; it should amuse someone who has never heard of Grice, while the second layer should delight someone who has. It must not lean on the word implicature, on a definition, or on the mere fact of being a joke about language for its laugh. Give me the joke, and then a short paragraph identifying which maxim is flouted or violated and exactly where the cancellation lands.
