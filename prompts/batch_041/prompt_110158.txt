I'm a science journalist at work on a long feature about the moral status of insects, and I keep running into the scale argument: the claim that because insects are so staggeringly numerous, even a very small probability that they are sentient could give them a moral weight that dwarfs most things we care about. I've been invited to run a session at a summer institute for graduate students in philosophy, biology, and public policy -- about twenty people, seventy-five minutes -- and I want them to feel the force of that argument in their bones rather than just nod along to the premises. No lecture up front. I want them to walk into the structure of the problem themselves, make choices, and only then see where the logic has taken them.

You're an expert in applied ethics and in designing simulation games for philosophy classrooms, so I'd like you to build me a runnable activity. Begin by brainstorming three possible mechanics that could make the scale argument land -- think about how to represent a tiny probability, an astronomical number, and the tension between intuitive caution and expected value maximization -- then choose the one you think is most robust and develop it in full. I'm imagining something like a portfolio allocation game with a random resolution of the uncertainty (participants commit to a credence, allocate tokens across interventions, and then a draw decides which interventions pay off), but I'm open to other designs. Give me the setting, the materials, the rules, a payoff scheme that doesn't require real money, and the exact sequence of play. The activity should force participants to commit to a credence in insect sentience and then live with the consequences of that credence in a way that makes the expected value calculation feel unavoidable; the logic of the scale argument should emerge from the structure of the game, not from a prior commitment to utilitarianism. Include a round that forces them to distinguish the probability of sentience from the probability of moral status, and to confront the possibility that moral status comes in degrees.

Then give me the debrief: map the moments in the activity onto the parts of the scale argument -- the small probability, the enormous number, the expected value, the fanaticism objection, the problem of moral uncertainty. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (where participants will get confused, game the system, or reject the framing) and how to handle that. Finally, be candid about which feature of the scale argument the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in discussion. Avoid leaning on standard trolley-problem framings or on citations to the literature; I want the intuition to be generated from the inside. Think carefully and take your time.
