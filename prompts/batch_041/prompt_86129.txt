I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy traning, but a guy on my team who's deep into longtermism lent me Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife (the 2013 book that came out of his lectures at Berkeley, I think) and I've been annoying everyone I know about it ever since. The bit I can't get past is his claim that the value of what we do now depends on there being people after us — his "collective afterlife" — and that if we found out humanity was going to end soon we wouldn't just be sad, we'd stop seeing the point of the things we care about. I want to use this in an argument I'm having with a friend about why extinction would be bad beyond the deaths it involves, so I need to know how much of it you actually buy. Please don't just summarize the book back at me, I've read it twice.

Give me your credence in this: "The value of our current projects depends on humanity surviving us — in a scenario where we knew humanity would end soon, our reasons for pursuing those projects would be undermined, not just our motivation." Number between 0 and 1, then decompose it. Terms I'd want to see: (1) the plain psychological prediction — would we actually be undermined, and how far can we trust an armchair thought experiment about a situation nobody has ever been in; (2) whether that prediction, even if it's right, tells us anything about our reasons rather than just about how we're wired; (3) whether the dependence is really about humanity as a whole or whether the same effect shows up for smaller continuations — my family, my city, my field, the codebase I maintain — which would make the thesis way less dramatic; (4) how much his conclusion even needs his conjecture, since it seems to me you could think extinction is a catastrophe on other grounds entirely (future people's interests, all the value that never gets made) and not care about the conjecture at all. A number and one line of justification for each, then how they combine, then which term you're least confident in and what argument or evidence would move it most.

Two variants I keep going back and forth on — tell me if your number goes up, down, or stays the same: (a) humanity survives fine but has zero continuity with us, no memory, no shared anything, they don't even know we existed; (b) the end is 500 years off and everyone knows it from birth, rather than a month away. My gut says (a) quietly kills the whole argument and (b) barely dents it, but I've been wrong about pretty much everthing else in this book.
