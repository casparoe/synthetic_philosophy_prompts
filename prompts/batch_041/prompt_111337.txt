I am a novelist, roughly two-thirds of the way through a book set in an invented free city called Ostrel, and I have written myself into a corner that is more philosophical than it is narrative. The city's charter contains a provision my characters call the Long Table: no measure depriving a citizen of property or liberty may take effect until it has lain a year and a day on a particular oak table in the Bureau of Records, untouched by anyone with the authority to act on it. Nobody now living knows why the interval is a year and a day, or why the table. The founding rationale was written down once and sealed, and the charter permits exactly one officer of the Bureau to break the seal, on the condition that the officer who does so forfeits, for life, the right to vote or speak in the assembly. Ostrel has therefore arranged — by design, by accident, or by the design of someone who wanted it to look like an accident — that the only person who could ever explain the rule is permanently barred from arguing for it. My two principal characters are Doren, a young administrator who must clear a fever-ridden district before the autumn rains and finds the delay indistinguishable from a death sentence, and Perrine, a clerk who has kept the table for forty years and whose entire defence of the provision amounts to the fact that the city has always done it this way — a defence she is intelligent enough to be ashamed of. I want a scene, or a whole story, in which that shame is the engine rather than the joke.

What I am circling, and cannot quite name, is that there seem to be at least three quite different kinds of rule whose reasons are unavailable. There are rules whose reasons were simply lost, the ordinary sediment of time. There are rules whose reasons were destroyed — deliberately, by people who wanted the rule gone and could not get it repealed, so they burned the justification instead and left the thing standing without its legs. And then there are what I have been calling trapdoor rules, which function only so long as their point stays unstated: a waiting period that works because no one plans around it, a rotation that restrains people only because they do not realise that rotation is its purpose, an interval that does its work precisely by being experienced as pointless. The argument usually credited to Chesterton — that no one should pull down a gate until they know why it was put up — assumes the reason is recoverable in principle by someone patient enough to look. I want to dramatise the case where it is not recoverable, and where the two obvious responses both fail. The conservative cannot state the reason, because stating it would dissolve the mechanism, and so is reduced to defending a rule on grounds she knows are inadequate; the reformer cannot refute a reason that no one is permitted to voice, and so is forced to act on the absence of evidence, which is exactly what the rule was built to make decisive. I also want the sealed file to contain something that cuts both ways: a fragment suggesting that the rationale was burned not in the deep past but two generations ago, by a previous reformer who tried to abolish the Long Table and failed. That would mean the rule's apparent antiquity is partly a forgery of its own history — and also that it has already survived one intelligent attempt to destroy it, which is not nothing.

So: please take the part of an experienced novelist with a serious, unshowy interest in political theory — the kind of writer who is patient with institutions and allergic to speeches — and write me the story, somewhere between two thousand and three hundred words, in the third person, limited to Doren. I want Perrine's inability to give a reason to be a genuine intellectual predicament and not a symptom of dimness, and I want at least one moment where the sealed file is either opened or refused, with real cost either way. No character may name Burke or Chesterton, quote anyone, or deliver a lecture on the wisdom of ancestors; whatever the story believes about inherited arrangements has to live in the action and the objects. Do not let Doren be obviously right or Perrine obviously wrong, do not resolve which of the three kinds of rule the Long Table actually is, and end with a decision genuinely taken — the table cleared, or the seal broken, or something worse — while leaving the reader unable to certify that the decision was correct. Think it through carefully before you begin: sketch the sequence of scenes, decide what the fragment in the file says and what it leaves out, and work out how Doren's fever district and the table's calendar collide. Then give me only the story, in continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no preface and no afterword.
