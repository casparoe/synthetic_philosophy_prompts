Next month I'm giving a paper at a small workshop on the history of sexual ethics and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense — the objections I'll actually get, from the people who'll actually be in the room. My thesis is that Bentham's case against punishing sex (the essay he wrote in the 1780s and never published in his lifetime, plus the sexual-morality material that was meant for the Not Paul, but Jesus volumes and only saw print in the last decade or so) is routinely read as an early draft of a consent-based harm principle, and that this reading is wrong in an interesting way. What he actually does, I argue, is three things: he attacks the principle of asceticism as a legitimate ground of legislation; he dissolves the unnaturalness objection by showing it either collapses into the disgust of the offended party or into a theological premise he's already thrown out; and he defends decriminalization on the ground that the pleasures and pains in question are real while the alleged harms are either nonexistent or manufactured by the law itself. The payoff is that his position is more radical than the harm-principle reading: it doesn't need consent as a master concept, and it can in principle license things most of us would still criminalize. The cost is that he looks shaky exactly where you'd expect — cruelty to animals in the bestiality discussion, which he dispatches in a couple of sentences, and whatever residual business he thinks the law still has with public indecency.

The room is: three or four Bentham Project people who've read the manuscripts and will care a lot about chronology and about which text I'm attributing what to; a criminal-law theorist who thinks the whole thing is just Mill avant la lettre and will want to know what work my reading does that the standard one doesn't; a historian of sexuality who is allergic to turning Bentham into a gay-rights hero and will suspect me of Whiggish recuperation; one Kantian who will say I've begged the question by treating persons as loci of pleasure and pain; and probably a grad student who works on animal ethics and will pounce on the bestiality bit. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come and in each person's own vocabulary, but ordered by how much damage they do to my argument rather than by how likely they are, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me what to concede up front so the rest of the talk doesn't get bogged down. I've been sitting on this draft for a while and its definately not clean, so be blunt about which parts of my framing are load-bearing and which I could give away for free. Throughout, separate what's actually settled among people who work on Bentham from what's genuinely contested — I don't want a prep sheet that serves me one side of a live scholarly disagreement as if it were the consensus, especially on the chronology and on how continuous the sexual-morality material is with the published utilitarian corpus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single biggest vulnerability and the single best line of defense — then the list. No preamble, don't restate my question.
