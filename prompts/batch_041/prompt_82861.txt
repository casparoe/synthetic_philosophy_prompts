I manage a small print shop and have never taken a course in philosophy or computer science, but for two years now I have been reading obsessively about the project of building ethical reasoning into machines, and I would like your help writing a parody.

What I am after is a product page of the sort found on a business software marketplace, advertising a module that companies license when they need their autonomous systems to act ethically. About 650 words. It should contain a short marketing description, a specification table with plausible numbers, two or three testimonials from invented organizations (a hospital network, a city transit authority, a package delivery company — all fictional), and a "known limitations" section. The voice should be the voice of enterprise software marketing: cheerful, precise, entirely deadpan. Nothing in the piece should wink at the reader or let on that it is a joke.

The target is not any particular company. It is the idea that ethics is a component you can license, install, and configure — that a moral question, once properly engineered, becomes a setting. I am not interested in robots turning evil, and I am not interested in the trolley problem as a punchline; that joke has been made.

Three things I have noticed in my reading, which I think are the real substance, and which I would like the humor to grow out of:

- The interesting decision in such a system is never the dilemma itself. It is the rule that settles what happens when two of the system's commitments collide, and that rule is generally a number that somebody picked. I have seen this called a constraint layer or a conflict-resolution policy; I am probably using the terms loosely, but the point seems right to me.
- The human overseer who is supposed to be the safeguard typically has well under a second to review the machine's recommendation and approves it almost every time. The oversight exists on paper, and the paper is doing the work.
- The explanation a system offers for a decision is frequently produced after the fact by a separate part of the system, for the benefit of whoever audits it. It need not be the reason the machine acted.

Build the piece around those three, and add anything else you judge genuinely load-bearing rather than decorative — the difficulty of ranking values that share no common currency, whose values actually get installed and who answers for them when they are installed badly, what a system picks up from watching people decide. I would rather the satire be fair than extreme: exaggerate what is really there instead of inventing absurdities, so that someone who works on this problem laughs because it is recognizable. A reader who knows nothing about the field should still be able to follow it.

No references or citations are needed, and please avoid naming real companies, products, or people.
