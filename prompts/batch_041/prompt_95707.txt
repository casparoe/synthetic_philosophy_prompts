I am presenting a paper to a hospital clinical-ethics committee whose members fall into two camps: clinicians who treat advance directives as unproblematic extensions of informed consent, and disability-rights advocates who suspect them of letting a competent person's earlier preferences tyrannize the actual preferences of the now-incompetent patient. My thesis is meant to be awkward for both: an advance directive binds not because the earlier self has authority over the later one, but because it is a public precommitment that constitutes rather than merely reports the later self's practical identity; I distinguish the directive's occurrent authority, which it lacks, from its constitutional authority, which it has, and I argue that what both camps are tacitly applying is a counterfactual ratification test -- would the later self, confronted with the directive and with its own present values, endorse being bound by it? Prepare me for the question period. Order the objections by how much damage they would do, phrase each in the vocabulary of the camp that would raise it, give a two-minute reply to each with a candid note on where the reply is soft, tell me what to concede up front rather than defend, and include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises.
