I am a staff software engineer — twelve years on backend and distributed systems, most recently on release engineering and build infrastructure, and I maintain a couple of open-source libraries — with no formal training in philosophy, geology, or history. I have been reading about the dispute over whether the Anthropocene should become a formal unit on the geological time scale, and I keep suspecting that it is really a versioning and governance argument rather than a discovery about rocks. I would like you to test that suspicion by restating the whole debate in the vocabulary I actually think in: version control, release engineering, semantic versioning, changelogs, telemetry and observability, and maintainer governance in a large open-source project.

Here is the domain material, so that you can do the restatement without guessing at facts. The geological time scale is maintained by an international body of stratigraphers, and adding a unit to it requires a formal proposal and a vote. A boundary between units is normally pinned to a specific physical reference point — an agreed layer in a particular rock or sediment exposure — rather than to a date; the point is physically marked on site, and the mark itself is only a placeholder for the agreement. These units are anchored at their base; the top of a unit is simply wherever the base of the next one falls, and the youngest unit has no top at all. Boundaries far enough back in time cannot be pinned this way, so they are defined by a fixed date instead. To be accepted, a marker has to be traceable worldwide and independent of the local conditions under which the sediment happened to be laid down. The recent proposal was for a new epoch beginning in the mid-twentieth century, anchored to a plutonium fallout layer from atmospheric nuclear tests preserved in the sediments of a lake in Canada; it was voted down, and the rejection was then confirmed by the parent bodies. Rival proposals put the start much earlier, at the Industrial Revolution or at the beginning of agriculture. Meanwhile the term itself is in wide informal use, which is a different matter from the formal unit.

Please do three things.

First, the literal translation. Map the components one at a time, as faithfully and concretely as you can: the time unit; the golden spike; the practice of pinning a boundary to a physical marker rather than to a date; anchoring units at their base with no top; the requirements of worldwide traceability and independence from local conditions; the ratifying body and its vote; and the gap between informal use of the term and the ratified unit. Where a component has no honest counterpart in my vocabulary, say so rather than inventing one.

Second, the positions. Restate the main positions in my terms: that the Anthropocene is a real change already recorded in the rocks, so the task is only to recognize and date it; that the unit is something we choose to declare, with political and pragmatic stakes the rocks do not settle; that the change is real but the boundary belongs much earlier, at agriculture or industrialization; and that an ongoing, human-driven process is not the kind of thing the time scale was built to name, so the proposal was a category error. For each, tell me whether it becomes the claim that the commit is already in the history and we are only adding the tag, or that the tag is a maintainer decision the history does not settle, or that we are trying to cut a release for a build that has not finished.

Third, the residue report, which is the part I care most about. Which parts of the original debate survive the translation intact, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? Specifically: what do geologists mean by real that I cannot reproduce with the recorded-versus-decided distinction? Does it matter that the record is incomplete and that time does not arrive in discrete commits, when in my world a commit either exists or it does not? Is there something in the requirement that a boundary be findable in the field by strangers, and not merely agreed upon, that has no analogue in a versioning convention? And does the residue indicate that my vocabulary is missing something, or that the original debate is less substantive than it looks?

End with a verdict: after the translation, is my suspicion — that this is a naming and governance dispute dressed up as a discovery — correct, or is the residue precisely where the substance lives? Keep the translation honest; do not smuggle in my framework's assumptions and present them as fidelity. I would rather have a mapping that breaks visibly than one that flatters my vocabulary.
