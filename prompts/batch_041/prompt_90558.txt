Here's the bit I mean, from Fabre's old insect books — I'm paraphrasing from memory of the translations, so dont hold me to the wording: the wasp hunts a cricket, paralyzes it with stings, drags it to her burrow, and then, before going in, leaves the cricket on the threshold and goes inside alone, apparently to check that all is in order. Only after she comes back out does she haul the prey in. Now move the cricket a few inches while she's inside. She comes out, finds the cricket not where she left it, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in to inspect again. Move it again, and she does the whole thing again. Fabre, or the tradition that reports him, says she'll do this forty times and never once just carry the cricket straight in, even though the burrow was already checked on the first trip.

What I want to understand is what exactly is being claimed when people use this as the poster child for behavior that's mechanical — sphexish, in Dennett's word. A few things confuse me. First, is the rigidity supposed to be a fact about the wasp or a fact about the description we give of the wasp? She's doing something quite intricate and appropriate for most of the sequence; the rigidity only shows up against an intervention that never happens in her world. So is mechanical here an empirical claim, a contrastive one, or just what it looks like when a fixed routine meets a world that isn't fixed the way the routine expects? Second, the usual moral drawn is about free will — if a creature can be wound up like this, maybe our own sense of doing otherwise is a story we tell ourselves. But I don't see how one case of a rigid routine bears on that. Being unfree and being predictable aren't obviously the same thing, and even a thoroughly deterministic system can be enormously flexible in how it reaches its ends. Is the real contrast supposed to be flexible versus rigid, or something else — say, a difference between a creature that can represent its own routine and revise it and one that can't? Third, and this is the one that actually keeps me up, I've half-remembered for years that the forty-times version is a philosopher's tidying-up of a messier observation, and that the real animal sometimes just carries the cricket in. I don't need a bibliography, I just want to know how much of the philosophical work the anecdote is doing survives if the tidy version is a fable. If the wasp is only sometimes sphexish, does sphexish name a real category of behavior at all, or a threshold we drew for our own purposes?
