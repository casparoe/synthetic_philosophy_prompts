I teach a 90-minute upper-level ethics seminar with about 16 students, and I want them to run into the force of Taurek's claim that the numbers don't count when lives are at stake, rather than hear it summarized. Design a two-stage activity. In the first stage each student acts as a lone rescue coordinator and answers a fixed list of forced choices — save the larger group, save the single person, or draw lots — where the size of the larger group runs from two up to a million, the single person is sometimes an identified individual described in concrete detail and sometimes a bare statistic, and one case pits a thousand people who would each lose a hand against one person who would die. In the second stage the students must compress their answers into a single procedure they would be willing to commit to before learning which of the cases they will face, and the facilitator arranges the cases into a chain that makes the cost of consistency visible: either the numbers always dominate, so the identity and situation of the one never matter, or the numbers never dominate, so drawing lots between one life and a million is no worse than a sure rescue of the million. Give me the case list, the rules for the commitment stage, the tally-and-chain reveal, and a debrief that maps what happened onto Taurek's argument that the many are not a single someone with a claim of its own, and onto the reply that aggregation is simply each person's separate claim counted once. Say where it will predictably misfire — I expect students to smuggle in "of course the many matter more" without noticing they have done so — and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the underlying dispute over whether lives are comparable at all, and the formal apparatus of social choice, which I will have to supply myself.
