- There's a safety paradigm where you treat the model you're deploying as a potential adversary rather than as something you've made trustworthy. Call it control. The setup: a strong model U you don't trust (it might be scheming) and a weak model T you do trust, plus a small budget of trusted human labour.
- Typical protocols: T rates the suspiciousness of U's output and, if it's over a threshold, T rewrites the output itself; or a second instance of U is asked to check the first instance's output for sabotage, with anti-collusion tricks like hiding whose output it is.
- Protocols get evaluated in a red-team/blue-team game: the red team plays U, knows the protocol in advance, and is trying to slip a sabotaged output past it while still producing outputs that look useful. Sabotage is operationalised as something like a logical error that passes the tests.
- What the paradigm claims for itself is that it doesn't assume U is aligned — only that U might not be.

Now the question. The crux I want is: where does the "safety" in a control protocol actually come from, and what does it quietly presuppose? A student who just says "control doesn't assume alignment, alignment does, control is better for near-term deployment" should be sitting at a C. To get an A they need to get into the weeds: that the guarantee is conditional on the red team's capability budget rather than on anything about the real model's motives; that what the evaluation yields is a capability claim, not a probability of catastrophe; that "safe" and "useless" aren't in tension, so a protocol can be perfectly safe simply by ignoring U (defer everything to T), which means control only earns its keep on the usefulness/safety frontier; that trust hasn't been eliminated, only relocated — the protocol bottoms out in trusting T and the humans, so "control needs no trust" is false; and that the framing is a design stance (assume the worst) rather than a belief about U's actual alignment.
