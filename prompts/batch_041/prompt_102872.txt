I've been using a Bayesian updating spreadsheet at work to decide whether to keep pushing a drug candidate forward, and it's gotten personal because my bonus is tied to the call. The problem is that my evidence is never a clean yes/no—the assay results come back as "strong positive," "weak positive," or "no signal," and I'm often not certain which one I actually saw. A friend of mine who did a philosophy minor and reads alot of LessWrong told me I need Jeffrey conditionalization. Here's how he summed it up:

"Jeffrey conditionalization is the fix for exactly this. Ordinary Bayes says new P(A) = P(A|E), but that only works if you're certain E happened. Jeffrey's rule says: when your evidence is uncertain, you condition on the proposition that you recieved the evidence, and you weight it by your new probability of the evidence. So if you're 70% sure you saw a strong positive and 30% sure you saw no signal, your new credence in the drug working is 0.7 times your old credence given strong positive plus 0.3 times your old credence given no signal. That's it—it's just strict conditionalization with a weighted average. The technical name is probability kinematics. The one real constraint is that you keep your old conditional probabilities P(A|E_i) fixed; that's the rigidity condition. It tells you how to set those weights from the evidence itself, so you don't have to guess them. And it guarantees you'll end up with the same credence you'd have if you'd learned the true evidence and then averaged over the possibilities, so it's the unique rational response to uncertain evidence. It also solves the old evidence problem, because now you can have uncertain evidence about a theory."

I can't tell how much of this is right. Some of it sounds like standard Bayesian stuff, but the bit about it being "just" conditioning on the evidence, and the bit about it telling me how to set the weights, and the old evidence thing all feel like they might be doing something sneaky. Also I don't know if "rigidity" is the real constraint or just a name he made up.

Go sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher of probability would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong. I don't want a menu of positions—I want your definite verdict on each claim, and then a bottom line: should I actually use Jeffrey's rule in my spreadsheet, and if so, what exactly do I have to keep fixed and what do I still have to decide myself? If you think I should use something else, say what and why. Be committed; I'm about to make a real call on this.
