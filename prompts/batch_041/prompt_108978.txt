Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about self-amending rules, and I would like a considered answer to the question we could not settle.

We started from Nomic, the game Peter Suber designed in 1982. Its central move is to propose and vote on amendments to the rules; the rulebook sorts rules into mutable and immutable ones, and an immutable rule can be altered only after it has first been converted into a mutable rule. Nothing is exempt — the victory condition, the order of play, even the rule requiring players to obey the rules are all amendable. The same shape turns up well outside games. Constitutions and organizational bylaws routinely contain the procedure for their own amendment; some systems declare certain provisions unamendable, and Bangladesh's constitution is unusual in writing that entrenchment into its text, while the Supreme Court of India has held, since Kesavananda Bharati in 1973, that a constitutional amendment can be struck down for destroying the constitution's basic structure even when the amendment procedure was followed exactly.

What does this family of practices presuppose? Here is the inconsistency that stopped us. The practice treats conformity with the amendment procedure as the test of a rule's validity, yet it also accepts as valid an amendment that changes that very procedure, without appeal to any higher warrant; and it sometimes treats a procedurally flawless amendment as invalid all the same. Those look like three commitments pulling against one another.

So: what theory of legal validity does the practice presuppose — validity as derivation from a higher rule, with the chain necessarily ending in something that is not itself validated? If so, what is the terminus, and does the practice commit itself to any particular answer (a presupposed basic norm, the convergent practice of officials, an act of constituent power, a brute decision), or is it silent on the point? Second, does it presuppose that the amendment rule stands both inside and outside the system it governs — a rule among the rules and also the condition of the system's identity over time? If so, what view of institutional identity does that require: that a legal order survives the wholesale replacement of its rules, or that it is constituted anew whenever the rule of change changes? Third, on entrenchment: does the practice presuppose that no rule is ever genuinely unamendable, only expensive to amend, as the two-step mechanism suggests, or that some provisions really are beyond amendment, as the basic-structure cases suggest? Are those two commitments inconsistent, or do they answer different questions — one about what the rules permit, the other about what a court will recognize?

Be explicit, finally, about how much of the practice is strictly presupposed as against merely fitting well with a theory, and about where the practice is underdetermined, so that several rival theories of validity and identity would license everything we observe. And tell me what would have to change if the presupposition were replaced — if, say, validity were taken to be purely a matter of what officials currently accept, or purely a matter of derivation from a founding act: would entrenchment clauses, judicial review of amendments, or the very possibility of a legal revolution survive?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
