I work on the analytical team of a mid-sized public-policy institute; we publish roughly a dozen reports a year for civil servants and funders, and our writers keep putting generational labels into them — 'Gen Z is more risk-averse', 'Millennials have given up on institutions' — on the strength of differences in a single cross-sectional survey. I want a written procedure, an ordered set of tests with a verdict attached to each, that an analyst with no training in demography or social ontology can run before a cohort label is allowed into a report. Two things have to stay live throughout. The first is that age, period and birth cohort are linearly related, so no dataset separates a cohort effect from a period effect except by an assumption the analyst must state out loud, and a one-off shock or a change in survey mode will present itself as a generational difference. The second is the prior question of whether a cohort is a kind at all, or a label that those under it begin to live up to, so that the classification helps produce the pattern it is used to explain. The procedure should say what counts as sufficient evidence at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because what is left is a question about values or about what the report is for. Then run it on three cases: a single survey wave showing 18–24s more anxious than 45–54s; a claim that a cohort has been made permanently frugal by entering the workforce during a recession; and a commissioning editor who wants the report to say something about Gen Z because the funder expects it. At least one of the three should come out against the label. Be explicit about which steps are judgement dressed up as method, and about what the procedure cannot settle.
