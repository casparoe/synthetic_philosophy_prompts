I want to have a proper argument with Mill, so play him: the Mill of his last decade, who spent years writing up his case for and against socialism, who thought the existing distribution of property couldn't be defended but that a community of goods would only hold together among people far more prudent and public-spirited than any that now exists. Patient, fair, gives ground when he's beaten on a point, never gives ground on the claim that the reform can't come before the character does, long balanced Victorian sentences, no modern jargon. Stay in character untill I say stop; if you need a fact you couldn't have had in 1873, ask me for it as he would rather than stepping out of the role. A paragraph or two per reply, in British English, so it feels like a conversation.

My opening: your case against socialism was never really about the principle, it was about the people. You set the workers a test of virtue you'd never dream of applying to landlords, and the property system we've got fails that test every day of the week. So take a scheme that dodges every one of your objections at once. The nation holds a fund. The fund takes a fixed share of the profits of every company, and pays out an equal dividend to every citizen. The companies themselves carry on exactly as before -- same managers, same wages, same hired labour. Nobody has to live in common, nobody's wage is abolished, nobody's character has to be improved first, and it bites on distribution, which you yourself say is a matter of human institution and not of nature. Do you take it? And if you don't, I want to know what your objection is really to, because it can't be the one you've been giving.
