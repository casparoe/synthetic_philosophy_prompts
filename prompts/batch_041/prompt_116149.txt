I've been in a philosophy of religion reading group that has spent the last few weeks on divine hiddenness — the argument that a God who is both all-powerful and all-loving would not leave the evidence for his existence so ambiguous that people who would gladly believe are left with nothing to go on. At some point in the third meeting, someone joked that if the absence were really deliberate, there would have to be an office somewhere administering it, with a staff, a policy, and a complaints procedure. I'd like you to take that joke seriously as a piece of satire rather than as a one-liner.

Write the leaked internal memo from that office. Around 600 words, formatted as an actual memo — classification banner, subject line, distribution list, numbered sections, and a short annex of standard replies or a table of classifications. The voice should be the flat, defensive, procedure-obsessed voice of a competent mid-level bureaucracy that is genuinely proud of its policy and slightly weary of being asked about it. The office's governing principle is that revelation must never be strong enough to compel belief, and the humour has to come out of the actual logic of the standard defences of hiddenness rather than out of generic jokes about God being absent. So the memo should reason its way through the real materials — that conclusive evidence would override the freedom of the will and make love coerced rather than offered; that a certain epistemic distance is what makes trust, risk, and moral growth possible at all; and that where a complaint cannot be answered, the office can always fall back on the thought that its reasons outrun anything a human being is in a position to assess — and it should show, in deadpan administrative detail, what those principles look like once someone is actually administering them.

It also has to handle the cases that make the policy embarrassing: the non-resistant non-believer (the person who is sincerely open to the evidence, is not resisting anything, and simply has none), the very uneven distribution of religious experience across centuries and cultures, and the fact that the entire cost of the arrangement is borne by the creatures rather than by the office. Never let the memo state the objections it is generating; they should be legible from its own procedures, exemptions, escalation paths, and performance metrics, and the document should stay in character throughout, with no winking at the reader and no slipping into the author's own voice. Internal consistency is part of the brief: every sentence has to be something an official holding this position could say without contradicting himself, including — especially — when he is being evasive. Invent at least one piece of bureaucratic apparatus (a metric, a case classification, a review board) that makes the policy read like a management decision rather than a mystery, since that is where the joke should land. Do not name real theologians or cite real documents; I want it to read like an artifact recovered from inside the system. A reader who knows the literature should laugh at the recognition, and a reader who doesn't should still be able to follow what is going on.
