For several years I have been caught in a recurring argument about the norm of assertion, and it has the strange property of never advancing. The positions are familiar. On the knowledge account, one may assert only what one knows. On the truth account, any assertion that is in fact true is in order. On the belief account, it is enough that the speaker believe what they say. A family of justification or warrant accounts demand only that the speaker be epistemically entitled to the content. Each is defended by the same method: a case is described in which an assertion strikes us as improper although the proposed condition is met, or as proper although it is not, and the counterexample is absorbed by the other side through a refinement — a restriction to primary propriety, a distinction between being blameless and being correct, an appeal to conversational context, or a redescription of the case that explains the intuition away instead of accommodating it. No account has been refuted, none has won, and the case-intuitions that are supposed to be the tribunal keep being reinterpreted until they no longer discriminate.

I want a diagnosis rather than another round of cases. My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but at least three conducted under a single heading, and that the disputants slide between them without noticing. The first is constitutive: what rule, if any, defines the act of assertion, so that a move failing it is not a bad assertion but not an assertion at all? The second is evaluative: under what conditions is an assertion correct or warranted or criticizable, where this concerns the speaker's epistemic position rather than the identity of the speech act? The third is social or conventional: what licenses a hearer to rely on an assertion, and what does a speaker commit to in making one? I suspect the same example yields different verdicts under these three questions, and that the appearance of a single stalled debate is produced by the silent oscillation between them.

Is that hypothesis right, or is the impasse more ordinary — a genuine conflict of bedrock intuitions about cases, or a hidden empirical question about what speakers actually do when they assert? To test the hypothesis, take a case in which a speaker confidently but falsely believes something on the basis of excellent evidence, and a second case in which an agent's assertions are produced by a reliable mechanism the agent cannot introspect; the accounts will disagree about both. Then tell me whether my three-way distinction actually dissolves the disagreement or merely relabels it, and whether the two-tier view on which the knowledge norm governs the speaker's commitment while the truth norm governs the assertion's correctness is a real reconciliation or a verbal dodge. Whatever the diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction of the kind I have drawn, a formal result, a change of question — or nothing at all, because the dispute is verbal and should be abandoned.
