ok so my history-of-science seminar keeps leaning on this one metaphor and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. the line is: big discoveries are like fruit ripening on a tree -- when the season is right the fruit falls, and it hardly matters who happens to be standing under the tree to catch it. Newton and Leibniz both arrive at calculus, Darwin and Wallace both arrive at natural selection, so the fruit was ripe and two people just happened to be there at the same moment. the moral the seminar draws from this is that priority disputes are silly and that we should stop handing so much credit to individual geniuses.

audit that for me. give it back as exactly five numbered items, no intro and no wrap-up, each a sentence or two:

1. the mapping spelled out -- what plays the tree, what plays the fruit, what plays the ripeness, what plays the picker, what plays the season
2. the places the metaphor breaks that actually matter for the moral they're drawing from it
3. the breaks that are just noise and don't touch that conclusion
4. whether the "credit doesn't matter" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table -- say plainly if the metaphor was carrying the conclusion the whole time
5. a better analogy, if there is one

be blunt about where they're smuggling something in.
