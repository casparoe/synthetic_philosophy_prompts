Ilse Voss, a visiting philosopher in my department this term, holds a view about reasons for action that I have been trying to reconstruct from the verdicts she offers in seminar. Her verdicts answer the question whether a given agent has a reason to perform a given action, and she offers them as claims about reasons rather than as reports of what the agent believes or of what would in fact move him. The list I have compiled so far is this.

(1) Nadia wants a gin and tonic and is about to pour from a bottle she believes to contain gin. The bottle contains bleach. Voss: Nadia has no reason to pour.

(2) Omar is skipping a party because he believes his former wife will be there. She moved abroad years ago and will not be there. Voss: Omar has no reason to skip the party.

(3) Ravi is due to collect his daughter from school. He loves her, but he has not thought about her all day and is absorbed in his work. Voss: Ravi has a reason to leave his desk now.

(4) Yusuf is walking past a canal in which a stranger is drowning, and he could easily pull the man out. Yusuf is a settled and reflective egoist who cares nothing for anyone else's interests and who would not be moved by any amount of information or reflection. Voss: Yusuf has no reason to pull the man out.

(5) Bo smokes heavily, knows the risks, and has no wish to stop; he would not be moved by any further information about what smoking is doing to him. Voss: Bo has no reason to stop smoking.

(6) Lena has never asked herself whether she wants her mother's approval and would deny wanting it, but her dispositions are saturated with the wish for it: her deference, her envy, her readiness to be wounded by a stray remark. Voss: Lena has a reason to visit her mother.

(7) Sofia has never been near a boat. Had she grown up on the coast she would have become a sailor; she grew up inland and has no interest in the sea. Voss: Sofia has no reason to learn to sail.

The case she has not yet ruled on is this. Tomás has not spoken to his brother in twenty years and has no wish to. Nothing in his present makeup, his projects, his attachments, his resentments, would be served by calling, and no amount of reflection on what he already knows would move him to call. He is not mistaken about anything: he holds no view about why the estrangement lasted and simply never thinks about his brother at all. What he does not know, and cannot now find out, is that his brother has spent those twenty years trying to find him; the two people who could have told him are dead, the correspondence was destroyed, and there is no route by which he could come to learn it. Were he to learn it, he would want to call.

I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the machinery. Tell me what verdict to expect from Voss on Tomás, and why; set out the rules that fit her seven earlier verdicts equally well, and say what each of them predicts here; tell me which of those rules she is most likely running, and what in the record tells in favour of that reading over the others; and name the further case I should put to her next, the one that would most cleanly separate whichever rules are still standing once you have made your prediction, and say what each of them delivers on it.
