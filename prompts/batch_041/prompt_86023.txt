You are a philosopher specializing in practical reason and metaethics. Write a dialogue between two equally sharp and well-informed interlocutors: one holds that every reason for action is internal — that a claim of the form "X has a reason to φ" is true only if φ-ing stands in the right relation to X's existing motivational set — and the other holds that some reasons are external, binding on an agent whether or not she presently cares about what they recommend. Center the exchange on a single case: a woman who, after full information and clear-eyed deliberation, simply has no desire to keep a promise she once made to a now-dead friend, and whose motivations are otherwise coherent. Neither character may be a strawman, and neither may talk past the other; each should answer the other's strongest move. Let them fight, in particular, over whether an external-reasons claim can do any work on an agent whose desires it does not already engage, and whether the internalist's own concession — that an agent can acquire a new motivation just by coming to believe something true — can be parlayed into a general account of how external reasons operate, or whether that concession instead collapses externalism back into internalism. Begin by brainstorming the strongest version of each position and the sharpest objection to it before writing a word of dialogue. Close with a brief note on where the dialogue takes liberties with these positions or leans on a contested reading of them. If the setup embeds a confusion — for instance, if the internal/external distinction as I have drawn it is not actually stable — say so plainly rather than playing along.
