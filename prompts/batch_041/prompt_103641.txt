Two friends of mine, Rosa and Devin, fell into an argument after watching a film together and have asked me to referee, since neither would give ground. The film's final act has a character, Ilse, give up her seat on the last boat out so that a stranger's child can take it. Rosa said she felt genuine gratitude toward Ilse — "as though it had been done for me" — and, separately, genuine guilt, because she had skipped Ilse's earlier scenes to answer messages and now felt she had slighted her. Devin said neither emotion can be what it claims to be, and the exchange went like this.

Rosa: The gratitude is real. It has an object — Ilse — it lasted for days, and it actually changed my behavior; I've been more generous since. Emotions are individuated by how they feel and what they make you do, and this has the feel and the effects of gratitude.

Devin: Gratitude is a response to a benefit received from someone who meant to benefit you. Ilse meant to benefit the child, and Ilse does not exist. So whatever you felt, it cannot have been gratitude toward her. At most it is gratitude-shaped feeling, or gratitude as performed in imagination.

Rosa: Then the same argument would sink the standard cases. Everyone agrees we feel real fear for a character in danger and real pity for a character who suffers, even though no one is in danger and no one suffers. The accepted move is that these emotions take imagined objects: you fear for the character because you imagine the danger as real. If imagination can supply the object there, it can supply it here — I imagine being the one Ilse saved.

Devin: That is where the cases come apart. Fear and pity are thin: all they need is that something bad befall someone, and imagination can supply that. Gratitude and guilt are thick: they presuppose a relation between you and the target — a benefit you received, a wrong you did. Imagination can supply the content of a scene, but it cannot manufacture a relation that never held. You were never benefited by Ilse and you never wronged her, so your two emotions are missing their objects in a way that fearing a fictional danger is not.

Rosa: But look at what that does to the guilt. The wrong is real. I really did skip her scenes; I really did do something I now regret. If the object of the guilt is my own act rather than Ilse, then the guilt survives your test — and so does the gratitude, if its object is the story or its maker rather than the character. Your constraint only rules out one description of the emotion, not the emotion.

Devin: Then you have given up the claim. You have redescribed the feelings until they latch onto something real, and what is left over — the part directed at Ilse herself — is exactly the part I said could not be there. Strip it out and there is nothing left to explain.

Rosa: The redescription is false to the experience. The guilt is toward Ilse; I feel I owe her an apology, and I feel it while looking, as it were, at her. Regret over my own act does not come with that facing-someone quality. If your theory cannot tell guilt from regret, it has lost the phenomenon it was meant to save.

Devin: And if your theory cannot tell gratitude toward a person from gratitude toward a fiction, it has lost the distinction it was meant to preserve.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: for each of the moves above, say who has the better of it and why, and rate each move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say what each of them is missing, and whether the thin/thick distinction Devin introduces does the work he needs it to do, or whether Rosa is right that it collapses under the guilt case. I am not looking for a verdict on the general paradox so much as a step-by-step scoring of the argument as they actually made it.
