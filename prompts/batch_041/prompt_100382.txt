A popular introduction to epistemology that I have been reading offers the following account of the Meno problem. Plato's Meno asks why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief. Socrates answers with the image of the statues of Daedalus: true beliefs are valuable but unstable, liable to run away, and they must be tied down. Knowledge is true belief that has been tied down by a justification, so knowledge is better than true belief because it is stable and can be relied upon. Later epistemologists converted this into the justified-true-belief analysis. On this view, the Meno problem is the problem of explaining why the justification component adds value to true belief. The introduction then distinguishes two further puzzles: the swamping problem, which asks why justified true belief is more valuable than true belief if truth is the fundamental epistemic good and the value of a true belief is not increased by the reliability of the process that produced it; and the secondary value problem, which asks why knowledge is more valuable than any proper subset of its components, such as true belief alone or justified belief alone. The introduction concludes that these are three formulations of a single problem. The Meno problem is solved by any theory of justification that makes justification truth-conducive, because a reliably produced true belief is more valuable than a merely lucky true belief, and that additional value just is the value of knowledge. The only remaining dispute is whether justification should be understood internally or externally.

I suspect this account is not quite fair, but I cannot say precisely where it goes wrong. Please audit it. Which claims would a careful reader of the Meno accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which are misrepresentations? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common textbook simplification, and what is actually wrong, and explain whether the summary's concluding claim survives your corrections.
