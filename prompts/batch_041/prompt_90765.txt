I am writing a seminar paper on religion and moral philosophy in the Victorian period, and I have run into a difficulty that I suspect is of my own making; I would rather be told where my picture goes wrong than be given a tidy answer to a question that was misconceived from the start. The picture is this. In the very years in which he was composing and repeatedly revising The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick was one of the founders and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and much of the work he took an interest in there — the experiments on thought-transference between the living, the census of hallucinations, the volume on apparitions — was meant to put the question of the mind's survival of bodily death on an evidential footing. This is a man, after all, who gave up his Trinity fellowship rather than declare himself a member of the Church of England, so the interest cannot be dismissed as mere conformism. And the motive looks to me entirely intelligible: having concluded that practical reason is divided between rational egoism and rational benevolence — the dualism of practical reason — he saw that the division would be healed only if the universe were so ordered that a person's own good and the good of the whole finally coincide, which would be the case if the world were morally governed. On this reading, psychical research is not a sideline at all; it is the foundation on which the ethics rests.

If that is right, then I cannot see how the argument of the Methods is supposed to stand, and this is my question. Does the rational authority of universal benevolence depend, on Sidgwick's own account, on an empirical result — one that he pursued for decades and never secured — so that his utilitarianism is either circular, in that the principle is recommended on the strength of a premise it was itself supposed to vindicate, or else hostage to evidence he could not produce? And if the evidence never came, is the honest upshot of the dualism that a rational agent may simply take his own good as his end? I would be grateful for a careful account of what Sidgwick actually claims and of what really follows from it, even if the upshot is that I have the relation between these two halves of his life backwards.
