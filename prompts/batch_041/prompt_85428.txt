I run the determinations unit of a mid-sized benefits agency. Every month my staff issue roughly two hundred written decisions granting or denying claims, and every decision has to state its reasons. A claimant who loses may write back and ask for a further statement of reasons, and under our rules we must answer. This has produced a pattern I cannot get to the bottom of. Some decisions stop at a statement to the effect that the medical reviewer's assessment is accepted; claimants call that question-begging, and I am no longer sure they are wrong. Some decisions stop at the fact that this is our established practice, which sounds like a reason but seems to consist of pointing out that we have said the same thing before. Some decisions cite other decisions that cite back to them. And some decisions simply keep generating paragraphs: when a claimant asks why, we produce a longer restatement of the same rule in different words.

I have come to think this is the old problem of how a chain of reasons can end, and that there are three serious answers to it. On the first, justification terminates in something that does not itself stand in need of justification — a finding of fact, an authoritative text, an observation that simply holds. On the second, nothing terminates the chain, but that is acceptable, because a reason is justified by how well it fits with everything else we accept, and there is no privileged class of stopping points. On the third, the chain genuinely never ends, and justification is not a terminus at all but the standing ability to supply a further reason when one is demanded, so the practical question is how to manage an endless obligation rather than how to close it. I understand these are usually called foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism.

What I want from you is a procedure my staff can actually run. Assume they have no philosophical training, have about twenty minutes per decision, and will have to defend the procedure to an outside auditor. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to distinguish a legitimate stopping point from a merely convenient one, how to tell when two reasons are propping each other up in a circle, what a writer should do when a claimant asks again after a stopping point has been reached, and which decisions must be escalated to a person rather than resolved by the procedure. Be explicit about which of the three answers your procedure implements, and, where it is a hybrid, which part of each it is using and what work that part is doing. Wherever the procedure tells us to stop, say what makes that stop legitimate rather than merely convenient, and be honest about the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three decisions: (a) one that accepts a reviewer's functional-capacity assessment and offers no support for the reviewer's competence beyond the fact that he is experienced; (b) one that denies a claim because our established practice denies claims of that type, citing consistency as the reason to follow the practice; (c) one that rests on an empirical assumption — that claimants with this profile recover within six months — for which the file contains no support. For each, tell me whether the procedure requires us to rewrite the stated reasons and, if so, what the rewritten reasons should look like. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us something I will not want to hear.

Two further matters the procedure has to address. First, a claimant whose objection is not to our reasoning but to a provision of the regulation itself, which we have no authority to revisit: I take it the chain stops there for reasons of authority rather than of justification, and I want to know whether the procedure should treat that as a genuine stopping point or as a different kind of thing altogether, and how staff should describe it in the decision. Second, say plainly what the procedure cannot do, and where it must hand the decision to a person.
