So my roommate (Dutch, did her undergrad in Leiden) and my cousin (from Ohio, poli-sci major) got into this whole thing at dinner last week and I got stuck refereeing, and honestly I couldn't tell who was winning. Here's how it went, as close as I can remember.

Roommate: "In Europe when we call someone liberal we mean the free-market, small-government type — the VVD, the FDP, Venstre. It's basically what you Americans would call a libertarian."

Cousin: "That's way too neat. The FDP is fine with a welfare state and with half of what Brussels regulates, and your VVD is not a libertarian party. Meanwhile in the US 'liberal' means left-of-center on basically everything. So the two words point in opposite directions — they're false friends, not translations."

Roommate: "Fine, but the American meaning is the weird one. Everywhere else the word descends from classical liberalism — individual rights, free markets, constitutional limits on power. The American left basically hijacked the label during the New Deal and never gave it back. So your usage is the deviation, not mine."

Cousin: "But European 'liberal' isn't pure classical liberalism either. In Spain and France and Italy the liberals were the ones fighting the church and the monarchy — anti-clerical, civil-liberties people, not laissez-faire economists. And the old British Liberal Party was the party of old-age pensions and Asquith and Lloyd George, which is closer to your American sense than to the FDP. So 'Europe' isn't one thing. And the German version is ordoliberal anyway — the Freiburg school, the social market — where competition is something the state has to actively build and protect, not something you get by getting out of the way."

Roommate: "Okay, sure, but there's still a systematic difference. In continental Europe 'liberal' has an economic core and a pro-EU, technocratic flavor. In America 'liberal' has a social-progressive core and is suspicious of big business. That's why a Dutch liberal and an American liberal can agree on abortion and gay marriage and still be on completely opposite sides about the welfare state and regulation. The social issues line up, the economics don't."

Cousin: "Then why does everyone use it as an insult? Europeans call Brussels 'neoliberal,' Americans call anything they don't like 'liberal.' If the same word can mean 'market fundamentalist' to one person and 'social justice warrior' to another depending on who's sneering, then maybe the fight isn't about what the word refers to at all — it's about whose coalition gets to keep the name."

They both looked at me to settle it and I couldn't, partly because I kept realizing they were each right about different things. Can you actually referee this properly? Go move by move — say which of them is right at each step, where each one is overreaching, and what both of them are missing. Then say who's more right overall. Neither of them is an expert and I don't want a wishy-washy 'both make good points' — if someone's move is just wrong, say so.
