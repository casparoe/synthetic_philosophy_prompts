Write a poem to close a graduate seminar on the backward-induction paradox in the centipede game, with the form doing the reasoning. Let the poem contract as it proceeds — each stanza one line shorter than the one before — so that the shape on the page is the argument running backward from the last node to the first, and end on a single line spoken by the player who moves first. Alternate the stanzas between the two players. The poem should be accurate enough that a game theorist recognizes the standard setup: a finite horizon with a common-knowledge last node, a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium of immediate defection, and the awkward fact that real subjects pass almost to the end. It should also hold in view why the paradox bites — that the backward induction is a proof one cannot fault and a prediction almost no one believes — without dissolving it. Do not resolve the tension; let the contraction itself carry the point that each player's reason to stop is only as strong as their reason to trust the other, and let the brevity of the final line do the rest. It should read as a real poem, not a mnemonic.
