I'm writing a short paper on why expert panels so often break up with exactly the spread of opinion they came in with, and I keep catching myself paraphrasing Aumann's agreement theorem from memory in a way I suspect is too strong; I would rather have a case built to specification than another summary of the theorem. Build me a situation with two agents who assign different probabilities to the same proposition at the actual state, and in which the fact of the disagreement is common knowledge in the full sense — each knows that they disagree, each knows that the other knows, and so on up the hierarchy — while nothing illegitimate is doing the work: the two share a prior that both know to be shared, both update by Bayes' rule on their own information, neither is stubborn or confused about what the other means, and no exotic machinery is invoked (no infinite hierarchies of belief, no non-Bayesian agents, no private theories about the other's psychology). The case must not violate the theorem, so an essential part of the job is telling me exactly which of the theorem's conditions fails, and where. I also need the case to be non-degenerate in three specific ways: neither agent may be perfectly informed; the disagreement must not hold at every state of the model, so that there are states in which these same two agents, with these same information partitions, would have agreed; and neither agent's posterior may be constant across the model. The setting should be ordinary — two people with partial evidence about some humdrum matter of fact — but I want the formal model underneath, since that is what I will be checking.

Give it to me in four parts. First, the scenario in a few sentences, as I would describe it to a colleague. Second, the machinery: the state space, the prior, each agent's information partition, the proposition, and each agent's posterior at every state, with the arithmetic shown rather than asserted. Third, the check: the common-knowledge component of the two partitions at the actual state, a state-by-state verification that the disagreement holds throughout it, and an equally explicit verification that it is not common knowledge what the two posteriors are — naming which of the two fails to be common knowledge, and why. Fourth, the contrast that matters most for the paper: suppose the two agents do the obvious thing and each announces her exact credence; work through what each does with the other's announcement, and whether the disagreement survives. If it collapses, tell me precisely which feature of the original case was holding it up, and what that implies for the slogan that rational people who share a prior cannot knowingly disagree — in particular, whether a defender of the theorem should say my case is a genuine counterexample to the slogan or merely a case in which the slogan was sloppy. Finish with the weakest joint in the construction, the move a sceptical reader would attack first.
