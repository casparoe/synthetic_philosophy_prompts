Ok so I'm in a reading group and we're doing Aquinas on lying, and I'm stuck on one passage. It's Summa II-II q.110 a.1, and I grabbed this off newadvent:

"However, the essential notion of a lie is taken from formal falsehood, from the fact namely, that a person intends to say what is false; wherefore also the word 'mendacium' [lie] is derived from its being in opposition to the 'mind.' Consequently if one says what is false, thinking it to be true, it is false materially, but not formally, because the falseness is beside the intention of the speaker so that it is not a perfect lie, since what is beside the speaker's intention is accidental for which reason it cannot be a specific difference. If, on the other hand, one utters falsehood formally, through having the will to deceive, even if what one says be true, yet inasmuch as this is a voluntary and moral act, it contains falseness essentially and truth accidentally, and attains the specific nature of a lie. That a person intends to cause another to have a false opinion, by deceiving him, does not belong to the species of lying, but to perfection thereof..."

And then in his reply to the first objection he says it's more opposed to truth to tell the truth while intending to tell a falsehood than to tell a falsehood while intending to tell the truth, which I honestly can't make heads or tails of.

Here's what's bugging me. I always assumed a lie was basically about getting someone to believe something false — the deception is the whole point. He seems to say the opposite: the essence of lying is the will to say what's false, and the intent to deceive is just a "perfection" of it, not part of what makes it a lie. So:

- If I say something literally true but arrange it so you'll draw a false conclusion (like "I didn't see him this morning" when I know full well he's in the basement), did I lie on his account? Seems like the answer has to be no, since my words matched my mind, but that's a wild result given that I did exactly what a liar is trying to do.
- Conversely, if I say something false that I sincerely believe is true, and I say it precisely because I want you to believe it too, is that a lie? Sounds like he'd say no, which also seems wild.
- And what even is the "formal falsehood" if it isn't the intent to deceive? Is it just that my utterance doesn't match my own belief? Because if so, lying turns into a purely private relation between my words and my mind, and the other person drops out entirely, which is weird for something he treats as a sin against a virtue governing how we deal with each other.

Can you walk me through the passage piece by piece, say what the material/formal distinction is actually tracking, and then tell me what his view definately commits him to about misleading-without-falsehood and about honest-but-deceptive speech? I want to be able to state the position, not just paraphrase it back at people. And if I'm misreading the thing about the first objection, tell me how, cause I have a feeling I'm missing something there.
