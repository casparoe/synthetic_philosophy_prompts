Ok so this is a strange favor, but I think you'll enjoy it. My old college — small liberal arts place in Ohio, about 900 students, way too much school spirit — has asked me to give this year's commencement address, and I've been trying to build it around the hinge-of-history idea: the claim that our moment might be the most consequential stretch of time in human history, the point where the long-run future of civilization actually gets settled one way or the other. I write about science and ideas for a mid-sized daily, so I'm use to making this stuff legible, but a podium is different. Two things I already know I want: I'm not name-dropping the effective-altruism/longtermism crowd from the stage (half the parents will hear that vocabulary as a recruitment pitch and check out, and I'd rather the idea arrive on its own), and I'm not leaning on epigraphs — no "as some philosopher once said," no quotations at all, partly because I don't trust half the attributions floating around and partly because I want the argument to stand up by itself.

Here's the knot I keep circling and want the speech built on: the evidence that we're living at a hinge and the evidence that we're merely hinge-drunk — my term for a society that's convinced it's at the turning point — look exactly the same from the inside. Every generation has had its apocalypse and its dawn; my dad was a historian and his favorite Thanksgiving provocation was that the people who were certain they were living through the decisive decade were wrong more often than not, and the ones who turned out to be right had no idea at the time. So the speech can't settle it, and I don't want it to. What I want is a ten-to-twelve minute commencement address, somewhere around 1,400–1,500 words, that takes the audience through both halves — the vertigo of "you might be the hinge generation" and the deflation of the base rate — without letting either one win on the cheap. One device I'd like worked in somewhere, if it earns its place: a museum-plaque test, where I ask the crowd to imagine the label a curator two hundred years from now would write for our decade, and then point out that being able to imagine it and being unable to imagine it are both compatible with the same set of facts. Don't force it if it doesn't fit.

Some structure notes, since I'll actually be standing outdoors at 10am in May with a lot of hungover people in folding chairs, and they've told me ten minutes knowing full well everyone goes fifteen. Let the ceremony furniture be in there — the hats, the crying parents in row six, the kid whose tassel is already crooked — and let the speech know that it's a speech. I want one honest moment where I admit that if we really are at the hinge, then spending the hour talking to a few hundred people about it is a bizarre way to spend the hour, and that I'm doing it anyway. And it has to work for a genuinely mixed room: a few philosophy faculty in the front who will catch it instantly if I state the hinge claim sloppily, or if I quietly swap it out for a different thesis halfway through — if "this might be the hinge" slides into "so you should all go work on AI safety," or into a generic "follow your dreams, but bigger." Then a lot of nineteen-year-olds and grandparents who are mainly there for the hats and can only follow one idea at a time, stated plainly, before their attention goes. Don't end on "you are the hinge," and don't end on "relax, everyone thinks that." Find a third place to leave them, and make me beleive that third place isn't a cop-out.
