I am convening a year-long working group on self-replication and what is often called the DNA–Gödel isomorphism, and I would like help deciding what the group should actually spend its time on. The members are myself (philosophy, with a background in logic), one theoretically minded computer scientist, and three or four capable graduate students. We have an academic year, a seminar room, and no laboratory. What I am asking for is not a survey but a defended agenda: an ordering of the open questions in this area, with the criteria for the ordering made explicit.

First, so that we are working with the same target. The phrase names an alleged structural parallel between two cases of self-reference. In the formal case, a system rich enough to encode statements about its own proofs can, in effect, talk about itself; Hofstadter's toy system of typogenetics — strands built from a small alphabet, encoding enzymes that operate on strands, so that a strand can under the right conditions bring about its own copy — is presented as a strange loop on a par with Gödelian self-reference. In the biological case, a nucleic-acid sequence specifies the enzymes that read, copy, and edit that same sequence, so that the code and the machinery that maintains it are tangled together. The question is how far the parallel actually goes.

The candidate questions I have been weighing are these:

1. Status. Is the isomorphism a substantive structural identity — two instances of a single phenomenon — or an evocative metaphor that leans on an unanalyzed notion of self-reference? What would it take to state it as a theorem rather than an analogy, and is there reason to think that no such statement is forthcoming?

2. Necessity. Must a self-replicating system contain a description of itself, and so contain a self-referential loop, or can replication proceed without anything like Gödelian self-reference — for instance through a description plus a universal constructor that are simply copied along with the rest of the configuration? If self-reference turns out not to be necessary, what remains of the isomorphism?

3. Codes. Is there a principled distinction between a genuine code and a merely causal chain, and can it be drawn without covertly appealing to an interpreter or an observer? Does the biological case force us to introduce such a notion, and does the formal case clarify it or merely dress it up?

4. Consequences. Does the parallel constrain anything — hypotheses about how self-replication might emerge from non-replicating chemistry, or about the emergence of new levels of description in open-ended evolution — or is it a redescription that forbids nothing and predicts nothing?

5. The loop itself. Is the level-crossing or tangled-hierarchy structure a genuine feature shared by the two systems, or a projection of the describer's interests? What would distinguish these two possibilities?

Please tell me how you would order these, and defend the order. I am more interested in the reasoning than in the ranking: which questions are upstream of which, in the sense that settling one would settle or dissolve others; which are tractable with the tools we actually have — a philosopher with logic and a computer scientist with no wet lab — as against which would require empirical biology we cannot do; which are crowded and which neglected; and what developments over the coming year would move a question up or down the list. Where you think I have misdescribed a question, or left out one that belongs above all of these, say so and explain why.
