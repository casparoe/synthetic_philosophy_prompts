I have been asked to referee, informally, a draft chapter written by a colleague in another department — a competent popularizer of the philosophy of science who is not a specialist in probability. He explains the Principal Principle to undergraduates and wants to know whether his treatment is defensible before he sends it to press. I would like you to audit the passage the way a specialist would. Here is how the relevant stretch of the draft runs:

"One of the deepest principles in the philosophy of probability is the Principal Principle, which we owe to David Lewis. Stated plainly, it says that your subjective probability for any event ought to equal its objective chance. If you know that a particular coin has a 0.5 chance of landing heads, then you are rationally required to believe that it will land heads to degree 0.5, no more and no less. The deeper point is that objective chance is the complete information about a situation: once you know the chances, everything else you know about the event becomes irrelevant, because the chance screens off your other evidence. This is why the principle is sometimes described as a principle of deference — objective chance is the expert and rational credence is the obedient apprentice. One might object that we rarely know the objective chances, but the principle is intended only for idealized agents who do; for the rest of us it functions as a regulative ideal. There is, however, a more serious difficulty. If the chance of heads is 0.5, then among the possible futures compatible with that chance are sequences in which the coin lands heads on every toss; but a coin that landed heads on every toss would not have had a 0.5 chance of heads in the first place. This shows that Lewis's principle is inconsistent, and it has accordingly been abandoned in favor of a corrected version that conditionalizes on the chance being what it is rather than simply deferring to it."

That is the whole of the passage.

Please go claim by claim through it and tell me what a specialist in this area would endorse, what is a defensible simplification for an undergraduate audience, what is a genuine error or misrepresentation, and what is merely arguable. I am especially interested in four things. First, the sentence that says one's credence ought to equal the objective chance: is that the principle itself, or a stronger relative of it, and does the difference matter for how the rest of the passage should be read? Second, the claim that chance "screens off" all other evidence, so that once you know the chances everything else becomes irrelevant — is that a fair statement of the underlying idea, and does it sit consistently with the undermining difficulty the author himself raises later, or do the two sit in tension? Third, the deference analogy: is it a harmless pedagogical metaphor, or does it smuggle in a substantive and contested thesis about what kind of principle this is? Fourth, the closing inference — that the principle is inconsistent and has been abandoned — does that follow from the undermining phenomenon he describes, and how do those who actually work on this divide over the question? I am inclined to think the last sentence overstates the situation, but I suspect the earlier, friendlier sentences may be the ones doing the real damage, and I would like to be told if I am wrong about that.

Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I know the literature and will supply the references myself. What I need is the audit. Where a question is genuinely open among people who work on it, say so explicitly and lay out the main positions rather than presenting any one of them as the settled answer; where something is simply settled, say that too.
