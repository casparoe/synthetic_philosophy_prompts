The background. In work on scalable oversight there is a family of constructs in which a question is answered by decomposition. A reasoner splits the question into subquestions, farms those out to further reasoners — copies of herself, or less capable ones, or contractors — and aggregates their answers; the process recurses until the leaves are small enough for a single reasoner to answer directly. The version usually discussed under the heading HCH bottoms out in a human and is treated as computing what a human would answer given unlimited time and help. A related programme, sometimes called factored cognition, uses the same decomposition both as a way to extract more capability from bounded reasoners and as a training signal: a weaker reasoner is trained to imitate the answers the decomposition produced when more capable reasoners stood at the leaves, so that capability is distilled upward in stages. Neither construct is meant as a description of how anyone actually thinks. Both are presented as idealizations of deliberation: as telling us what a person would think, or what the right answer is, "in the limit."

My half-formed idea — turn it into a proposal, and repair it where it is confused. The phrase "in the limit" appears to be doing at least three different jobs, and the alignment arguments that lean on these constructs tend to slide between them:

(a) An idealization of the agent's own process with the limits relaxed — unbounded time, memory, and patience — so that the counterfactual "what would she have thought" has a determinate answer even if no one can compute it.
(b) A substitute process, executed by different reasoners with different dispositions and different errors, which need not converge on (a) even as the decomposition deepens; on this reading the construct is a substantive empirical hypothesis about the structure of human thought rather than a harmless idealization.
(c) A fixed point, defined by a reflection operator: the answer is one that the reasoner would not revise on further reflection, whatever process produced it.

The question I want the proposal to answer is this: when someone says that HCH is an idealization of deliberation, are they making a claim of type (a), in which case the fact that no one reasons that way is irrelevant and the construct is as innocent as a frictionless plane; or a claim of type (b), in which case we should ask what evidence could bear on it and what becomes of the oversight argument if it is false; or are they retreating to (c), and if so, does defining the ideal by a reflection operator merely relocate the problem, since the operator is itself a process we chose? A secondary question, which I suspect is where the real work is: does the construct idealize deliberation, or does it idealize something else — computation, or division of cognitive labour — and does the answer change what the idealization is entitled to conclude?
