I am preparing a position statement on skepticism and the structure of inquiry for a small workshop, and I would like a consistency audit of the package before I circulate it. The views below are mine. They were prompted by my reading of the later Wittgenstein, On Certainty in particular, but I am not asking for exegesis and I do not need references; what I need is to know whether the package holds together. I will call the commitments in question load-bearing commitments.

1. Presupposition. Every inquiry into whether some claim is true proceeds against commitments that the inquiry itself cannot establish: that there is an external world, that my memory is broadly reliable, that the people I am addressing are not automata. Any attempt to justify one of these from within inquiry either circles or trails off.

2. Illegitimacy, not merely failure. The demand for such a justification is not just unanswerable but out of place. Someone who insists that I justify my confidence that I have two hands has not found a hard question; they have misunderstood what is going on when we inquire. Skepticism is to be diagnosed rather than refuted.

3. Rules, not descriptions. These commitments function more like rules for the use of our words than like descriptions of how things stand. They are not properly assessed as true or false, and when I say of one of them that I know it, I am not reporting a cognitive achievement.

4. Acquisition. We come by them through training, immersion, and habituation rather than through evidence. They do not feel like conclusions, and no one holds them because of an argument.

5. Mobility. They are not permanent fixtures. Which commitments play this role varies with the question under discussion and with historical period; a commitment can descend from presupposition to live hypothesis and later ascend again.

6. Objectivity. All the same, I want to say of someone whose commitments diverge sharply from mine — a young-earth creationist, someone who consults the dead — not that they are playing a different game but that they are wrong, and that a form of life built on consulting the dead is worse than one that is not. When I say that a student who claims to be unable to rule out that they are a brain in a vat has stopped doing something rather than discovered something, I mean that as a criticism of them.

7. Genealogical critique. A commitment of this kind can be criticized from the outside by a genealogical argument — by showing that it was adopted through processes we now recognize as distorting, such as coercion, wishful thinking, or an accident of institutional history — even though nothing that happens within the practice could count as evidence against it. I have not seen this put quite this way, and I am the least confident in it.

What I would like from you is an audit. Which of these are genuinely in tension with which, and how severe is each tension? Which pairs look inconsistent but come apart once the right distinction is drawn? Where I take two of these to be independent, am I in fact helping myself to something I have ruled out elsewhere? For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest revision and what that revision costs elsewhere in the package; then rank the tensions by severity and tell me which single revision would clear up the most. I have no idea where the weak joint is and no attachment to any particular item surviving, so do not assume that I would rather keep any of them.
