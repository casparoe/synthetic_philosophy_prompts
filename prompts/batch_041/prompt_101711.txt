I have been trying, with only partial success, to pin down what I actually think weakness of will is, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. I keep a running list of cases and my reactions to them. (a) Someone concludes on reflection that they should skip dessert for the sake of their health, and at the table, still endorsing that conclusion and regarding it as their own, orders the tart anyway: this is the paradigm case, weakness of will if anything is. (b) Someone resolves in the morning to run at six; at six, tired, they reconsider and sincerely judge that resting is the better choice tonight, and rests: not weakness of will — they changed their mind, even if the change was predictable and convenient. (c) Someone who only ever sort of intends to quit smoking, without ever having judged that quitting is what they should do, has another cigarette: not weakness of will — there was no judgment for the action to violate. (d) Someone addicted to a drug, in the grip of a craving, who says that they know they should not and uses it anyway: I hesitate, and lean toward saying this is not weakness of will, because it looks more like compulsion than like will. (e) Someone who at nine o'clock sincerely prefers that they leave the party at ten, who at ten sincerely prefers to stay, who saw the reversal coming and even set an alarm as a commitment device, and who silences the alarm and stays: weakness of will, and a clearer case than (b) even though both are reversals of a prior preference. (f) Someone on autopilot who eats the second biscuit without ever bringing the relevant judgment to mind: I want to say this is still weakness of will, but a lesser and more absent-minded variety.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (b) and (e): in both, the person acts on a preference or judgment they genuinely hold at the moment of action, yet I count one as weakness of will and the other not — apparently because in (e) the reversal was foreseen, or because the earlier judgment is the one the person identifies with, or something else I cannot name. A friend insists the whole list is just hyperbolic discounting in disguise: that the cases differ only in the shape of the discount curve and in whether the reversal is anticipated, and that the talk of judgment adds nothing. I suspect the friend is leaving something out, but I cannot say what. I am also unsure what work the occurrent judgment is doing in (a) and (f): does weakness of will require that the contrary judgment be present to mind at the moment of action, or can a standing commitment, not currently rehearsed, do the same work?

Please give me the principle, or the smallest set of candidate principles, that best fits these verdicts. State each candidate precisely; tell me which case each clause exists to handle; say what the principle predicts about a case I have not listed — someone who deliberately hands a decision to their future self, arranging in advance to be swayed and judging that this is the sensible thing to do; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and what I would have to believe about judgment, time, and the self in order to keep it.
