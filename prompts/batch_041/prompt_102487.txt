I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a fourth-century Athenian household in which a slave is also his master's philosophy tutor, so I have spent months reading my way into the scholarly quarrel over Aristotle's doctrine of natural slavery — the claim, in the first book of the Politics, that some people are slaves by nature, and that for such people it is both just and advantageous to be ruled by others. What I cannot manage is to get the parties to meet. One camp treats the doctrine as an embarrassment or an incoherence Aristotle ought to have seen — the slave is said to lack the deliberative faculty, yet is at the same time expected to obey reason and even to be capable of virtue in some measure. A second camp treats it as a serious and internally consistent answer to a real question, continuous with Aristotle's general account of authority in the soul and the household. A third says the whole thing is a defense of existing institutions dressed up in nature-talk, so its internal coherence hardly matters. Read side by side, the three seem to me not to be disputing what the text says but to be operating with different senses of what a "natural" capacity is, and different assumptions about whether the doctrine is load-bearing in Aristotle's ethics or a piece of local prejudice he could have dropped without cost. My hypothesis — and I offer it only as a hypothesis — is that the camps are answering different questions under a single heading: one asks whether the doctrine is true, another whether it is coherent given Aristotle's own premises, a third whether it does any work in his system. Diagnose this for me: what kind of disagreement is this actually, and what, if anything, would count as progress toward settling it? I do not want a survey of the secondary literature or a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement.

Please answer in three titled sections, in this order. First, "The shape of the impasse" — two to three hundred words describing, in your own terms, what each side is committed to and precisely where the sides talk past one another. Second, "What kind of disagreement this is" — four to six hundred words giving your diagnosis and your reasons for it, including whether the question is ill-posed or merely hard, and whether my three-camps hypothesis survives contact with the actual positions. Third, "What would count as progress" — roughly two hundred words on what, given your diagnosis, would move the dispute (a distinction, a reconstruction, a reinterpretation of the key passages), or an argument that nothing should be expected to move it and why.
