I'm a master's student in translation studies, and this term I've been sitting in on a joint seminar between my department and the computational linguistics group, mostly to eavesdrop on the arguments. Last week two of the regulars went at each other for most of an hour and the moderator never intervened, so I'd like you to. Marek is a literary translator who had just reread Douglas Hofstadter's 2018 Atlantic essay on the shallowness of Google Translate and kept invoking it, along with Warren Weaver's 1947 line that a Russian article is 'really written in English' but has been 'coded in some strange symbols'; Ines is a machine-translation engineer who thinks the essay is a stack of anecdotes dressed up as an argument. I couldn't tell who was winning — partly because I don't know enough about how the systems work, and partly because I couldn't always tell when they were disagreeing about facts and when about what the words 'understand' and 'translate' are supposed to be doing.

The exchange ran roughly like this. (1) Ines: every test anyone proposes for understanding, the system passes eventually — it gets pronoun genders right that it can only get right by tracking a referent, it renders idioms with idioms, it catches register. And the only evidence you have that I understand anything is my behavior; you've never been inside my head either. So 'it's just statistics' isn't a finding, it's a demand for something you can't specify and don't require of people. (2) Marek: and when it gets those cases right, the cue was sitting on the surface — 'mother,' 'she,' a word that happens to sound informal. Show me a case where the text is silent and the right choice follows only from something outside the text, then change that outside fact while leaving every word of the text alone. A human translator who learns the customer is a stranger switches to the formal address and rereads the whole paragraph differently; the system doesn't have a world it's translating about, it has a distribution it's sampling from. (3) Ines: that's unfalsifiable as stated — every success you relabel as cue-following, every failure as the absence of a world model, and the 'for the right reason' clause does all the work. You've never seen the referent either, only words, so cues are all anyone ever has. Your own translations lean on dictionaries and parallel corpora, which is the statistics you claim to distrust, and translation students fail those same referent cases, so by your standard most of them don't understand English either. What observation would make you say it does understand? (4) Marek: then take a different cut and look at the shape of the errors rather than at individual ones. My students' errors and mine cluster around how strange the situation is — an unfamiliar legal genre, an alien custom, a period we don't know. The system's cluster around how strange the surface is — rare words, unusual constructions — and its errors are fluent, confident, and bear no relation to how hard the passage would be for a person. That asymmetry in the error profile is observable, and more data doesn't remove it, because the profile is set by what the training set covers. (5) Ines: that's a claim about data coverage rather than about the mechanism, and it's testable, so test it: match sentences for surface rarity and vary how strange the situation is, and see whether the gap survives. Human error profiles track surface rarity too — hapax legomena, garden-path sentences, unfamiliar cultures. If the profiles differ only in degree, then understanding is a matter of degree and your objection is quantitative and empirical rather than conceptual. (6) Marek: degree is fine, but the gap isn't in degree, it's in kind: the system can be uncertain about the text and never about the world, so it can't be right or wrong about the world, and a translation is exactly a claim about the world. And it shows in what happens next — I can be told the customer is a stranger and revise; it has to be retrained.

Please adjudicate, move by move: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating in a sentence or two, say which of their disagreements are empirical (and what experiment would settle them) and which are verbal or conceptual, and flag any point where they are talking past each other rather than disagreeing. Then tell me which of them is closer to right overall, and what both are missing. Don't cite literature or give me reading — I want the reasoning, not the references. Keep the whole answer to 900 words or fewer, leading with your overall verdict in two sentences before the move-by-move ratings, and closing with a short paragraph on what the exchange left out.
