I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and we've spent the last three weeks on the multiple drafts model -- the picture where there's no single place in the brain where everything comes together and gets shown to the inner viewer, just a bunch of parallel processes that get written, revised, and probed, so that asking "when exactly did she become conscious of X?" may have no fact of the matter. Our next session is a "bring something" session and I drew the creative slot. So: a short story, maybe 1200-1800 words, that dramatizes this instead of explaining it. One thing I care about a lot -- give the Cartesian theater its best shot rather than a strawman. I want the reader to feel the pull of "ok but somebody in there has to be watching" before it gets complicated, otherwise it's just a hatchet job.

Here's the premise, I've been sitting on it for a while: a company sells a brain implant plus a wristband that outputs a single timestamp -- the moment you became aware of something. It's pitched to insurers, employers, and courts, and the whole selling point is that it turns "did you know?" into a number. My protaganist is one of the engineers who built it, and the story turns on a defect that isn't really a bug: two subsystems in the device disagree about the timestamp, one because it reconstructs the experience after the fact and one because it reacts as the signal arrives, and no experiment, no introspective report, and no third measurement can settle which one is the "real" moment. I'd love a scene where the engineer tries to make the disagreement matter -- takes it to a regulator, or to a court, or to the person whose life that number decided -- and discovers that everything anyone could ever observe is compatible with either story. If you want a flavor of the older lab-demo weirdness (the thing where two flashes in different colors come out as an experience in which the first flash somehow already has the second color), steal from that freely, but don't feel bound to get any particular experiment's details right. Invent names, a city, a specific case if you need one -- I keep picturing a cyclist and a self-driving car, but do whatever serves the story. Please don't end with a lecture or have a character explain the moral; if the philosophy lands it should land as something a person notices about their own life.

After the story, a short author's note: where you took liberties with the ideas or leaned on a contested reading of them, how confident you are in the picture of the debate you're presupposing (I'll be defending this in front of people who know the literature better than I do, so I'd rather have the soft spot flagged than papered over), and the one point you're least sure you got right.
