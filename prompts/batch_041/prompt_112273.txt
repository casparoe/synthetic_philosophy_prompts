I co-edit, with a historian of science, a handbook on the Lakatosian appraisal of scientific research programmes; her verdicts are the most internally consistent part of her work, and I have come to rely on being able to predict them. We have one episode left to classify, the volume goes to press before that episode is over, and I need to know what she will say about it — and how much of her past record actually constrains the prediction. I do not need a summary of the framework; I need to know which part of it she is really applying.

Her verdicts on completed episodes, as I have recorded them:

1. A programme whose central postulate its own practitioners took to be refuted by a decisive experiment, and which they nevertheless retained while rebuilding everything around it, whereupon the rebuilt theory yielded two predictions of previously unsuspected phenomena, both confirmed before the measurements were reported: progressive throughout, including the years immediately after the apparent refutation.

2. A programme that absorbed each anomaly by a modification which saved the phenomenon without anticipating any new observation, and that had no outstanding problems left to solve, although it remained the standard working tool of its field and generated a large body of applications: degenerating, and its practical success irrelevant to the verdict.

3. A programme whose final phase produced a striking confirmed prediction, but only by revising an assumption its founders had treated as non-negotiable, and only in response to an accumulated anomaly rather than by pursuing an agenda of its own: the prediction credited to a successor programme, the original programme's last phase degenerating.

4. A programme that contemporaries judged degenerating for some fifteen years, and that then, after a reformulation which left its central postulate untouched, produced a run of confirmed novel predictions: progressive — and she added that the contemporaries had not been mistaken, since these verdicts are retrospective.

She has also said, in seminar, that one cannot rank programmes that are still running.

The episode we must classify is a thirty-year-old programme whose founders declared its central postulate non-negotiable. Ten years in, the postulate was retained while the surrounding theory was rebuilt, and the rebuilt theory yielded a confirmed prediction of an unsuspected phenomenon, published four years before the relevant measurement was reported. It has since spent a decade responding to anomalies raised by rivals, and its adjustments, each of which narrowed what the theory claims, have produced two further predictions, both made before their measurements and both confirmed. A faction within it now proposes to drop the central postulate; the faction's revised scheme already has an agenda of its own and has itself yielded one confirmed prediction made before its measurement. A renewal decision falls due next month, which is why I cannot simply wait to see how things turn out.

Predict her verdict on the programme and on the faction's scheme. Tell me the rule she appears to be running, which other rules fit her four verdicts equally well, what each of those rules predicts here, whether the fact that the recent adjustments narrowed the theory's claims should count for or against it, which prediction you would bet on and why, and the further episode that would decide between the rules you cannot separate.
