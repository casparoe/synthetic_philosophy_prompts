I am trying to locate exactly how much weight the Condorcet jury theorem can bear in the epistemic defense of majority rule, and I keep finding that discussions of it either restate the textbook result or gesture at correlation without saying what follows from it. Here is the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can manage:

(P) The independence condition in the Condorcet jury theorem is not a harmless idealization that real deliberating bodies can be expected to approximate; it is a substantive and effectively unverifiable premise, and once it is relaxed to permit any shared source of error, the theorem no longer licenses the claim that a larger electorate is epistemically superior to a smaller one or to a single well-informed expert.

My own credence in (P) is 0.7; a colleague who works on social choice puts it at 0.35. Give me your number, but treat it as the summary of a decomposition rather than the deliverable. If you think (P) is too coarse to have a truth value, say which precisification you are answering and why. I want a probability and a one-line justification for each of the following, then an account of how they combine:

1. The model-level question. Take a group of voters who each receive a private signal that is correct with probability greater than one half, and who also share one common influence that is not perfectly correlated with the truth. Is there a natural way of specifying how that common influence enters each voter's decision on which the probability of a correct majority is bounded away from one as the group grows, and in some such specifications driven below one half? I believe the failure is elementary in at least some specifications — for instance, where a voter's verdict is right only if the shared influence is right — but I want you to say whether the failure is an artifact of how the shared influence is modeled or a general feature of relaxing independence, and to exhibit the specification you have in mind rather than describing it in general terms.

2. The re-description question. Suppose correlated votes are represented as exchangeable draws from a common urn, so that they become conditionally independent given the urn's parameter, namely the probability of a correct vote. Does this restore the theorem, or does it merely relocate the difficulty? If it restores it, what must be true of the prior over that parameter? If it does not, what does the probability of a correct majority converge to, and under what conditions is that limit below one?

3. The condition question. Is the condition of independence conditional on the truth the epistemically relevant one, or is something weaker sufficient — for example, that a group's shared influences are at least as likely to point toward the truth as away from it? If the weaker condition suffices, is it defensible without assuming the conclusion that the group tends to be right?

4. The interpretation question. Are the democratic theorists who invoke the theorem actually committed to the unconditional claim in (P), or do they invoke it only as a how-possibly explanation of how aggregation could help at all, in which case the correlated-error objection does less damage than it appears to?

5. The upshot question. If shared error is the problem, does the theorem still do real work for bodies in which independence is deliberately engineered — juries instructed not to discuss the case before voting, ensembles of classifiers trained on different data, prediction markets with heterogeneous participants — and if so, does that constitute a defense of democracy at scale or only of small, specially designed institutions?

Then tell me which of these five my colleague and I most plausibly disagree about, given that our overall numbers differ by 0.35, and which of them your own number is most sensitive to. I am not after a literature review or citations; I want the reasoning and where it breaks. Throughout, keep separate what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is genuinely contested: in particular, do not present either the claim that the theorem is simply false for real electorates or the claim that it is untouched by correlation as the consensus position, because as far as I can tell neither is, and I would rather see the seams than a smooth answer.
