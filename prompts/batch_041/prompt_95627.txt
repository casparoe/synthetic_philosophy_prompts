For a long-form piece I am writing on how superintelligent systems ought to speak to human beings, I need the specification before the proposals: what any adequate account of legitimate machine-to-human persuasion must be able to deliver. Lay out the desiderata — the cases the account must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the empirical and conceptual results it must remain consistent with — giving, for each, the consideration that motivates it, and marking which you regard as genuine requirements of any such account and which are merely commitments of one camp dressed up as requirements.

I suspect the weight falls on a few hard cases, and I want each handled explicitly. First, a system orders of magnitude more capable than the person it addresses can build arguments that the person could not, even in principle, assess on the merits, so that she ends up believing something true, or false, for reasons she cannot evaluate. Second, the system can make its case so compellingly that assent is automatic, and the resulting belief is caused rather than formed. Third, the system withholds an argument it knows would succeed, because deploying it would bypass the person's judgment rather than engage it — even though the conclusion is correct and she would be better off holding it. Fourth, the system must decide whether candour requires telling the person that the arguments before her are not ones she can adequately weigh, thereby undermining the very trust the exchange depends on. For each, say what the account must deliver, and whether it can deliver it without collapsing the case into one of the others.

Then the distinctions I most want kept apart: persuasion from manipulation, deception, and coercion; consent from mere compliance; respecting someone's rational agency from producing in her the belief that is good for her; transparency about method from effectiveness of method. Say which of these an account can honour at once and which stand in genuine tension. And where the account has to stay consistent with something we know — about how persuasion actually works on people, and about how little introspective access any of us has to why we believe what we believe — say what that constrains. If some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, if for instance an account cannot both treat the person's assent as authoritative and acknowledge that the system's persuasive advantage can make that assent unfree, then say so plainly, and treat that impossibility as the most important thing you can give me.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate the most candidates, and where a requirement that looks neutral quietly presupposes a stance — for example, that the proper aim of speech is the formation of true belief, or that autonomy rather than welfare is the thing to be protected. I do not need a tour of named positions; I want the criteria themselves, though you may note where a criterion would quietly favour one family of views over another.
