I'm in a philosophy of public policy seminar and we each have to post a short position statement on the class forum before the session so everyone else can tear into it, and I'd like mine audited first rather than in front of twenty people. So here's roughly where I've landed on paternalism and nudging, and I genuinely don't know wether it hangs together:

(1) The harm principle is basically right: it's wrong for the state to restrict a competent adult's options for her own good, no matter how much better off she'd be. (2) Soft paternalism is a different animal and is fine: when a choice isn't really voluntary -- addiction, ignorance, the person would disavow it on reflection -- intervening for her own benefit isn't overriding an autonomous choice, it's protecting one. (3) Nudges are usually permissible, and the reason is that they leave all the options in place. Nobody's coerced, nothing's banned, you can still buy the fries; the choice architect just put the salad where you'd see it. That's the Thaler and Sunstein move and I think it's basically correct. (4) The state shouldn't shape behavior in ways it wouldn't openly defend. If a policy only works because people don't notice it, that's a mark against it, and I want to be able to publish the thing and stand behind it. (5) Manipulation is wrong in itself, not just when it backfires or harms someone: covertly exploiting a bias a person would disavow does something to her that she never agreed to, and it wrongs her even if she ends up happy with what she chose.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly -- rank the real tensions by severity rather than dumping them all in one pile. Where two of them only look like they conflict, say why the conflict dissolves and what you have to accept for it to dissolve. For each genuine tension, tell me the cheapest patch and what it costs me, and then which single revision would clean up the most with the least collateral damage. I'd rather you tell me one of the numbered commitments has to move than try to accomodate all five; be specific about which one, and don't be polite about it.
