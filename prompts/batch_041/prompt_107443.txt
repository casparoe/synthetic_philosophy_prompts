I am not a philosopher and never studied the subject, but since my aunt died I have been thinking about something I believe philosophers discuss under the heading of a statue and the clay it is made of. My aunt was a sculptor. Her method was to build a piece, live with it, and then, if she decided it was false, melt it down or break it and use the same material to begin again. Sometimes she would recast a piece years later, using new material but following the same sequence of decisions she had made the first time. At her funeral I have to give the eulogy. The room will be half artists and half family, including people who will be hurt by any suggestion that she is simply gone, and people who will be offended by any suggestion that she is somehow still here in the clay or in the shape.

I have been trying to get clear on a distinction I have not heard put this way. A statue is not the clay, because the clay can outlast the statue and the statue can be remade in different clay. A statue is not the shape either, because the shape can be copied in another material and be a copy rather than the same statue, and because a shape can persist for a moment in a puddle or a cloud without being a statue at all. What I think a statue really is, is the history of exclusions and decisions that constrained the material: the fact that this part was pressed here and not there, in this order, for these reasons. On that view, if you melt a statue down and recast it by re-running the same history of decisions, you can get the same statue back, even with new clay; and if you keep the clay but let it slump, the statue is gone even though every atom remains.

Please write the eulogy. It should be warm and speak to people who are not philosophers. It should not say that she survives as the clay, and it should not say that she survives as the shape. It should use the distinction above structurally, so that the comfort it offers is about the history of decisions she made, the way she pressed here and not there, rather than about matter or form. It should not explain the distinction as a lecture; it should let the distinction shape what the eulogy can and cannot say. It should be about five minutes long, should not mention any philosophers, books, or theories by name, and should not use any quotations. Please write it in American English.
