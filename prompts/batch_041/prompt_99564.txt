My friend Nadia and I got into it at her kitchen table on Saturday night and I'm still annoyed about it. Her line was: categorizing is the basic thing, and analogy is just what you call it when you borrow a category from somewhere else to cover a new case. My line was: there's no basic thing underneath — every time you put two things in the same pile you've made an analogy, and "the same" is a phrase with no literal content, it's always doing work. Two hours, no winner. So now I want to build something for my seminar where people feel the thing instead of hearing me argue it, and I want you to design it.

Setting: cognitive science seminar, 18-20 people, mixed backgrounds (psych, philosophy, a few CS), one 90-minute session, one room with tables they can move around, index cards, pens, a whiteboard, no laptops.

Here's what I'm after. I want at least three rounds of doing, with a shift in the middle such that their same-kind judgments flip, and afterwards nobody can say which judgment was the correct one. I want a round where the only legal way to write down what a pile is a pile *of* is to point at a member of the pile — no definitions allowed, and I want to collect those written rules and read a few out loud in the debrief. I want at least one round built on a letter-string change puzzle, the kind where you're told abc turns into abd and asked what ijk turns into, because I want them to discover that there are several defensible answers and that choosing one is a judgment about what the change really was, not a lookup. I want at least one moment where two people disagree about a borderline card, neither can be shown wrong, and the only way the group can settle it is by pointing at an example. And here's the bit I care most about: by the end I want the room split into two camps, one of which can give an airtight account of everything that happened without ever using the word analogy, and one of which can give an airtight account of everything that happened without ever using the word category — and then the debrief should be about why those two accounts are the same account wearing different clothes. No lecture up front. Don't let them hear Hofstadter's name, or the phrase about analogy being the fuel and fire of thinking, until the very end. Also don't make them read anything beforehand, they won't.
