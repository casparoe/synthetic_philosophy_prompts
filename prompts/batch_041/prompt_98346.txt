For about a year now I've been meeting once a month with a group of roughly a dozen adults — a couple of retired clergy, a maths teacher, a nurse, a few people who just like arguing — to talk about the attributes of God, and we keep circling omnipotence and getting nowhere. Someone raises the old question about the stone, everyone laughs, someone says it's a silly word-game, someone else says no, it shows something real, and we end where we started. I'd like to stop arguing and instead run a single ninety-minute session as a structured exercise, so the group feels the difficulty in their hands rather than hearing me summarise it. What I want them to come away seeing is that every way out of the difficulty costs something they'd rather not pay, and that they can discover this themselves rather than being told.

The frame I have in mind: the group is a commission drafting one sentence — a definition of omnipotence for a statement of faith — and it must be adopted by a two-thirds vote at the end. Each participant arrives with a private conviction card stating one thing they personally will not surrender; I'd want cards of the sort that pull in different directions, e.g. 'God can do absolutely anything, with no exceptions', 'God cannot do what is self-contradictory', 'every well-formed yes-or-no question has a definite answer', 'power is not a power to fail', 'God cannot be the author of evil', 'God cannot make himself cease to be God', 'whatever God can do, he could have refrained from doing'. The commission gets only three amendment tokens for the whole session: whenever a test case puts the current definition in conflict with somebody's card, the group must either spend a token to revise the wording (and the revision needs the two-thirds vote) or the holder must visibly surrender the card, and the group's final score is the number of cards still on the table. One facilitator plays an Advocate who presents the cases one at a time and does not argue — only asks whether the definition says yes, no, or that the question is malformed — and a scribe keeps the running text on a board. I want a battery of cases that goes past the famous stone: creating a being whose choices he cannot override, making a past event not to have happened, making a being greater than himself, making it true that he never existed, commanding what he forbids, making two and two five, and a couple more of your choosing that are less worn. I'm told some medieval thinkers split God's power as it is in itself from his power as he has bound himself to act, and some early modern ones held that his power reaches even what we call contradictions; if either helps the design, use it, but I don't want the session to depend on the group having heard of anyone. Nobody should hear the phrase 'omnipotence paradox', or the names of the standard resolutions, until the debrief.

Please give me one definite design — the rules, the cards, the token economy, the case battery, a minute-by-minute timeline, the roles, and the exact wording I should use to open without giving the game away — and then the debrief, which should map specific moments in the session onto the three or four real escape routes (trim what omnipotence means, loosen what counts as a contradiction, or deny that these questions have answers at all) and show what each route gives up. Be concrete about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect one person to dominate, and I expect the group to reach for 'that question is meaningless' far too early because it feels like a win — and tell me how to handle each failure without rescuing them. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: it cannot make anyone feel the actual weight of the claim that God's power is real and good, and it can't reproduce the possibility that some thinkers regard the whole thing as a pseudo-problem, so tell me plainly what I'll have to supply from the front of the room. And commit: tell me which escape route you think is actually the most defensible, and why, rather than listing them evenly and leaving me to choose.
