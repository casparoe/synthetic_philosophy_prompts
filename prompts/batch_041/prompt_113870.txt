I've spent the last two months reporting on negotiations — a hospital ethics board, a zoning fight over a wetland, a union contract, a couple of ugly divorces — and I keep noticing that my gut sorts the deals into two piles even when the structure looks the same. I'm supposed to write a piece about what makes a compromise legitimate, and I cant get my own verdicts to line up. So I'm outsourcing the theory part.

Here's what I think, case by case.

Roommates, one vegetarian, one not. They agree meat gets cooked at home only on weekends. Fine. Both prefer it to the silent-war alternative.

Two parents, one observant Catholic, one atheist, agree the kids go to mass twice a month. Fine. Nobody's core thing is being sacrificed, and the alternative — each quietly trying to win — is worse.

A hospital ethics board where the family wants everything done and the doctors think more treatment is futile. They land on a two-week time-limited trial and then withdrawal. Fine, and honestly maybe the best item on this list.

Union local gives up a pension bump for job security. Fine.

Two neighbors, one wants quiet, one plays drums. Drums before 8pm only. Fine.

Now the ones that bother me.

A county council splits the difference on a wetland development: half the acres developed, half preserved. Both factions hate it. Nobody thinks the resulting plan is defensible on the merits. I think this is worse than either side winning outright, and that it isnt really a compromise at all, it's an average.

A legislator who thinks surveillance expansion is a serious rights violation trades his vote for it in exchange for a healthcare expansion he badly wants, and would take the deal again. Both provisions pass. I think that's a genuine moral failure, even though he prefers the outcome to the status quo and nobody is made worse off by the trade as such.

A peace deal grants amnesty to people who did terrible things, in exchange for ending the war. The victims aren't at the table. I think this can be the right call — but I can't say why it's different from the legislator, since in both cases something that was never really the trader's to trade gets traded away.

Two siblings split an inheritance. One can afford to litigate for years, so the deal lands 80/20. I don't think that's a compromise. It's a shakedown with paperwork. But if 80/20 is what they'd agree to if neither knew in advance which side of the deal they'd be on, I suddenly think it's fine, which feels like cheating.

A friend of mine and her brother agree never to discuss politics. Nothing changes hands. Is that a compromise or just a truce?

And the one I flip on: a scientist who thinks the evidence is on her side agrees to soften the conclusions of a report in exchange for a stronger recommendation on something else in it. Some days that's ordinary tradecraft. Other days it's selling out the very thing she's supposed to be a witness to.

My tentative theory is that a compromise is legit exactly when it's a trade — each side gives up what it cares less about to get what it cares more about, so both come out ahead — and not when it's an average, and not when what's being traded belongs to somebody who isn't at the table. But the amnesty case breaks the second clause, and the inheritance case breaks something in the first. Also, doesn't "whoever cares less concedes" just reward whoever is loudest about caring? That seems like a differnt problem.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, tell me which clause each case is doing the work for, where it has to bite the bullet and overrule me, and what it predicts for a case I haven't thought of — ideally one where the trade reading and the averaging reading come apart.
