I've spent the last several weeks in a small reading group on confirmation theory — we meet in a back room of a pub, and the membership is me, a doctoral student in statistics, and a retired biologist who keeps trying to bring everything back to field methods — and the one topic that has genuinely unsettled me is the raven paradox. The setup is easy enough to state: the hypothesis that all ravens are black seems to be supported by its positive instances; the equivalence condition says that "all ravens are black" and "all non-black things are non-ravens" are the same claim and so must be confirmed by the same observations; and then a white sneaker, a red herring, or a green apple looks like it lends the hypothesis some support. Everyone agrees this is absurd, and nobody in the group has been able to say precisely where the absurdity lives, which has turned the whole thing into a running joke in my household. I want to make something of it for the departmental newsletter, which runs one humor piece per issue and is read by people who will notice if I get the logic wrong, so I'd like you to draft it for me.

What I have in mind is a parody of a first-person lifestyle or self-help column — the kind with a punchy first-person headline, a byline, and a numbered list of principles or steps. The narrator is a man who has decided to make a real contribution to ornithology from his apartment. He has absorbed the paradox halfway and drawn exactly the wrong lesson from it: convinced that he can do rigorous empirical work without ever going outside, he has resolved to examine as many non-black non-ravens as he can find and to log them as confirmations of "all ravens are black," and he has now worked his way through some thousands of white shoes, green apples, red herrings, and a single grey sock. His tone should be serene and slightly aggrieved — he genuinely believes he is being persecuted by a hidebound ornithological establishment — and the piece should never wink at the reader. The reader has to see the absurdity while the narrator remains perfectly content, and the whole thing should be legible to a colleague who has never heard of the paradox, even as the jokes reward someone who has.

Two things matter more than the jokes themselves. First, the epistemology has to be exactly right: every claim the narrator makes about the equivalence condition, about what counts as an instance of a generalization, about the difference between a thing supporting a hypothesis and a thing supporting it a lot, and about the fact that his white shoes seem to support "all ravens are black" and "all ravens are grue" equally well, should be a correct statement of the position he is parodying. The comedy should come from the application, not from any logical error he commits, and he should be internally consistent from first line to last — if he slips into a fallacy the reader can catch, the piece fails. Second, it should land, without ever stating it, the standard deflation of his project: that a single observed black raven would give the hypothesis vastly more support than his entire inventory of shoes, that confirmation comes in degrees, and that the whole appearance of paradox depends on treating "some support" as if it were "support worth having." I'd like a short block quote from the one friend who has actually read the Bayesian literature — an invented colleague, not a real named person — delivered in a tone of weary patience, and misheard by the narrator as encouragement. Around 800 words, headline and byline included, and please don't hang the piece on citations, titles, or dates of any actual papers; the narrator can mention Hempel or Goodman or grue in passing, but he should never be made to produce a bibliography he can't vouch for.
