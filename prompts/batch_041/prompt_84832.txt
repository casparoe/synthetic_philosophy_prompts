I am a high school student preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club on whether calling a condition a disease is a scientific judgment or a value judgment, and I would rather have one case built to order than a general summary of the debate. I want the case to make the difference practical, so that under one verdict the person counts as a patient and under the other they do not, and I want it to stand on its own, since nobody in the club has read much philosophy.

Please build the case for these two views. The naturalist view: a condition is a disease exactly when some part or process of the body is not doing what it is there to do; whether the person is harmed by it makes no difference to whether it is a disease. The normativist view: a condition is a disease exactly when it is bad for the person who has it, in the sense that it keeps them from living the life they have reason to want; whether any part or process of the body is failing makes no difference.

The case has to meet every one of the following conditions.

- Realistic: present-day medicine, an ordinary adult patient, an ordinary clinic or hospital, no science fiction and no invented biology.
- The naturalist must conclude that this is not a disease, and the normativist must conclude that it is. That is the hard direction; a case that embarrasses the naturalist is easy, and I want the one that embarrasses the normativist.
- The person must be seriously badly off, badly enough that a doctor would feel that something has to be done, and the harm must be serious and observable rather than a matter of mood.
- Nothing in the person's body may be failing to do what it is there to do. Name the parts or processes that matter and say why each one is working exactly as it should.
- The harm must not come from other people's attitudes, or from a poison, an injury, or an infection; it should come from an ordinary body doing its ordinary job in the circumstances the person is in.
- There must be something identifiable about the person, a state or a response that can be described and measured, for the two views to disagree about. The argument must be over whether that thing is a disease, not over whether anything is there at all.
- The two verdicts must be produced by the views as I have stated them, not announced. If you report that the naturalist concludes it is not a disease, I want the reason visible in the facts of the case.
- I cannot look anything up while I work on this, so do not cite anything or send me to a reading list; everything needed must be inside the case.

Please give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no opening paragraph, no closing summary. Keep each item to a sentence or two, though the case itself may run to a short paragraph.

1. The case.
2. The naturalist's verdict and the reasoning that forces it.
3. The normativist's verdict and the reasoning that forces it.
4. The one feature of the case that is doing the work, followed by two neighbouring versions of the case in which that feature alone is changed, namely the harm removed while the body stays normal, and a genuine malfunction introduced while the harm stays the same, with each view's verdict in each version and a note on whether the disagreement survives.
5. How a defender of the normativist view would attack the case in order to avoid the awkward verdict, and what that defence would cost.
