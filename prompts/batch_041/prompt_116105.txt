I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course on collective action and social structure, and the instructor has asked me to grade one of the midterm questions. The question asked students to apply the framework we built in lecture, so let me restate that framework briefly. On our account, a multipolar trap obtains when (i) two or more actors choose among options, (ii) the choice available to each is individually rational given the others' choices, (iii) the resulting outcome is worse for nearly every actor than some feasible alternative, and (iv) no actor can reach the better outcome by acting alone. We used the word "Moloch" to name this dynamic, and I stressed in lecture that it names a pattern of incentives rather than an agent, a spirit, or anything with aims. We also distinguished closed traps, in which the set of relevant actors is fixed and known to all, from open traps, in which new actors can enter and thereby shift the payoffs. The exam question read: "Question 3 (20 points). Using the framework from lecture (conditions (i)–(iv), together with the closed/open distinction), analyze the following case. Fivestar is a competitive online marketplace for hand-tooled leather goods. Buyers sort sellers almost entirely by two visible signals: price and dispatch speed, the median time between a buyer's purchase and the seller's marking the order as shipped. The quality of materials and finishing is not observable to a first-time buyer, although roughly a third of buyers are repeat customers who do learn over time which sellers maintain quality. Sellers can spend their limited working hours either on quality or on speed. The return on quality is not fixed: when most sellers maintain quality, buyers come to expect it and will pay a premium, so quality pays; when most sellers cut quality, a seller who alone maintains quality is out-competed on price and speed and cannot recoup the cost. Over the past year, several sellers have begun marking orders as shipped before the goods have actually left their workshops, a practice the platform's rules forbid but detect only when buyers complain, which is rare and slow. Sellers say privately that they would prefer to compete on quality, but a seller who slows down to improve quality loses ranking and sales within weeks, while a seller who pads dispatch times gains ranking and rarely faces consequences. New sellers may join the platform at any time. (a) Is Fivestar's situation a multipolar trap, and if so, is it a closed or an open one? Explain the structural features that support your answer. (b) Sellers often propose that they collectively agree to slow down and compete on quality rather than on dispatch speed. Explain why that proposal is unstable, and state what, if anything, could make the better outcome reachable. (c) Suppose a seller claimed she could simply ignore the speed metric and compete on quality. Does the availability of that option show that this is not a multipolar trap? Explain."

The student's answer read: "3(a). This is a multipolar trap, and it is an open one. A multipolar trap occurs when several actors each pursue their own interest and thereby produce an outcome worse for all of them than some feasible alternative. That is what happens here. The mechanism is comparative: the return on spending hours on quality falls as other sellers cut quality, because the seller who keeps spending on finishing is out-competed on the two signals buyers actually use, price and speed. Since defecting—cutting quality or padding dispatch times—is a strictly dominant strategy, no seller can improve her position by unilaterally holding to the higher standard; she simply loses sales. The trap is open rather than closed, because the platform admits new sellers at any time, and each new entrant renews the competitive pressure on the incumbents. 3(b). The usual proposal—that sellers agree to slow down and compete on quality—cannot work, because any such agreement is unstable. Each signatory gains by secretly defecting: she keeps whatever benefit comes from the agreement while still padding her dispatch times. The platform cannot detect the defection, because it relies on buyer complaints, which are rare and slow. This is why the case deserves the name 'Moloch': a force that drives rational agents toward an outcome none of them wants. It follows that the outcome is bad by definition. The only real remedy is an external authority able to detect and punish defection, since internal coordination among self-interested sellers is impossible. 3(c). One might object that a seller could simply ignore the speed metric and build a reputation for quality. This does not show that the case is not a multipolar trap. Reputation takes months to accrue and is invisible to the first-time buyers who dominate the market, so ignoring the metric is not a route by which a seller can stay in business and do better; she would lose sales and exit before the reputation paid off. So the availability of the option does not violate the trap's condition that no actor can reach the better outcome alone."
