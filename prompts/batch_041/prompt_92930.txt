The question the student chose came from the unit on assertion, and read as follows:

Question 3 (40 marks). The knowledge account of assertion holds that one may assert a proposition only if one knows it.

(a) Set out the knowledge account and the central argument offered for it. [8 marks]

(b) Set out one rival account of the norm of assertion and explain how it deals with the same data. [12 marks]

(c) Read the following case, then say which account, if any, handles it best, and why. [20 marks]

The understudy. Nadia is a trainee anaesthetist in her second year. Over eighteen months she has watched Dr Osei intubate several hundred patients, and she has become extremely good at reading the monitor and the movement of the patient's chest: in every case so far her judgement about whether the tube has gone into the oesophagus rather than the trachea has been correct, and she arrives at it immediately, without being able to say what cues she is going on. During one procedure Dr Osei is scrubbed and cannot see the monitor. Nadia can, and she says out loud, "It's gone down the wrong way." She is right, and the team corrects the placement. Afterwards she is reprimanded: hospital protocol reserves that announcement to the operator, and her remark caused two members of the team to freeze. Nadia is confident she was right, and she was. Assess the case in the light of the accounts you have discussed, and say what it shows about the norm of assertion.

Here is the script as the student wrote it, transcribed with no corrections:

(a) The knowledge account says that the norm of assertion is knowledge: one must assert only what one knows. The standard argument for it turns on the oddity of asserting something while admitting that one does not know it. "The bank is open, but I don't know that it is" is not merely a conversational infelicity; it sounds like a contradiction, in the way that "The bank is open, but I don't believe it is" does. The knowledge account explains this directly: the first half of the sentence represents the speaker as meeting the standard for asserting, and the second half represents her as failing it. Accounts that make the norm weaker — truth, or justified belief — cannot explain why the admission of ignorance is damning, since on those accounts the speaker may still have satisfied the standard. So knowledge must be the norm. It follows that an assertion is defective when its content is not true, since knowledge entails truth; a justified true belief that falls short of knowledge also fails the norm, as does a lucky guess. And since the admission of ignorance is what makes the assertion bad, a speaker who knows has done everything the norm requires: there is nothing left for her audience to object to.

(b) The main rival is the truth account: one must assert only what is true. Its defenders will say that the oddity in (a) is explained by the fact that assertion, like belief, aims at truth, and that the appearance of a knowledge requirement comes from the conversational impropriety of advertising one's own epistemic shortfall rather than from a norm of assertion as such. The difficulty with the truth account is that it lets a speaker assert anything at all, however badly supported, so long as it is true. If I have bought a ticket in a fair lottery with a million tickets, I may not assert that my ticket will lose, even though it is overwhelmingly likely that it will, and I may certainly not assert it if I happen to turn out to be right. The truth account has to say that such an assertion is in order, which is the wrong result, and it cannot distinguish the lottery case from the case of someone who has checked the ticket list. The knowledge account also has the resources to explain testimony: a hearer who has been told something by a competent source, and who has no reason to doubt it, may pass it on, which is what we would expect if knowledge is the norm.

(c) The understudy case is best handled by the knowledge account. Nadia's belief is formed by a process that has been reliable across several hundred cases, and she does not have to work it out; on any reasonable view, she knows that the tube is misplaced. Her assertion therefore satisfies the norm of assertion, and its content is true as well, so the truth account also permits it. The trouble is that her assertion was improper all the same, and the reason has nothing to do with truth or knowledge: the protocol reserves the announcement to the operator, and the announcement had bad consequences. This is a difficulty for the knowledge account, because it shows that a speaker can fully satisfy the norm of assertion and still be legitimately criticised for asserting. A complete account would need a further condition, perhaps that the speaker be entitled to speak for the team, which the knowledge account does not supply. The reasonable-belief account does better here, because Nadia's belief is reasonable, and the criticism is really about the effects of her speaking rather than about her epistemic position. The case does suggest a limit, though: if we describe Nadia as having a strong hunch rather than knowledge, then whether she may assert depends on whether a reliable process that she cannot describe yields knowledge, which is exactly what is at issue between reliabilists and their critics.
