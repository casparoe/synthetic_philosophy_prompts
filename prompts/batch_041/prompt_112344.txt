I'm writing the final for an upper-level seminar I teach on 18th-century political economy, and I want one essay question on Hume's monetary essays that can't be answered by reciting "Hume was a quantity theorist, so money is neutral." The question should force students to reconcile three things that sit uneasily together: (1) the oil-and-wheels line in "Of Money" (money isn't properly one of the subjects of commerce, it's the oil that makes the wheels turn more smooth and easy); (2) the claim in that same essay that while prices are still adjusting to a new influx of metal the augmentation "may have some influence, by exciting industry," but that "after the prices are settled, suitably to the new abundance of gold and silver, it has no manner of influence"; and (3) his argument in "Of Interest" that the rate of interest isn't derived from the quantity of the precious metals at all. I want students to have to say what work the long-run/short-run distinction is actually doing here — is the transition-period effect a real exception to neutrality, or something else entirely (a distributional story about who gets the new money first, a claim about expectations lagging prices, a claim about industry being "excited" before the price level catches up)? — and whether his nervousness about banks and paper credit fits that same picture or pulls the other way. Don't let them coast on "Hume thinks money is a veil," because he doesn't quite say that: he concedes that plenty of money is useful in wars and foreign negotiations, and in "Of the Balance of Trade" he says dreading that all our coin will leave is about as sensible as dreading that all our rivers and springs will dry up.

Two things I want them to avoid: turning Hume into a proto-New Keynesian, and turning him into a simple monetarist. Both are anachronistic, just in opposite directions.

So: write the essay question itself, then a model answer of about 600 words, then a marking scheme. Build the marking scheme around five must-hit points, marks out of 20, and for each one a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong. The model answer should model the reasoning I'm after rather than just asserting conclusions.

It's a closed-book 90-minute final, so the question has to be answerable without any texts in front of them. Everything in American English, please. And thanks — I've been going back and forth on this one for a while and I definately want the crux to be the transition-period passage, since that's where the interesting reading lives and where most of my students just gloss over it.
