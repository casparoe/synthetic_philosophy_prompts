My friend Nadia and I got into it for like three hours last night about whether it even makes sense to say that one person is better off than another, or that one person's suffering is worse than another's. It started from something dumb — our department has a tiny discretionary fund and we were arguing about whether it should go to the chronic pain program or the befriending/loneliness one, and whether saying "the pain program does more good" is a claim about the world or just a way of talking. Nadia is basically an ordinalist hardliner: she thinks any statement that ranks my pain against your loneliness is either a disguised ethical fiat or a convention we've agreed to adopt, and that policy and economics can and should get along on ordinal preference information alone. I'm on the other side — I think there's a fact of the matter about at least some of these comparisons, that you can't even state most distributive questions without them, and that the fact that they're vague and hard to measure isn't the same as their being definately invented. We kept talking past each other and I think alot of it was a fight about words.

I want the document we should have written instead of shouting at each other: a joint statement we could both actually sign. Agreed claims first, numbered — about what each of us is and isn't claiming, about what ordinal data can and can't settle, about what it would take for an interpersonal comparison to be "meaningful" at all, about whether vagueness is fatal, and about the difference between a comparison being true and a comparison being measurable or checkable by a third party. Then the disagreements, each one worded so that the person who holds it would accept the wording as fair — that's the hard part, so don't strawman either side and don't write a mush that neither of us would really put our name to.

I especially want the cruxes, and I suspect the real ones are these: (i) whether "comparability" is a claim about the structure of welfare itself or about the availability of a canonical measurement procedure — I think we were sliding between those two the whole night; (ii) whether a meaningful comparison has to stay within a kind (my pain vs your pain, my loneliness vs your loneliness) or can cross kinds (my pain vs your loneliness, my grief vs your lost career) — Nadia floated something like "comparisons are meaningful exactly when the two states are states of the same determinable," and I never got a straight answer about whether that was supposed to be a claim about meaning or about measurement; (iii) whether the fact that nobody can get inside both heads at once is any evidence that there's nothing there to compare. For each crux, write down a case, argument, or thought experiment that we would both agree bears on it — something neither of us could just wave away — and then write what each of us would predict about that case, in advance, in our own voices. If you think one of those three isn't a real crux, or that some part of the fight is purely verbal, say which part and why. And finish with a short bit on what would actually move each of us, because I want to know whether this is the kind of disagreement evidence could ever settle or whether it's downstream of something more basic that we haven't named yet.
