I chair the research-integrity committee at a mid-sized university, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's hearing on a case that has split us badly. Our misconduct policy recognizes exactly two grades of violation: a "knowing" one, which triggers the severe sanction (a multi-year funding ban and a notation in the personnel file), and a "negligent" one, which draws a lesser sanction plus a remediation plan. There is no third box to check. The faculty member before us failed to disclose a long-running consulting relationship with a company whose product his lab was independently evaluating; the relationship was substantial, and everyone agrees it should have been disclosed. His defense is not that he forgot. He says that over several years he genuinely came to regard the arrangement as "just science" and never as a financial interest at all — and, this is the part that unsettles me, he describes steering away from his own disclosure forms, letting the renewal emails sit unread, and arranging his schedule so that the question of whether this counted as a conflict never quite came up. He does not appear to be lying now.

My friend, who teaches philosophy, spent most of dinner arguing that the whole plea is incoherent: to deceive someone you have to know the truth you are concealing, but to be deceived you must not know it, so the man either knew and is lying or never knew and is merely negligent. I pushed back that this seems too quick — that the interesting case is precisely the one where the belief is cultivated rather than chosen, where someone is responsible for the drift of their attention even if no single moment of self-lying can be located — and we ended up against the same wall: whether what he did was a strange but real species of self-deception, or a self-serving story that merely borrows the vocabulary of self-deception. I have to write something the committee can actually act on, and half of them will not have thought about this before.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete disposition — whether his conduct is "knowing," "negligent," or whether we should create a third category and what it would cover — with the reasoning laid out so that a room of non-specialists can follow it, and it should take the strongest opposing consideration seriously rather than brushing it aside. If you think the policy's two boxes are themselves the real problem, say so and propose the fix; but give me a bottom line I can defend in the meeting.
