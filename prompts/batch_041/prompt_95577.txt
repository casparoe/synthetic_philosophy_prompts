I am a high school student, and the argument that has most changed my mind concerns the unknowability of consequences. The position I now hold is this: because the effects of any action ramify without limit through an interacting web of people, institutions, and physical processes, the total long-run balance of good and harm produced by an ordinary act is not merely hard to estimate but permanently beyond anyone's reach; and therefore a theory that identifies right action with the action producing the best total consequences cannot, even in principle, tell anyone what to do. I have seen this called cluelessness, and I am inclined to treat it as close to decisive.

What would count against it? I would like the objections sorted by how much they should move me, from fatal to merely awkward, and stated as defeaters rather than as expressions of distaste. In particular:

First, which premises does the argument need, and what would have to be true for each to fail? It seems to require at least these: that causal influence disperses rather than concentrates; that a moral criterion must be usable by the agents it judges; and that the failure lies with the theory rather than with our limited information. Take each in turn and say what the world would have to look like for it to be false.

Second, is the problem symmetric? Deontological and virtue-ethical acts also have ramifying effects. If cluelessness afflicts every view, does that exonerate consequentialism, or does it indict the whole idea that a moral theory should guide action? And if the problem is not symmetric — if a view that forbids certain acts regardless of their consequences can dodge it entirely — that strikes me as a serious point in its favor, so I want to know whether the dodge really works.

Third, does the expected-value response defuse the argument or merely relocate it? If the consequentialist says that only a probability distribution over outcomes is needed, and not knowledge of them, does cluelessness become a demand for information we could in principle have? If that move is allowed, does it run straight into the opposite objection — that once the distribution has a long enough tail, expected value is dominated by minuscule probabilities of enormous goods, so the theory becomes fanatical rather than clueless? Do cluelessness and fanaticism together form a pincer that no version of consequentialism escapes, or is there a version that avoids both?

Fourth, I have wondered whether the argument quietly assumes that influence scatters rather than settles — that small perturbations are absorbed by larger stable structures instead of propagating forever. What would count as evidence either way? If most effects are damped and only a few leverage points matter, does the argument survive?

Fifth, does it matter whether the value of an outcome depends on how it was brought about? If some downstream effects cannot count for or against the act at all, the problem shrinks; if value attaches only to states of the world, it grows.

Tell me what a defender of the argument would concede counts against it, not only what critics say. And be honest about whether the argument has been insulated into unfalsifiability — whether every counterexample can be absorbed by widening the uncertainty — and whether that is a defect in a thesis about our ignorance or simply what such a thesis looks like. What I would personally find most persuasive is a class of actions whose consequences we can rank with confidence; if there is one, the argument is weaker than I think.
