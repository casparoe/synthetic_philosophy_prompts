I'm running a one-off session for a general-audience discussion group I go to -- mostly non-historians (a couple of doctors, an engineer, a retired teacher), no assigned reading, and I've got about an hour. The topic I pitched is "blaming a war": I want to walk them through the Fischer controversy -- Fritz Fischer's claim in the early 60s that Germany bore the primary responsibilty for the First World War, and the fight it kicked off among historians -- but I don't actually care whether they remember Fischer or his book; what I want them to come away with is a clearer grip on what it even means to hold a nation responsible for a war. So don't explain the controversy to me, plan how I should teach it. What has to come first so that the question makes sense to them at all? My instinct is that I have to get the difference between "who caused this" and "who is to blame for this" in place before anything else works, and maybe also the idea that blaming is something we do, a verdict we issue, rather than a fact we discover lying around in the archives -- tell me if that instinct is wrong or just incomplete, and what has to come before it. Then: what single episode should carry the whole thing? I was going to build it on the July Crisis as a whole, but I suspect that's too big and too familiar, and that they'll just nod along because they've seen the assassination of Franz Ferdinand a hundred times; would something narrower -- the blank cheque, or the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia -- work better as the load-bearing example, and what does the right example need to have and need to lack?

The misconceptions I'm expecting, roughly: that the debate is over and Fischer won; that if a lot of countries were responsible then Germany sort of wasn't; that you can assign guilt to a nation the same way you assign it to a person, as one quantity on one scale; that the war-guilt clause in the Versailles treaty was a historical finding rather than a political instrument; and that historians just read the documents and the documents tell you who did it. For each of those, tell me where in the sequence it should get headed off -- I assume some of them can't even be stated until something earlier is in place -- and what the audience will say out loud to signal it's forming. I'd also like one question I can put to them at the end that would actually show me whether they've got the concept rather than just absorbed my framing, and a list of what I should openly refuse to go into at this level (I'm thinking the alliance-system minutiae and the whole parade of historians' names, but tell me if I'm cutting something that's actually load-bearing). And keep two things apart for me throughout: what historians now more or less agree on versus what is still genuinely fought over, because I don't want to stand up there and present one camp's framing as if it were the settled story.
