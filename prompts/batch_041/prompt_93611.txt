My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes, and he left me with a chain of reasoning that I cannot fault step by step but whose conclusion I refuse to accept. It runs as follows:

(1) To hold someone morally responsible is just to be disposed to react to them with attitudes such as resentment, indignation and gratitude — the reactive attitudes.
(2) As a matter of psychological fact, we are disposed to react in these ways to almost everyone almost all of the time.
(3) So we hold almost everyone responsible almost all of the time.
(4) To be morally responsible is to be someone whom it is fitting to hold responsible.
(5) So almost everyone is morally responsible almost all of the time.
(6) Hence the standard excusing conditions — infancy, derangement, coercion — do not remove responsibility; at most they alter the flavour of the resentment we happen to feel.
(7) But the young child and the person in the grip of a delusion are plainly not responsible.

Tell me which step to give up, why it looked plausible in the first place, and what giving it up costs. I would sooner keep (1), (2) and (7) than keep (6), but I want to know what I am paying for that. If the chain turns out not to be an argument at all — if the task as I have posed it cannot be done well, because rejecting any step is absurd or because the conclusion simply does not follow from the premises — then say so plainly rather than manufacturing a tidy diagnosis to spare my feelings. Please write in British English.
