I sit on the collections and restitution panel at my university's museum — I'm the history department's rep, which is to say I'm the least qualified person in the room. There's a claim I have to write up before Thursday and the two philosophers on the panel have turned it into an argument I genuinely can't follow. The short version: a carved and painted wooden figure, accession 1912.7, came to us from a collector in 1912, and a community in our Pacific collections is claiming it back under the 1990 agreement with them, which covers "objects of cultural patrimony, whether or not currently on display." In 1961 our convervators found the bottom third of the wood riddled with insect damage, cut it away, spliced in new wood and repainted the whole surface to match. The pieces they cut away are still in storage, in a box labeled "original material." In 1974 a curator had the fiber skirt replaced. The figure is also out on loan until March, which counsel says complicates any handover.

Here's where I get lost. One panel member says the figure is a four-dimensional object composed of temporal parts, so the 1961 box of wood is literally part of the figure and handing over anything less than all of it is handing over a fragment. The other says that's backwards — the figure is wholly present at every moment of its existence, and she says the first view can't even explain how the figure can be whole in 1912 and damaged in 1960, calls it "the problem of temporary intrinsics," and considers it settled. They keep using the words "endures" and "perdures" and I've stopped asking. A third person said that if losing a part makes it a different object, then the figure stopped existing in 1961 and we've been displaying a replica for sixty years. And the community's position, as far as I can tell from the delegation's letter, is that the power of the figure lives in the original wood — that what the conservators did is essentially a costume the original is wearing. I'm inclined to think they're right that the wood is the thing, but our conservators will definately explode if I put it that way.

So: can you write the short written opinion I'll circulate at Thursday's meeting? I need an actual recommendation — what we hand over, in what order, and on what timeframe relative to the March loan return and the delegation's visit in June — plus the reasoning, and the strongest argument on the side I don't choose treated honestly, because if I misrepresent either the conservators or the community the meeting will go badly. Please don't just tell me what the agreement says; the real question the panel has to answer is what counts as the same object across its own history. So I'd also like to know which of those three arguments I should take seriously, which are confused or talking past each other, and whether the community's way of drawing the line — identity living in the material rather than the shape — is something a reasonable person could hold or is just a non-starter. No reading list needed, just the memo.
