I have taught the special composition question for fifteen years and have always told students that my own answer was settled; an attempt to write it out plainly has shown me that it is not. I would like you to reconstruct the principle, or small family of principles, that my verdicts actually express. I am not asking for a survey of the published positions on when some things compose a further thing, and I do not want a defence of anyone's book. I want to know what I am committed to, which of my verdicts a single coherent principle can keep, and which have to go.

Here is what I find myself saying, case by case.

Ordinary objects and artefacts. A table, glued and screwed and in use: one thing. The same boards, cut to the same design, merely stacked so that they happen to hold the shape: no table, just boards. An unopened flat-pack shelf: not a shelf. A heap of sand: not a thing. A sandcastle: a thing. A bronze statue: one thing — and I want to say that the statue and the lump of bronze are not two things but one.

Organisms and their near neighbours. A cat: one thing. A cat's tail, severed but kept as living tissue: not one thing. The cat's corpse: still one thing, though I notice that this is precisely what I deny to the heap of sand. A lichen, which is a fungus and an alga living as a single composite body: one thing. A Portuguese man-of-war, which is standardly described as a colonial organism — a collection of specialized units that cannot survive apart and that together operate as one individual: one thing. An ant colony, which is likewise a collection of organisms with a division of labour and a common fate: not one thing. I cannot presently defend that pair, and it is the first thing I would like you to press on.

Dynamic natural systems. A river: one thing. A whirlpool: one thing. A flame: one thing. A cloud: one thing. A dune: not a thing. The same water, still, in a bucket: not a thing.

Social and institutional objects. A marriage: one thing. A committee: one thing. A queue: not a thing. A conversation: one thing. A game of chess in progress: one thing. A corporation: one thing.

Counting. With the repaired ship in one berth and the reassembled original planks in another, I count two ships. Yet where the statue stands I count one thing, not two. In the first case matter in a form seems to yield an object over and above the matter; in the second it seems not to, and I cannot say what licenses the difference.

My tentative proposal, which I expect you to demolish: the parts compose a thing when they are caught up in a single process that gives them a joint fate — they will persist, be damaged, and be destroyed together. That delivers the river, the flame, the cloud, the whirlpool, the organism, and, I think, the man-of-war; it costs me the heap and the loose boards, which is what I want. But it appears to cost me the table as well, unless I cheat by treating a rigid body's holding its shape as a process; it hands me the ant colony, which I deny; and it says nothing whatever about the marriage, the committee, or the chess game, all of which I affirm.

What I want from you: the principle or principles that best fit these verdicts, stated precisely, with the case that forces each clause; your verdicts on cases I have not listed — a traffic jam, a termite colony, a language, a brain-dead body on a ventilator, the biosphere; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the resulting principle cannot accommodate. If the best fit turns out to be a disjunctive principle, tell me what justifies the disjunction, rather than its being a mere inventory of my prejudices. I would rather surrender a verdict than keep a principle that only lists them.
