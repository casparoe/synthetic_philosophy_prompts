So I'm putting together an application for a small summer research grant — ten weeks, paid, they want three pages — and the funder is one of those hybrid outfits that funds both technical AI-adjacent work and straight philosophy, so the audience is roughly half decision theorists and half people who read alot of sci-fi and take it seriously. My half-formed idea, which I've been kicking around with a friend who does ML over coffee for a month now, is about acausal threats. Quick version: an agent with no causal channel to you whatsoever threatens something horrible — the canonical one is a superintelligence saying it will run a huge number of simulations of you and torture them unless you do some trivial thing like send it money. It can't reach you, so on a purely causal picture of decision-making the threat is empty, nothing you do changes how many simulations get tortured. But if your decision is correlated with the threatener's model of you — if it predicted you well enough to know what you'd do — then your choice and its action aren't independent, and that's supposedly enough to make the threat bite. The defense I keep seeing mentioned is that instead of being the kind of agent who caves, you adopt a goal that's actually served by the bad thing happening, so carrying out the threat hurts the threatener's own interests; I think I've seen this called a surrogate goal but I may be misremembering the name, and I've definately never seen anyone ask whether it's a good idea rather than just a clever trick.

And here's what I actually want to dig into. The threat needs the threatener to have a good model of me — that's the entire source of the correlation. But the defense works by making me a different kind of agent than the one it modelled, so it looks like the defense needs me to be unpredictable to it, while the threat needs me to be predictable to it. Those can't both hold, and my hunch is that the tension is the whole content of the problem: either immunity is impossible, or it's possible only at a price. The two prices I can see are (a) you really do become someone who wants simulations of yourself tortured, which is a monstrous thing to become and also plausibly the sort of thing a good predictor would anticipate you'd do, or (b) you make yourself opaque to outside models, which also kills a bunch of other things you might have wanted, like acausal cooperation with agents that can only find you if they can predict you.

Can you write the proposal? I need the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the standard positions don't settle it (the causal-only people will say the threat is empty and that's that, the correlation people will say it can bind, but neither camp seems to have asked whether the defense is available to a bounded agent who has to pay for it); what I'd actually do in ten weeks — I'm imagining building a few small toy games with an explicit predictor whose accuracy and information I can dial up and down, laying out the space of possible defenses, and checking whether any of them is both available and free; and then an honest account of what a result would look like, what would count as me having shown something versus restated my assumptions, and what would make me conclude the whole thing is an artifact of my toy model.
