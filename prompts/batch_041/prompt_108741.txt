Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I retired two years ago after thirty-odd years as a physical chemist — molecular dynamics, mostly, little boxes of water and protein rattling around in a simulation — and since then I've been sitting in on the philosophy of science seminars at my old department. They've been kind about letting me ask what are probably naive questions, and now I want to apply for one of those fellowships that pays a retired scientist to spend a year on a philosophy project. Deadline's in three weeks and I need an actual proposal.

Here's the half-formed idea, and I'd like you to turn it into the document. In my old line of work the second law was never much of a mystery; it was just what you got when you coarse-grained. Bin up phase space, or average over a grid, and the entropy climbs. But the binning was always something *I* chose — the cell size, the cutoff, the level of description. When I press the philosophers on this they either point to the low-entropy initial condition (fine, that's a fact about the early universe, but it seems to be doing a different job) or they shrug and say the coarse-graining is just epistemic and doesn't matter. Neither answer has ever satisfied me, because in my simulations the grain did matter — change it and you change what the arrow looks like, sometimes you even loose it entirely.

So the question I want to pin down is roughly this: does the thermodymanic arrow of time survive as an objective feature of the world if the coarse-graining is an arbitrary choice, or does its objectivity depend on there being principled, non-arbitrary grains — the ones the dynamics picks out for itself, slow modes, near-conserved quantities, that sort of thing? And if it's the latter, is that a fact about physics or a fact about us? What I find interesting is a third possibility: that the arrow is real but *relative*, relative to a grain, which is neither the standard subjectivist line nor the standard low-entropy-initial-condition line.

What I need from you is the proposal itself. State the question so a reasonable person could disagree about the answer. Explain why the going positions leave it open. Don't bother with citations or a reading list — I'll fill those in myself, and I've been warned not to guess at references. Explain what I'd actually do, month by month, over a year, and be honest that I'm a chemist with a lot of catching up to do; the method will have to be conceptual plus small numerical experiments I can code myself, so say why that's enough to make progress here rather than the obvious alternatives. Say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and whether the whole thing is too big for a year. Two pages, no more.
