I am convening a two-year working group on the epistemology of conspiracy theories — myself, two other philosophers, two social psychologists, and a journalist who covers disinformation — and the first meeting is where we decide what the two years are for. The field as I read it supports two enterprises: arguing over how to define the category without begging the question against conspiracies that turn out to be real, and arguing over whether conspiracy belief is a distinctive epistemic failing or simply ordinary error sustained by bad evidence. I want to put a third possibility in front of the group, without assuming it survives scrutiny: that both enterprises aim at the wrong object, because much of what gets called conspiracy belief is not a report of what the speaker takes to be true but a performance — an assertion whose work is to mark allegiance, test loyalty, or announce that one is not fooled — and an epistemology built to assess the first thing cannot assess the second.

Which questions should a group like ours take up, and in what order? We will commit to three as the spine of the project, so the ordering is not a formality. I want the reasoning more than the inventory: which questions are upstream of which; which are tractable in two years for people with our mix of skills; which would change something downstream — how we teach, how platforms moderate, how journalists report — and which are fashionable but would matter little even if settled. Tell me what would move a question up or down the list, and which part of the framing above you would abandon first.
