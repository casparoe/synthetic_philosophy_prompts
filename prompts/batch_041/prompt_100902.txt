I am thirty-four, and my partner and I have been trying to decide whether to have a child. In the middle of that, I read an argument — the antinatalist one, that coming into existence can be a harm to the person who comes to be but never a benefit — and I have not been able to put it down. Rather than ask whether the argument is sound in the abstract, I want to lay out my own reactions to particular cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to, what that principle says about cases I have not considered, and which of my reactions it forces me to give up.

Here is what I find myself thinking.

A child born with a condition that guarantees a short life of continuous pain, with no compensating good: bringing that child into being is a serious harm to the child. Not merely unfortunate, not merely sad — a harm done to someone.

A child whose life will be, on balance, positive — more contentment than misery, though not by a wide margin: bringing them into being does not benefit them. I would not say the parents did them a good turn by conceiving them, the way I would if they later cured them of a disease.

My friend's daughter, now grown, would say her life has been good, and I agree that it has been good. But I still cannot say that her having been born was a benefit to her. That her life is good and that her birth benefited her seem to me different claims, and only the first strikes me as true.

An existing person in severe pain, whom we could relieve at modest cost: we must relieve it. The duty is strong, and it is owed to them.

A future person who would have a happy life, whom no one is waiting for and whose existence would cost no one anything: I do not think anyone is under an obligation to bring them about, and no one is wronged if they are never conceived.

But now the pair I cannot square. I think that refraining from conceiving a child who would live in unrelieved misery is a genuine good, a real reason — even though there is no one who is spared anything, since the child who would have suffered never exists to be better off. Yet I do not think that conceiving a child who would be happy is a genuine good in that same impersonal way; I do not feel the force of a reason to bring them about. Both cases compare existing with not existing. If the comparison can yield a good in the first case, I do not see why it cannot yield a good in the second; and if it cannot yield anything in the second, I do not see how it yields anything in the first.

And a case I keep returning to. A couple conceives a child in order that the child's tissue may save an existing sibling; the new child's own life will, let us grant, be good. I do not think the new child is harmed. I still think something has gone wrong.

Here is the principle I have been trying to hold, and I would like you to test it. Nothing can be bad or good for a person unless that person is worse or better off than they would otherwise have been; but before a person exists there is no one whose condition could be worse or better, so coming into existence can be neither a harm nor a benefit to the one who comes to be. Suffering, however, is bad wherever it occurs, and its prevention counts as a good even when no subject is thereby made better off; pleasure does not have this impersonal standing. Does that principle get my reactions, or does it get some only at the cost of others? Where it fails, is there a repair, or do I have to give up a reaction — and if I do, which one should go? Please also say what the best version of the principle predicts for at least two cases I have not listed, including one in which the person who would be created is guaranteed a life that is positive but only barely so.

I would rather have the truth about which parts of this are settled and which are not than a tidy answer. Among people who actually work on this, tell me plainly what is agreed and what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Where you are defending a position rather than reporting one, say so.
