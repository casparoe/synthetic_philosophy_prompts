I have a habit of writing down arguments I have witnessed or taken part in, together with my own verdict on whether the parties were genuinely disagreeing or only talking past each other. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to, and where that principle has to break. The list follows; each entry gives the case and then my verdict.

1. Hot dog. At a picnic, a cousin maintains that a hot dog is a sandwich and a brother maintains that it is not. Verdict: merely verbal. Let them agree on a use of the word and neither has learned anything, and nothing either of them cares about has changed.

2. Tomato. A botanist tells a cook that a tomato is a fruit; the cook says it is not. Verdict: merely verbal, and both are right in their own vocabulary, the botanist about the botanical category and the cook about the culinary one.

3. Birds. A museum label says that birds are living dinosaurs; a visitor says that birds are birds and that dinosaurs are extinct. Verdict: substantive. The museum has told us something we discovered about the world, and the visitor is wrong about the world rather than using a word differently.

4. Pluto. When astronomers voted to stop counting Pluto as a planet, I took the quarrel between those who wanted it kept and those who wanted it demoted to be merely verbal: they knew the same orbits and the same masses, and were deciding how to use a word.

5. Tables. A philosopher says there are no tables, only particles arranged tablewise; her host says there is a table directly in front of them. Verdict: substantive. I cannot point to any fact about the world that they disagree about, and a stipulation about how to use "exists" would very likely end the argument; nevertheless I cannot bring myself to call this a fight over a word.

6. Zero. Two mathematicians argue over whether 0 is a natural number. Verdict: merely verbal, and I cannot imagine a clearer case.

7. Average family. A neighbour and I argue about whether the average family in our town has 2.4 children. Verdict: not a disagreement at all, and not merely verbal either. He is confused about what an average is; there is nothing here for a stipulation to settle.

8. Money. A friend says that money is a social construction and that therefore a banknote is not real; a banker says that it is real. Verdict: merely verbal.

9. Octopus. My aunt and uncle argue about whether it is wrong to eat octopus. Verdict: substantive; neither of them would accept that the question had been settled by agreeing on how to use the word "wrong".

My own rough test has been this: a dispute is merely verbal when a stipulation would end it, that is, when a third party could say, let us use the word this way, and both parties would agree that nothing about the world had been settled, only a vocabulary. If a stipulation would leave one of them feeling that a claim about the world had been waved away, the dispute is substantive. I already know that this test is too crude. It makes the table case verbal, which I will not accept, and the only repairs I can think of make the hot dog case substantive, which is worse.

Please do the following. First, give me the principle, or the smallest set of candidates, that comes closest to my verdicts; state each clause precisely, and under each clause name the case that forces it. Second, apply the principle to the three cases I have not yet listed, below, and to one further case of your own choosing that you think is the hardest available test of it. Third, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot keep, and whether I should abandon the verdict or pay for it with an extra clause. Fourth, return to the case I keep going back and forth on: my colleague says that a language model understands the sentences it produces, and I say that it does not. Some days this seems to me exactly like the hot dog, since we would agree about every fact of the network's weights, training data and behaviour and would differ only over when to apply the word; other days it seems exactly like the birds, where one of us is simply wrong about a real feature of the thing in front of us. Say what your principle implies here, and say honestly whether the answer is stable or whether this is the case that breaks it.

The three new cases are these. (a) A housing code requires landlords to provide a working kitchen. A tenant says that a microwave, a kettle and a small refrigerator on a counter do not amount to a kitchen; the landlord says that they do. (b) Two doctors argue about whether a patient whose heart has stopped but whose blood is being circulated by a machine is dead. (c) I have kept a sourdough starter alive for eleven years, and not one cell of the original flour and water remains in it. My friend says that it is not the same starter; I say that it is.
