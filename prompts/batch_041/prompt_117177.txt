I write speculative short fiction as a hobby, and for a while now I've been trying to write a story that turns on a genuine metaphysical puzzle rather than borrowing one for atmosphere — my drafts keep collapsing into two characters reciting positions at each other while the plot waits outside the door. I'd like you to write the story for me, and to let the puzzle carry real structural weight instead of being explained in a monologue. The setting I have in mind is a few decades from now, where a firm called Cadence markets a service it calls origin portraiture: from a client's genome and their parents' stored gametes, it reconstructs the space of conceptions that could have produced a child of those parents — the same two people, the same night, but a different one of the hundred million sperm, or a different egg — and then renders a portrait of any point in that space the client cares to see. The narrator is an identical twin. When she sits for her portrait, the system reports that in the overwhelming majority of the reconstructed space the fertilised egg never divided, so the point corresponding to her own gametes is a singleton: same genome, same parents, same conception, no sister. It prints that portrait and hands it to her, and her twin, who has come along, refuses to look at it. Cadence's standard contract, I think, describes a portrait as "not a person and not a promise," which is the sort of clause a firm writes when it suspects it is selling something it cannot deliver.

I want the disagreement between the sisters to turn on the actual content of the question whether a person's origin is essential to them — whether the particular sperm and egg, and perhaps the division of the zygote itself, are part of what makes her her, so that there is or is not a possible world in which she exists and the twinning never happened. I'd also like at least one character to press the sharpest version of the objection, and to have it land as a turn in the scene rather than as a lecture: if origin is essential, then the sisters share an origin and are nevertheless two people, so sharing an origin cannot be what makes someone the same person; the thesis individuates nothing, and the portrait is trading on a confusion between a necessary condition and a sufficient one. The resident theorist Cadence keeps on staff to reassure clients should be genuinely committed to the origin view and should have a real answer ready, not a strawman's one — I'd like the reader to feel the pull of both sides.

A few constraints. Keep the science-fictional apparatus confined to the reconstruction technology: no brain uploading, no time travel, no simulation twist, and the counterfactual siblings should be inferred and rendered, never visited or conversed with. Let the story end with the metaphysical question genuinely open but with at least one of the sisters having had to decide something concrete — whether to frame the portrait, whether to show their mother, whether to count it among the family, whether to grieve it — so that the stakes are emotional rather than merely theoretical. Aim for roughly two thousand words, and give it a title.
