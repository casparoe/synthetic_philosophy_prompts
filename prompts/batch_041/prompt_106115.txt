ok, I've been reading about the discursive dilemma and I'm pretty sure I've found the obvious way out, but nobody seems to take it, so I figure I'm either missing something or everyone's overcomplicating this.

Set it up like a tenure committee. Three members, and the decision comes down to three linked questions: is the research strong, is the teaching adequate, and do we grant tenure (which requires both). Give them individually consistent views — one says strong research, weak teaching, no tenure; one says strong, adequate, tenure; one says weak research, adequate teaching, no tenure. Now tally it and you get a majority saying the research is strong, a majority saying the teaching is adequate, and a majority against tenure. So the committee as a whole is endorsing 'research is strong', 'teaching is adequate', and denying tenure. Blatantly incoherent.

Fine. But the fixes look trivial and I don't get why this is treated as some deep impossibility instead of a design problem. Fix one: before you aggregate, just check that each person's own answers are consistent. Fix two: don't vote on the sub-questions at all, just ask everyone 'tenure, yes or no' and take the majority — then the group's answer is consistent by construction and you're done.

So which of these is supposed to be broken? My suspicion is the premise-vote is just always the wrong call and the conclusion-vote is obviously right, and the 'dilemma' only exists because people are attached to counting the reasons seperately. But everyone treats it like there's no way out, so tell me what I'm missing — is one of my two fixes actually not available, and if the conclusion-vote is clean, why would anyone ever do it the other way?
