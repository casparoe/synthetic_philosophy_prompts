I'm a second-year PhD student in the philosophy of mind, and I have to open next week's reading group on pain asymbolia. The phenomenon is this: patients with insular damage report that a pinprick hurts, can localise it and say how intense it is, but show no aversion to it, do not pull away, and say it does not bother them. The session is about what such cases do to the thought that the badness of pain is intrinsic to the sensation, rather than residing in the affective and motivational response that normally comes with it.

Write me a villanelle in the voice of one of these patients. The two refrains should be the two halves of the dissociation, one carrying the sensory report and the other the aversion that never arrives, and the way they take turns and then finally stand side by side should itself show why the case is hard to place: the sensation comes through intact and on time, and something that ought to have come with it does not. Somewhere in it I want the clinic's scale, the nought-to-ten question, because the patient cannot answer it as asked: nothing about the intensity is in doubt, and nothing about it is unbearable. Leave it open whether what the patient has is pain minus its badness or something that no longer deserves the name, and keep the clinical and philosophical vocabulary out of the poem itself; a specialist should hear the dissociation in the refrains, not in the terminology.
