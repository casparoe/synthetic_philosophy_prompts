ok, I've been spiraling on the Kripkenstein / rule-following stuff ever since a friend tried to convince me that the "quus" move — you know, the function that agrees with plus on every sum I've ever actually performed and then goes feral on anything bigger — shows there's no fact of the matter about what I mean by anything. I don't buy that conclusion, but I've ended up with a pile of commitments that I'm honestly not sure are mutually consistent, and I have a reading group Thursday so I'd rather find out now than in front of everyone. Here's where I've landed:

1. There's a determinate fact of the matter about what I mean — when I say "plus" I mean plus, and "I mean plus" is true, full stop, not a useful fiction we tell ourselves.
2. What fixes that fact is my dispositions — meaning supervenes on how I'm disposed to apply the word, together with whatever I've actually applied it to so far.
3. The skeptical argument is basically sound: no finite run of past applications, and no disposition I actually have, can single out plus over quus, because for any finite stretch there's a deviant function that matches it perfectly.
4. The escape is the community: what makes it the case that I mean plus rather than quus is that my use is answerable to, and disciplined by, the shared practice of the people around me.
5. Relatedly, I think a Robinson Crusoe who's been alone since birth could still mean things by his words — being part of a linguistic community is how meaning gets fixed for us, not some precondition for having it at all.

One friend says (2) and (3) obviously can't both be mine, and another says (4) is just (2) with a social coat of paint. I can't tell if either of them is right, or whether some of these only look like they clash. What I want is an audit, not a lecture — I already know the arguments, I need to know which of my own commitments are actually in tension, which are fine, and what the cheapest thing to give up is. Please don't just say "they're all in tension, it's subtle" — discriminate. Also I'd rather you not lean on which of these Kripke himself actually endorses; I care about whether *my* set hangs together, so treat it as a consistency problem about the five claims.

Format, roughly:
- "My read of the package" — one short paragraph on what kind of view I've actually committed myself to, since I suspect it's more of a Frankenstein than I think.
- "Real conflicts, worst first" — the genuine tensions, ranked by severity, worst first, each with a sentence on why it bites.
- "False alarms" — the pairs that look like conflicts but aren't, and exactly why they dissolve.
- "Cheapest fixes and their price" — for each real conflict, the least costly revision and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package.
- "Bottom line" — a few sentences on what, if anything, I have to change to walk into the reading group with a coherent story.
