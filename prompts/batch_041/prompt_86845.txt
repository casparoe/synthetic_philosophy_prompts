I need an analogy audited and I can't do it myself. Setup: I'm in a small ethics reading group and one of the members has a move she pulls whenever we deadlock. Last week the question was whether she should break a promise to one person in order to help a bunch of other people. She's a consequentialist maybe 60%, a social-contract type 30%, and the leftover 10% is some Kant-ish thing on which breaking the promise isn't just a cost, it's forbidden, full stop. Her line was: moral uncertainty is still uncertainty. Its no different from not being sure whether the bridge is out — you put numbers on the possibilities, numbers on the outcomes, multiply, take the option with the highest expected value. 60/30/10, weighted up, breaking the promise wins, so she breaks it. Then the kicker: refusing to do it because one of the views says no is just refusing to do expected-value reasoning because one branch of the tree is bad. That's not caution, that's paralysis.

I'm fairly sure the analogy is carring weight it hasn't earned, but I can't say where. Audit it for me. I don't want a reading list, just the audit, and please don't leave me with "analogies are imperfect but useful" — I need to know which imperfections are load-bearing and which are trivia.

Format, in this order:

1. Bottom line — two or three sentences. Does the analogy hold well enough to carry her conclusion, yes or no.
2. The mapping — a table, one row per element: her moral theories, her credences in them, the "value" of an outcome, the outcomes themselves, the maximization rule, the option of gathering more information before deciding. 4. Which breaks matter for what she's using it for — about 200 words, sorting them into harmless-for-her-argument vs. fatal-to-it.
5. Does her conclusion survive — about 150 words, verdict first, then what an honest version of her argument would have to add to get there.
6. A better analogy, if there is one — about 150 words. My own hunch is that the real analogue isn't not knowing the state of the board but not knowing the rules of the game. Better, or just a fancier way of saying the same thing? If you've got a genuinely better one, say so.
