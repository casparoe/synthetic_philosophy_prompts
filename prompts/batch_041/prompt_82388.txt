It's the dollar auction setup from week 6, just with different numbers, and the prof said a bare grade gets us nothing so I need to justify every mark I give and take. Question 3 (10 marks)

A seller auctions a £20 note. Bidding opens at £1 and each new bid must be at least £1 higher than the one before it. The twist is that when the bidding stops, the two highest bidders both pay the amount they bid. The highest bidder gets the £20 note; the runner-up gets nothing.

(a) Explain what keeps the runner-up bidding once the top bid has gone past £20, and say whether the escalation is best explained as rational loss-minimisation or as the bidders being irrational. (4)

(b) The seller now changes the rules so that only the highest bidder pays, and the runner-up owes nothing. Does the escalation disappear? Say what the bidding looks like and what the seller can expect to end up with. (3)

(c) The seller instead keeps the two-payer rule but announces that everything he collects goes to a food bank and he takes nothing for himself. Does that change the bidders' behaviour? (1)

(d) Is the runner-up's decision to keep bidding a case of the sunk cost fallacy? Explain. (2)

Their answer:

(a) The runner-up keeps bidding because dropping out is the expensive option. Say the top bid is £20 and I'm sitting on £19: if I quit now I owe £19 and get nothing, but if I bid £21 I pay £21 and receive the £20, so I'm only £1 down instead of £19. So the bid that looks insane, £21 for a £20 note, is actually the better of the two options open to me, and the same arithmetic repeats at every level. What matters is the gap between my bid and the top bid, not how big the top bid is. That said, the escalation has to stop somewhere. Once the top bid is above £20 the runner-up isn't bidding for a profit any more, and a rational bidder stops as soon as the next bid costs more than the prize is worth, so in a room of rational bidders the bidding should top out around the £20 mark. The crazy numbers you hear about, people paying £50 for a £20 note, must come from bidders who aren't doing the sum in my first sentence, because anyone who does it can see it's a bad auction to be in.

(b) Yes, it disappears, because the whole reason the runner-up was dragged along was the risk of paying for nothing. If only the winner pays then quitting costs me nothing, so there's no loss to run away from. It becomes an ordinary auction: people bid up towards £20 and one of them takes it at something close to £20, and the seller is left holding roughly the £20 he was auctioning off, so he ends up with nothing out of it. He'd have no reason to run this version.

(c) It should calm things down. The trap only exists because the seller profits from the escalation, that's why he runs it, so if he hands the money to a food bank he has no reason to set the trap and the bidders can treat it as a normal auction again. Maybe a few people bid higher out of goodwill, but the escalation itself shouldn't happen.

(d) Yes, this is the sunk cost fallacy. The bidders are letting money they've already put in drive a decision that should only be about the future, which is exactly what the fallacy is, throwing good money after bad. If the runner-up ignored their earlier bid completely and just compared the next bid with the £20, they'd quit as soon as the next bid went past £20, which is where I said rational bidders stop in part (a). Same mistake as pouring money into repairing a car that's already cost you more than it's worth.
