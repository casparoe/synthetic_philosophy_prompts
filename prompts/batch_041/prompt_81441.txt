The question:

"Kripke's rule-following argument concludes that there is no fact of the matter as to whether anyone means addition rather than some deviant alternative. (a) Reconstruct the central skeptical scenario, including the deviant function often used to pose the challenge and why finite past usage cannot rule it out. (b) Explain the dispositional response to the skeptic and assess whether it succeeds, distinguishing the objections it must answer. (c) Describe the 'skeptical solution' and say what makes it skeptical rather than straight. (d) Briefly assess whether the skeptical solution answers the skeptic."

The student's response:

"(a) Kripke asks us to imagine a subject who has performed only finitely many additions, and only with small numbers. Now consider a function — call it 'quus' — that agrees with addition on every pair the subject has ever encountered but diverges beyond them, returning some fixed value instead of the sum. Every answer the subject has ever given is compatible both with his having meant plus and with his having meant quus. Since his past behavior, and any formula, image, or instruction he could point to, is a finite item, it cannot single out plus over quus. And if we try to make the rule explicit, the same difficulty recurs for the terms of the explicit statement. So no fact about the subject distinguishes meaning plus from meaning quus.

(b) The natural reply is dispositional: the subject means plus because he is disposed to give the sum, and would answer with the deviant value only if he meant quus. Kripke's objection is that this confuses what the subject will do with what he ought to do. The dispositionalist's difficulty is at bottom a single one: the subject's dispositions are finite, so they cannot fix answers for infinitely many cases. The much-discussed 'normativity' objection is not really a separate problem; it restates the same point, since where there is no disposition to answer, there is nothing for an answer to be right or wrong about.

(c) Kripke's 'skeptical solution' accepts the skeptic's negative conclusion — there is no fact of the matter of the kind the argument demanded — but denies that this licenses abandoning talk of meaning. Meaning ascriptions are not descriptions of a special inner state; they have conditions of assertability rather than truth conditions. They are warranted by the subject's participation in a communal practice: we are entitled to say he means plus when his responses agree with ours and we can correct him when they do not. This is, of course, Wittgenstein's own resolution — the private-language considerations show that correctness cannot lie in anything an individual does in isolation, so it must lie in communal agreement — and Kripke's real contribution is chiefly to have sharpened the paradox that makes the communal move necessary. The solution is skeptical in Hume's sense: we give up the problematic fact and explain the practice that remains.

(d) The solution does not refute the skeptic on his own terms; it concedes his conclusion and shifts attention to assertability. It earns its name precisely because it is not a 'straight' solution: it does not produce the fact the skeptic denied. But it supplies what we need in order to go on — a warrant for our ordinary attributions of meaning — and it suggests that the demand for a hidden fact was itself a philosophical prejudice."
