I'm in a third-year decision theory course and I've volunteered to run one of our sessions: 75 minutes, about twenty students, movable chairs, nothing fancier than index cards. What I want them to feel is the thing that makes Death in Damascus harder than Newcomb's problem — not that a predictor is accurate, but that deliberation has no stopping point: each verdict, once adopted, licenses the other, and the causal line ("the prediction is already fixed, so my choice cannot move Death") loops exactly as badly as the evidential one. They should run into it themselves, so nothing before the debrief should name or explain the puzzle.

Here is the situation, so you don't have to look it up. Death will search exactly one of two cities tomorrow — the one you are in now, or the one a day's journey away — and will search whichever city a predictor named before you decided. The predictor has never been wrong, and nobody gets to learn what it said. Staying costs nothing; the journey costs a night's sleep.

Two additions of my own. First, a side bet offered before the choice that pays out only if the participant survives, so that everyone has to commit to a number for their own survival. Second, one round in which a single participant's prediction is auctioned to the highest bidder, with the rule that a prediction anyone learns is thereby void, since they can act on it.

Give me the activity: roles, materials, a scoring scheme that needs no money, a round-by-round timeline with the words I read aloud at each step, and the point at which I should keep quiet. Then the debrief — which moment in the game maps onto the evidential line, which onto the causal line, which onto the looping itself — plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, what I have to say in words because the game cannot reproduce it, and how to keep the oracle's mechanism something I can explain truthfully at the end. If I get cut to 45 minutes, tell me which round to drop.
