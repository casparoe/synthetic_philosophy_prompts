I have been keeping a private list of my own verdicts about a single ethnic epithet, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they express. I will write the word as W and the group it targets as G. Everything below concerns that one word; where a case turns on a different word, I say so. What I want is the rule that best explains the pattern, even if it costs me one of the verdicts. I do not need names, citations, or a tour of the literature — I want the principle and what it implies.

Background I take for granted: W has been used for well over a century to demean members of G; it is now among the most severe things one can call a G person; some members of G use it among themselves in informal registers; and there is a separate word, call it W*, that sounds exactly like W but is the ordinary, respectful name for G in G's own language.

My verdicts:

1. Two members of G, old friends, greet each other: "Hey, W." I hear nothing derogatory here — not "licensed but still ugly," but nothing at all.
2. A member of G, enraged at another member, says, "You are nothing but a W." Derogatory, and not only toward the individual: it is G that is being demeaned.
3. A demographer who is not a member of G writes, in an otherwise neutral report, "Most Ws in the county worked in agriculture in 1950." I call this derogatory. He intends no contempt, and the word still does damage.
4. The same sentence, spoken by a member of G about G's own history in a documentary. Not derogatory; at most faintly odd.
5. A historian who is not a member of G reads aloud from a nineteenth-century letter that uses W. Not derogatory.
6. An actor who is not a member of G performs a scene written by members of G, in which a G character says W. Not derogatory: the word is the character's, not the actor's.
7. A speaker who is not a member of G, warm and affectionate, calls a G friend "my W." Derogatory. Good intentions do not reach the word.
8. A bigot calls a passerby a W; the passerby is not a member of G. Derogatory, and derogatory toward G, even though no member of G is present.
9. A student who has just arrived from abroad, does not yet know English, and is practicing the sound, repeats W without knowing that it is a word at all. Not derogatory. She has said nothing.
10. A native speaker of G's language, speaking that language, uses W* about her own family. Not derogatory; it is not the epithet.

The cases I cannot decide:

A. A fan who is not a member of G sings along at a concert to a song by G artists whose lyrics contain W.
B. A text-to-speech system reads a court transcript aloud, and the transcript contains W.

What I want:

First, the candidate rules. I assume the usual options are: (a) the derogation is part of what W means, so any literal use commits the speaker to it; (b) W expresses contempt, so the speaker's attitude is what counts; (c) W is a prohibited word whose use is licensed only for members of G, so what counts is who is speaking; (d) W carries a standing convention of derogatory use in the community, which survives the absence of contempt; (e) something about whose voice or perspective the utterance is made from. Say which of these, or which combination, actually fits my list, and be honest about the slack: if two accounts fit equally well, say so, and name the case that would separate them.

Second, apply the account you prefer to A and B.

Third, tell me which of my ten verdicts it forces me to give up, and whether I should surrender the verdict or complicate the principle to keep it.
