I retired from the lab a couple of years ago (biochemistry, forty years of it) and I've been trying to teach myself philosophy ever since. Slow going, but I've got a bit hooked on the experimental philosophy stuff — the little vignette studies where you ask people whether someone did something on purpose, and the answer seems to flip depending on whether what happened was good or bad. I think you'll know the one I mean: a senior figure in a company doesn't care about anything except the bottom line, is told the new programme will also do X as a side effect, says he doesn't care, and X happens. When X is polluting a river, people say he did it on purpose; when X is cleaning up a river, they say he didn't. I can't remember who ran it and I don't need the reference — I'll chase that up myself.

Here's my complaint, as a lapsed experimentalist. As a piece of design it's a mess: the two versions differ in several ways at once and nobody's bothered to control for anything. I can see at least three seperate things that could be doing the work, and I'm not sure the literature has pulled them apart:

- the plain good-or-badness of the side effect itself, which is what most people seem to assume;
- how much skill, foresight or control the agent needed in order to bring that side effect about at all (maybe harming takes less of all three than helping does, so we credit him with one and not the other);
- whether the side effect is the sort of thing that normally happens in that kind of situation — a normal consequence of a normal decision might get treated quite differently from a freak one, quite apart from whether it's good or bad.

There may be a fourth, something to do with our appetite for blaming people, but I haven't got my head round it.

What I'd like from you is a properly controlled pair of vignettes, in the spirit of that study, that seperates these explanations from each other rather than jumbling them up. Specifications:

- Version A: two foreseen side effects of one action that differ in moral goodness/badness but are matched on everything else — same agent, same indifference, same level of understanding of what will happen, same skill and control required, and equally statistically normal in that setting. Version B: same kind of setup, but now the two side effects are matched in moral valence (both mildly bad, or both neutral — your call) and differ only in how statistically normal they are.
- No executives, no companies, no pollution-versus-profit examples. Realistic, ordinary settings only — a household, a school, a farm, a workshop, whatever fits. No exotic machinery, no tricks of physics.
- Each vignette one short paragraph, written plainly enough that a person off the street would answer without confusion. Do not put the verdict in the participants' mouths, and do not build the answer into the story — the vignettes have to generate the judgements, not stipulate them.
- Don't lean on any names, authors or references. If you need a label for an explanation, invent one.

Then do the accounting. For each version, say what each of the three (or four) candidate explanations predicts, and why. Tell me which explanation survives both versions, and name the single feature of the case that is doing the work. Say what happens if you remove or neutralise that feature — does the asymmetry vanish, reverse, or stay put? Then put on the other side's hat: how would someone who backs the explanation that comes out worst attack my design — what would they say I've smuggled into the stories? Finally, if these two versions genuinely can't be made to come apart without cheating, say so plainly and tell me which of my specifications has to give and why.

Write it in British English, and finish with a last line in exactly this form, naming whichever explanation is left standing after both tests:

Verdict: <one word or short phrase>
