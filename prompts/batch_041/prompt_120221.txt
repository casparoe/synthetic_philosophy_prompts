I am taking a seminar on karma and rebirth, and a recurring disagreement in class has left me unsure how to adjudicate it. A classmate insists that karma is at bottom a theory of moral desert: on his reading, the familiar saying 'you reap what you sow' asserts that the universe guarantees that anyone who does wrong will eventually suffer in proportion to the wrong, and that this suffering is therefore deserved. Our instructor responds that karma is better understood as a causal law than as a judicial verdict — it operates without a judge, and no one deserves anything. I suspect the saying is doing several jobs at once, some of them platitudes, some of them substantive and defensible, and at least one of them neither, and I would like them pulled apart. Could you set out the distinct precise claims that 'you reap what you sow' could be making in a karma context — what kind of causal connection is supposed to hold between act and consequence, whether that connection is one of fittingness or of mere production, whether a persisting subject is required for desert, whether the consequences must be proportional to the act, and when and to whom they are supposed to accrue — and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or dubious, and whether it would actually support my classmate's conclusion? Please present this as a menu of readings with verdicts rather than settling on a single interpretation.
