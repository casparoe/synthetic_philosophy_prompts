I am a public-health physician by training and have spent the past several years doing excess-mortality estimation and injury-surveillance work in and around conflict zones. More recently I have been asked to sit on a small documentation working group whose remit is to describe, in a standardised and defensible way, what is happening to particular communities in one of those settings, and I have found myself in repeated arguments with colleagues — lawyers, historians, and one very determined statistician — about whether the word genocide belongs in our outputs at all. I know the legal history in outline: Lemkin's coinage, the 1948 Convention and its definition, the odd fate of the political groups that Lemkin wanted covered but the drafters left out, the special-intent requirement, and the whole contested business of who is entitled to do the naming and when. What I have no clear sense of is how the moral and legal debate maps onto the only analytic vocabulary I actually think in, which is the vocabulary of case definitions and surveillance. So I would like you, acting as a philosopher of law with a working knowledge of epidemiology and public-health surveillance, to do a careful translation. Take the central positions in the debate over genocide as a legal and moral category — Lemkin's original conception of the crime, the narrower Convention definition and its special-intent requirement, the charges that the category is at once under- and over-inclusive, and the politics of naming atrocities — and restate each, as faithfully as you can, in my terms: notifiable-disease reporting, clinical versus surveillance case definitions, sensitivity and specificity, misclassification and under-ascertainment, diagnostic thresholds, and the difference between a case definition adopted for legal or administrative purposes and one adopted for scientific ones. Brainstorm the mapping before committing to it, think carefully about where the analogy is doing real work versus where it is merely decorative, and do not be shy about telling me when a term of mine is being stretched past usefulness.

Then give me the residue report, which is really what I am after. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly — I suspect the under-ascertainment and case-definition material will, but I want to know whether that is because those parts are genuinely structural or merely because I am pattern-matching my own trade onto someone else's. Which parts survive only with distortion — I am thinking in particular of the intent requirement, and of the fact that the crime is constituted by the group-membership of the victims rather than by any intrinsic feature of the act. And which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all; my guess is that the normative claim that genocide is uniquely grave, the worst of crimes, will be one of these, but I want to know whether that is a defect of my framework — it simply cannot say what matters — or a sign that the legal debate has smuggled in a moral seriousness its own definitions do not actually deliver. For each residue item, take a definite position on which side of that fork it falls and give your reasons, rather than laying out the options for me to choose between. I would far rather have a committed answer I can argue with than a balanced survey, and I would like you to flag any point at which your confidence is low and say why.
