I am a novelist, and there is a pattern of behavior I can dramatize but cannot name, and I would like to know whether the concept already exists. A character of mine decides she wants a new apartment and, rather than fixing in advance what would count as good enough, sets her bar by surveying what is on offer: the first few listings establish a range, and her standard settles just under the best she has seen. She then rents something that a friend with a lower, self-imposed bar would have been delighted by, and she feels precisely as satisfied as the friend does. When the market is bad she is just as content; when it is good she is just as hard to please, so the actual quality of her surroundings barely registers in her happiness. I have versions of this in a subplot about choosing a spouse and another about choosing which of two finished drafts to send out. The odd part is that she is neither settling from exhaustion nor deceiving herself about what she wants; her standard simply tracks the best thing in front of her, so it can never perform the filtering work a standard is supposed to perform.

Is there a name for this? It is close to satisficing, but my understanding is that satisficing turns on a threshold that does real filtering and, when it moves, moves gradually in response to experience, whereas here the threshold is read directly off the current option set and shifts at once. It is also close to what I have heard called adaptive preferences, but those seem to concern people lowering their sights under deprivation, and my character's contentment is not confined to bad circumstances. If there is no established term, please coin one and define it; either way, say precisely where the described thing sits relative to satisficing, to adaptive preferences, and to whatever nearby concept you think fits best, including what it is not. Tell me how confident you are in your answer and single out the one point you are least sure about. Write in American English.
