I run the formal epistemology reading group in my department and last week's session turned into a proper fight between two of the regulars, Ana (third-year, works on credal norms) and Kenji (came over from the stats side, annoyingly good at this stuff). I was chairing so I stayed out, and honestly by the end I'm not sure I could've picked a side anyway. Can you adjudicate for me?

What we were doing was the dominance argument for probabilism — the one where any credence function that violates the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated by some coherent alternative, so incoherence comes out as a mistake about accuracy, no pragmatic stakes needed.

Ana's opening: the beauty of it is that it doesn't turn on the Brier score. Take any scoring rule that's proper, and the dominance still goes through, so nobody gets to say the result is an artifact of one particular way of measuring accuracy. Accuracy alone gives you probabilism.

Kenji's first worry: he says he's fairly sure the fully general version of the theorem needs the score to be additive across propositions — you score the whole credence function by summing the scores of the individual credences — and that once you drop additivity the dominance claim can actually fail, so the argument is assuming more than she's letting on. His second worry, which he clearly cared about more: fine, coherence. But dominance is a very weak test. It only rules out functions that get beaten in every possible world. It says nothing about which coherent credence function you should have. So where do the other norms come from? What makes my credences rational given my evidence?

Ana: that's what the expected-accuracy machinery is for. Conditionalizing on your evidence maximizes expected accuracy, and that holds for any proper scoring rule. So accuracy gives you updating too — you don't have to bolt on a seperate evidential norm.

Kenji: but that looks circular. Expected accuracy gets computed with your current credences. And any coherent credence function already expects itself to be the most accurate one available — ask someone sitting at 0.5 on a coin flip which credence she expects to score best and she'll say 0.5. So "maximize expected accuracy" can't tell you to change anything, at least not if you're already coherent; by its own lights your current function already wins. So the real argument for conditionalizing has to be about the credence function you'll have after you learn E — and that comparison is still run from your current credences, which means it presupposes that your present self is the thing to defer to. Deference to your present self isn't an accuracy norm. It's an extra premise.

Ana: two things. If you're already coherent, of course no purely accuracy-based norm is going to move you — but the norm isn't empty for someone who's incoherent, and that's who it's aimed at. And deference isn't an extra premise, it's what caring about accuracy consists in. Your current credences are your best estimate of how the world is; if you want to know which credence function is more likely to be accurate, the only non-arbitrary thing to use is what you already think. That's not circular, that's the only way accuracy can guide you at all.

Kenji: then it's vacuous for exactly the people it's supposed to guide, and the norm can't even speak to the case I want it for. My officemate is certain she's getting her grant renewed. If she's right she's got a perfect accuracy score, and by her own lights 1 is the most accurate credence available. Accuracy-first has nothing to say to her, and that's the case where I most want a norm. Also, "take your own information seriously" is the thing we're trying to justify, not the justification.

They where going in circles like that for another ten minutes — Ana kept saying that's just what accuracy is, Kenji kept saying that's exactly the thing I'm asking you to justify — and I called time. Then tell me what both of them are missing. And be honest about how confident you are in the verdict, and which single point you're least sure of — I'd much rather have a verdict with a clear flag on it's weakest joint than a confident-sounding one that's quietly a coin flip.
