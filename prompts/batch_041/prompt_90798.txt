I'm putting together the exam paper for a third-year undergraduate module on philosophy of biology (mostly biologists who've wandered into a philosophy option, plus a few philosophers who've never done any biology), and I need one essay question on what life is. Closed book, no phones, 45 minuites longhand in a sports hall, so it has to be doable in that time.

Before you start writing, put yourself in the shoes of someone who's marked this question a hundred times and is sick of reading the same answer. Take a minute to brainstorm what the actual crux is, and what a student would have to do to show they'd got it, rather than just writing down that viruses don't have their own metabolism. Then draft.

The thing I want the question to turn on is this pile of cases, which I'll be putting on the paper as a stem:

- a flu virus sitting on a door handle, and the same virus inside a cell
- a viroid: a scrap of RNA that, as far as anyone can tell, codes for no proteins at all, and yet gets itself copied
- a prion: a misfolded protein that makes other copies of itself misfold; no genome, no metabolism
- a mule: alive by anyone's lights, sterile, so no reproduction
- a mitochondrion: has its own genome and its own metabolism, but can't survive or replicate outside a host cell
- a digital organism in a computer simulation: it burns through a simulated resource, reproduces with heritable variation, and gets selected
- and my own invention: a chemical system in a lab that grows, splits and evolves, but only because a technician feeds it a catalyst that doesn't occur anywhere in nature. Turn off the feed and it stops dead. Is it alive, or are we the life support?

I want the question to force them to notice that each criterion we normally reach for (metabolism, reproduction, evolution by natural selection, cellular organisation, homeostasis) gets yanked in a different direction by this list, so a tick-box definition gives daft answers — and then to ask the deeper thing: is that a problem with our definitions, or is life not that kind of concept at all? Should we keep hunting for a definition, settle for a cluster or family-resemblance account, or say the concept is doing practical work (astrobiology, planetary protection, medical ethics, deciding when a patient has died) and is better left as a rough tool than sharpened? That's the crux. Please don't let it collapse into a list-the-criteria question.

Deliverables:

1. The exam question itself, a couple of sentences plus the stem. Answerable in 45 minutes.
2. A model answer, around 700 words, at a level a good third-year could actually reach.
3. A marking scheme: five must-hit points, marks totalling 25, and for each point one line on how students typically blow it.
4. A short note on what seperates a first from a 2:1, and a 2:1 from a fail.

They can't look anything up, so nothing in the question or the model answer should need a citation, and please don't build the marking scheme around whether they can name the right people. I want marks for moves, not names. Also, the scientists in the room will want to answer it with chemistry and the philosophers with conceptual analysis; a top answer should do both, so make sure the scheme rewards that. British spelling throughout, obviously (I'll be docking marks for 'behavior' — only joking, but still).
