I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I get the vocabulary wrong — but I've got a character in a state I can't get a clean handle on, and I've decided the fix is to have somebody build me a thought experiment to order instead of me fumbling around in the dark.

Here's what I need. Build me a case where the three big answers to "what is an emotion, anyway" give different verdicts about whether someone is actually in a particular emotion. The three I mean: (1) the judgement view, where the emotion just is the evaluative judgement — fear is judging that you're in danger; (2) the feeling view, where the emotion is the felt bodily commotion, the heart and the knot and the cold hands, and any judgement is either the cause or the afterthought; (3) the perception view, where the emotion is a perception-like state that presents the situation as being a certain way, with correctness conditions, the way a visual experience presents the dog as dangerous.

The direction I want is the awkward one: I want the perception theorist to be the one who says no — she isn't in it — while the judgement person and the feeling person both say yes. If that turns out to be impossible without rigging it, then don't rig it. Tell me straight that it can't be done, show me the case that comes closest, and explain what's blocking it.

Constraints, and I'll be strict about these:
- Present day, ordinary world. No brain in a vat, no evil neuroscientists, no aliens, no sci-fi. If it needs a lab, it's the wrong case.
- She has to be someone I could actually put in a novel: a particular person, a particular room, something at stake. A medication or a condition that real people really have is fair game. An invented syndrome is cheating.
- No stock examples. No fear of flying, no rollercoasters, no spiders, no grief at a funeral, no stage fright in a woman about to give a speech. I've read alot of those and so has everyone else.
- Don't stipulate the verdicts in the setup. If the scenario says "she felt nothing in her body but believed she was in danger," you've built the answer into the premise and the exercise is dead. The verdicts have to fall out of the theories.

Then do the work: one paragraph for the scenario, then a check on each of the three — what it says, why, and what it's counting as the emotion. Be explicit about which version of the perception view you're running, because some of them make the bodily state the thing that gets perceived and some make the evaluative feature itself the thing that gets perceived, and that choice flips the verdict, so I want to see you own it.

Then tell me the one feature of the case that's doing the real work, and what happens to the whole thing if I take that feature away — does it collapse back into the boring case where everybody agrees?

And finish with the counterattack. The perception theorist loses in my version, so tell me how they'd fight back, what they'd say the case really shows, and whether they'd have a point.

One more thing, since it changes what counts as cheating: this is for a scene where the chapter turns on whether she's lying when she says she isn't scared, so the verdict genuinely matters to me — it isn't just a puzzle. It probably wont matter to you, but it matters to the book.
