In my lab meetings a certain move keeps recurring: someone says that we only get one shot at this, and uses it to argue that a proposal to run a large empirical red-teaming study is a distraction, on the grounds that no result from it could be trusted when what we actually care about is the worst case. I suspect that sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and I want them separated.

Set out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing, from the nearly tautological to the very strong, and for each say whether it is plausible and whether it would actually license the use it was put to. Please include at least these readings: that some failures are absorbing, so there is no second trial; that expected-value reasoning is therefore the wrong framework, and worst-case guarantees should take priority over average-case performance; that the agent who gets one shot is humanity, while the shot itself is taken by a lab or a field; and that the worst case is not well defined until the space of options is specified, since the worst outcome over every conceivable policy is simply whatever the worst policy produces. Tell me which of these the person in my meeting probably meant, which are false, and which would genuinely support shelving the empirical study. Lay the readings out as a menu rather than settling on one.

I would also like to know whether the one-shot premise survives translation to settings in which engineers do manage irreversible risks without formal guarantees — aviation safety, reactor design — and what that comparison does to the argument. Write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a long email, and in British English.
