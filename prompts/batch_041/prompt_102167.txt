I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and each spring I teach a small seminar on modern moral philosophy to journalism students. The midterm asked for a short essay on Nietzsche; the question and one student's answer are below. I'd like a grade and a set of comments I can hand back.

Question. Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality is often read as a debunking argument: it uncovers the lowly, interested origins of our moral values and thereby discredits them. In a short essay, (1) explain how Nietzsche distinguishes the noble, or master, mode of valuation from the slave mode, and say what each treats as the opposite of "good"; (2) explain the parts played by ressentiment and bad conscience, and how the two differ; and (3) assess the debunking reading — does the Genealogy set out to show that our moral values are false or unjustified, or is it doing something else? Anchor your answer in at least one specific episode or image from the text.

Answer. Nietzsche distinguishes two basic modes of valuation. In master morality, "good" is whatever the strong, healthy, and noble affirm in themselves — power, courage, fullness of life — and its opposite is evil, meaning everything weak, timid, and petty. The nobles do not need anyone else to authorize their values; their valuation is spontaneous and self-affirming. Slave morality, by contrast, is born of ressentiment. The weak, unable to act out their resentment, take their revenge in thought: they first call the nobles "evil," and then, by inversion, declare themselves "good." This is the slave revolt in morality — a revaluation in which the values of the powerful are redescribed as vice.

Ressentiment is thus the engine of slave morality. Bad conscience is a different matter: it arises when the instinct to act, to discharge cruelty outward, is blocked and turns inward, so that the person becomes his own tormentor. The two converge in the ascetic ideal, where the priest gives suffering a meaning by reading it as guilt and sin.

The debunking reading is largely right. If our moral values are the product of the weak's resentment rather than of reason or insight into the good, then they are not justified; the genealogy shows morality to be a historical artifact with a shameful origin, and its claim to authority collapses. Nietzsche does not, however, simply recommend a return to master morality — his aim is to make us ask what these values are actually worth.
