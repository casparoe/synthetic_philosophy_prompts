I have drafted the essay below for an edited volume on historical periodization, and I would like your comments on it. The editors have asked for arguments rather than surveys, so please engage with the argument as it stands and treat its terminology as stipulative. I am not looking for a literature review and would rather you not tell me who has said what; I want to know where the argument breaks. Assume the reader knows how the ordinary period labels are used and needs no background on them.

Periods as Contrasts

1. Historians disagree about whether the Renaissance, the Middle Ages, and the Scientific Revolution are real. The realists say the boundaries track something in the world — a change in what people read, how they painted, what they measured. The conventionalists say the boundaries track the interests of the nineteenth-century scholars who drew them, and that the Renaissance is a projection. Both sides argue about whether the term names a thing, and both treat the answer as a single verdict: the Renaissance either is or is not an entity that the word picks out.

2. The dispute is badly posed, because period terms do two jobs at once. They index: they let us say when. And they contrast: they say that something was more so on one side of the line than on the other. Indexing is cheap and almost always succeeds. Contrasting is expensive and often fails. Nearly every argument about whether a period is real is an argument in which one party defends the indexing job with evidence appropriate to the contrasting job — we can date a change in the curriculum, therefore the Renaissance is real — or attacks the contrasting job by pointing at the indexing job's conventionality: the boundary was drawn for convenience, therefore the contrast is arbitrary.

3. The proposal is to drop the question of whether this or that is a real period and ask instead whether the contrast the term carries survives. A contrast survives when it satisfies three requirements.

Robustness. The contrast must hold across indicators other than the one used to fix the boundary. If the line is drawn at a change in painting and the contrast reported is a change in painting, nothing has been learned.

Scale. The contrast must not depend on the resolution at which we look. It has to survive shifting the boundary by a few decades in either direction — the boundary is often placed at 1500 for no better reason than that round numbers are easy to remember — and it has to be large relative to the variation within the spans it separates. Many period contrasts are visible at one scale only, which is why they can be defended and attacked indefinitely by adjusting the zoom.

Availability. The feature carrying the contrast must be one that people on both sides of the boundary could have recognized and could have disagreed about. Not that they would have used our words for it, and not that they would have agreed about its value, but that the disagreement was available to them. A contrast that can be stated only in retrospective vocabulary is not a contrast between two stretches of time; it is a contrast between two of our descriptions.

4. Applying the requirements. The Middle Ages is a residual: it is whatever lies between the classical world and the Renaissance, and its content is what those two boundaries leave over. It therefore fails Availability in a peculiar way. The term is not merely negative; it is parasitic. Whatever explanatory work "medieval" does is borrowed from the contrast it was introduced to set up, and the borrowing runs one way: we explain the Renaissance partly by its not being medieval, but we do not explain the medieval by its not being Renaissance. The Carolingian revival of learning is a problem for the term not because it resembles the Renaissance but because it shows the residual has internal structure that the term forbids it to have. A residual interval is legitimate bookkeeping and a bad explanation, and the requirements separate those two facts.

The Renaissance fares better than the conventionalists allow. There is a contrast in the circulation and imitation of a specific body of texts, and it satisfies Robustness and, at some scales, Scale. But it satisfies Availability only for the small elite whose self-description the term echoes; for everyone else the contrast is between the vocabulary of the few and the silence of the rest. The period is real for the people who invented the word for themselves and thinner for everyone else. This is not a defect of the requirements; it is the correct result.

The Scientific Revolution fares worst. The contrast usually drawn is between inquiry that deferred to authority and inquiry that submitted to method, but the feature carrying that contrast is the existence of a single domain — science — within which authority and method could be opposed. That domain is ours, not theirs; the participants grouped astronomy, mechanics, anatomy, and chemistry under different headings and had no reason to think of them as one enterprise. What survives is a much smaller contrast: an increase in the practice of publishing measurements and of settling disputes by repeatable procedure. That contrast is robust and available. It is also unexciting, and it will not support the claims usually made on the period's behalf. The grand period fails; the modest one succeeds. That is the shape of every honest verdict here.

5. The worry is that this makes periodization arbitrary. If contrasts are selected by historians relative to the questions they bring, then which contrasts survive is relative to those questions, and the requirements merely launder the arbitrariness. My reply is that arbitrariness is bounded by Robustness. Some contrasts hold no matter which indicator you start from, and those are the ones we should be willing to call real. Reality, on this view, is a property of contrasts rather than of spans, and it comes in degrees: the Renaissance is real to a degree, the Scientific Revolution is real only in its modest version, the Middle Ages is real as a residual and not otherwise. That the predicate admits of degree is not a concession. It is the whole point.

6. The weak point is Availability, which does most of the work and has the least defense. If the requirement is right, the Middle Ages and the Scientific Revolution are largely artifacts of our descriptions; if it is wrong, they come back and my verdicts fall. I suspect the requirement is roughly right but needs a criterion of availability that does not itself depend on the historian's vocabulary, and I do not have one. I also do not know whether Robustness can do the bounding work I ask of it without an account of when two indicators count as independent — independence is judged against a body of theory, and if the theory is ours, the bound is ours too.

What I want from you:

1. The equivocation. Somewhere the essay uses the word contrast in two senses — the difference itself, and the historian's claim about the difference — and if that equivocation is real it does damage in section 5. Give me the sentence where it happens, or tell me it does not happen.

2. Independence of the requirements. Are Robustness, Scale, and Availability independent? For each pair, say whether one can be satisfied while the other fails, with an example. If any requirement is entailed by another, say which should go.

3. The Availability requirement. I claim the Scientific Revolution fails it and the Renaissance half-passes. Test that with the hardest case you can construct — a period or contrast where the participants had no vocabulary for the relevant feature and where the contrast nonetheless looks real to you. Then tell me whether the failure is in my requirement or in my application of it.

4. The concession in section 5. If arbitrariness is bounded by Robustness, and Robustness needs an account of indicator-independence that I admit I do not have, is the position doing any work that "periods are useful organizing fictions" does not already do? Answer without naming anyone. Also say whether "real to a degree" is a defensible position or a way of not giving a verdict. If the answer is that the position is doing no work, tell me what the essay would have to give up to become interesting again.

5. Rewriting. Take the paragraph that carries the most weight — my candidate is the one on the Middle Ages — and rewrite it in your own words at half the length, keeping the argument and cutting the rhetoric. Then tell me what was lost.

6. Cutting. The volume's limit is 6,000 words and this will run long. Give me an ordered list of what to cut, with one line on what each cut costs.

7. The objection I have not answered. State in one paragraph the strongest objection to the essay that the essay itself does not address, and say whether it is fatal or merely unfinished. Include, if there is one, the objection a reader sympathetic to the conventionalist side would find most annoying rather than most damaging.

Do not worry about being encouraging. If the central move is a relabeling, say so in your first sentence.
