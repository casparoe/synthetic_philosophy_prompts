I'm building a teaching unit on the naturalism-vs-normativism fight over what makes something a disease, and I want a case built to order instead of the usual recycled stuff (deafness, homosexuality, the trolley-grade examples everyone's seen).

Build me a thought experiment with two paired scenarios. In the first: a condition that is a real biological dysfunction — some part or process is failing to do the job it was selected to do, in the evolutionary/functional sense — but it causes the person zero suffering, zero lost capability, and she wouldn't change it if she could; she'd honestly be a little annoyed if you called it a disease. In the second, mirror it: a condition that is NOT a dysfunction at all, the machinery is doing exactly what it's supposed to, but it reliably produces genuine suffering and real social disadvantage, and she wants it gone.

Constraints: match the two scenarios as tightly as you can on how much suffering and how much social disadvantage are on the table, so the only thing left varying is the dysfunction itself — I want to be able to see that the dysfunction is doing the work and not some confound sneaking in. Keep it realistic (something a GP could plausibly see), no sci-fi, no gene-editing hypotheticals, no perfect-foresight doctors. And don't let the "dysfunction" in case one be something trivially debatable like a harmless cosmetic variation — I want the functional-failure verdict to be hard to deny. Same for case two: the "not a dysfunction" verdict should be hard to deny, not a fudge.

Then, for each scenario, give me three verdicts: what the pure naturalist (dysfunction-based) says, what the pure normativist (harm/disvalue-based) says, and what the hybrid "harmful dysfunction" view says. Six verdicts total. After that, tell me which single feature of the design is carrying the disagreement, and what happens to the whole thing if you remove that feature. Then play defense attorney: for whichever view comes out looking worst, give its best non-question-begging reply — the strongest thing a smart defender would actually say, not a strawman.

One more wrinkle I want you to handle: add a third mini-case where the person's own comfort with the condition is itself the suspicious thing — a normativist would say she's only fine with it because of how she's been treated and what she's been taught to expect. Does that wrinkle move anything for any of the three views, or is it just a red herring that changes no verdict?

Last thing: flag how confident you are in the verdicts you're assigning, and single out the one you're least sure of and say what would change your mind about it.
