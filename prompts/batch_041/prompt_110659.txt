My friend and I spent most of yesterday arguing about whether human extinction would be bad in a way that outruns the deaths it involves, and we kept hitting the same wall: neither of us could say what exactly is supposed to be lost. I defended the Scheffler-style thought that what we stand to lose is an afterlife — being survived by people who go on valuing things. He then pressed two cases I could not handle cleanly. In the first, our descendants survive and flourish but have abandoned or forgotten everything we cared about. In the second, no descendants of ours survive at all, yet the machines we built carry the projects forward, libraries and arguments intact, indifferent to us. I found the first worse than the second and could not justify the verdict, and I still cannot say why the absence of future valuers disturbs me when the absence of past ones does not.

I want to turn this into a short agenda rather than a survey, and I have one semester to spend on it. Which five open questions in this area most deserve the work, in order? For each, say briefly why it sits where it does: how many other questions in the vicinity depend on its answer, whether it looks like something argument can settle rather than stipulation, and what would move it up or down the list. Treat the afterlife conjecture as one live option rather than the fixed frame, and tell me which questions are upstream even of it.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. No reading list, please; I want the reasoning, not the literature.
