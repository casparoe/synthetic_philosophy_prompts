I want to stress-test a position about inferring what people want from what they do. The position: a person's preferences constitute a determinate object that is in principle recoverable from behavior -- i.e., there exists a preference ordering (equivalently, a reward function, up to the usual invariances) such that an idealized inference procedure, given enough of the person's choices, would converge on it, and that recovering this object and optimizing for it is both feasible and the correct target for alignment.

I want the case against it, but not the standard list. Begin by asking what this position predicts that its rivals do not, and then work through the defeaters in order of how decisive they are rather than how often they are repeated. I expect the usual suspects -- the degeneracy of reward functions under optimal behavior, the entanglement of preference with belief and capability, the confound between reward scale and the noise parameter in any Boltzmann-rational account, preference reversals and intransitivity -- but I want them developed for what they actually threaten, and separated into the ones that are merely awkward and the ones that would genuinely sink the position.

Two wrinkles I want handled explicitly, because I think they are where the position gets interesting. First: suppose the demonstrator knows they are being modeled and selects actions to be legible to the learner, so the behavior is generated partly as a function of the inference procedure aimed at it. Does this make the recovery problem merely harder, or does it turn it into something self-referential that no fixed reward function can capture? Second: distinguish the reward function that actually generated someone's choices from the one they would endorse on reflection -- cases of akrasia, addiction, self-deception, or simply changing their mind. Which of these two objects is the position committed to recovering, and does that choice make it more or less exposed? If a defender tries to stay neutral by falling back on the notion that the choices just are the preferences, say what is lost.

Finally: tell me what a committed defender of this position would themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to what only a critic would say; whether the position has been qualified into something unfalsifiable -- by retreating to infinite data, to an idealized reasoner, or to revealed preference by stipulation -- and whether that insulation is a defect or a sign the position was never really an empirical claim to begin with. I would personally be most moved by a result showing that no amount of behavioral data can separate the preference hypothesis from a rival that fits equally well but implies different alignments; if such a result exists, spell out its scope, and if the best the defender can do is assign a prior, say so plainly.
