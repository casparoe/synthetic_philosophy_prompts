Someone in my reading group has taken to ending arguments about contested history with the line that history is written by the victors. It came up again over a town's official centenary account of a massacre, which differs from the descendants' oral tradition on several specifics: who gave the order, how many died, whether any warning was given. He invoked the slogan to argue that the descendants' version should win by default, since the official record is, as he put it, just the victors' version.

I think he is running together several claims that come apart under pressure, and I want a menu rather than a verdict. Lay out the precise theses the slogan could be asserting, from the near-trivial (the surviving documents were produced by whoever held power, so the archive is skewed toward them), through the substantive (the categories, periodization, and evidentiary standards of professional historiography encode the winners' interests), to the radical (there is no fact of the matter about the past independent of who narrates it, so no record has epistemic privilege over any memory). For each reading: whether it is true, trivial, or false; whether it bears on disputes about what happened or only on disputes about what it meant; and which of them would actually license his conclusion that the community's memory should override the official account by default. Then tell me whether the reading he needs also cuts against him, since memory is edited by whoever holds power inside a community, and what he would have to give up to hold the slogan consistently.

We are both longtime readers of the rationalist blogs, so I would rather have the trade-offs framed in terms of which account is more likely to track the facts than in terms of whose truth it is.
