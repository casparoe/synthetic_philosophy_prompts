I am a novelist three hundred pages into a book set in a jurisdiction that has abolished its prisons in favour of preventive confinement and supervised release — a public-health model of crime, as the statute's author calls it — and I have just noticed that the book keeps changing its mind about which of its characters is being punished and which is merely being managed. I need a procedure I can run scene by scene, in the time it takes to finish a page, for deciding when a character is getting what they deserve. Fix the order of the questions I ask about an act and its agent, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure concludes that a character is being punished as they deserve and where it concludes that they are being handled rather than punished, and mark the point at which it must stop and hand the judgment back to me because the question is metaphysical rather than narrative. Ground it in the dispute between the compatibilist and the free-will skeptic — whether anything like basic desert survives the thought that the agent could not have done otherwise, and what is left of responsibility if it does not — and tell me at each step which of the two positions that step commits me to, since I do not want to be borrowing one side's premise while telling the other side's story. Run it on three cases already in the draft: a man who beats a stranger during a dissociative episode and is confined indefinitely under the new statute; a woman who defrauded elderly clients and must repay them; and a guard who follows the statute exactly and comes out blameless at every step. Then tell me where the procedure returns an answer I will not want, which I suspect will be that my most sympathetic character is not being punished at all — a book I did not think I was writing — and what the procedure cannot decide. Assume I cannot do research mid-draft, will not put footnotes in a novel, and need every result to be something a character could say aloud in an argument without sounding like a textbook.
