I've been working through a puzzle for a seminar on social norms, and I'd like you to argue for a position rather than survey the terrain. The puzzle is this. Everyday conventions — where you stand on an escalator, whether you form a queue or crowd toward the door, whether you leave a tip, whether you cross against the light on an empty street — are enforced by those around you with the full moral vocabulary of blame, resentment, and contempt, as though breaking them were a wrong. Yet the same act carries opposite valence elsewhere: a tip that is all but obligatory in one country is an insult in another, and the pedestrian who jaywalks with impunity in one city is a menace in the next. If the very same act can be a duty here and an offense there, it is tempting to conclude that these conventions are not moral obligations at all but local preferences dressed up in moral clothing, and that a visitor from elsewhere is therefore under no obligation to observe them. I want you to argue against that tempting conclusion, but only halfway: defend the claim that some everyday conventions are genuine moral obligations, while granting that the class is far smaller than locals typically suppose and that a visitor's obligations are narrower than a resident's.

To give the argument some spine, please build it around a distinction I have been sketching. Call a convention structural when conformity to it is what makes a practice possible at all — where the point of the practice is to coordinate many people over a scarce resource or a shared hazard, so that nonconformity does not merely offend but defeats the practice or expropriates something from others (queueing for a shared resource, keeping to one side of the road, signaling before you turn). Call a convention expressive when the practice survives the defection of any single person without anyone losing anything they were entitled to, its function being instead to mark membership, taste, or mutual regard (a tip, a handshake, a particular form of greeting). Argue that structural conventions generate genuine obligations binding visitors and residents alike — but that what grounds the obligation is not the convention's locality, so the when-in-Rome slogan misdescribes its own foundation — whereas expressive conventions generate at most obligations of courtesy, owed by a visitor only to particular people she has already engaged with and never to the community as such.

Finally, press on the hard cases and the obvious objection. What should happen when the two sorts of convention collide — a visitor who finds a local expressive convention repugnant, or a structural one from whose practice she has been excluded: is she bound, excused, or merely permitted to opt out, and does it matter whether the exclusion is her own doing? And address the charge that your structural/expressive line is just a redescription of the intuitive difference between conventions backed by harm and conventions backed by taste. Write this as a continuous argument with a clear thesis stated up front, anticipating the strongest reply to each move rather than listing objections and rebuttals mechanically.
