I'm putting together the final session of a small philosophy of language reading group; we have spent the term on Twin Earth and the various externalisms it spawned, and I want to close with a piece of fiction rather than yet another paper. The piece is Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote": a minor French symbolist concludes that the interesting thing is not to write another Quixote, which anyone could do, but to write the Quixote itself, and he sets about composing a few chapters that turn out to coincide with Cervantes's word for word; the narrator then insists that the Menard text is richer than the original, because the very same sentences, written by a twentieth-century Frenchman with a particular intellectual biography behind him, are loaded with a history that Cervantes's sentences could not have carried. What I want from you is a reading of the story through the externalism we have been studying — the thesis that what a speaker's words mean, and what her thoughts are about, depends on facts outside her head: her environment, her community, the causal history of her terms — and I want the reading to be faithful in both directions, to the story and to the philosophy, rather than using one to illustrate the other.

The difficulty I keep running into, and would like you to take seriously rather than dissolve, is that the analogy is not obviously an analogy at all. Twin Earth holds the subject's inner state fixed and varies the world; Menard seems to hold the text fixed and vary the history standing behind it, so it may be an argument that meaning is not in the text rather than a parable about meaning not being in the head, and those are different claims. I would like you to say which of these the story is doing, or whether it is better read as pressing on something the standard debate tends to leave out — the identity of works of art, say, or the difference between what a sentence means and what a speaker means by it, or the thought that there might be a narrow layer of content that really does stay in the head whatever the world is like. Several people in the group work on aesthetics rather than language, so please keep half an eye on whether Menard has written the same work as Cervantes or a different one, and on whether that question can be separated from the question about meaning. If the story contains a moment that an externalist cannot comfortably accommodate, say so instead of smoothing it over, and end by saying what the story contributes to the debate that a journal article could not.

Please write it in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; just paragraphs, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague — and write in British English. Above all, keep apart what is more or less settled among people who work on content externalism from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading of either the story or the debate as though it were the consensus. I have a suspicion that the connection I am drawing is a bit of a stretch; if it is, I would much rather be told so than handed a tidy synthesis.
