My roommate got clipped by one of those campus delivery robots a few weeks ago -- nothing serious, but the incident report was wild: the path planner did what it was trained to do, the remote operator was watching four robots at once and had six seconds to intervene, the safety team had signed off on the route, and the company's line was basically that the system performed within spec. Everyone did their job and a person still got hurt, and no one seems to be the one who did it. I've been reading about the responsibilty gap ever since and I want my students to feel that, not just hear the phrase. I run a 90-minute philosophy club for about 15 undergrads, mixed majors, and I want a runnable activity or game where they actually run into the gap themselves before I name it. The setting can be an incident review board, a mock hearing, a liability negotiation, whatever works. I want them to come away seeing that you can have a real harm, a chain of agents who each had some slice of knowledge or control, and still no one who has the full package that ordinary blame seems to require. And then I want them to feel why the obvious fixes -- put a human in the loop, make the company strictly liable, let the insurer sort it out, give the machine a log and call it accountable -- each buy something and each cost something.

The mechanic I want at the center: a responsibility ledger with two kinds of tokens, knowledge tokens and control tokens. To assign blame to a role at the moment of the incident, that role needs both a knowledge token and a control token for the relevant decision. You can transfer tokens between roles during the review, but every transfer has a cost or side effect -- e.g. moving a control token also moves a chunk of liability, moving a knowledge token reveals something embarrassing about the role's own process, and the system itself can hold tokens but can't be blamed. Roles should include at least: the manufacturer's safety engineer, the fleet operator, the remote supervisor, the software update lead, the regulator, the victim's advocate, the insurer, and maybe the robot's log as a non-agent role. No real money; use points, reputation chips, or a simple payoff table. The activity should have a clear timeline: setup, role assignment, incident briefing, ledger negotiation, a forced verdict, and then the debrief. Make the rules so that the first thing that happens is everyone tries to dump the whole thing on the easiest target, and then they discover that the ledger won't close. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the pieces of the idea: the control condition, the epistemic condition, the many hands problem, blame vs accountability vs liability, and the difference between ex post blame and ex ante risk regulation.

Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- e.g. someone will try to blame the victim, someone will argue the robot is a moral agent, the group will just vote and ignore the ledger, the quiet student will get steamrolled -- and how to handle each without killing the point. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: it can't settle whether the system itself could be a moral agent, it can't reproduce the full metaphysics of free will, and it probably can't make the case for or against meaningful human control feel as strong as the real arguments do. And in the debrief notes, please seperate what people who work on this mostly agree on from what is genuinely contested. I think it's pretty settled that there's a gap in some cases, but whether that gap is a metaphysical problem about agency or an institutional problem about how we assign liability is contested, and whether adding meaningful human control actually closes it is contested too. Don't present any one camp's answer as the consensus. I don't need citations or a reading list -- just the activity, the materials, the debrief, and the failure modes.
