A friend in my ethics reading group dropped this on me and now I can't stop chewing on it. We were talking about moral foundations theory — you know, Haidt's thing: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, plus the liberty/oppression one people add, and the familiar story that self-identified liberals basically run on care and fairness while conservatives spread their weight across all of them. His claim was that this doesn't just describe the left/right split, it dissolves the question of who's right. Here's the argument he gave:

(1) When two people genuinely disagree about a moral question, at most one of them can be right.
(2) Whether you're right about a moral question depends on whether the faculty that produced your judgment reliably tracks moral truth.
(3) Moral foundations theory shows the liberal faculty and the conservative faculty are calibrated differently, and that the calibration traces to temperament and upbringing rather than to evidence or argument.
(4) A faculty calibrated by temperament and upbringing is not calibrated to track moral truth.
(5) So neither faculty is reliable.
(6) So neither side is right, and there's no fact of the matter that could make one side right.
(7) So the psychology of left and right tells us the question "who's right" isn't a real question at all.

I find (1) through (3) pretty solid and I'd like to keep them. And honestly (7) has a pull on me. But the thing definately proves too much: run the same steps and *every* moral faculty anyone has is calibrated by temperament and upbringing, so it's not just liberal vs. conservative, it's "slavery is wrong" vs. "slavery is fine." He says fine, that's the price of honesty. I don't buy it, but I can't find where it slips.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If it's (4), tell me why it looked so plausible in the first place. If it's the move from (5) to (6) or (6) to (7), tell me exactly what I get to keep and what I lose. Lead with the step number you'd give up, then price the alternatives. Keep it under 600 words.
