Six months ago, after a year I would rather not describe, I read Franklin's Autobiography and — being evidently the sort of person who takes eighteenth-century self-help literally — made myself the little book: a page for each virtue, red-ruled columns for the days of the week, a line for each fault, and a black dot every evening when I had failed. I lasted about eleven weeks, and I want to ask about the thing that stopped me, because I don't think it was ordinary backsliding. The dots did thin out, which felt wonderful, and the wonderful feeling was the problem. The virtue I had added last, copying Franklin and putting humility at the bottom, was the one I could never get a clean line on, and not because I kept catching myself boasting. It was that every evening I got to leave that row blank, I felt a small, unmistakable satisfaction at having left it blank, and I could not tell whether that satisfaction was the fault I was tracking or the reward for not having it. Franklin's own remark, that if he could conceive he had completely overcome pride he would probably be "proud of my humility," had always read to me as a charming joke; sitting with my own book, it stopped being one, because the instrument I was using to detect the fault seemed to be made of the same material as the fault. And then the ending was stranger than the failure. I didn't stop because I had cleared the book. I stopped by re-describing what the book was for — telling myself the point had been the aiming rather than the clean page, that a speckled ax was good enough — and I think I am in fact somewhat better than I was, but the book cannot tell me that, and the moment I stopped treating it as a verdict is the moment it may have started working.

I have a strong sense that this is not one phenomenon but a knot of them, and I can't get the edges right. Part of it resembles the general point that once you start measuring something the measurement takes over from the thing measured, but that seems to be about a proxy drifting away from a goal, whereas here the metric and the goal have exactly the same content, and nobody is gaming anything — I am the auditor and the audited. Part of it resembles the old observation that some goods cannot be pursued head-on, that the person who chases contentment or spontaneity loses them by chasing, but that is about pursuit, and my trouble was in the assessment. Part of it resembles the thought that certain virtues are self-effacing, that the virtue itself tells you to stop attending to it, but that seems to be a claim about what the virtue demands, whereas my trouble was epistemic: from inside the practice, success and the appearance of success are indistinguishable, and any report of success is itself a candidate counterexample. I also keep wondering whether the self-administration is essential. If my partner kept the ledger and marked the dots, would the pattern survive in some form, or would it simply become an ordinary case of being judged by someone else? And I wonder whether the termination matters: is it part of the same phenomenon that these practices tend to end by the goal being re-described rather than met, or is that a separate thing about how people abandon projects without admitting it?

What I want is not a label but a carving. Is there an established name for this configuration, and if there is, does it cover all of it or only a piece? If there isn't, coin one and define it, and tell me where its boundaries fall: is the self-assessment essential or incidental; is the unverifiability of the success state essential, or does the pattern also occur where success is checkable but the checking corrupts it; does the failure have to be self-referential in the way the humility case is, or does any self-improvement regimen with an internal scorecard exhibit it; and is Franklin's case the paradigm or a near miss? I would also like to know whether the same shape appears outside moral improvement — someone cultivating non-attachment who becomes quietly attached to their detachment, a diarist cultivating honesty who begins performing honesty for the diary, a person training themselves out of needing praise who is pleased with how little praise they need — or whether those are merely analogous and the moral case is special because the success condition is a state of character rather than a behavior. Name the nearest concepts, say precisely how each falls short, and if nothing fits, give me a term with a definition, two clear instances, and at least one case that looks like an instance but isn't. A bare name will not help me; I already know what to call it in my own head, and my own word is no better than yours.
