I want a real episode read through Nietzsche's three ways of writing history in the second Untimely Meditation, and I want you to tell me where the reading stops working rather than smoothing it over.

The episode is the Historikerstreit, the public quarrel among West German historians and intellectuals in the mid-1980s over how the Nazi past should sit in the national story. The spark was the 1985 Bitburg ceremony, where Kohl and Reagan honored German war dead in a cemetery that included Waffen-SS graves, which many took as the first step in normalizing the Nazi period. In the arguments that followed, a group of conservative historians pushed for a past Germans could inhabit without permanent guilt; Ernst Nolte was the most conspicuous, arguing that the Holocaust was not singular and should be understood comparatively, as a fearful response to Soviet crimes, so that Germans need not bear a special burden. Jürgen Habermas and others answered that this relativized and thereby excused the Holocaust, dissolving the one thing that made German memory a task instead of a comfort. Michael Stürmer, in the same years, was calling for a history in which Germans could take pride. That is the material.

Nietzsche's typology is the lens. Monumental history shows the man of action that greatness was once possible; antiquarian history keeps the preserver in touch with where he came from; critical history lets the one who is oppressed by a past judge it and break with it in order to live. Each can rot: into costume drama, into mummification, into mere resentment. The obvious mapping is Nolte and Stürmer on the monumental or antiquarian side, Habermas on the critical. I want you to show why that mapping is at best too quick.

Three things in particular.

First, sort the moves properly. Is the demand for a usable national past monumental or antiquarian, and does it change the diagnosis that the action it is supposed to serve looks like quietism and self-congratulation rather than greatness?

Second, look hard at Habermas, because I think the roles invert. Critical history, in Nietzsche's sense, destroys a past so that one can live; but Habermas's insistence is that the victims be remembered and that the memory remain an open obligation, which looks more like an antiquarian keeping of faith, or even a monumental one, than like critical history at all. If the inversion is real, say what it costs the typology.

Third, bring in the half of the essay that usually gets dropped: the unhistorical and the suprahistorical, the claim that a culture needs forgetting as much as memory, and the idea of plastic power, the capacity to absorb and transform the past rather than be crushed by it. Both sides of this quarrel are fighting over memory, and Nietzsche is the philosopher who suspects too much of it. Does his preference for forgetting license the normalization he would otherwise scorn? Is the life that forgetting would serve here a life he could approve of? Or does the episode show that life is doing no work as a criterion, that it names whatever the speaker happens to want?

Then the payoff, in both directions. Which features of the quarrel does the typology actually illuminate, and which does it flatten or misdescribe? And what does the episode show about the typology itself: does it confirm the three modes, or present a case Nietzsche never anticipated, a mass-mediated, state-level, democratic contest over memory rather than a private or cultural relation to the past? And his elitism, the view that history matters for the great few and the masses are only copies of them: is that a defect that disqualifies the typology for a public quarrel like this one, or is it a feature?

If, on inspection, the typology cannot carry the case without being stretched past recognition, or if I am simply wrong that this is the right kind of episode for it, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a fit. I would rather be told that Nietzsche's categories cut the case somewhere unexpected, or nowhere, than be handed a tidy three-column table.
