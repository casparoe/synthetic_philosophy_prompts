I'm putting together a two-page proposal for our department's internal seed-grant round -- the one that buys out a single course for a semester, nothing fancy, two pages single-spaced, and they want the question, not a lit review. I've got a half-formed idea I keep failing to pin down, so I want you to write the proposal around it and tell me where it's mush. The idea: everyone knows the loose version of Goodhart's law -- aim at a measure and it stops being a good measure of what you cared about -- and everyone has the usual examples, teaching to the test, citation-chasing, waiting-time targets, whatever was in the news last week. My hunch is that we're lumping together two things that only look alike, and that the lumping is why the standard remedies (better metrics, more metrics, audits, "measure what you actually care about") keep failing in the same way. Call the first one gaming: there's a target that exists independently of the measure, the measure is a more or less decent proxy for it, and agents respond to the measure in ways that pull the proxy off the target. Proxy divergence, effort substitution, the familiar stuff. Call the second one hijacking: the target doesn't have a life of its own, it's partly constituted by the measure -- what counts as a good school, a creditworthy borrower, an excellent department, maybe even a healthy body is at least partly whatever the instrument says it is, and the instrument's uptake is what makes it so. You can't game a target that the metric is making up as it goes, but you can do something arguably worse: capture the instrument, or freeze a contingent settlement into a fact about the kind of thing you're measuring. And the interesting question stops being "is the metric good" and becomes "who decided, and can it be renegotiated," which is a governance question wearing a measurement question's clothes.

So what I want the proposal to do is make that distinction earn its keep instead of just being a nice pair of labels. It needs a question stated sharply enough to have a determinate answer -- something like: is constitutive measurement a distinct phenomenon from proxy divergence, and does the distinction generate different prescriptions? It needs an honest account of why the existing work leaves this open rather than already settling it. My guess is that each camp has half the distinction and nobody has both halves: the psychometrics and validity people have the machinery for proxy divergence but treat the target as fixed and given; the performativity crowd in STS and economic sociology have the constituting story but are often allergic to the normative question about legitimacy; the philosophy-of-measurement fight between representational and realist views is about whether measurement tracks a structure that's there anyway, which is exactly the hinge I need but they aren't asking my question; and the specification-gaming literature in AI is all gaming, no hijacking, because there the reward function is stipulated. I need you to say that carefully and specifically, and I don't want it to boil down to "nobody has put these two literatures together," which is a boring claim and usually a false one. Then the method, which is conceptual plus a structured comparison of three or four cases chosen so they can't all be redescribed the same way: at least one where the target really is independent (straightforward gaming), at least one where the measure is doing genuine constituting work, and at least one borderline case I have to argue into one box or the other, because the borderline case is where the proposal either has teeth or doesn't. Then what a result looks like -- a criterion for telling the two apart that doesn't just restate the distinction, plus at least one prescription that comes out different depending on which one you're facing -- and what would count as the project failing. I'd say failure is either (a) every case of the constitutive kind turns out to be redescribable as proxy divergence under pressure, or (b) the distinction is real but normatively inert, same remedies either way, in which case I've got a taxonomy and not an argument.

Practical constraints, since I know you'll ask: one semester, me alone, no fieldwork, no interviews, no IRB nonsense -- all the cases are documented in things I can read. Two pages, so the framing has to be tight and the "why isn't this already done" part has to be short and specific rather than a tour of the field. Don't put any references in, I'll add them myself, and anyway the point of the proposal is the question, not the reading list. Be blunt if you think the distinction collapses; I'd rather find that out now then in month three.
