Write a dialogue between two programmers arguing about the two-envelope puzzle. A says you should always swap: whatever $x is in your envelope, the other one is either $2x or $x/2 with equal probability, so it's worth $1.25x on average — free money. B says that can't be right, since before you open anything the two envelopes are perfectly symmetric, so swapping can't help, and A has clearly snuck in a bogus 50/50. Make both of them sharp — neither should be a strawman, and they should actually respond to each other's arguments instead of talking past each other. To make it concrete, have the host announce that the smaller amount is a whole number of dollars picked uniformly from 1 to 1000, and have them work out what you should really do after you open your envelope (does it depend on how much you see? what if you see $1500?), then what changes if the host drops the upper limit and just says the smaller amount is some whole number of dollars with no ceiling. I'm a programmer with no philosophy backround, so if anyone starts throwing around "improper priors" I want it spelled out in plain English. And one thing I've never gotten my head around: I've heard there's a version where you open the envelope, look at the amount, and then use some outside randomness (say, swap only if your amount is below a random number you generated yourself) and supposedly you come out ahead on average no matter how the host chose the amounts — if that's a real thing, make them explain it and say why it doesn't contradict B's symmetry argument.
