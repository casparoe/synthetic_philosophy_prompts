I have been reading Dennett's distinction among Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — the idea that minds come in grades: some are wired with fixed responses, some learn by reinforcement, some can run a hypothesis internally and discard it before it costs them anything in the world, and some think through external tools and notations they inherit and modify. What struck me is how neatly this taxonomy maps onto, and seems to be quietly presupposed by, the way we now train large language models, and I want to know whether that presupposition holds together.

Consider the actual pipeline. First, the model is shaped by reward: raters score outputs, a reward model is fit to those scores, and the policy is nudged toward higher-scoring outputs and away from lower-scoring ones. This looks like operant conditioning, and it seems to presuppose a Skinnerian creature — one whose dispositions are tuned by consequences and which need not represent the goal at all. Second, and in tension with that, the current fashion is to reward the model for producing an explicit chain of thought, an internal deliberation it is supposed to run before answering; here we seem to be treating the model as a Popperian creature, one that can try out candidate lines of reasoning in a sandbox and abandon the bad ones without paying for them in the world. And when we hand the model a scratchpad, a calculator, a set of symbols to manipulate, we treat it as Gregorian, a creature whose cognition is partly offloaded into tools.

What I want from you is an account of what view of mind this composite practice presupposes. Does it require that the creature really is Popperian or Gregorian, or is the chain of thought epiphenomenal and the whole thing Skinnerian under the hood — reward all the way down, with the visible deliberation a mere side effect? Are the layers even consistent, or does rewarding the process rather than only the outcome covertly import a representational view of mind that the outcome-only version of the practice was designed to avoid? Which rival theories of mind does the pipeline rule out — for instance, does it presuppose that there is no in-principle barrier to a purely associative, non-representational learner acquiring what looks like reasoning? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, and tell me what would have to change in the training pipeline if we took seriously the claim that the model is a genuine Popperian creature that forms and tests hypotheses, rather than a Skinnerian one that merely emits whatever was reinforced.
