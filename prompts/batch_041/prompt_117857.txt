I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in bioethics and philosophy of mind. Sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, one room, nothing needed but paper and pens. Design a single activity for that session whose point is to make participants feel the problem of the authority of the earlier self over the later one — the problem that arises when someone who is competent writes a binding instruction about how they are to be treated at a later time, at which point they will have values they no longer hold, or no coherent values at all, and the earlier voice is absent at the moment of application while the later person is present and does not ratify what was written.

The hard part is not that the earlier self might have been insincere. It is that we cannot say, without assuming the answer, which of the two selves is the person whose good is at stake, and on what basis the one acquires the right to bind the other. Do not lecture on this at the start, and do not let the students hear the standard labels — precedent autonomy, substituted judgment, the reductionist challenge to the significance of identity, self-binding as a species of promise or of self-legislation — until the debrief. The structure of the activity, not the facilitator's framing, has to do the work.

I want the group to collide with at least four things before any of them is named.

First, a directive written in earnest and then read strictly — with no clause allowing the later self to override it simply by asking — should turn out to bind someone in a way they actively resent, and the activity should make visible that honoring the directive means overriding a present, articulate protest.

Second, at least some participants should face the case of two competent utterances by the same person at different times: a directive made while unimpaired, and a later revocation also made while unimpaired. Whatever rule the group adopts should be shown to fail on that case rather than merely to be complicated by it, so that the group cannot settle matters by deferring to the competent person.

Third, the asymmetry between refusals and demands: participants should be able to notice in their own behavior that they are willing to be bound to refuse something and reluctant to be bound to require something of others (or the reverse), and the debrief should say whether that asymmetry tracks anything real or is an artifact of how the cases were described.

Fourth, and this is the one I care most about: the activity must not let the later self be a fully articulate debater in every case. In the situations that generate the actual disputes, the person whose good is at issue is frequently the least able to speak for themselves, so the contest is between the earlier self's written word and third parties' interpretations of what the present person wants or enjoys. Build in at least one case where nobody in the room is entitled to speak for the present person, and make the group decide anyway.

Wherever possible, the earlier self and the later self should be the same participant: the force of the thing comes from having written the instruction and then having to live under it, or from watching one's own stated justification for the instruction's authority be applied, against one's wishes, to one's own case.

Deliver, as a runnable plan: the materials; a minute-by-minute timeline; what each participant writes, and exactly how those artifacts are collected, edited, and returned so that the fiction holds; the roles, including who may speak for whom and who is barred from speaking; the decision rule or payoff structure, with an account of why it produces pressure rather than merely posing a question — the stakes should be real to the participants without being material; and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the positions at issue, in plain language that a student who has read nothing can follow. No citations, no reading list, and nothing that requires me to have read a particular book.

Also tell me: the three most likely ways the first run goes wrong and what to do about each; the point at which the design is most likely to tip the group toward one answer, and how to counteract it; and an honest account of what this activity cannot reproduce, and therefore what I will have to supply myself. I would rather have a design that is sharp and limited than one that covers everything.
