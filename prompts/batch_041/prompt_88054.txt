I have been trying to draw the line between laws of history and mere trends, and while I accept the point that a trend is not a law, I cannot turn that into a criterion that matches my verdicts. The cases below are ones I would stake something on; the verdicts are what I actually think, not what I think I ought to think.

1. If a population grows faster than its food supply and trade cannot close the gap, per-capita food falls. — A law. It would have licensed prediction in any century, and it holds wherever the antecedent is realized.
2. Every society moves through feudalism, then capitalism, then socialism. — Not a law. It is a claim about one sequence of episodes, however broad.
3. Industrializing societies see fertility fall. — Not a law, though I am confident it is true of the last two centuries. A trend of our period.
4. If an army outruns the supply lines it can defend and no ally can resupply it, it loses the ground it has taken. — A law.
5. Empires tend to overextend and then contract. — A trend, even though it is as well supported as (4).
6. If a regime is losing a war of attrition on its own territory and its ruling coalition splits, it falls. — A law.
7. Revolutions are more likely when food prices spike. — A trend; a capable state can defeat it, and nothing can defeat (1).
8. If a prediction that a particular leader will fall is published, believed, and acted on by his rivals, his fall becomes more likely. — I want to call this a law, but then a law can help bring about the very episode it predicts, and any law of history that implies a unique outcome would be self-altering once known.

My best attempt at a principle: a law is a generalization whose consequent follows from its antecedent under constraints that hold whatever people happen to believe or want — the arithmetic of mouths and food, the geometry of supply lines — while a trend is one whose consequent depends on how people happen to behave in a given period. That gets (1) and (4) and keeps (3), (5) and (7) out, but it costs me (6), since whether a coalition splits, and whether the split brings the regime down, turns on what the elites believe and want. It is also not obvious that (4) escapes the same dependence, since an army's collapse turns on its soldiers' willingness to keep fighting. A second criterion I have tried — a law is a generalization that no competent agent can defeat — keeps (6) and (7) where I want them but threatens (3), and I cannot tell whether that is a cost or a correction.

What I want is the best principle, or the best small set of them, that fits these verdicts; which of my verdicts it cannot fit, and why; and what it says about a case I have not listed — that when a technological change destroys an old elite's revenue base, that elite loses political power within a generation. State the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and be honest about the verdict that has to go. End with a single line of the form \answer{short label for the principle; the verdict it forces me to give up} and nothing after it.
