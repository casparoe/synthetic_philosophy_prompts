I retired from a metallurgy lab about four years ago and, partly to keep the brain ticking, I've been sitting in on the philosophy department's MA seminars at the local university — metaphysics this term, which is where I've run into the statue-and-the-clay business. I spend one morning a week in the conservation store at the local museum as well, so perhaps that's where the itch comes from. I keep feeling that the whole puzzle is set up with a lump of matter that isn't like anything I ever handled, and that alot of the trouble comes from that. There's no such thing as a formless lump in my old trade: every bit of stuff has a microstructure — a crystal lattice, grain size, dislocations, sometimes several phases intergrown — and that microstructure is doing real work in fixing what the stuff can and can't do. Annealed copper and cold-worked copper are chemically identical and share a lattice, yet one bends and the other snaps. So when philosophers say the statue and the lump are two things, or one thing, or no things, they seem to be treating the matter side of the pair as featureless, and I suspect the problem looks different once you let matter carry its own levels of form. My half-formed thought is that there's a hierarchy here — lattice, grain structure, macroscopic shape — and that the statue/clay puzzle is really about which level of form is doing the individuating, and whether that question has a principled answer or only a conventional one. My old lab partner, who has no patience for any of this, tells me I'm overthinking a parlour game.

I'd like you to turn this into a short research proposal — say for a one-year fellowship application, a couple of pages — and I'll fill in the references myself, so please don't bother with citations or a literature review by name. I want the question stated precisely enough that there's a definite answer to aim at: roughly, whether the fact that every candidate lump already has microstructural form gives us a reason to prefer one of the going answers to the constitution puzzle over the others, or whether it simply dissolves that puzzle into a different one about levels of organisation. Tell me honestly why the existing positions don't already settle this — the ones that say there are two coincident objects, the ones that say the statue just is the clay, the ones that deny that artefacts like statues exist at all, and the ones that give objects temporal parts — and what an argument that actually engages with the materials science would have to look like, as opposed to one that just gestures at it. Sketch the method month by month: what I'd need to get clear first, what I'd try to write up, where the likely snag is, and what sort of intermediate result would tell me I'm onto something versus the sort that would tell me it's a dead end. Say what would count as the project failing, and whether the whole thing is too big or too small for a year of half-time work.

Two cases I'd like it to test itself on: a bronze bell that gets re-cast from its own melted metal, and a wrought-iron gate in which every bar has been replaced over a century of repairs, one at a time. And if you think the microstructure thought is a red herring, please say so plainly — I'd rather hear that now than in month nine. Could you write it in British English, please; I've got into the habit of colour and realise and it looks odd when a draft comes back half-American.
