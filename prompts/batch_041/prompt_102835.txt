My paraphrase goes like this. Hume opens by saying a wise person proportions belief to the evidence. Testimony is a species of evidence, and it draws whatever force it has from our experience that people generally tell the truth — but that experience is never perfectly uniform, since witnesses sometimes lie or misreport. So every particular bit of testimony arrives with some chance of falsehood attached. Now suppose the thing testified to is a miracle, which he defines as a violation of the laws of nature. The laws of nature, on his account, are just the summary of an extremely uniform and steady experience — the most uniform experience we have. So we've got two experiences pulling opposite ways: the experience of witness reliability, and the experience of nature's regularity. His rule for adjudicating is that we should credit the miracle only if the falsehood of the testimony would itself be a greater miracle — a bigger violation of uniform experience — than the event the testimony is trying to establish. Then he appears to say that because the law of nature is backed by the most uniform experience available, testimony can never outweigh it; though he also hedges that in principle it could if the testimony were strong enough, before spending the second half arguing that in practice it never is.

What I can't get past is whether this is a real argument or whether the deck is just stacked. Since the law of nature is defined as the thing our experience supports most firmly, and the miracle is defined as a violation of that law, it feels like the conclusion is guaranteed before anything gets weighed.
