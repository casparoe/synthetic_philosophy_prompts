My parents' fiftieth wedding anniversary is next month, and I have been asked to give the toast. The room will be split in a way I find daunting. Half of it is family and old friends who have never read a word of philosophy; the other half is my father's colleagues and former doctoral students from the mathematics and philosophy departments where he spent his career. For forty years my father has argued — in print, in the lecture hall, and at every dinner table — that the infinite is real only as a potential, as the never-finished possibility of taking one more step, and never as a completed whole; that Cantor's completed infinities, and the hotel that is somehow full and yet still has room for more, are at best a useful fiction. He corrects people about this, gently, and he will be listening. A Cantorian colleague of his will be at our table.

Please write the toast, four or five minutes long, in the first person and in my voice. It has to be warm enough for my mother's relatives and honest enough that my father will not hear a single sentence he considers false. That second condition rules out most of the ordinary furniture of an anniversary speech: I cannot say that their love is infinite, that the years have been endless, that they will be together forever, or that there is no limit to what the two of them have made together. Each of those turns the infinite into a finished thing, which is exactly what he denies. What I can say is that they have kept adding one year, and then one more, and that no last one is in view. I would like the toast to make that difference do real work — to let the room feel why 'one more, and then one more' is not a poorer thing to wish two people than 'forever' — and to land that feeling on the non-philosophers without ever teaching them the theory or naming it. It should honor my mother as much as my father; she should not sit there feeling that the speech is really about his philosophy with her as the illustration.

There is one memory I want included. As a boy I told my father I loved him infinity, and he answered, with affection and without irony, that infinity was not an amount of anything, and that what I had said was not so much false as unfinished. Please find a place for that, and let it come out as tenderness rather than as a correction. If the image of the always-full hotel tempts you, use it only in a way that keeps faith with his view that no such hotel could exist.
