Chaos theory is often invoked in popular histories to defend the thesis that great events turn on small accidents. A chaotic system, after all, is one in which arbitrarily small differences in initial conditions can grow into arbitrarily large differences in outcome; so the loss of a horseshoe nail can cost a kingdom. Yet the same mathematics is also cited for the opposite moral: a chaotic system is perfectly deterministic and merely unpredictable, so the very feature that makes history look accidental makes it, at bottom, a matter of law. The two uses of the same result pull in opposite directions.

Write an essay on the following question: can deterministic chaos legitimately be used to support the for-want-of-a-nail conception of history, on which the large outcomes of history are contingent in the sense that small, accidental perturbations would have produced radically different outcomes?

Address at least the following, weighing arguments rather than surveying opinions:

(i) Is sensitive dependence on initial conditions best understood as a property of the world or of the models we use to describe it, and what follows for a historian who wants to appeal to it?

(ii) Does unpredictability entail indeterminism, and is either of these what the for-want-of-a-nail thesis actually needs?

(iii) Consider a distinction I have found useful between a system's being perturbation-sensitive and an outcome's being perturbation-dependent. Does the first ever license the second, and if so under what conditions? Test your answer against a concrete case — for instance, the consequences of the 1914 assassination in Sarajevo, or the collapse of a dynasty following a minor illness — and say what would have to be true of the world, and of our evidence, for the small event to count as the difference-maker.

(iv) Does chaos give us any reason to prefer the narration of accidents to the explanation of structures — class, demography, geography, institutions — or does it leave the case for structural explanation intact?

Reach a definite conclusion rather than ending on a non-committal note. A qualified verdict is welcome — for instance, that chaos supports contingency-talk in one sense but not in another — provided you say precisely which sense survives. There is no need to cite literature or supply a reading list; argue from the concepts.

End with a final line of exactly this form:

Verdict: <one or two sentences stating your conclusion>
