You're a philosopher who knows population ethics cold and has actually written comedy that lands in front of a seminar room, and I'd like your help with something specific. I'm a graduate student, and at the end of term my population ethics seminar holds a session in which each of us opens with a couple of minutes of something light before the discussion proper, and I have drawn the topic of Benatar's asymmetry. I want to open with a joke that is really a joke — a setup and a punchline, tellable standing up in under two minutes. What I don't want is the joke everyone already knows how to make, the one where the humor comes entirely from noticing that antinatalism is a gloomy position: life is mostly suffering, so don't have children, the antinatalist walks into a bar, and so on. The joke has to turn on the machinery itself. The machinery, as I have it: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even if that good is not enjoyed by anyone, and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody for whom the absence is a deprivation. The third clause is read impersonally and the fourth is read person-affecting, and that seam — a good that needs no one to enjoy it sitting immediately next to a harm that needs somebody to be deprived — is roughly where I would like the punchline to live. Benatar's list of further asymmetries may be useful too: that we can regret, for the sake of a person, having created them and their being unhappy, but can never regret, for the sake of a person, not having created them; or that we feel sorrow at the thought of a place where people came into existence and suffer, and none at all at the thought of a place where no one came into existence and no one is happy.

Here is the seed I keep returning to, though you should replace it if you find something sharper. Set the joke in a small bureaucratic office — a complaints window, an intake desk, some counter where a clerk must process claims about states of affairs, and where the office's rules are the asymmetry taken literally. This clerk can log, as a good, suffering that did not occur, without needing anyone to have been spared it; but the same clerk cannot log, as a harm, a pleasure that did not occur, unless there is a someone to have been deprived of it. The comedy should come from the file that this rule cannot close: I want the twist to be something the office genuinely cannot adjudicate on its own terms, not merely a clerk being obtuse, and I'd like the second desk over to be handling the regret-asymmetry cases on their own forms. Invent the office freely — its forms, its stamp, the sign on the wall. A specific invented detail will do far more for the joke than a general remark about nonexistence.

Before you write, talk through in prose — no lists — which of the four clauses and which of the further asymmetries give you the most comic leverage and why, and settle on your vehicle; then give me the joke itself as a short scene with a few exchanges; then, in one short paragraph, say what the punchline turns on and which clause it leans on, so that I can explain it afterwards if the room goes quiet. Keep all of it in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and take your time, because the obvious version of this joke is not what I am after.
