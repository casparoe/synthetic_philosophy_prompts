I run a monthly workshop on moral status for a mixed group — four philosophy graduate students and three veterinary residents — and next month the slot is mine: seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, and Kant on animals. The previous session was Bentham, so they will arrive with sentience as the obvious ticket into the moral community and a settled sense that everything before Bentham is a catalogue of errors. I do not want an exposition of Kant's views; I can read the text myself. I want a teaching plan. Put yourself in the position of someone who has taught Kant's ethics for years, including to audiences with a professional stake in animals, and give me the order in which the pieces have to be introduced so that the position becomes intelligible rather than merely reported, the one case that carries the session, the misconceptions the group will predictably bring or form and the exact point in the sequence at which each has to be intercepted, one question I can put to them at the end that tells me whether the view has landed rather than been memorized, and an explicit statement of what I should refuse to open at this level and why. Treat me as a colleague who knows the Groundwork well and this corner of Kant badly.

The hinge I want the sequence to turn on — check me on this, since I may have it wrong — is the difference between whom a duty is owed to and what a duty is about: on Kant's account our duties concerning animals are not owed to the animals, yet cruelty to an animal is not thereby a matter of indifference, because it is a wrong done to humanity in the person of the agent. If that distinction is really the hinge, then nothing they already believe about Kantian ethics will let them hear the standard formula about indirect duties correctly; they will hear that animals matter only instrumentally, as gym equipment for the agent's character, and that is the first misconception to intercept. Others I expect: that the whole thing is a museum piece, given that they have already conceded the field to sentience; that Kant's talk of animals as analogues of humanity means he quietly credited them with a sliver of reason, which I associate with the Leibnizian material in the lecture notes and am not sure belongs to his considered position; that having no direct duties to animals entails either that he licensed anything or that he forbade meat; and that the whole topic is just the marginal-cases objection in period costume. Tell me where in the arc each of these has to be intercepted, which of them are one misconception wearing several hats, and which of my five is a confusion of mine rather than theirs. For the carrying case I am tempted by Kant's own example of the man who kills the dog that has served him long and faithfully now that it is no longer useful — I believe it is in the Metaphysics of Morals but I would not swear to it, so judge the case on its merits and replace it if it cannot carry the weight. I would also like the session to end where the veterinary residents actually live, on something like so-called convenience euthanasia, a healthy animal put down because keeping it is inconvenient, so that the check for understanding is not recitation of the doctrine but a statement of what the view implies here and an honest account of where it falls silent or embarrasses itself.

Be decisive: I want the plan you would actually run, not a menu. But if my framing is mistaken — if the owed-to/about distinction is not the load-bearing beam of Kant's position, or if seventy-five minutes cannot get a Bentham-primed audience to the interesting question, or if the order I am implicitly assuming is simply the wrong order — say so plainly instead of politely building around it. Begin by sketching two or three candidate arcs before committing to one, and tell me which you discarded and why. Work from what you know; I want no reading list, no citations, no footnotes, and if an attribution matters to the plan, say only that it matters and let me verify it rather than supplying it from memory.
