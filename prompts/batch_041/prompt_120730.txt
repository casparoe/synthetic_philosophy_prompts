I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on characters who make agreements with their future selves: a woman who signs a contract with a firm that will fine her if, on a stated date, she has not left her husband; a man who takes a pill so that he will stop doubting his wife; a club whose members enforce one another's vows and profit from one another's lapses. I have read enough of the literature on diachronic rationality to know that I am borrowing trouble, and I would rather know the shape of the trouble before I finish the draft. Adequate for what? For this: I need to know, of each of these characters, whether she is being irrational or merely unlucky in her tempters, and whether her contracts are binding in any sense I can defend in prose.

So I am not asking for a survey. I am asking for a specification: what must any adequate theory of rationality over time deliver? Give me the requirements, each with the case that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp. At a minimum I want the list to cover:

1. The exploitation cases — the pump in which an agent with a cycle of preferences trades his way back to where he began, minus a fee at each turn; the book that can be run against a person whose credences shift over time; the dial that can be turned one notch at a time for money, where every notch is worth taking and the end of the dial is not. Say what each is supposed to show, and whether the theory must convict the agent of irrationality or only of being exploitable. The two verdicts are not the same, and I need to know which one my characters deserve. I also want the theory to say whether regret on its own is evidence of irrationality, or whether the pump is doing all the work.

2. Whether the requirement is genuinely diachronic. If every case can be redescribed with time-indexed preferences and credences, then all the incoherence is synchronic and the theory has a very different shape. If it cannot, say what the irreducibly diachronic element is.

3. Precommitment, without paradox and without question-begging. I will not believe a theory that makes the ropes irrational, and I will not believe one that declares the untied man's defection irrational by fiat. If those two verdicts cannot be had together on a single standard, that is the result I most want from you.

4. The difference between a change of mind and a failure of coherence — including the person who has become someone else and repudiates her contract without regret, and the person who foresees her own reversal and is the worse off for foreseeing it. The theory must not assume that the satisfaction of the present self's preferences is the measure of the agent's good, or it cannot describe either of them.

5. The unit of assessment: the act, the agent at a time, the temporally extended person, or the person plus her commitments. And whether the theory is committed to treating the future self as, in some degree, another person.

6. Cases where the binding technology is absent or fails. Resolve is not a rope. Must a requirement of rationality be satisfiable by an agent who has no device available to her?

7. The active tempter. The standard pump assumes a bookie who takes what he is offered; my commitment firm has an interest in my character's lapse and cultivates it. Does the verdict change when the other party engineers the inconsistency rather than merely profiting from it?

8. Belief as well as preference. If the pill works and she stops doubting, has she been irrational, or has she done precisely what a person who distrusts her own future evidence ought to do?

9. The authority of the requirement: is it a requirement of rationality or of prudence, and does "you will regret this" give her a reason now?

Items 7 and 8 are my inventions, not items in the literature; treat them as cases to be handled rather than as things to be looked up.

I expect at least one pair on this list to be jointly unsatisfiable, and I would like you to say which. My own suspicion falls on 3, set against the demand that every act be assessed by the reasons the agent has at the moment she performs it. Close by telling me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories, and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail: what you would have to be shown, or which case would have to be described differently, for you to drop that requirement or reverse your verdict on the pair. Keep any scoring of particular positions to a sentence or two; the criteria are what I need.
