I run a platform team split between Amsterdam and Tokyo, and I keep hitting a question I don't have the vocabulary to settle. When someone ships work that isn't good enough, my Dutch engineers will say so in the review, plainly: the error handling is careless, this isn't shippable. My Tokyo colleagues, looking at the same code, tend to say nothing in the room and then raise a small concern afterwards, often framed as their own confusion rather than as a defect in the work. Both groups would describe themselves as honest, and I don't think either group is lying to me. I'm aware this may be partly an artifact of my two small offices rather than anything deep, but I'd like to know what the underlying question actually comes to, and I want a number rather than a general essay on cultural relativism.

Give me your credence in the following proposition:

P: In an ordinary case of this kind — a negative assessment of a colleague's work, where the speaker's aim is to be accurate and not to cause gratuitous harm — the permissibility of the blunt version and the permissibility of the heavily indirect version do not vary with local convention. That is: if the blunt statement is permissible in the Amsterdam office, it is permissible in the Tokyo office in an otherwise identical case, and vice versa. Local norms change the social cost of each option, and perhaps what the hearer expects, but they do not change what is permitted.

Decompose the number. I'd like a rough probability and one line of justification for each of the following, plus anything I've left out that belongs:

- The probability that the two versions even put the same thing before the hearer — that the indirect version asserts the same content rather than something weaker, or nothing at all, or something quietly misleading. If they don't, part of the comparison is an illusion.
- The probability that the moral weight of a speech act attaches to deception (did the hearer come away with a false belief?) rather than to the breach of an expectation (was the hearer dealt with as they were entitled to be?).
- The probability that the hearer's expectations are part of the act rather than mere background to it: bluntness where bluntness is normal might be straightforward respect, while the softened version might read as condescension or as withholding; indirectness where indirectness is normal might be the only form the hearer can accept without losing face, while the blunt version is an imposition they can't decline.
- The probability that norms governing how we speak are conventional in the way table manners are, rather than non-conventional in the way that keeping a promise is — and whether that distinction survives being pressed on.

If you think P is too vague as stated to be true or false, tell me which sharpening you're adopting and give me the number for that version instead. Then combine the terms, give me the resulting number, and tell me which one the final figure is most sensitive to. Finish with the evidence or argument that would move it most — in particular, whether there's anything I could observe in my own two offices that would count as evidence either way, and what a study or experiment would have to show for you to shift your number by more than a little.
