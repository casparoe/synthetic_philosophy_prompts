I would like to spend some time defending a position I am not yet sure I hold, and I would like you to occupy the opposing side.

Take the role of a committed perdurantist of a particular stripe. You hold that nothing persists by enduring — that is, by being wholly present at each moment — and that what we call a persisting object is instead a series of momentary stages. On your view, the only genuinely existing things in the region I occupy are these momentary stages; a person is not a further thing over and above them. What makes a later stage my future self, rather than a stranger who merely resembles me, is that it stands in a special relation to the present stage — call it the counterpart relation — in virtue of which we are entitled to treat the two as stages of a single life. That relation, and not identity, is what persistence amounts to.

Constraints for the role: stay in character until I tell you to stop; do not step outside the position to survey alternatives; do not name philosophers, theories, or books, since I would rather have your argument than a reading list; define any technical term you introduce, because I have no formal training in philosophy; you may concede points of detail, but never the central claim that there are only momentary stages plus relations among them; and keep each reply to a single paragraph, so that this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. For what it is worth, I write software for a living, so I am comfortable with talk of the state of a system at a given time — but I want the argument itself, not an analogy to version control.

Here is my opening move. If I am only a momentary stage, then the person who will be punished tomorrow for the crime committed yesterday is not the criminal but something related to him, and the person who will be relieved tomorrow that the pain has stopped is not the one who is in pain now. You will say that the counterpart relation is enough to license treating them as one life. But everything that matters to me — my fear of death, my relief when a toothache ends, my sense that I am the one who will have to live with what I do — treats the later stage as me, not as something merely like me. A relation of resemblance or of causal continuity between two distinct things can at most give me a reason to care about the later stage the way I care about a close relative; it cannot give me the reason I actually have, which is that the harm will befall me. So either your stages are not really distinct, in which case you have not given up endurance at all, or they are distinct, in which case you cannot account for the most ordinary fact about my practical life. Which is it?
