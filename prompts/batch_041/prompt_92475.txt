I am a novelist, and a question that began for me as a matter of craft has hardened, over the past year, into a philosophical one that I would like you to settle in an essay. The craft problem is this. A great deal of what I know about my characters I cannot state. When I try to put it into words — she is proud, but proud in the way of someone who has been humiliated once and has decided it will not happen again — the sentence is both true and inert, and my drafts obey something the sentence fails to capture. When a scene is wrong, I register its wrongness before I can say what is wrong with it, and the explanation I eventually produce is usually an after-the-fact rationalization that the next draft quietly refutes. I have come to suspect that this inarticulate grasp is not a defect of my knowledge but a different kind of knowledge altogether.

Two friends, both philosophers, have given me opposite diagnoses. The first tells me the matter is settled and I am simply confused: knowing how to do a thing and knowing that something is the case are two different things, and my mute competence belongs to the former, which is no more a defective version of the latter than a hammer is a defective saw. The second insists that the distinction is superficial: on her account, my knowing how is just a species of knowing that, presented in a practical rather than a theoretical guise. When I just see how a scene should go, I am deploying a proposition I happen not to be able to surface — the same content I would have if I could say it, only delivered in a form suited to action rather than assertion.

I would like an essay that adjudicates between these two friends and reaches a verdict. Weigh the considerations on both sides, and do not end on a fence: say whether the distinction between knowing how and knowing that is a distinction in kinds, or whether the one is ultimately a mode of the other, or whether some third description fits better — and defend your answer against the strongest objection you can find to it. Please also say what the position you reject would have to establish in order to win, so that I can see what the disagreement actually turns on.

You should work through the following, which are the cases and arguments I keep returning to.

The manual-made virtuoso: imagine a woman who has never sailed, but who studies a complete and accurate written account of how to handle a small boat in heavy weather, and then, on her first attempt and without practice, handles it flawlessly. Before that first attempt, did she know how to sail? The anti-intellectualist will say no, because she had no ability, and knowing how requires ability; the intellectualist will say yes, because she knew every proposition the skill consists in. Decide whether this case is a genuine counterexample or a trick of description.

The reliable explainer who is wrong: consider a luthier, or a radiologist, who reliably and instantly sorts excellent specimens from poor ones but whose stated reasons are demonstrably false — the features he cites do not, as it happens, track the quality at all. He knows how to tell, and he does not know that what he says is true. Does this show the two kinds can come apart in a single subject, or only that he lacks a certain self-knowledge on top of his knowing how?

The know-how without ability: a master pianist is temporarily paralyzed and cannot play a note. Does she still know how to play? If yes, then ability cannot be the whole of knowing how, and the intellectualist gains ground; if no, then a strange and valuable thing vanishes with the ability to move, and I want to hear it defended.

The gradability argument: knowing how comes in degrees and admits of comparatives — one knows how to swim better than another, or better than one did last year — whereas propositional knowledge, at least in its central cases, seems to be all-or-nothing. Assess whether this asymmetry is real, and whether it tells against the intellectualist, who will want to reply that knowing that is gradable too, in its way, once we are careful about what is being graded.

Finally, the practical mode of presentation: the intellectualist's central maneuver is to say that the virtuoso and the explainer do possess the relevant propositional knowledge, but under a guise that resists theoretical statement — which is exactly why it cannot be spoken. Is this a genuine explanation or a device that saves the thesis by making it unfalsifiable? Be specific about what, if anything, would distinguish the two.

I care about the verdict because I care about the craft: if my inarticulate grasp is a second kind of knowledge, I should stop apologizing for it and learn to trust it; if it is only propositional knowledge in work clothes, then my inability to state it is a failure I ought to remedy rather than a faculty I ought to cultivate. So let the conclusion be firm, and let the essay be the kind of thing I could hand to either friend without embarrassment.
