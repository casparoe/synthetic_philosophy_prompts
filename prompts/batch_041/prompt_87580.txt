I've ended up chairing the funding panel for a small grant-giving circle I'm in — six of us, maybe a dozen proposals a year, and the rule is we pick exactly one to fund per round: no splitting the pot, no runner-up, winner takes all. The first two rounds were a mess. Everyone quickly worked out that the way to get your favourite through was to rank it first and rank the frontrunner dead last even if you actually thought the frontrunner was perfectly fine, and this has lead to a winner nobody particularly wanted plus a load of quiet resentment afterwards. I'm a longtime lurker on the usual rationalist blogs and LessWrong-adjacent forums, so I've been reading arround the theory of this — Gibbard–Satterthwaite, strategy-proofness, the whole "no non-dictatorial rule makes honesty a dominant strategy once there are three or more options" thing — and I want you to turn it into something I can actually run at the next meeting. I'm not after a reading list or citations, just the ideas put to work, and my panel-mates are normal people who will not sit through a proof.

What I want is a procedure: the steps in order, what we do at each one, what we're entitled to expect from it and what we aren't, and — this is the bit I care about most — where it tells me that the thing I actually want, a rule where everyone's best move is simply to say what they think, is unavailable to us, and what the least-bad substitutes are. Assume I'm fine with a bit of maths but nobody else is. Assume we can't pay anyone (no transferable utility, it's a volunteer circle), but we could plausibly restrict what counts as a fundable proposal, run the round in stages, or use randomisation if that genuinely buys something rather than just feeling fair. Then run the procedure on two rounds: (a) one where the twelve proposals are all over the place with no structure whatsoever, and (b) one where they're really just variations on two themes, so everyone's preferences sit along a single left-right-ish axis — my hunch is that the second is where the procedure should tell me something better is possible, and I want to know exactly what and why. Please also flag the case where it gives an answer I won't like; I suspect it's going to tell me that my instinct that "the chair should just pick the compromise everyone can live with" is quietly doing something illegitimate, and I'd rather hear that from you now than discover it at the AGM.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words, in British English, and lead with the one-line bottom line on whether an honesty-proof rule is available to us at all.
