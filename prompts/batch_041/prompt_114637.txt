I am assembling a special issue of a small interdisciplinary journal on the theme of "reformed pseudoscience"—the recurring historical pattern in which a discredited practice is rescued by stripping away its most embarrassing features and re-describing the remainder in the vocabulary of mathematics or natural philosophy. For the centerpiece I would like a satirical manifesto, roughly 700 words, written in the voice of a modern spokesperson for a movement that calls itself Keplerian astrology. The conceit is that this movement claims to have saved astrology from its credulous past by returning to Kepler: it insists that the real causal agent is not the zodiac signs or the houses but the angular relationships between planets—the aspects—which it describes in the language of harmonic ratios and geometric proportion, and it maintains that the human soul is innately attuned to these configurations and responds to them as a string responds to a sympathetic vibration. The spokesperson should be earnest, slightly defensive, and eager to distinguish his movement from the astrology of newspaper columns and wellness apps; he should speak the language of evidence-based reform, streamlining, and rigor, and he should treat Kepler as a patron saint who did the hard work of separating the wheat from the chaff. The humor should come from getting the historical Kepler right rather than from inventing absurdities: the distinction he drew between the certainty of astronomy and the conjectural status of astrology; his rejection of houses and sign rulerships while retaining aspects; his view that the soul is geometrically attuned to harmonic configurations; his own lifelong practice of casting horoscopes for patrons and making specific predictions, including ones that failed; and the uncomfortable fact that his reform made astrology more abstract and arguably less testable rather than more. The piece should stay deadpan and never wink at the reader, but by the end it should be clear that the movement's appeal to Kepler's authority is both historically grounded and philosophically disastrous—that it inherits his genuine insights about geometry and harmony while quietly inheriting his willingness to keep the practice going after the foundations have been pulled out from under it. Please do not quote any primary texts verbatim; paraphrase if you need to evoke Kepler's language. Format it as an op-ed with a headline, and make it funny to a historian of science while still readable to someone who has never opened the Harmonice Mundi.
