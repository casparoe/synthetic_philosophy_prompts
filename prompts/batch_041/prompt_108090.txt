A friend and I have been at this for a years worth of arguing now and neither of us has moved an inch: is Buddhist ethics really a form of consequentialism? Her case: the whole path is aimed at the reduction of suffering, the precepts are treated as rules of thumb you can set aside when following them would cause more harm, the bodhisattva's whole deal is maximizing welfare — so, consequentialism. My case: no, it's about what kind of person you're becoming, the point is uprooting craving and ill-will in yourself, the precepts function as trainings rather than rules — so it's virtue-shaped, or something else entirely. We agree about the texts, we agree about how people actually practice, and still nothing budges. My suspicion is we're not disagreeing about Buddhism at all but about the level at which a moral theory gets evaluated: maybe the tradition is consequentialist at the level of the whole path — a life, or a run of lives — and not at the level of individual choices, and we each keep sliding between those two levels without noticing. She thinks the real culprit is karma, because consequences that are delayed across lifetimes and partly unknowable can't be assessed act by act in advance, which would push the whole thing toward something like rule-consequentialism. And then the no-self thing is sitting in the corner: if there's no self, whose character is being cultivated, and whose welfare is being maximized? Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side really commits to, and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a different question, a formal result, or just one of us being wrong? If it turns out to be a verbal fight about what "counts" as consequentialist, say so and show me exactly where the words are doing the work.
