I'm a novelist, not a philosopher — I did one term of philosophy of language at university and mostly remember the coffee — and I'm stuck on the middle of a book. Two of my characters are academics who've spent twenty years arguing about the same thing, and the plot turns on them being forced by their funding body into an "adversarial collaboration": they have to sit in a room and produce a single document they can both sign, or lose the grant. I need that document to actually exist, because a chapter of the novel is basically the document, and because several of my readers are philosophers who will smell a fake in about four seconds.

The arguement is about the norm of assertion. One of them thinks you should only assert what you know — assertion is the game where knowledge is the entry ticket, and if you assert something you don't know then you've done the thing wrong, even if it's true, even if you couldn't have done better. The other thinks the only thing that makes an assertion correct is truth: assert what's true, and if you've got a true thing to say then nothing about your evidence or your knowledge enters into whether the assertion itself was in order. Everything else people want to police — evidence, warrant, the fact that you're talking out of your hat — belongs to some other department (the ethics of belief, conversational manners, whatever).

I want you to be the person they'd have hired to chair the thing: someone who knows this literature cold, can hold both positions in their head at once, and won't let either side get away with a formulation that's really a sneer in a suit. Think it through carefully before you write anything.

Deliverable, please:

1. A short brainstorm at the top — five or six candidate ways of drawing the line between the two views, and for each one a note on which side would object and why. I'd definately rather see your rejected drafts than have you pretend there's only one sensible way to state it.
2. The joint statement itself, somewhere between 1,200 and 2,000 words. Numbered agreed claims first (what the question is, what data any decent view has to accommodate, what "norm" is doing in this context, what counts as an excuse as opposed to a permission, and so on), then a disagreements table with, for each crux: the crux in neutral wording; each side's own position, phrased the way its own side would sign off on; the case, argument or observation that both sides agree bears on it; and each side's prediction about it, written down in advance.

Things I think the cruxes are, though tell me if I'm wrong, and drop any that don't earn their place:

- the lottery ticket — "my ticket won't win" — is true, is very probably true, and sounds awful to say. Whose problem is that?
- someone who was entirely justified in believing p, asserted it, and p turned out false. Is she a norm-breaker who's excused, or a norm-keeper who got unlucky?
- the difference between asserting and being challenged to defend what you asserted, and the difference between both of those and "I take it back" when your evidence evaporates. Is "I take it back" the same norm talking, or a different one?
- whether a norm of assertion is supposed to explain why testimony can give us knowledge, or whether that's asking a norm to do a philosopher's job.
- assertion with no audience — the diary, the note to yourself, the soliloquy. If the norm is knowledge, does it still bite? If it's truth, does anything bite?

Two bits of my own that you can keep or bin:

(a) I'd like the document to keep three questions apart: what makes an assertion wrong, what makes it blameworthy, and what makes you answerable to a demand for your warrant. I've got a feeling half of this fight is about which of the three everyone's actually talking about when they say "the norm".
(b) I'd like it to keep apart the norm you answer to and the norm other people are entitled to hold you to. My money says one of the two camps is quietly talking about the second one.

Also: if any part of the disagreement turns out to be verbal — if they're using "assertion" or "norm" or "correct" in different senses — say so plainly, and say which part. And if a crux turns out to be one where both sides accept the same data and differ only in bookkeeping, say that too.

No citations, no reading lists, no "as so-and-so has argued". I can't check any of it and I don't want invented references turning up in a novel. Positions, not people.

British English throughout, please — my copyeditor is British and the book is set in Cambridge. And don't write it in character; I want the document itself, flat and clean, the way two people who've finally stopped scoring points would write it.
