I have to turn in a "philosophical self-portrait" for my minds and machines seminar and then defend it, so I want a consistency check first. Here's where I've landed on beliefs in machines: (1) I'm all in on the intentional stance — to say a system believes something is just to say that treating it as if it has beliefs is the best way to predict what it does, and there's no further fact about whether the belief is "really in there"; (2) but I'm not a fictionalist about beliefs either — when the stance genuinely earns its keep I think the beliefs are real, not useful make-believe, which is why I'm not an eliminativist about my friends having beliefs; (3) I'm certain people have beliefs, me included; (4) I'm equally certain current LLMs don't — when a chatbot says it thinks the meeting is Tuesday, attributing an actual belief to it is a category error, at best a metaphor; (5) I don't think humans have anything the models lack in kind — no special sauce, we're just physical systems shaped by selection instead of gradient descent.

What's nagging me is my labmate's model. She fine-tuned a small open model to hold a fixed set of views about her research area, and now, if you tell me those views, you can predict its answers to questions she never asked it about as reliably as I can predict my own advisor's opinions. I say the thing believes nothing; she says I'm doing species chauvanism with extra steps, and honestly (1), (2) and (5) look like they're on her side.

So which of the five do I actually have to give up, or is there a clause I can add that saves all of them without being ad hoc? Which pairs only look inconsistent? Rank them by how bad they are — I need to know what to defend first.
