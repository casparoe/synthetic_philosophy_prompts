I am in my final year of an undergraduate degree and am taking a module on the ethics and governance of advanced AI; as part of the assessment I have to run one of the seminar sessions, and I want to use the slot for something other than a presentation. After last week's class a few of us argued for an hour about the competitiveness constraint — the thought that a developer who unilaterally spends more on safety, or slows down, simply hands the field to developers who do not, so that whatever it costs to make a system safe rather than merely capable is paid first by whoever moves and harvested by everyone. What struck me afterwards is how bloodless the argument is when it is run as a dilemma on paper, and how much more it bites once you have to occupy the position of someone who must actually choose under it. I would like you to design that activity for me. Please take on the role of someone who both designs simulation games for philosophy and policy teaching and knows the technical governance literature well enough to keep the mechanics honest; think carefully before committing to a mechanism, and reason at the level of incentives rather than slogan.

The setting is a ninety-minute session with eighteen students, a mix of philosophy and computer science students, a few of whom have taken machine learning and a few of whom have not; the room has a projector and chairs, no other equipment, and I would rather not use real money. By the end the participants should have felt, rather than been told, that the competitiveness constraint is a claim about the structure of incentives and not a moral claim about developers; that its force depends on safety effort being a private cost with a widely shared benefit; that the argument changes shape if safety knowledge behaves more like a club good — non-rival, shareable among those who pay in, and not capability-conferring — and that a second constraint, the difficulty of verifying anyone else's safety claims, may bind before the competitive one does. I want the activity to make that second constraint visible without my having to announce it, and I would prefer not to name the concept at all until the debrief.

Give me the full design: the rules, the roles and the private briefs for them (including at least one participant whose private information makes them prefer that everyone else slows down), a payoff scheme in points rather than money, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, at least one mid-game event that alters the structure of the situation rather than merely raising the stakes, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the concepts above. Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you sketch three candidate mechanisms, say which one you would use and why the other two are worse for this group and this time slot, and only then give the finished design. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — how students will misread the rules, where the outcome will come out too tidy, which players will be tempted to break character — and how I should handle each of those. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which I shall therefore have to supply myself in discussion. Please write in British English.
