A philosopher of science whose seminar I have been sitting in on this term works on classification in the special sciences, and she is willing, when pressed, to give verdicts on particular categories. I have been keeping a list. Her verdicts so far are as follows.

The chemical elements are natural kinds in the fullest sense: being gold fixes a structure that explains nearly everything else true of the stuff, and the same holds for water. Jade is not a kind — the name covers nephrite, an amphibole, and jadeite, a pyroxene, two minerals of different composition and structure, and there is nothing to generalise over except a hardness that has its own explanation. The grey wolf is a kind, notwithstanding that wolves share no structure that could serve as an essence; common descent and gene flow do the work. Endotherms — warm-blooded animals — are a kind, even though endothermy arose separately in birds, in mammals and in a few fishes, so that the group has no single ancestor; she says the shared thermoregulatory machinery, and the selective regime that produced it, hold the cluster together well enough. Human racial categories are not kinds: the correlations that make them look informative are social rather than biological. The diagnostic category of schizophrenia is not a kind: it groups conditions of different course and cause, and generalisations about it fare badly. And 'weed' is not a kind: it names an agronomic inconvenience rather than anything with a unity of its own.

Now a group of clinical researchers has put a case to her that she has not yet answered. Clustering symptom profiles together with two blood markers drawn from a cohort of some forty thousand patients, they have identified a group they call type K. It cuts across the existing diagnostic categories, is stable on retesting, is substantially heritable, and predicts, with a large and repeatedly replicated effect, which patients respond to one particular treatment. No mechanism has been proposed for it and no shared causal process is known; the researchers are candid that the cluster is, so far, a pattern and nothing more.

Predict her verdict on type K. Set out the rules her earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with — I expect more than one — say what each of them predicts here, and give me your best guess together with your reasons for preferring it, including how much weight you think the fact that the elements were recognised as kinds long before their structures were understood ought to carry. If her record genuinely leaves the matter open, tell me what commitment she would be taking on with each answer. Finally, name the case that would best tell the candidate rules apart, and say which verdict on it would favour which rule.
