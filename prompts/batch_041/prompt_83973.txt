Ok so I've somehow agreed to run a 45-minute session for the reading group I'm in — it's a humanities-ish crowd, they've read a bunch of popular stuff about longtermism and AI risk, but basically no maths since school — and the thing I pitched is the unilateralist's curse. I don't want the explanation, I want a plan for how to explain it.

Here's roughly what I want them walking out with: when lots of people can each decide on their own to do some action, and the action is fine if a few do it but bad if lots do, then the action gets taken by whoever is the most optimistic about it. Each person's estimate of whether it's a good idea is noisy, so the fact that the one who acts is the extreme of that distribution means the decision gets made on an estimate that's biased optimistic. So over many independent decisions of this shape, actions that are net negative once you pool everyone's information still get done — and not because anyone's reckless or selfish, everyone can be careful and unbiased. I want them to see it as structural rather than a moral failing, and to be able to say which real situations it applies to and which it dont.

What I need from you:

- the order the pieces have to go in, and why each one can't come before the one it depends on
- the one example that carries the whole thing. I want it mundane — not nukes, not AI doom, the drama in those eats the structure — and it has to make the number-dependence obvious, since the entire point is that the badness depends on how many people do it. Tell me what the example needs to have and what it needs to lack (I'm worried that anything with a shared resource will have them jumping straight to tragedy of the commons)
- the misconceptions in the order they'll actually show up, and the exact point in my sequence where I kill each one. I'm already expecting: "this is the tragedy of the commons"; "this is just people being overconfident, so the fix is being humble"; "so we just need better information"; "isn't this the winner's curse"; and "so the answer is a global regulator" (I'd like to complicate that one rather than endorse it)
- one question to ask at the end that shows whether they've actually got it rather than just nodding — ideally something where they have to say which way the bias runs in a new case, or whether the curse is even present
