i host a small interview podcast about decision theory, and next week's guest is a decision theorist who's spent years arguing that satisficing is the correct account of practical rationality for finite agents, not just a description of how we cut corners when we can't do the math. she gets visibly annoyed whenever someone says satisficing is just expected-utility maximization with a search-cost term bolted on, and she thinks the whole "bounded rationality" label is a backhanded compliment. i don't want her coasting on the Simon stuff about aspiration levels, i want to actually push her. can you draft 9 or 10 interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give?

please make sure the set includes at least:

- one on the absorption objection -- why isn't satisficing just optimization once you add search costs, and if she says the costs are themselves unknowable, what stops the view from collapsing into "do whatever"?
- one on the flatness worry: once the aspiration level is met, are all above-threshold options equally choice-worthy, and if so how is that different from indifference with a cliff in it?
- one on aspiration-level adaptation -- levels drift up when things go well and down when they don't, so doesn't that make the satisficer easy to manipulate, or prone to sour-grapes revision of what counts as good enough?
- one on dynamic consistency: can a satisficer be walked into a money-pump-ish sequence, and does she accept that as a cost or deny it's a problem?
- one comparing her view to optimal-stopping results -- in something like the secretary problem there really does seem to be a best stopping rule, so what does that do to the claim that there's no privileged threshold?
- one on satisficing in a strategic setting: if everyone satisfices, what plays the role of equilibrium, and is that notion even well-defined?
- one that forces her to separate the descriptive claim (this is how we choose) from the normative one (this is how we should), since she tends to slide between them
- a closer asking what would have to happen, empirically or conceptually, for her to give up the view

no "what drew you to this" fluff, and dont ask her to recommend papers or anything like that -- just the questions. a couple of them should be weird enough that she cant answer with a canned paragraph.
