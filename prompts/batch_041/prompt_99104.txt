I'm prepping a film-club thing on Twelve Angry Men and I want to read it against the Condorcet jury theorem, not just trot out the usual "one brave man saves the system" civics lesson. Here's whats nagging me: the theorem says that if every juror is even slightly better than a coin flip and they vote independently, a bigger jury is more likely to land on the truth, so a 12-person jury should be epistemically pretty strong. But the entire dramatic engine of the film is that the jurors arent independent at all -- they're a herd, one man holds out, and the cascade finally breaks. So does the movie vindicate Condorcet or quietly refute him? I want a reading that takes both sides seriously: where the theorem actually illuminates a scene or a particular juror's decision, where the film leans on a confusion between "the majority is probably right" and "the majority is right" (the theorem tells you nothing about who's actually correct, and the movie plainly assumes the holdout is), and what the independence and competence assumptions are really doing in that room. And where does the film resist the reading -- is the juror with the grudge against his own son a counterexample to competence, or does that just show the assumptions are idealizations we shouldn't expect real juries to meet? Dont quote the script at me, I know it. Finish by saying whether the film adds anything to the debate that the math alone doesn't.
