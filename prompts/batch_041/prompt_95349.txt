I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I keep returning to the cluster of decision-theory puzzles around counterfactual mugging and blackmail. I'd like you to act as a decision theorist — someone who has thought carefully about the disagreements between causal, evidential, and updateless decision theories — and help me work out what I actually believe. Please begin by restating the cases and my verdicts in your own words, then brainstorm the candidate principles before committing to one; I would rather see the reasoning than a polished verdict.

Here are my verdicts. (1) Parfit's hitchhiker: I am stranded in the desert, and a driver will give me a ride only if I promise to pay $100 on arrival; once we reach town I will never see them again. I have promised. I pay. (2) Counterfactual mugging: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a coin. If it comes up heads, Omega pays me $10,000 if it predicted I would have paid $100 had it come up tails. If it comes up tails, Omega asks me for $100. I am in the tails branch, being asked. I do not pay. (3) The same, except I am offered the chance to pre-commit before the coin flip: I pre-commit to pay. (4) The same as (2), except Omega runs the scenario many times and I know it: I pay. (5) Logical counterfactual mugging: instead of a coin, Omega uses the parity of a digit of pi that I do not know, and I am in the branch where the digit is even (the tails analogue), being asked for $100: I do not pay. (6) XOR blackmail: a blackmailer tells me and a stranger that unless exactly one of us pays him $100, he will release damaging information about both of us, and we cannot communicate. I would randomize — flip a private coin to decide whether to pay — on the theory that if the stranger does the same, there is a decent chance exactly one of us pays. I am not confident that is principled rather than evasive.

My tentative principle is something like: pay when the benefit of your policy would accrue in a world that your current information has not ruled out, and do not pay when the benefit accrues only in a world you have already ruled out. That seems to get the hitchhiker, the one-shot mugging, pre-commitment, and iteration, and it might explain why I balk at the logical version. But I suspect it is either a real insight or a restatement of "decide as if you had not yet learned which branch you are in," and I do not know what it says about the randomization case. What I want from you: a precise principle, or a small set of candidates, each with the case that motivates it; the verdicts it gives on at least two cases I have not listed; and an honest statement of which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the best principle forces me to give up one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why.
