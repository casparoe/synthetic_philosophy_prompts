I'm writing a novel in which a character's wants reverse themselves over the years, and a broker of sorts trades with her at each stage, so that she ends by paying, in installments, to undo arrangements she had earlier paid to secure. My editor tells me this is a version of the money pump for diachronic rationality, and that it shows nothing: his view is that the person at thirty and the person at fifty are, for purposes of choice, like two different people, so a reversal between them is no more a lapse of reason than it is irrational for one man to prefer tea and another coffee, and a pump run on someone across time is merely the ordinary exploitation of a group whose members disagree. How far does the analogy hold? Give me the correspondence first — what plays the role of the distinct agents, the goods, the trades, the currency, the moment of exchange — then the places it breaks: the features the "two different people" picture quietly imports (separate memories, separate bodies, the capacity to bargain, the absence of a common umpire, an outside option) that the successive stages of one life do not have. Separate the disanalogies that bear on his conclusion from those that don't, and say whether the conclusion — that the pump indicts no one, because diachronic consistency was never required in the first place — survives once they are on the table. If a better analogy is available, give me that instead.
