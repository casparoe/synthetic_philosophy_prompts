I clerk for one of the judges on the Marovia Tribunal. We sit over the 1991–2001 war, and our statute is more or less the London Charter with the names changed — same three core crimes, same architecture — except ours was adopted in 2004, after the fighting stopped. So every single case gets the same objection from the defence: you're punishing us under law that didn't exist when we acted, you're the winners writing the rules after the fact, this is Nuremberg over again. We've now ruled on that objection five times and I have to draft the memo for the sixth. I can't tell anymore whether we have a rule or just five outcomes that happen to sit next to each other, and the defence is going to cite whichever one helps them.

Our track record, roughly:

- **The aggression case (2007).** The foreign minister was charged with planning and launching the invasion of the Kestrel Valley. Planning a war wasn't a crime under Marovian law, and Marovia had never ratified anything on aggression. Motion dismissed: the prohibition was already settled in the practice of states well before 1991, the statute only declares it, and the accused had personally voted for resolutions condemning aggression, so he can hardly plead surprise.
- **The peacetime atrocities case (2008).** His successor, charged with crimes against humanity for killings of his own minority inside his own country, before any armed conflict had started, all of it lawful under Marovian law at the time. Motion dismissed: acts like that offend the elementary requirements of humanity and were criminal under general principles recognised by all nations; nobody needs a domestic statute to tell them not to do it.
- **The police chief case (2009).** This is the one that went the other way. A police chief was charged with aiding and abetting a massacre by failing to warn the village, where Marovian law put no duty on him to act and he wasn't the superior of the men doing the killing. We quashed that count. The massacre was plainly criminal, but the mode of liability — a bare failure to act where no law imposed a duty — had no basis in any law in force at the time, and we said we wouldn't supply by construction a duty that no law had imposed. Two judges dissented: he knew exactly what was going to happen and can't claim surprise.
- **The duress case (2010).** Soldiers shot prisoners under orders and under threat of being shot themselves. Motion dismissed: neither superior orders nor duress is an answer where the act is manifestly unlawful, which our statute says and so does the law of every civilised nation.
- **The sentencing case (2011).** A convicted man argued his thirty-five years was retroactive, because Marovian law capped the nearest equivalent crime at twelve. Motion dismissed: Marovian law allowed life for murder, so nothing heavier was imposed on him, and anyway the guarantee goes to what conduct is criminal, not to how long you serve.

Now the sixth. The prosecution wants to charge Dušan Radek, who was district registrar in Bela Reka from 1995 to 1997. His office kept what everyone called the ethnic register — the lists saying which families were of the minority, where they lived, how many children. Those lists went to the paramilitaries and were used to evict and deport the families. Radek is charged with persecution as a crime against humanity, on the theory that running that register was itself the crime. He says Marovian law required him to keep it and he'd have been prosecuted for refusing, he was told the lists were for ration cards, he never knew they went anywhere else, and nobody ever told him paperwork could be a crime against humanity. The prosecution doesn't really dispute that he didn't know where the lists ended up, though they say by 1995 anyone with eyes knew what an ethnic register was the first step towards. The actual killing was done by men who've all been convicted or are dead.

What should we do with Radek, and what would I be able to say we were doing?

Give it to me in this order. (1) **Bottom line**, two or three sentences: your outcome and the shortest honest statement of why. (2) **The rules we could be running**, a numbered list of the genuinely different rules that fit all five of our rulings, each stated precisely enough to generate predictions, with a note on which rulings it explains cleanly and where it has to strain. (3) **Closest precedent**, about a hundred words on which of the five is the nearest analog and which one is the tempting-but-wrong analogy. (4) **What each rule does with Radek**, about two hundred words, running every rule from (2) through his facts. (5) **What I'd recommend**, about a hundred and fifty words, the outcome that best coheres with our own precedents plus a plain statement of what principle we'd be adopting either way — I have to defend this in conference and I'd rather not find out halfway through that we've been doing victors' justice with extra steps. (6) **The motion that would settle it**, about a hundred words, the next case that would actually seperate the rules, ideally something a prosecutor might plausibly bring.
