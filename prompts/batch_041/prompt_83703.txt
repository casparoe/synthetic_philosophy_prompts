I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy writing on the metaphysics of death, and next month I am co-teaching a single ninety-minute session with an intensive-care physician for a deliberately mixed group: roughly eight critical-care fellows and senior ICU nurses, and six philosophy graduate students. The clinicians can execute the hospital's death-determination protocol without thinking but have never been asked what makes a body dead; the philosophers have absorbed the debates in the abstract but have never stood at a bedside. Take the role of someone who has both served on a hospital ethics committee and taught this material to graduate students, and reason carefully before you answer.

I do not want an explanation of the brain-death versus circulatory-death controversy. I want a teaching plan for the session: the order in which the ideas have to be introduced, with an account of what depends on what; the single carrying example the whole session should hang on; the misconceptions the audience will arrive with or form, and the exact point in the sequence at which to defuse each; the check for understanding that would show the structure has landed; and what we should deliberately leave out, said out loud as a deliberate omission.

Work through the following, in this order, before you write the plan. First, sketch the dependency structure. In particular, decide where the distinction between what death is, how we tell, and what test we run has to fall, and where the distinction between a heart that will not restart on its own and a heart that cannot in principle be restarted has to fall — and defend those placements rather than assuming them. Second, brainstorm three or four candidate carrying examples and rank them before committing to one; tell me what you discarded and why. I am inclined toward a case in which a patient's heart has permanently stopped but circulation and oxygenation are being maintained by an external circuit, so that the team must decide what "circulation has ceased" means when a machine is doing the circulating; if you can do better, say what your example buys that mine does not, and what it costs. Third, map the misconceptions onto the sequence. I expect at least these: that brain death is a deep coma; that brain death is merely a legal convention for harvesting organs; that a stopped heart makes death obvious and therefore uncontroversial; and the reverse of that last one — that if a machine keeps the organs perfused, no one in the room is really dead yet.

Give me one closing question, with a sketch of what a good and a poor answer to it would look like, that separates a student who has grasped the definition/criterion/test structure from one who has merely memorized the criteria. Do not supply citations, a reading list, or references to named court cases or authors: assume no one in the room has heard of any of them, and that the concepts must carry the weight on their own.
