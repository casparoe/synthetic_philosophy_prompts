I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about the continuum hypothesis, and I should like to close the argument with a poem rather than another round of it. His position is that the question has a determinate answer and that our present inability to decide it within ZFC is a temporary embarrassment: that some richer axiom, or some principle of maximality we have not yet hit upon, will in the end reveal whether the continuum is the first uncountable cardinal or whether there is an intermediate size, and that until then the independence results are merely a sign that we are working with too thin a theory. My position is the duller one that Gödel's constructible universe and Cohen's forcing give us models on both sides, so that the axioms as we have them certify both answers and neither, and that the feeling that there must be a fact of the matter is a feeling and not a theorem.

I should like a villanelle that takes his side seriously enough to enact it. Let the two refrains be the two competing verdicts about the size of the continuum, and let the form's compulsion to bring the refrains together in the closing couplet do the work of showing why the dream of a single settled answer is so hard to give up: the last two lines should stand side by side as a forced marriage rather than a resolution, and a set theorist ought to be able to recognise in them both Gödel's constructible universe and Cohen's forcing without either being named. Please write it in British English. Beyond the accuracy of the mathematics, I should like a short note on how confident you are in the reading of the independence results you have built into the poem, and on the one point where you are least sure that the poem is faithful to the mathematics rather than merely to the mood.
