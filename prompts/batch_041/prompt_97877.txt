My friend and I argued late into the night about the gender-equality paradox, and I want you to settle it. She defends what I will call the release view: the finding that richer, more gender-equal societies show larger average differences between men and women in what they prefer and choose reflects deep-seated dispositions that surface once scarcity and formal barriers recede. I defend what I will call the staging view: those preferences are partly constituted by whichever status hierarchies and institutions a society leaves standing, so dismantling one set of gender constraints can make gender a more salient axis of self-presentation and thereby widen the measured divergence, making it a product of culture rather than a window onto nature. Compare the two: what each predicts that the other does not; how each copes with the pattern's sensitivity to how equality and preferences are measured and aggregated; whether it matters that the divergence shows up more cleanly in expressed preferences than in realized outcomes; and what each implies about whether the pattern is a matter of justice at all. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
