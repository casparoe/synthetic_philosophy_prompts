I'm assembling a position on the structure of value and I want an audit before I commit to it in a handout. Here is what I hold.

(1) Betterness is transitive: if A is better than B, and B better than C, then A is better than C.

(2) Betterness is not total. Some pairs of goods — freedom and welfare, for instance — are genuinely incomparable, and the incomparability is a feature of the values themselves, not a gap in my knowledge.

(3) Take the population spectrum: at one end a small population whose lives go very well; at the other a far larger population whose lives are barely worth living; each intermediate world is a small adjustment of its neighbour. I hold that every step is an improvement — there is no point at which the sequence stops getting better.

(4) I do not believe in thresholds. Any principle that singles out a particular point in the spectrum and declares improvement to cease there is arbitrary, and I reject it.

(5) The repugnant conclusion is false: a vast population of lives barely worth living is not better than a small flourishing population.

(6) Comparisons are local and graded. When two worlds differ only slightly, the comparison between them turns on that small difference alone, not on the absolute levels involved — a small improvement is a small improvement, the same small amount, wherever in the spectrum it occurs.

Tell me whether this set hangs together. Which commitments pull against one another, which pairs only appear to and can in fact be held together, and — for the genuine conflicts — how severe each is, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs me. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and if not everything can be kept, say what has to go and what the least costly revision leaves standing.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single commitment, if there is one, that has to go — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
