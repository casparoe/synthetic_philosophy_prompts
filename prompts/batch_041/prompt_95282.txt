I chair the governance committee of a regional transit authority, and in six weeks we vote on a proposal to settle a stalled capital decision by prediction market rather than by our ordinary deliberation. I need the decision memorandum I will circulate to the committee three days before the vote.

Background. The board has been deadlocked for three budget cycles over the same three-mile downtown corridor. Option A converts the corridor to bus-priority lanes with transit signal priority: about $50 million in capital, fourteen months of construction, service changes within two years. Option B builds a light-rail spur along the same corridor: about $600 million, six to seven years, contingent on a federal match we have not been awarded. The board splits 5-4 along lines that track district boundaries, the citizens' advisory panel is split the same way, the transit operators' union has reservations about the bus option's effect on layover space, and the mayor wants a decision before the next budget.

The proposal. One member wants us to commission two conditional prediction markets before the vote, one per option, each resolving against a stated outcome metric measured two years after implementation, and to adopt a resolution committing the authority to build whichever option's conditional market clears at the higher price. The design as offered: volunteer traders; a $5,000 prize pool donated by a nonprofit, because state law bars us from paying cash to private parties outside procurement; board members, staff, and current contractors barred from trading; prices published under our open-meetings and public-records obligations; and no attempt to bind a successor board. Our counsel advises that a binding delegation is probably unenforceable and may sit awkwardly with the statute that places the choice with the board, so in practice the markets would be advisory.

What I need. Recommend one of three courses — adopt as proposed, reject, or adopt in modified form, specifying the modification — with the motion language the committee could actually move and a timetable that fits the six weeks. Assume the committee has no background in market design. If markets are to be used at all, make the design decisions rather than listing them as open: which metric or metrics the markets resolve on, chosen from candidates we have already floated (ridership gain per dollar, corridor small-business revenue, on-time performance, cost per new rider) or something better, and why that metric does not pick the winner by construction; who may trade and with what incentive to trade honestly; how the prices reach the board; and what weight the board gives them. Give the two or three strongest objections a fair hearing — in particular whether conditional prices can be driven by traders' expectations about what the board will do, or about how the metric will eventually be measured, rather than by which option serves riders better — and say plainly which objections are decisive and which are not. Close with what would have to be true, or what we would have to observe, for the committee to revisit the question. Two pages at most.
