I am a comparative legal scholar giving the after-dinner address — six minutes, no slides — at the farewell dinner for a colleague who is retiring after a career spent studying how the four big Western democracies actually finance religion. The room is genuinely mixed: half are specialists in these jurisdictions, half are the retiree's family and a few foundation trustees who will glaze over at the first piece of legal detail. Write the address.

I want it built around a thesis of mine rather than merely decorated with one, and the thesis has to do structural work: that there is no such thing as the absence of an establishment, only the relocation of it into the fiscal and administrative machinery of the state. The material to work from: England keeps an established church that almost no one attends, funded now largely by endowment and voluntary giving — its compulsory parish church rate was abolished long before anyone else separated anything; Germany establishes no church, yet the state collects the church tax as a surcharge on the income tax, which is the bulk of church revenue, and the state, not the church, keeps the roll of who belongs, so that one leaves a church by making a declaration to a civil authority — the state administers apostasy; France has been formally separated since 1905, except in Alsace-Moselle, where the Concordat of 1801 survives, the state pays the salaries of the clergy of four recognised faiths, and religious instruction is taught in state schools; the United States establishes nothing and exempts churches from tax, which obliges its revenue service to decide what counts as a church and what counts as religion at all. Sort the four along four dimensions — legal preference, fiscal entanglement, who keeps the membership roll, and who must decide what counts as a religion — and let the speech's surprise be the inversion on the last two: the state's doctrinal judgments are largest exactly where the country's self-description is most separationist.

The thesis imposes constraints I want honoured. I cannot thank my colleague for a career spent advancing secularism or religious freedom, because on my account those descriptions are part of what conceals the establishment rather than what dissolves it; the praise must be re-described in terms I can actually stand behind. The non-specialists have to follow without a glossary, so each abstract point needs one concrete image. Keep the tone affectionate and the occasion present: this is a dinner, not a lecture.

Then, separately from the speech, give me a JSON object with exactly these fields: "thesis" (one sentence); "claims" (array of objects with "claim", "jurisdiction" — one of England, Germany, France, United States, comparative — and "confidence", one of high, medium, low); "least_confident" (an object with "claim", "why", and "what_would_settle_it", naming the single point you are least sure of); "liberties" (anything in the speech that leans on a contested reading or simplifies); and "platitudes_avoided". Nothing from this second part may appear inside the speech.
