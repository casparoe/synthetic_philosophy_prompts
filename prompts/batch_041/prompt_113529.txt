I've been running a graduate seminar on epistemic circularity, and the unit on bootstrapping has itself stalled — not because anyone has run out of arguments, but because the arguments have stopped making contact. Here is the shape of it in my own words, so that nothing turns on your having read the same things we have.

The bootstrapper forms a belief by a process and then uses that very belief as a premise in an argument for the reliability of the process. She looks at a wall; it looks red to her; she believes the wall is red. She then notes that if the wall is red it will look red to her, a conditional she can know without knowing anything about how her vision works; she already takes herself to know the wall is red; together those give her that the wall is red just in case it looks red to her; and from that she concludes that her colour vision is reliable. The same pattern runs with a fuel gauge (the gauge reads full, the tank is full, so the gauge is reliable) and with memory or testimony in place of perception.

Four things are said about it. (1) It is a reductio: any account of warrant that licenses it licenses too much, since it lets a subject establish the reliability of a process from beliefs that process produced, with no independent check, and the right response is a principle that rules the pattern out whatever else that costs. (2) It is valid, and its conclusion is warranted whenever the process really is reliable; the bad feeling is a mistake — a confusion between having warrant and being able to defend the belief to a sceptic, or between circular reasoning and circular warrant. (3) A middle verdict is available: the reasoning is fine but yields no knowledge, or the subject may hold her perceptual belief yet may not use it as a premise in this inference. (4) The cases are underdescribed and the intuition flips with the description — whether the subject is said to be checking, whether she has any inkling of the circularity, whether alternative explanations of the reading are in view — so the debate is about descriptions rather than principles.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about which principles of warrant are true, a verbal one about the words knowledge and justification, a disagreement about what a theory of knowledge is for (matching the extension of a concept versus explaining the order of reasons), or a clash of intuitions about a case that argument cannot reach? And I want the diagnosis to earn its keep on two cases the familiar ones do not settle.

(a) Double-checking. A bookkeeper adds a column, gets a total, redoes the addition from the bottom, gets the same total, and takes the agreement as a reason to trust her arithmetic. The second pass is the same faculty as the first; there is no independent check anywhere; yet the agreement seems like a reason, and not a foolish one. If the bootstrapper's inference is ruled out, is this ruled out with it? If not, what is the difference — two draws from the process rather than one, the conditional independence of the errors, the absence of a presupposed reliability premise — and does the difference survive when the subject is described as knowing nothing about how the process fails?

(b) Credence. Put the bootstrapper's position in degrees of belief: given that the wall looks red, does her credence that her colour vision is reliable rise at all? If the appearance of probative force in the all-or-nothing version is an artefact of the idiom, say so. If the credal version shows a small genuine confirmation, say what it depends on — the space of alternative failure modes, the base rates — and whether the subject can have what it depends on from the inside.

My hypothesis, offered as one: the stall is not about the cases but about what a warrant has to be — something the subject can reason from, in which case circularity is fatal, or a status a belief can have whether or not the subject can put it to use, in which case the intuitions are tracking our discomfort at a belief we cannot defend. If that is right, the sides are not disagreeing about knowledge but about the direction of explanation in epistemology, and the remedy is to say which question one is answering rather than to keep generating cases. Tell me whether the diagnosis is right, what the disagreement really is, and what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or why none should be expected. I don't need a reading list.
