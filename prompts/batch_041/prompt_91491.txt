I cover politics and ideas for a weekly, and I also do a bit of teaching on the side — I'm an associate lecturer on an MA in political thought, which is how I've ended up setting next term's paper. I need one essay question on the boundary problem, the 'who belongs to the demos' thing, and I want it to be the sort of question that can't be answered by reciting the standard positions. Most of my students will have read the usual material on all-affected interests and all-subjected-to-coercion and will produce a tidy summary of the circularity, and then stop. I want them to have to actually think, and to take a side.

So here's my plan. I've built them a case — a composite of a few referendums I've covered — and the question hangs off that. The case: a region is holding an independence referendum, and two fights about the franchise turn out to be the whole story. Inside the region, the pro-independence side wants the electoral roll frozen at a date years in the past, so that people who've moved in since — mostly civil servants and mainlanders — can't vote; the unionist side says that strips residents of equal political standing and hands the result to whoever happened to be living there at the right moment. Outside the region, everyone agrees the rest of the country will be materially changed by the outcome (debt, borders, the lot) and nobody out there gets a vote. The fudge that eventually gets agreed is a concurrent majority: the proposal carries only if it wins inside the region and also wins a seperate advisory ballot in the rest of the country.

The question needs to ask them whether that fudge solves the boundary problem, dissolves it, or just moves it back a step — and it has to force a verdict on the underlying issue, namely whether the circularity is a genuine regress that any theory of democracy has to live with, or a false dilemma that comes from a bad picture of what democratic legitimacy is for. I'd also like it to push them on whether the compromise is even internally coherent, since it seems to be running two different inclusion criteria at once, one for the inner ballot and another for the outer one, and I want to see whether they notice that and what they make of it.

No citations, no reading lists, no 'as X argues' padding in the model answer — my students can't get online in the exam anyway and I'd rather the marks went on the argument. It's the final question on a two-hour paper and they get about an hour for it, out of 20 marks.

So could you give me: the exam question itself, with the case set out above it as a short scenario; a model answer of roughly 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. The scheme has to say clearly what separates an A from a B — name the specific moves an A has to make that a B won't, not just 'shows understanding'.

One last thing: write the whole thing in British English, please. I'm a stickler about it and my students get marked down for 'organize' and 'behavior'.
