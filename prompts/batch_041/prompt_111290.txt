I teach a seminar on Buddhist ethics and consequentialism, and I keep losing the same argument in office hours. My students want to say that once you strip off the cosmology, the whole tradition is just consequentialism with nicer branding: minimize suffering, maximize welfare, done. The colleague in the next office over insists it's virtue ethics, because the precepts are training rules aimed at character rather than a calculus. I think they're both wrong, but every time I try to say what the third thing is I end up gesturing at compassion and then trailing off, which is not a good look in front of grad students. So here is what I actually think about a pile of cases, and I want you to tell me what principle, if anything, they add up to.

The classic bodhisattva ship-captain case: a captain learns that a passenger intends to murder the five hundred merchants aboard, and out of compassion he kills the man himself rather than warn anyone. I think that's permitted, and maybe even what a bodhisattva ought to do; I don't think he violates the precept against killing, though I do think he takes on something heavy. Now a structurally similar one: a soldier shoots an enemy sniper who is about to fire into a crowd of civilians. I think that's permitted too, but I feel much worse about it, and I'd resist calling it an act of compassion. Then the footbridge case — shoving a large man off a bridge to stop a runaway cart and save five — I think that's flatly forbidden, arithmetic be damned. A monk lies to a hunter about which way the deer went: fine, no problem, no bad karma that I can see. A doctor lies to a dying patient about the prognosis to spare them fear: wrong, or at least not obviously fine, even though it's a compassionate lie with good consequences. Withdrawing a ventilator from a patient who won't recover, at the family's request: fine. Giving that same patient a lethal injection at their own request: not fine, and I can't defend the distinction by outcomes, since the outcomes are identical. Culling a deer herd to head off mass starvation: against it, though the consequentialist case is honestly decent. And a bodhisattva who does the externally right act while quietly preening about how compassionate he is — I think that's worse than the same act done without the preening, which is a strange thing for a consequentialist to have to say.

A case I keep going back and forth on: a politician who could secretly have a genocidal leader assassinated, ending a war, with near-certainty it would work and near-certainty no one would ever know. My gut says no, but my gut says yes to the ship captain, and I genuinely can't tell you what the difference is supposed to be. So: is there a principle that gets all of this, or close? I suspect it needs at least three moving parts — something about the agent's intention, something about what the act does to the victim's own karmic trajectory, and something about not using anyone merely as a means — but I want you to tell me whether those clauses are doing real work or whether I'm just patching. State the principle as precisely as you can, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (specifically: killing one person to save five when the one is a fully informed, willing volunteer — I want your verdict on that), and be honest about which of my verdicts the thing forces me to abandon. If the cleanest version has to sacrifice the ship-captain case, say so and tell me whether I should accept that. And if my colleague is right that this is virtue ethics in robes, or the students are right that it's consequentialsim, say that too — but only if the cases actually support it, not because those are the two familiar boxes.
