I run a monthly reading group for graduate students in cognitive science and neuroscience, and the next session is on Molyneux's problem. I don't want the familiar cataract-surgery case as the anchor; I want the group to work through a contemporary analogue I'll hand them: an adult blind from birth who has for years identified objects using a sensory-substitution device that converts a camera's output into a soundscape she has learned to read, and who then gains sight. Plan the session for me rather than answering Molyneux's question. Give me the order in which the components have to be introduced and why each depends on the last — in particular, what has to be on the table before the question stops sounding like a yes-or-no about surgery; the one case that should carry the discussion and what it must have and must lack to do so; the misconceptions the group will arrive with (I expect: that this is an empirical question the sight-restoration literature has settled, that "shape" names a single capacity, and that the device has simply taught her what things look like) and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; the single closing question that would reveal whether they have understood the problem rather than picked a side; and what I should openly refuse to resolve at this level.
