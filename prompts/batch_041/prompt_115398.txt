I am trying to decide how much credit a long-run social-scientific theory deserves when its timing was badly wrong. Consider the proposition that the Malthusian mechanism -- population growth tends to press against subsistence unless checked, so durable per-capita gains require preventive checks or repeated productivity shocks -- was right but early: operative in the relevant historical episodes, with the missed timing due to variables the theory could in principle have included (technological substitution, fossil fuels, trade, and the demographic transition as an endogenous check) rather than to an ad hoc rescue that insulates it from falsification. I put my credence at 0.4. Give me yours, decomposed into at least: (1) the probability that the mechanism is true as a conditional; (2) the probability that the historical escape was contingent rather than a structural refutation; (3) the probability that the demographic transition counts as a Malthusian check rather than an escape; (4) the probability that the right-but-early verdict is a legitimate epistemic status rather than a rescue; and any further term you think belongs. For each term give a number and one line of justification, then combine them. Finally, tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it most. If you think the proposition is not truth-evaluable as stated, replace it with the nearest truth-evaluable version and give a number for that.
