Right, I'm deep in a chapter on Dennett's quining-qualia move and I need an actual number out of you, not another tour of the literature.

Setup, quickly. Two tasters at Maxwell House, both used to love the coffee, both now can't stand it. One says the coffee tastes exactly the way it always did and he's simply gone off it; the other says the coffee itself has changed, it's bitter and metallic now. Dennett's line is that nothing in the world or in their behaviour settles which of them is right about the taste, so if qualia are supposed to be intrinsic, private, ineffable and handed to the subject incorrigibly, here's a case with no fact of the matter to latch onto — and a posit that can't have a fact of the matter is a posit to drop.

Heres the thing that's making me doubt the example. Post-covid parosmia. Loads of people — three that I know — came out of an infection with coffee smelling like sewage or burning rubber, they can describe it in ridiculous detail, and it isn't just talk: the receptors are damaged, the distortion tracks the damage, and when the epithelium recovers the smell reverts and they report the old smell coming back. So here's a first-person report about what an experience is like that (a) is stable and repeatable, (b) is predicted from outside the head, and (c) seems to have a determinate answer to the question of what the coffee smells like to that person at that time — except the answer looks like it's carried by the nose rather than by any inner property of the experience. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a point for Dennett (the report is tracking the periphery, not a quale) or against him (we appear to have found a fact of the matter where he said there couldn't be one).

So: what's your credence that qualia, in that strict four-feature sense, are an incoherent posit — that is, that there is no coherent set of conditions under which the concept is satisfied, as opposed to just our being wrong about which things have them?
