I am a novelist at work on a comic novel set inside a lavishly funded consulting firm whose entire business rests on a category error. The firm sells what it calls incompleteness strategy: it assures corporate clients that no system can fully know itself, that every compliance apparatus is necessarily incomplete, that their five-year roadmaps contain propositions that are true but unprovable, and so on — all of it delivered in the borrowed vocabulary of mathematical logic. I need one document from inside that world, and I need it to be funny because it is exact rather than because it is loose.

You are an expert in mathematical logic and the philosophy of mathematics, and you are also a first-rate satirist. Please write the text of the firm's client-facing FAQ: eight to ten numbered questions with answers, roughly 800 words, deadpan from first line to last, in the voice of a confident and faintly pompous technical writer who has no idea that he is talking nonsense. The humor must come from the content, so that a logician laughs in recognition rather than wincing. No winking at the reader, no narrator's aside explaining the joke.

Before you write, spend a few lines thinking through the distinctions you intend to exploit, and then build each answer on one of them: the difference between provability and truth; the difference between a theory's being consistent and its being able to prove its own consistency; the difference between completeness in the sense that belongs to first-order logic and incompleteness in the sense that belongs to arithmetic; the conditions under which the incompleteness results actually apply at all — consistency, effective axiomatizability, sufficient expressive strength — and what happens to the whole apparatus when a "system" is a company, a market, or a person and satisfies none of them. I would also like at least one answer whose premise quietly converts the familiar claim that a human mind outruns any formal system into something the theorems do not license without further argument, and at least one answer that treats an undecidable sentence as though it were merely an unsolved problem awaiting more resources.

The target of the satire is the practice of borrowing incompleteness as a universal metaphor for epistemic limitation, not any particular person; an invented founder or house guru is welcome, but do not name real living figures, and do not include citations, references, or reading lists — this document exists only in the world of the novel. Make definite choices: commit to a single firm name, a single founding anecdote, a single doctrinal slogan, and a single house style, rather than hedging between options. Write as though you believe every word of it.

Close with a short note, in your own voice, on where the piece takes liberties with the mathematics and on which of its readings of the theorems a logician would contest — I want to know exactly where I am cheating, so that the cheat is a deliberate one.
