I teach a grad seminar on archives and historical method — 14 students, 90 minutes, one seminar room, no computers. I want an activity that makes them feel how an archive produces its own silences without anyone ever deciding to hide anything. The point I want them to run into is that the filtering happens in the boring technical rules (what counts as a record, who counts as a creator, what the subject index lets you say, what's too fragile to hand out), so that the exclusion is nobody's decision and everybody's outcome. They should hit that wall themselves before I say a word about it.

Here's the setup I've half-built, fix it as you like. I've invented a city archive — Eastvale, made up, call it whatever — that has just taken in a big accession: the complete business papers of a demolition and redevelopment firm, plus a much smaller box of stuff from the neighbourhood the firm levelled. I want the students to sit as the appraisal committee, and I want the archive's own intake rules to be the trap. So write me three or four rules that look neutral and professional, of roughly these kinds, and make them airtight enough that nobody can just ignore them: (1) nothing gets catalogued unless it has a named creator or office of origin and a date range; (2) anything retained has to be indexed using the archive's controlled subject list — give me that list, maybe 25 or 30 terms, and make it the kind of list that looks generous and inclusive but is built out of the categories of municipal government (public works, planning, licensing, public order, charitable organisations, that sort of thing); (3) anything too fragile to be consulted gets kept but closed for 25 years; (4) some kind of budget, so they can't keep everything — I was thinking shelf-metres and processing hours rather than money, since money makes it feel like a funding problem instead of an intellectual one. Give me the accession cards as a printable handout: volume, condition, processing cost, provenance status, and a line or two of what's actually in each one. The firm's records should be enormous, well-organised and easy to describe; the neighbourhood material should be a mess of undated letters, a rent book, a hand-drawn street plan, a cassette of songs, someone's minutes from a tenants' association that never incorporated. Nothing exotic — the point is that the awkward stuff is awkward for boring reasons.

Then I want a second phase where three researchers show up with questions and the students have to answer them out of what they actually kept. Pick questions where the failure is predictable and diagnostic: something the firm's papers answer beautifully, and one or two that the neighbourhood material would have answered but that now can't be answered at all, or can only be answered in a way that has to be hedged to death. Then a late arrival — a shoebox that came in after the paperwork was signed, no creator, no dates, dialect in it that isn't in the subject list. Let them decide what happens to it and let the rules decide for them.

Give me the full runnable thing: materials, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief. In the debrief I want each moment of the activity mapped onto the part of the machinery that produced it — intake, appraisal, description, access — and I want the group to notice that the silence wasn't an absence, it was a set of records that exist and can't be found. One thing I'd like if it's cheap: a way to run the appraisal twice with the roles shuffled, so they can see the outcome is stable across people and therefore isn't about anyone's bias.

Two more things. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm expecting at least one student to game the subject list by stretching a term until it covers a rent strike, and another to refuse the rules outright and demand a new category, and I don't know yet whether those are bugs or the best part of the session. And be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce: my suspicion is that it can't give them the scale of it, the fact that real silences are laid down over decades by people who each did something defensible, and that it can't give them the other end of it either, the position of the person whose life never made it into the box.
