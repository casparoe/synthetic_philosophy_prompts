I'm teaching Merchant of Venice next term to a seminar that's half law students, half philosophers, and I want to use the trial scene to teach the textualism/purposivism fight instead of rehasing Scalia vs Breyer for the hundredth time. Here's the reading I've half worked out — shore it up or knock it down.

Portia wins by out-literalling Shylock: the bond gives him a pound of flesh and says nothing about blood, so he gets nothing. Fine, but notice what the move requires, and notice how quickly the literalism gets spent. Shylock stakes everything on the exact words of the bond; Portia beats him by taking those words even more exactly than he does. Now, textualists (Scalia loudest of all) always insist they aren't strict constructionists — you read a text for its ordinary public meaning, not for the most literal possible sense of each word — and Portia looks like exactly the strict constructionist they keep trying to distance themselves from. So: is she a textualist, a strict constructionist, or is she doing equity in textualist drag? Is her move interpretation at all, or a refusal to enforce dressed up as a reading? And when the court pivots from the bond to the statute against aliens, it stops caring about exact words entirely, so was literalism ever the court's theory, or just its weapon of the moment?

Second thing. The instrument the case is ostensibly about is a private contract; what actually destroys Shylock is a statute of Venice about an alien plotting against a citizen's life. So the court reads the contract the way you'd read a public statute, and then reads the actual statute however it likes. Does the play even hand us the right kind of legal text for this debate? Contract interpretation is supposed to be about the parties' intention, not the purpose of a rule, so Portia may be smuggling a public-law theory of reading into a private instrument — and then the Duke doesn't interpret anything at all, he just declines to impose the statutory penalty, on Antonio's plea, with Shylock's conversion as the price. If interpreting a rule and pardoning a breach of it come apart like that, which one is the play actually about?

Third, I want the casket subplot in the reading, because I think it's the play's other theory of reading and it points the opposite way: the suitors who read the inscriptions as straightforward promises about what they'll get loose, and the one who picks the casket that asks something of him instead wins. So the legal plot seems to reward literalism and the romantic plot seems to reward something closer to purposive, self-implicating reading. If that's a real tension and not just me overreading, what is the play saying about interpretation? Or is the honest answer that there's no consistent doctrine here, just a demonstration that method gets chosen to fit the outcome? Because that's the reading I'm tempted by: the court gets to be literalist exactly when literalism kills the outsider, and generous exactly when mercy costs the insider nothing.

What I don't want is a survey of the debate or a list of articles. I want the reading: where the textualism/purposivism frame genuinely illuminates the scene, where the play resists it and I should stop pushing, and above all whether the play dramatizes something the Scalia/Breyer fight misses. My hunch is that what it gets that the theorists don't is the politics of interpretive method — that the choice of method is itself an exercise of power. Tell me if that's too cynical for the text, and whether it changes the answer to whether Portia is applying a theory at all.
