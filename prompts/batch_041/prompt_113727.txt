I am assembling materials for a seminar on confirmation, and I want a poem that will do the work a diagram does: it should let a reader feel the raven paradox from inside the reasoning rather than hear it summarized. Please write it in American English.

The structural burden falls on contraposition. Write two unrhymed blocks of fourteen lines each — twenty-eight lines in all, plus a title. The first block states and tests the hypothesis in its familiar form, every raven is black, and reasons through it by the principle that a generalization is supported by its instances. The second states the same hypothesis in its contrapositive form, every non-black thing is a non-raven, and reasons through it by the principle that logically equivalent hypotheses must be confirmed by the same evidence. As nearly as sense permits, the second block should be the first block read backward, with each key term replaced by its complement, so that the last line of the second block answers to the first line of the first, and the two blocks can be read as one claim spoken twice. Where the mirroring cannot be exact, let the strain show: the poem should not pretend that the two forms sit together as comfortably as the logic says they do. The turn belongs at the hinge between the blocks. Use unrhymed iambic pentameter if you can sustain it; where you cannot, prefer plain syntax to ornament, and do not force rhyme for its own sake.

The observation should be concrete, and it should be made by someone who cares about ravens — a person running a count, say, who stops for lunch and finds a green apple in their bag. Describe the object in particulars; do not name a type and move on. Nothing in the poem should appear in quotation marks except words the speaker says aloud, and no philosopher should be named.

By the end the poem should have registered the following without lecturing:

- that the apple is a confirming instance of the raven hypothesis, and that this follows from two principles each of which looks obviously right taken alone;
- that the increment of confirmation is real and vanishingly small, given how many non-black things there are for every raven — one line can carry the arithmetic, and it should land as a fact about the world rather than as a formula;
- that the hypothesis would be satisfied by a world containing no ravens at all, and that this is part of why the apple counts;
- the question of what the speaker was doing when they looked — whether the object was chosen for its color or drawn from the non-black things without regard to what it might turn out to be — left open rather than settled;
- the asymmetry of feeling, that the raven seems to tell us something and the apple nothing, when by the logic they are the same evidence for the same claim. The poem must neither treat that feeling as a mere mistake nor treat it as decisive.

The closing line should land as the contrapositive of the opening one, so that the poem ends where it began, in the other form, with the puzzle still standing. Someone who knows the paradox should be able to say which block argues from instances and which from equivalence, and someone who does not should still find it a good poem. Give me the poem alone, with a title.
