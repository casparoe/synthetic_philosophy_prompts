I'm producing a little philosphy podcast — part seminar, part late-night call-in show — and next month's guest is a philosopher who's been developing a view she calls graduated sentientism. I'm a radio producer, not a philosopher, so I may be mangling the terms. As I understand it from her very drafty manuscript, moral status isn't a switch but a matter of degree, and the degree tracks how much an entity's states matter to it: not just whether it detects damage or registers a noxious input, but whether there's a felt ouch that it cares about. She adds a second twist I haven't seen put quite this way: she thinks sentience comes in at least two dimensions, a valence dimension (can it suffer or enjoy?) and a temporal-integration dimension (does it have a life that hangs together over time?), and she wants to say moral status is a vector across those dimensions rather than a single scalar. She also floats a precautionary asymmetry: the worse the possible experience, the lower our confidence threshold should be before we take the entity's interests into account. I'm sympathetic, but I don't fully see how the pieces fit, and I want the interview to actually test the view rather than let her give a TED talk.

Draft me eight to ten questions for the interview, plus a follow-up for each that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question on the difference between nociception and felt pain — and whether that distinction survives once you've already accepted degrees of sentience — at least one on where insects and other invertebrates land on her two dimensions, and at least one on the thermostat case: what exactly is supposed to be missing there, and can her view explain why a thermostat isn't a tiny moral subject without just stipulating that it isn't? I'd also like a question that presses the aggregation problem: if moral status is a vector and not a scalar, how are we supposed to compare a bee's short valenced life with a dog's richer one, or with a human's, without smuggling in a single common currency? And one about whether her view makes moral status too cheap to lose or too easy to gain — if a system can have a flicker of valence, does it get a flicker of rights? Please don't ask her to recommend books or cite who said what; I want questions that stand on their own. And no softballs like what got you interested in animal minds — press the actual machinery.
