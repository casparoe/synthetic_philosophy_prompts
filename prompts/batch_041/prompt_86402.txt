I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of economics, about sixteen students, and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel, rather than be told, what it means to say that utility is a representation of choice rather than a quantity anyone has. Build it in two stages. In the first, participants make a sequence of choices over bundles under a payoff scheme that uses no real money (points toward a small prize), and the choice sets must be engineered so that a substantial fraction of participants produce a preference cycle; say how you engineered that. I want them to see their own data fail to admit a utility function before anyone explains what that means, so no lecture up front and no names until the debrief. Include a role for a broker who may trade with anyone and profits by walking a cycler around the loop, with every individual swap looking like an improvement to the person who accepts it. In the second stage, hand each participant a different set of numbers that represents their choices and have the group try to design an experiment that could show whose numbers are correct; they should discover that any monotone relabeling is undetectable. Then let them choose between lotteries, and let them discover that a monotone relabeling which is not affine now makes a detectable difference. Give me rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the underlying distinctions: revealed preference as a definition of preference, the difference between failing a consistency requirement and merely being unusual, ordinal utility as fixed only up to monotone transformation, expected utility as fixed only up to affine transformation, and the money pump as the price of intransitivity. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, including participants who suspect the point and start choosing consistently on purpose, participants who report that their choices were arbitrary, and Allais-style violations in the lottery stage, with a way to handle each. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot show: that satisfying a preference is good for the person whose preference it is, and that expected-utility numbers license comparisons between people or any talk of units of happiness.
