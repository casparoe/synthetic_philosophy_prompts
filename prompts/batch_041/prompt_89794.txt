I retired about three years ago after 34 years in devlopmental biology — chick limbs mostly, then a long stretch on how embryos keep time — and I've been spending the retirement reading philosophy, which I'm bad at but enjoying. I've gotten stuck on something and I'd like your help with it.

Here's the bit I mean. As I understand it, Kripke's claim is that the particular sperm and the particular egg I came from are part of my essence: a being that grew from a different pair would not have been me, however much it looked and talked like me. Not just that my parents are essential to me, but the very cells. And the odd thing is I think I believe it, even though my whole career was spent showing how much you can change about an animal by poking an embryo at the right hour.

I don't need the implications for naming, reference and necessity — I've read enough to get the shape of that and I can't follow the details anyway. What I want is the most important implications for things outside the seminar room, and I'll tell you the three places it's actually biting me.

First, regret. I keep doing the 'if I'd taken the Basel job' thing, and I'd like to know whether the view makes that incoherent, or just makes it sad in a different way. Is the guy who took the Basel job someone I could have been, or is he a person who was never going to exist, so that I've been mourning a stranger?

Second, reproduction. My nephew and his wife just did IVF; they had five embryos and picked one to transfer, and the whole family talks about it as if they were choosing features for one child who was coming either way. Does the view say they chose which person exists? And if it does, were the other four ever anyone? I have a feeling this connects to something in population ethics but I don't know what it's called.

Third, and this is the one that nags: I'm an identical twin. My brother and I came from the same sperm and the same egg, as far as anyone can tell. If the origin is what makes me me, then either that's a counterexample to the whole thing, or the view is saying less than I think it's saying. Which is it? And while you're at it, tell me what a developmental biologist's facts ought to be doing to this view: twinning, obviously, but also cloning (no sperm, no egg, and a sheep who was famously not the same sheep as her mother), tetragametic chimeras, embryos that split late. Do these embarass the view or just make it fiddly?

Be blunt about where 'same origin' is doing real explanatory work and where it's just a fancy label for an intuition. Flag anything I've got wrong about the view itself — I'm new at this and I'd rather be corrected than humored. And if you had to rank the implications, tell me which one you think is the big one. No reading list please, I can't keep up with the literature anyway.
