I am a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy late, and I would like you to build a case to order — one that puts the spandrel critique of adaptationism under strain rather than simply illustrating it. What I am after is a single trait in a single organism about which an adaptationist and a critic of the adaptationist program agree on every observation and still disagree about what the trait is.

Specifications:

1. One trait, one organism, set out in a short paragraph. Keep the anatomy plain enough that a reader with no zoology can follow it. Invent the organism if that serves the case; if you use a real one, choose something whose basic biology is common knowledge.
2. No stock examples. The human chin, male nipples, the spaces between the arches of an actual building, and the panda's thumb are all out, as is anything else that turns up routinely in textbooks.
3. The two parties must accept the same evidence, and it must be decisive for neither. The disagreement has to be about inference rather than about facts, and you may not arrange matters so that one side wins by having more data or by having the relevant evidence conveniently missing.
4. State the observations in neutral terms: what the trait is, what it costs, what it does now, how it varies, what is known about how it develops, and what is known — or not known — about how it arose.
5. The case must turn on a single variable. Name that variable, and say what happens when it is removed: which verdict survives, which collapses, and why.
6. Keep separate the question of where the trait came from and the question of why it is still there. A claim about origin may not do the work of a claim about maintenance, or the reverse.
7. The critic may not win by invoking drift as a residual category. The critic's alternative must be as concrete as the adaptationist's.
8. You may stipulate a history only if you also say what observation would, in principle, settle it. No untestable ancestral narrative.
9. Finish the construction by naming the observation, obtainable by ordinary means, that would actually discriminate between the two verdicts.

Then, in order: (a) give the adaptationist's verdict and the strongest reasoning that reaches it; (b) give the critic's verdict and the strongest reasoning that reaches it; (c) identify the feature of the case that is doing the work, and show that it is a feature of the case rather than an assumption smuggled in at the edges; (d) say how a defender of the framework that comes out worse would try to defuse the case, and whether that reply succeeds. If the reply consists in locating the adaptation somewhere else — at a different level, in a different trait, at a different time — say plainly whether that move is a genuine defense or a rescue that makes the framework unfalsifiable.

Since the purpose of the exercise fixes what counts as cheating, I will say what the case is for. I want it to show that the adaptationist inference is underdetermined by the evidence, not that adaptationists are careless. A case that merely stipulates a byproduct origin, without showing why the very same observations are equally compatible with a history of selection for the trait's present use, fails the brief. Both verdicts must be live options on identical evidence.

I do not want a literature review, citations, or a reading list. I want the case itself, argued out. Please write everything in American English.
