I sit on the complaints panel of a small voluntary register for counsellors and psychotherapists, and I've got to draft our written descision before Thursday's meeting. I'd rather you drafted it, because I keep going round in circles on it.

The case. A member with fifteen years' standing and no previous complaints, a practising Buddhist, was seeing a woman in her fifties whose mother had died suddenly — aneurysm, no warning, they'd spoken the night before. A few sessions in, the client says, she asked him whether he thought there was any meaning in it. He told her that in his understanding the death was the ripening of karma from a previous life, and that the grief she was carrying was itself karmic. She says she heard "you did this to her". He says he never used the word blame, that she asked, that she knew what he was, and that refusing to answer would have been its own kind of dishonesty. She stopped coming after that session and complained. He isn't denying the conversation, only her reading of it.

What I need: the decision itself — uphold or dismiss, the finding, the reasoning, any sanction — and then the part I actually care about, a short guidance section for members on karma-talk in the consulting room, because we'll get more of these and I don't want us improvising case by case.

Three things I want the reasoning to do rather than gesture at. First, I don't want it to turn on whether she consented to the answer. The real question is what kind of claim "your mother's death was the ripening of her karma" even is. If karma is an impersonal law — action and consequence working themselves out with nobody keeping score — then that's a claim about causation, like saying the tree came down in the wind, and the fault is tactlessness, not falsehood. If it's a claim about what she deserved, it's an accusation. I want the decision to say which of the two it's treating it as, and why, and to show its working, because I suspect that in practice the two can't be seperated and I want that argument rather than the assertion. Second, please test it by substitution: if he'd said "God took her home", would we be sitting here? If not, what's the principle that distinguishes them — and if the answer is that karma-talk sounds more like a verdict, say so, and say why that isn't just us finding one religion's metaphysics uglier than another's. Third, the thing that nags me: in the Buddhist telling there isn't a persisting self to be the bearer of the debt in the first place, so I'm not sure "deserved" is even available — the karma that ripens isn't owed to anyone. I may be mangling that, and if I am, the decision should say where. But if I'm not, it matters, because then he was telling her something his own tradition doesn't quite license, which is a different finding from "he told her the truth unkindly". And it has to sort the doctrinal version from the popular "what goes around comes around" version, since I think it's the popular one doing the damage.

Give the losing side its best shot too. I can see the argument that we're a secular body policing the vocabulary of a tradition we don't understand, and that the client's distress isn't evidence the claim was false. And make the guidance usable by a volunteer panel — a rule we can actually apply to the next one, worked through on two harder variants: the client who is herself Buddhist and wants to hear it (is that different, and why), and the client who volunteers "I think I'm being punished for something" (can he agree? can he disagree?).

One page, numbered findings, then the guidance. We're not a court, so it shouldn't read like a legal ruling, but it shouldn't read like an email either.
