I am writing to ask a favour of the kind you have granted me before, and to apologise in advance for the physics. I have been invited to contribute a short essay to a collection on the idea of information, and I have built the piece around Maxwell's demon — a figure I have loved since I first met him and understood, generously, about a third of. I would be grateful for two kinds of comment. First, on the physics: I am confident I have got at least one thing wrong and I suspect I have got several things wrong, and I would rather hear it from you now than from a reviewer later. Second, on whether the conceit does any real work, or whether it is a handsome costume for a familiar complaint about writing. I would also value your view of the paragraph on looking, which is the passage I am least sure of and most attached to. The draft follows. Please be blunt; I have until Friday, and my vanity can survive the weekend.

The Demon at My Desk

For about a year now I have been telling people that a novelist is a Maxwell's demon. The demon stands at a small door in a box divided in two. Molecules drift toward the door; he admits the fast ones and bars the slow ones, and in time one half of the box is hot and the other cold. Heat has moved from cold to hot; disorder has become order; the second law of thermodynamics, which forbids precisely this in a closed system, has been broken by nothing more than a doorman's judgement about whom to admit.

The novelist, I want to say, does the same. Experience arrives as a blizzard of detail, all of it at a single temperature. The writer stands at the door and lets through the vivid, the telling, the fast; she holds back the dull and the merely true. What comes out the other side is a paragraph — a small region of order, a hot side and a cold side, carved out of the noise. I have never found a better description of what revision feels like.

The demon, of course, is a scandal, and I want to show that the scandal is mine as well. He appears to give you something for nothing, and physics has spent a century and a half explaining why he cannot. The resolution, as I understand it, is that the demon must forget. To keep sorting, he must clear his memory of the speeds he has learned, and forgetting is not free: erasing a single bit costs at least kT ln 2 of energy, and when all the erasures are tallied they cancel, to the last joule, the order he produced. The engine he seemed to run for nothing turns out to run on the labour of forgetting. Szilard, I believe, is the one who finally put the demon to rest, by showing that the demon's knowledge was itself a kind of fuel — and that the fuel is spent the moment the slate is wiped.

Someone more careful than I am once told me that the demon's knowledge is best described as a correlation: his memory becomes correlated with the gas, and the entropy that seems to vanish is hiding in that correlation. I confess I do not see how this helps. A correlation is a fact about two things, not a third thing; it is bookkeeping, not matter. If the entropy is only hiding in the books, then the demon has done nothing worse than hire a clever accountant.

But it is not the ledger that grips me; it is the looking. The demon must observe each molecule before he can judge it, and observation, I am told, is never free. To know a molecule's speed is to disturb it; the demon pays for his knowledge in the very coin the box is made of. Which is why I have come to believe the demon is not merely improbable but impossible — you could no more build one than you could watch a thing without touching it. And this is where the figure stops being a metaphor for me, because I have always felt that to notice a thing is to alter it: the moment I decide a detail is worth keeping, I have begun to falsify it.

And then the deeper heresy, which I set down plainly for you to strike through: I suspect the second law is not, at bottom, a law about heat at all, but a law about information — about what can be known and what must be forgotten. Entropy is disorder, and disorder is only information we have not yet put in order. If that is right, then the demon is not a paradox but a portrait: he is every mind that sorts, and his price is ours, paid in the heat of forgetting.

So: where have I gone wrong on the physics, and which of my errors are the kind a reader with a physics degree will notice and resent? Is there a version of this essay in which the demon is doing genuine work — in which the figure and the argument depend on each other — rather than standing in for a mood? And what do you make of the paragraph on looking; can it be saved, or should I cut it and end on the ledger?
