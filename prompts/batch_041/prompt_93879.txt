I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've somehow agreed to run a ninety-minute session for a friend's little interdisciplinary salon — about fifteen people, mostly writers plus a couple of psych grad students, no pre-reading, whiteboard, wine afterwards. The topic they handed me is cognitive penetration of perception, which I only care about because my last book was a marriage told twice, once from each spouse, and the thing I kept circling while writing it was whether those two were literally seeing different things or just drawing different conclusions from the same stuff on the retina. So I want the room to actually arrive at the precise claim — that a belief, a desire, or an expectation can change what you experientially see, not merely how you judge what you see — and I've got ninety minutes to get them there. Plan it for me. Not the explanation itself; the plan. What has to be in place before that claim is even statable, and in what order, so that by the time we reach the controversial part the room feels the pull of it instead of shrugging? I assume some see-versus-judge distinction has to come first but you tell me, and I want to know what depends on what, not just a list of topics.

And what should carry the whole thing? I have candidates and I suspect at least two of them are traps. There's the Müller-Lyer illusion — you know the lines are equal, they still look unequal — which I like because it's cheap and everyone's seen it, but I wonder if it's really about a belief doing any work or just about the eye being dumb. Then there's my own case: a husband convinced his wife is drifting away, who sees a perfectly neutral face as cold. That one's mine and I'm fond of it, but tell me honestly if it's a trap, because I can feel it sliding into 'well, he's just interpreting'. And then there's the old forties stuff where kids from poorer families supposedly drew coins bigger than the rich kids did — I half-remember it and would love to use it as the historical hook, unless it's actually a cautionary tale about how these claims get overhyped and then don't replicate. So: pick the one that should carry the session, say what it must have and what it must not have, and tell me which of the others to cut, and why. Then the misconceptions — I'm betting the big one is the slide to 'so we all live in different worlds and there's no fact of the matter', runner-up 'isn't this just attention?', and maybe a third where they collapse the whole thing back into judgement — but I need to know which ones they'll walk in with versus which my own sequence will manufacture, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, because I don't want to spend my rebuttal budget in the wrong place.

I gather the skeptical side is the modularity crowd — Pylyshyn, Fodor — and the people who think the phenomenon is both real and epistemically alarming are over on the Siegel side of things, but please don't take my word for any of that. What I want back is exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrap-up, so I can tape it to the inside of my notebook: (1) the order and the dependency structure; (2) the carrying example and its requirements; (3) which of my candidates to cut and why; (4) the misconception map with the point in the sequence where each gets headed off; (5) one question to put to the room near the end that would show me the precise claim actually landed rather than just that they enjoyed the wine — plus what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, so I don't get dragged into the neuroimaging or the modularity fight or whether it still counts when it's years of expertise rather than a passing mood, since I gather the purists don't let the expertise cases in at all and I'd rather say that out loud than mumble it. And in that last item, flag which parts of all this are genuinely settled and which are live fights among the people who work on it — I have a horror of standing up there teaching one camp's picture as if it were the textbook, so wherever you'd be doing that, just say so.
