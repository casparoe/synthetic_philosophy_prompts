I'm a grad student in the history of econ who also TAs the intermediate micro sequence, and I keep having the same fight with my roommate, who has read exactly one self-help book and now believes that what you do reveals what you actually want, full stop, and that anything you say about your own feelings is cope. The annoying thing is that she's basically endorsing a very old and very serious position -- that preference is defined by choice, that we learn what someone wants from what they pick when they could have picked otherwise, and that talk about utility is either a tidy summary of those choices or a metaphysical cheat -- and she just isn't bothered by any of the problems with it. I want to see the position taken seriously and made funny at the same time.

So: you're a philosopher of economics with a good ear for deadpan comedy. Write me a satirical agony-aunt advice column, roughly 600-700 words, in the voice of an advice columnist who is a strict revealed-preference theorist and cannot be argued out of it. Give the column and the columnist names, format it as a real advice page -- two or three short letters from readers, each with the columnist's reply, maybe a closing postscript -- and keep it completely straight-faced. No winking, no author's note, no moral tacked on at the end; the reader should figure out what's being sent up from the letters themselves. The jokes have to come from the actual content, so that someone who has taught this stuff laughs instead of wincing, but a smart non-economist should still follow it. Before you write, spend a moment brainstorming which technical points you want to land and which letter carries each one.

Things I'd like the columnist to do along the way, though please don't present any of this as a checklist inside the piece, just let it happen: never accept a stated feeling as evidence of anything, and keep asking what the alternatives were, since a choice only says something if other options were live (the reader who drinks the cheap wine every night because it's what he can afford has revealed nothing about what he likes); respond to a reader whose choices cycle -- she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A -- by concluding that the reader isn't a coherent agent yet rather than by loosening the method; at some point cheerfully note that any sufficiently regular pattern of choosing can be redressed as the maximization of some quantity or other, and treat that as a selling point rather than an embarrassment; and let the method cost one of the readers something real, so that by the end the reader of the column feels the actual question of whether utility names anything beyond the choices it's inferred from. Don't name any real economists, don't cite anything, and don't lean on jargon that only survives if you've read a specific paper -- I want this to stand up on its own. Also, one of the letters could be a woman whose husband says he loves her, and one could be someone who stayed in a job he claims to hate; take those wherever you like.
