I'm a TA for a second-year course that mixes biology and philosophy students, and next week I have seventy-five minutes and a whiteboard to teach the section on the two accounts of biological function — the etiological one, which ties a trait's function to the selection history that explains why the trait is there, and the causal-role one, which ties it to the contribution the trait makes to some capacity of the system it sits inside. I don't want you to explain the distinction to me; I want a plan for how I explain it. Which idea has to be in place before which other one, and why the dependencies run that way rather than the reverse — my own instinct is to open with the apparent normativity of function-talk, the sense that a heart that cannot pump is defective in a way that a heart that cannot make a thumping noise is not, but I cannot tell whether that should come first or whether it should be held back as the payoff that makes the two accounts matter at all. I need the one example that should carry the whole session and a statement of what it must have and what it must not have; the misconceptions the students will arrive with and the further ones they will form along the way, in the order those will surface, with the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off; a single closing question that would tell me whether they have got the distinction or merely memorized two labels; and an explicit list of what I should refuse to explain at this level and announce that I am refusing.

One complication: half the section are biology students who will think the whole thing is verbal and that "function" obviously means what the trait was selected for, and half are philosophers who will not know what drift is and will therefore treat any appeal to history as hand-waving; the plan has to keep both groups in the room. I also want it to do more than tour two labels. Two things must be in it, and I would like you to place them in the sequence rather than merely mention them. The first is the difference between a trait's being favoured by selection and selection's favouring the trait because of the effect in question — I take it the etiological account collapses without that distinction, and I suspect it is precisely where the biology students' confidence breaks. The second is that the causal-role account is only ever as good as the system and capacity you relativize it to, so that the same organ can have the function of pumping blood or of making a noise depending on how the containing system is carved; the students need to feel that this is a genuine problem rather than a cheap win for the other side. The usual carrying examples, the heart pumping and the vestigial organ, are the cases where everyone already agrees, so they cannot do the work. I have been toying with an invented case and want your verdict on it: a highland snail carries a ridge on its shell that arose under selection for resisting a crab that crushes shells, and in a cave population where the crab has been absent for forty generations the ridge has become the anchor for a mucus strand the snail uses to snag flies. If that case can carry the session, say what each account should be made to say about the cave snails and the highland snails and where the accounts come apart; if it cannot, say what it is missing and what I should use instead. I would also like the plan to say where the middle positions belong — a trait with no selection history whatsoever that is nevertheless disposed to be favoured, and a trait that persists only because of drift — and to tell me where the teleosemantics payoff and the technical formulations of the hybrid views fall, so that I know what to cut.

Write this as continuous prose in the manner of a long email to a colleague: no headings, no numbered steps, no bullets, no bold, no tables, since the sequence has to come through in the writing itself. And because a teaching plan is a prediction about other people, finish by telling me which part of it you have least confidence in and what would change your mind — what you would need to see in the room, or to learn about how the two accounts actually come apart, before you would reorder the sequence, replace the carrying example, or move one of the misconceptions earlier.
