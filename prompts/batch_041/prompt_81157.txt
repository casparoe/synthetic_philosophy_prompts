I'm building a session for a memory-culture seminar around Grave of the Fireflies — the 1988 Ghibli one, Takahata's — and I want it read through the philosophy of collective memory and the ethics of remembrance. So take the role of someone who works on transitional justice, think it through before you commit to a line, and then write me the reading.

Here's what I'm after. Setsuko and Seita are victims of a war their own state started, and the film asks us to grieve for them without ever asking us to reckon with what Japan was doing in Asia in those same months. I want you to seperate two things a memory culture can do with its own dead: mourning that eventually turns outwards, so the dead are honoured partly by owning what was done in their name, and mourning that quietly does the opposite job and substitutes for it. Call them completed and substituting grief, just for this piece. Which one does that ending — the two of them healthy again on the hilltop bench above present-day Kobe — pull us towards? And is the beauty of the thing, the animation and Mamiya's score, doing memory work, or is it doing consolation? One detail I want you to use: Nosaka wrote the story as an apology to his little sister, who died of malnutrition, and admitted that in real life he ate when she didn't and that he left that out of the book. The film is built on an absence. Does that absence constrain what it can say politically?

Then, briefly, the comparison the seminar needs — Germany, Turkey, the American South. Not a survey, just enough to say what's distinctive about the Japanese case, and whether any of the others has its own beloved, tear-jerking, victim-centred work doing the same job. Takahata himself refused to call it an anti-war film, so tell me whether you think he was right, and where the film resists my reading. Point me to the two or three scenes to put at the centre of the discussion. Around 1,200 words, British English please, and no references needed.
