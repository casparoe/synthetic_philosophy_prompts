I work on causal inference with observational data — potential outcomes, directed acyclic graphs, identification, bad controls, instrumental variables, natural experiments, the distinction between a mechanism and an effect. That is my native vocabulary and I am fluent in it. I read Weber's essay on the Protestant ethic once, in translation, years ago; I remember the shape of the argument and little of the detail, so treat what follows as my statement of the position rather than as a rendering of it.

The position, as I understand it, comes in three versions that are usually run together. The strong version says that ascetic Protestantism, and above all the Calvinist doctrine of predestination, caused the emergence of modern rational capitalism in the West. The weak version says it was one contributing condition among several. The affinity version says there was no causal relation at all, only an elective affinity between a religious ethic and the conduct an emerging commercial order required — a fit rather than a push. The mechanism in the middle is a chain: predestination leaves the believer unable to know whether he is among the elect; pastoral advice converts that anxiety into a duty to prove election through methodical worldly labour; the result is an inner-worldly asceticism that forbids spending the gains and so forces accumulation; and the whole thing is unintended, with the ethic producing the spirit of capitalism as a by-product and then outliving its own religious foundation, leaving a rationalised economic order that no longer needs it. Weber's own claim to novelty is that the acquisitive drive is ancient and what is new is its ethical sanction as a calling. He also gives a counterfactual account of historical causation, on which a condition counts as a cause if, had it been absent, the outcome would not have been objectively possible; and he insists that he is tracing one strand among others in a singular historical configuration, and that his ideal types are constructs rather than measured attributes.

Restate all of this in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: the unit, the treatment, the outcome, the estimand, the graph, the identification threats, which covariates a modern analyst would refuse to condition on and why, what could serve as an instrument and whether the exclusion restriction could ever be defended, what a natural experiment here would look like, and how the three versions map onto three different estimands — or onto none.

Then the residue report. Go claim by claim and say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. I am particularly interested in whether a treatment indicator can capture a belief whose content, rather than its label, is supposed to do the causal work; whether elective affinity is a causal claim, a selection claim, or neither; whether an ideal type can be a variable; what happens to a claim about a singular historical outcome when it is asked to be a claim about a population of units; whether unintended consequences and the closing diagnosis of a secularised order survive at all; whether Weber's own counterfactual theory of causation means the translation is more faithful than I expected and the residue lies somewhere else entirely; and what interpretive adequacy at the level of meaning demands that no design-based framework can supply.

Finally, the verdict. For each item of residue, say whether it is a defect of my vocabulary — something it cannot express about meaning, reasons or singular cases — or a defect of the position, a claim that is not a causal claim at all or is too under-specified to be one. Then tell me whether the translation changes the verdict on the general question, whether culture can be a cause, or whether it shows the question was never well posed.

I do not want citations, a reading list, or a survey of the literature; where an argument needs empirical support, describe the design that would supply it rather than naming a study. Write in British English.
