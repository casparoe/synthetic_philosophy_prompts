Write a dialogue between two longtermists at a workshop on existential risk. Both treat the simulation argument as a serious possibility rather than a sci-fi trope: either civilizations tend to go extinct before becoming posthuman, or posthuman civilizations tend not to run ancestor simulations, or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. The first argues that this possibility should dampen fanaticism about the far future. Because our world may be simulated, our actions may have little leverage over the real cosmos, and the simulators' values may be indifferent to ours, so far-future interventions deserve a heavy discount; call this the simulation tax. The second argues that the simulation argument does not dampen fanaticism and may even intensify it. Uncertainty about whether we are simulated expands the reference class of possible beneficiaries, including simulated minds, and an impartial expected-value calculus should not discount a world merely because it is nested. Let them press each other on whether the simulation argument is better modeled as a discount rate on influence or as a change in the population of moral patients, and require each to introduce at least one further distinction of their own to sharpen the disagreement. Neither may dismiss the other's premises, and each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them.
