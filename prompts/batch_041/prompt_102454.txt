I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in social ontology, and I would like a decision tree that maps the positions one can hold on whether generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and their successors — are real kinds. The audience is students who already accept the ordinary empirical claims and need the metaphysical options laid out for them, so please build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions that split the space, then the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual positions located in that space, with unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Every reader is presumed to share one starting commitment: that birth cohorts differ, in measurable and durable ways, in attitudes and practices — in how they use media, what they expect of institutions, how they think about work. The tree should take that commitment as its root and ask what follows about the metaphysics of cohorts.

Each internal node must be a question answerable without knowing which position it leads to, and phrased over a concrete case wherever possible. I would like the following distinctions to be load-bearing, because they are what the seminar is actually about:

- Whether a cohort kind is causal or merely descriptive: does being born in a given window constrain or explain an individual's attitudes, or does it only correlate with them?
- Whether cohort membership is intrinsic to the member or relational and positional — a fact about a group's relation to other cohorts and to events, not a property anyone carries.
- Whether the kind requires a common cause, such as a shared formative experience that imprints its members, or tolerates a motley of unrelated causes that happen to coincide in time.
- Whether the kind requires internal cohesion or self-consciousness — members who recognize themselves as a generation — or whether it can be real without anyone being aware of it.
- Whether the kind is mind-dependent in the looping sense: the label is applied, people take it up, and the kind becomes more real as a result. Distinguish this from the weaker claim that the label merely tracks something already there.
- The reference-class problem: by what right does a roughly twenty-year birth window pick out a kind, when a five-year window, or a single birth year, or sharing a birthday, does not? Say what principled answer a defender of each leaf can give, and what the leaf must say about where the boundaries fall and why they are not arbitrary.

Please make sure the tree can reach at least the following leaves, though you may add others if the logical space demands them: a robust realism on which a generation is a genuine social kind individuated by a shared formative experience; a deflationary or statistical realism on which cohort differences are real but the kind is nothing over and above a distribution; a nominalism on which generation terms are convenient labels with no kind behind them; a relational or positional realism; a social-constructionist view in which the kind is sustained by looping effects; an eliminativism that would drop cohort talk from serious explanation; and the ideological critique on which generation-talk is better understood as ageism or marketing than as ontology.

Two further requirements. First, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to separate them — do not paper over the gap. Second, for each leaf give one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, the thing its defenders would least like to be asked.

Keep the instrument to no more than ten leaves. There is no need for references, reading lists, or attributions; I want the positions described in plain terms, and any view you name should be recognizable from the description alone.
