The debate over illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view associated with Keith Frankish and anticipated by Daniel Dennett, that phenomenal properties as ordinarily conceived do not exist and that our introspective convictions are a systematic misrepresentation — has run for years against realist critics, David Chalmers among them, without either side moving. The illusionist treats the realist as protecting a datum that only seems undeniable from the inside; the realist treats the illusionist as having denied the one fact any theory of mind must save, and as incoherent besides, since a misrepresentation of experience is itself something that seems a certain way to the subject. Each side believes it has answered the other and that the other is begging the question. My hypothesis is that this is not a single disagreement but several stacked: a verbal dispute about whether 'consciousness' should name the phenomenal kind or a deflated functional one; a substantive dispute about which facts a theory may treat as brute rather than as targets of explanation; and a disagreement about which evidence — first-person conviction or third-person explanatory success — is the tribunal. Is that the right diagnosis, and if so, which strand is actually keeping the impasse alive? Diagnose the disagreement, say what kind of disagreement it is, and tell me what — a distinction, a reformulation of the question, an empirical result, or nothing — would count as progress. Take a definite position rather than laying out the options, and give your reasons.
