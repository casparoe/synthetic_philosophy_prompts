I'm a journalist who covers economics, decision theory, and the migration of formal ideas into public argument. In a few weeks I'm running a ninety-minute session at a residential workshop for about twenty-four mid-career journalists. They are numerate and argumentative but have no formal game theory, and they will resist anything that feels like a lecture. I want an activity, not a talk, that makes them feel the force of iterated elimination of dominated strategies when dominance is allowed to be by a mixed strategy rather than by a single pure strategy. The thing I want them to run into is this: a strategy can look entirely reasonable when you compare it with any one alternative and still be beaten by a weighted combination of the others. I have been calling that a ghost strategy — an option that no single rival option beats, but that a lottery over two rival options beats. Once a ghost strategy is removed, a different strategy that had survived can become dominated, so the elimination has to be run again. I want the group to do the eliminating, and I want the moment when someone realizes that the winning objection to a strategy is not another strategy but a lottery over strategies to be the hinge of the session.

Please design the game and the debrief. I need rules, materials, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, roles if roles help, a timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal ideas. The game should be playable by twenty-four people in a room with no special equipment, and it should produce a shared record of choices and payoffs that we can look at together. Ideally the group discovers the ghost strategy themselves, or at least feels the pressure that forces them toward it, rather than being handed a matrix and told which cell is the answer. I also want them to feel the difference between a strategy that is beaten everywhere by one other strategy and a strategy that is beaten everywhere only by a weighted combination of the others, and to see why the order of elimination is innocuous for strict dominance but not for weak dominance. If the activity can stage a weak-dominance case as well, so that the group sees order mattering in one version and not in the other, that would be ideal. A three-by-three game is probably enough; one well-chosen matrix, played in a way that makes the group commit to choices, is better than a lot of noise.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: where the group will get confused, where the game will collapse into guessing my intentions, where the payoff arithmetic will swamp the conceptual point, and how I should handle each failure. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, which part of the formal result the facilitator has to state outright because no finite classroom game can make the group feel it. And give me the debrief as a sequence of questions or moves, not a lecture, so that the formal vocabulary arrives only after the experience has landed.

I would like your answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write an email to a colleague who is going to run the session.
