I'm enrolled in a seminar on convention, coordination, and social norms, and one thread has run all term without anyone shifting position: whether the everyday conventions we live among — how much to tip, how to queue, whether to cross against the light — impose genuine moral obligations, and whether a visitor who does not share the convention is bound by it regardless.

Two positions have hardened. The first treats these obligations as real but practice-dependent: what is owed (a particular tip, a place at the back of the line, waiting for the signal) is fixed by the local convention, and the obligation attaches because the convention is in force here and the agent has joined the practice it governs. On this view a visitor is bound precisely because stepping into the restaurant or the queue is entering the practice, and it is the convention's content, not some convention-independent moral reason, that tells them what to do.

The second position treats the only real obligations in these cases as the convention-independent moral reasons underneath: do not free-ride on a norm that benefits you, do not treat people with contempt, do not expose others to needless risk, do not help sustain an unjust arrangement. On this view the convention itself adds no obligation; where it tracks the underlying reasons it is a useful shortcut, and where it diverges — a well-paid server in a tipping culture, an empty street at three in the morning, a visitor from a city where no one crosses against the light now standing at a red light in a city where everyone does — it has no force, and the visitor is bound only by the reasons that actually apply.

We have stalled. The first camp says the second is not defending the conventions at all but dissolving them into something else, and cannot explain why the obligation is to tip this much rather than some other amount. The second camp says the first is fetishizing an arbitrary practice, and that any residue it points to is either a convention-independent reason in disguise or nothing at all.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute, with both sides agreeing on every action-guiding consideration and differing only over whether to call the residue an obligation? Is it substantive, turning on whether there are genuinely practice-dependent reasons that are not reducible to the underlying moral ones? Is it a disagreement about which cases are central — whether the well-paid server is the paradigm that exposes the convention's emptiness or a marginal case a proper account should explain away? Or does it bottom out in intuitions about arbitrariness and respect that neither side can argue for? My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading: one about what a participant in a practice is committed to, the other about what a person owes another person irrespective of any practice. If that is right, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a decisive case, a way of keeping the two questions apart — and if it is wrong, say what the real structure of the impasse is and why no resolution should be expected.
