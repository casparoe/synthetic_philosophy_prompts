I am a retired systems neuroscientist — thirty years of laboratory and clinical work, the last several spent reading philosophy with more appetite than method — and I am applying for a one-year residential fellowship at an interdisciplinary institute. I need a two-page research proposal, and I would like you to build one out of the following half-formed idea.

The idea. The extended mind literature has argued for a long time over whether a notebook, a phone, or some other external resource can be a proper part of a person's cognitive system, and over the standard objections that the proposed criteria are too permissive or that they mistake coupling for constitution. What strikes me is how much of that debate quietly presumes a particular kind of subject: one who owns the resource, controls it, and automatically endorses what it delivers. The technologies I spent my career around violate each of those presumptions. A deep brain stimulator is internal in the anatomical sense and yet its parameters may be set by a clinician, retuned remotely, or adjusted by a closed-loop algorithm the patient cannot inspect. My patients described the stimulation in shifting terms — sometimes as themselves, sometimes as a force acting on them. A cochlear implant, a wearable prosthesis, a predictive-text interface owned by an employer: each is functionally integrated with a person while remaining outside that person's control or ownership, and each seems to sit on a different side of the line the extended mind tradition wants to draw.

The question I want the proposal to pose, precisely: when a cognitive resource is functionally integrated with a person but not under that person's control, should the boundary of the cognitive system be drawn by functional integration, by the agent's control over the resource, or by the agent's felt sense of ownership — and can the clinical cases adjudicate among these criteria, or do they merely illustrate conclusions reached on other grounds? Make clear why the existing positions leave this open, and why it is not simply the old coupling-versus-constitution dispute in new clothes.

The method is conceptual, not empirical, and the proposal should say so plainly: assemble a graded series of cases from the ordinary to the clinical, derive what each criterion predicts for each, isolate the cases on which the criteria diverge, and argue about which criterion earns its keep once the counterexamples are pressed. Set out what I would actually do month by month, what a finished result would look like, and what would count as the project failing — for instance, if the three criteria turn out to converge across every case that matters, if the clinical material adds nothing to the familiar examples, or if the question dissolves into a verbal dispute about how to use the word mind. Be honest about the scope: tell me where the project would become too large for a year, and which tempting extensions (group cognition, personal identity, moral responsibility) should be fenced off.

I will supply the citations myself; do not construct a bibliography or reading list.
