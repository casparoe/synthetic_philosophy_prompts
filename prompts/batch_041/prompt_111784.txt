I run a 90-minute session for an upper-level seminar (decision theory + ethics, 14 students) and I want a game that makes them feel expected-value fanaticism from the inside — the thing where EV maximisation requires you to take a 1-in-10^30 bet on a colossal payoff, and every attempt to say "no, thats too small a chance" either comes out arbitrary or comes out as "well then I'm just not an EV maximiser."

The sequence I want them to run into, without me announcing it: (1) everyone happily takes the first bet on the menu; (2) the menu is a staircase, every rung strictly better in EV than the one below it, and they can see that as they climb; (3) at the top they all refuse, and when I ask why, no one can give a reason that doesnt also condemn rung 1; (4) and this is the part I actually care about — they then have to write their stopping rule down as a policy covering every possible gamble, and a "demon" player reads the policy and constructs a bet the policy rejects that is obviously better, by the students own lights, than a bet the policy accepts. I want the demon's move to be a real dilemma and not a gotcha: a policy with a probability floor gets beaten by a scaled-up payoff, and a policy with no floor means you're the fanatic and have to own the bet you swore you'd never take. The demon should be running a mechanical procedure written out on a card, so any student could run it against any policy — I dont want it winning by being clever or by talking fast.

Also, some of them will say "I'm not maximising EV, I'm maximising expected utility of a bounded utility function." Thats a legitimate exit and I want the debrief to treat it as one and say what it costs, not paper over it.
