I am a journalist who writes long-form essays on science and ideas, and for the past two years I have been reporting on how a mid-sized European city commemorates a wartime atrocity that took place there eighty years ago: the annual public reading of the victims' names, the museum on the site, the unit in the school curriculum, the plaques at the rail siding. In an essay I am publishing next month, I argue that this apparatus, and the many others like it, has quietly converted a living act of attention into a permanent institutional obligation, and that the obligation now does less good than we assume. What I propose is a policy of graduated forgetting: memorial commitments should be subject to periodic public review, and any commitment not deliberately renewed should be permitted to lapse — a sunset clause for memory. I lean on Margalit's own distinction between the ethical community, bound by thick relations to its particular dead, and the moral community, which owes no such particular debt to anyone; if the duty to remember is an ethical rather than a moral duty, I argue, it is owed by a community that can also release itself. I lean as well on Rieff's suspicion that entrenched memory can keep a grievance alive rather than lay it to rest. And I introduce a second idea of my own, which I call mnemonic triage: a community has finite collective attention, and a serious ethics of memory has to decide what to let go, not only what to keep.

I am presenting this argument next week at a two-day symposium on memory and reconciliation, and I need to be ready for the question period. The room will be: public historians and museum curators who believe their job is to preserve evidence rather than to adjudicate feeling; two academic philosophers who work on collective memory and who know Margalit's and Rieff's arguments far better than I do; a social psychologist who studies how commemoration shapes intergroup attitudes; and a delegation from a survivors' association, who will hear my proposal as a proposal about them.
