I am a postdoc working at the intersection of vision science and philosophy of mind, and in ten days I will present at a joint meeting of a psychophysics lab and a philosophy of perception reading group. My thesis is deflationary: the standard evidence offered for cognitive penetration—memory colour, fear and distance, linguistic colour categories, and the seeing-is-believing paradigms—does not establish that cognition alters the phenomenal character of perceptual experience. At most it establishes shifts in decision criteria, response bias, or the gain of a sensory channel. I allow one narrow exception to the deflationary claim, which I call gain penetration: a cognitive state may change the precision-weighted gain on a sensory channel without changing the content of the percept, and I argue that this is not cognitive penetration in the philosophically interesting sense. My positive proposal is a twin-stimulus design: interleave physically identical stimuli across penetrated and unpenetrated conditions, matched for adaptation state, criterion, and attention, and use a null-cue manipulation in which the cue predicts reward on 80% of trials but is orthogonal to the target's orientation, so any change in orientation sensitivity cannot be a strategic use of the cue. The audience is about ten people: six or seven hard-nosed psychophysicists who think in signal-detection terms and are allergic to talk of phenomenal character; two Bayesian predictive-processing researchers who will want everything recast as precision-weighted prediction error; and one philosopher who thinks the very notion of cognitive penetration is incoherent because cognition and perception are not natural kinds. They know the standard literature well, they are not shy, and they will be sceptical of both my deflationism and my exception.
