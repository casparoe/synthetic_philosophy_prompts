I'm putting together a session on advance directives for our ethics reading group and I want to use Memento as the case study, since it's basically the thought experiment the bioethics literature keeps failing to write. Leonard can't form new memories, so his tattoos, Polaroids and notes are literally a directive written by an earlier self for a later self who has no way of evaluating it — and by the end he's doctoring the record himself, burning the photo that would have told him his revenge was already done and writing the warning on Teddy's photo that sends his future self after Teddy, so that he kills a man on the authority of an instruction he can never audit. (There's also Teddy's claim that the Sammy story is Leonards own, which suggests the directive system is encoding a false history and not just a false instruction.) What I want the group to chew on is what actually grounds the authority of the earlier self over the later one. The usual defence is Dworkinian-ish: we honour the directive because it's the last competent exercise of the patient's own self-government, and because it protects the things she cared about most rather than whatever she happens to enjoy now. The usual objection, Dresser-ish, is that this isn't respecting a person at all, it's letting a stranger boss someone around, since the woman who wrote it and the woman it now binds aren't the same moral agent. Read the film against both — does it refute them, or only the naive versions? Is Leonard's set-up a Ulysses contract gone wrong, or something else entirely, given that a Ulysses contract assumes one continuous self anticipating its own weakness and he doesn't have that? Say honestly where the analogy strains, and what the film adds that the arguments don't; my hunch is that it shows directives can be weapons and self-serving fabrications rather than just promises, but I'd like to be argued out of that if it's wrong.
