So my buddy and I got into a three-hour argument last night about whether there can be reasons without anyone actually doing the reasoning. He kept insisting a "reason" is by definition something a mind has, full stop, and I kept saying no — evolution hands you reasons all the time (the bird flies south because winter's coming, and nobody has to be home thinking that thought). Turns out Dennett already has a name for exactly this, free-floating rationales, which I definately wish I'd known mid-argument.

Anyway, it got me thinking about Hitchcock's The Birds, which I rewatched the other day. The whole movie basically runs on this idea: the birds do things that look for all the world like they have a point — they wait, they mass, they target, the attacks escalate like some kind of coordinated campaign — but the film flatly refuses to hand you a reasoner behind any of it. No motive, no explanation, no mind, just birds. So here's what I want: read The Birds through the free-floating rationale idea. Are the birds themselves a free-floating rationale (reasons with no reasoner), or does the movie secretly need a reasoner and just withholds it, which would make it a different and creepier thing? Point me to where the idea actually lights up a scene, and where the film pushes back and an honest reading has to admit the analogy breaks.
