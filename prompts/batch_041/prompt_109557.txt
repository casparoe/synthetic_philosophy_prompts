I am a first-year PhD student in philosophy of mind who spent ten years as a systems programmer before switching fields, and the analogy my new colleagues reach for in every seminar — the mind stands to the brain as software stands to hardware, so mental states are multiply realizable, psychology is autonomous from neuroscience, and a system of the right functional organization would have the same mental life no matter what it is made of — is one whose pull I feel and whose joints I can hear creaking. Please act as a philosopher of cognitive science and audit the analogy rather than the functionalist thesis it is used to support. Start by giving me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the program, the machine, the specification, the compiler, and the relation of one program running on two machines. Then give me the disanalogies, separated into those that bear on the work the analogy is doing here and those that are merely decorative. Tell me which features of the software/hardware relation the analogy quietly imports into the mind/brain case — artifact and designer, a function specified antecedently of any implementation, a hardware/software boundary that is itself relative to an interpretation — and whether the mind/brain case contains anything corresponding to each. Then say whether the multiple-realizability and autonomy conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying them all along, and finish with the analogy you would use instead, if there is one. Think it through carefully before you write. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
