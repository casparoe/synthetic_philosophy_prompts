I am writing a paper on how much of Arrow's theorem is a fact about collective choice and how much is a fact about the formalism in which it is stated, and I would like your credence in a specific proposition, decomposed term by term.

Call the proposition A: There exists a procedure for making collective choices among three or more alternatives, over a finite electorate of at least two, that is weakly Paretian; independent of irrelevant alternatives, in the sense that the social preference between any two alternatives depends only on individual preferences between those two alternatives; defined for every profile of individual preference orderings; non-dictatorial; and such that no voter has a standing complaint about the procedure's treatment of her, where a standing complaint may appeal both to the ex ante distribution of influence and to the pattern of decisions across the whole sequence of choices the procedure governs, and not only to how the procedure treats her on a single decision.

My own credence in A is about 0.35; a colleague with whom I argue about this is at 0.85. What I want is not so much an adjudication between us as a picture of where the number comes from. Give me your credence in A, decompose it into the sub-questions it actually turns on, each with a rough probability and one line of justification, and then show how those terms combine.

I expect the decomposition to include at least the following: how much of the theorem's force rests on each of its conditions being a genuine requirement of fair procedure rather than an artifact of the formalism; whether two escape routes I have been considering preserve what is worth preserving — first, a lottery that selects a voter uniformly at random and imposes that voter's ordering on the decision, and second, a history-dependent procedure whose current output depends on which coalitions prevailed in past decisions, so that no voter is systematically the winner — and in particular whether the second is an escape at all or merely relocates the dictator to each decision considered separately; whether permitting outputs other than a complete transitive ordering (partial orders, sets of undominated alternatives, lotteries) changes the question or changes the subject; and whether the phrase "no standing complaint" is determinate enough for A to have a truth value at all.

Then tell me which term my colleague and I most likely disagree about, which term your own number is most sensitive to, what consideration would move it most, and whether adding strategy-proofness as a further requirement would move the answer up or down.
