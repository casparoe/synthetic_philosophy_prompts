I'm a retired field ecologist -- spent most of my career counting fish and arguing about carrying capacity -- and since I retired I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars, mostly naturalism and philosophy of religion. I keep running into the claim that religion should be studied as a natural phenomenon, and I realize I don't know what would count as a good theory of it. Everyone seems to argue for their favorite mechanism (byproduct of ordinary cognition, adaptation for group cohesion, cultural parasite, etc.) by pointing at one striking case, and I'd like the full specification first. Could you write me the desiderata: what any adequate naturalistic theory of religion has to deliver -- the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the empirical results it must not contradict -- with the consideration behind each requirement? I don't want a bibliography or citations, just the criteria and why they're criteria.

I'm especially interested in the ones that pull against each other. For example, it seems like a theory has to explain why religious traditions are so good at spreading even when their official doctrines are vague, inconsistent, or poorly understood by adherents -- the cognized religion is not the same as the official religion -- and also explain why some traditions invest enormous resources in costly, hard-to-fake displays and buildings and rituals. Those two demands might push toward different pictures: one about cheap transmission of minimal counterintuitive ideas, another about expensive commitment signals. Is that a real tension or can one theory handle both? Also, since I'm an ecologist, I can't help thinking of religions as strategies in a niche: they have to recruit attention, calories, reproductive effort, and transmission opportunities from hosts, and they compete with other religions and with secular institutions. Does that framing add a genuine desideratum -- something like a requirement that the theory explain the demographic and energetic conditions under which a religion grows, stabilizes, or goes extinct -- or is it just a metaphor that will mislead?

So: give me the requirements list, mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp, say if any subset can't be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular current theories. I care more about the spec than the scorecard, but a short scorecard at the end is fine if it's brief. And please keep it non-technical enough for someone whose last formal philosophy class was in 1974.
