I need help writing a speech, and I'd rather it be thought through properly than just nicely phrased. Take the role of a philosopher who works on ethics and on life-writing — biography, hagiography, the whole "we learn virtue by admiring people rather than by memorizing rules" tradition — and who has also read enough Plutarch to be annoying about it at dinner. Here's the situation. I'm chair of the governors at a small school that turns 120 this year, and I give the Founder's Day address every November. Our founder, Edith Marlow, is celebrated locally for basically one thing: during the 1905 typhoid outbreak she kept the school open and taught the girls herself in the fever ward when the staff fled — except a historian has just published a book showing that the famous version of that story is largely Edith's own later embellishment, that she very likely was never in the ward at all, and that she spent the following decade fighting to keep the school from admitting the mill-workers' daughters she is now celebrated for teaching. The book is solid, the historian is coming to Founder's Day (we invited her before any of this, long story), some alumnae want me to just tell the old story, the head wants "the usual" and has asked me not to relitigate it, the founder's great-granddaughter will be in the second row, and the students mostly want it to be over.

So: write me the address, about 900 words. It has to be genuinely edifying — these people came for that — without asserting anything the book has cast doubt on, and it has to get past the trap of "well, the myth is true in a deeper sense," which I think is a cop-out unless it's actually argued. Use what I've been chewing on lately: Plutarch's insistence that he's writing lives and not histories, and that a small thing — a phrase, a joke — often reveals character better than a battle does; the old hagiographic convention that a saint's life is written to be imitated, which means it has to be legible, and legibility costs you detail; and the modern biographer's suspicion of exactly that trade. The real question I want the speech to answer out loud, not just by implication, is what a life can still be used for morally once the edifying version is gone — whether learning from someone requires that they actually did the thing, or only that the life (or the story) makes a possibility visible. And I'd like it to say something about the difference between admiring a person and learning from a life, since I suspect that's where the alumnae's hurt is actually coming from. Before you write, brainstorm for a bit: list the ways a life can be morally useful and the ways it can be misused, and tell me which of them survive a debunking — then draft. Don't make the historian the villain and don't make the myth the hero; half the room loved the old story and half are embarrassed by it, and I want both halves to be able to sit there.
