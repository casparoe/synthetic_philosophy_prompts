I'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of medicine and I have to open next week's session on the definition of death, so I'm putting together the framing memo and I keep getting tangled. The trouble is everyone in this literature seems to be talking past each other. One camp says death is when the whole brain stops; another says it's only when the parts that subserve consciousness are gone; another says brain death isn't the death of the organism at all, because the body left behind is still one integrated system -- it holds temperature, heals wounds, fights off infections, and in at least a few well-known cases has been kept going long enough to carry a pregnancy to delivery. Then there's the transplant side, where hearts are now recovered from donors declared dead by circulatory criteria and restarted in somebody else's chest, which seems to need the donor to have been dead *and* the heart to have been perfectly fine; and there are the protocols that put the organ on a perfusion machine to warm it back up, which makes me wonder what we think we're doing. And circling all of it is the dead donor rule. I don't want you to pick a side for me.

What I want is the spec first. Act like a philosopher of medicine who's actually sat on a hospital ethics committee and once had to defend a criterion of death to a transplant team at three in the morning. Think this through carefully before you start writing, and maybe begin by just listing to yourself all the cases and facts you think any criterion has to survive. Then give me the desiderata: what any adequate definition/criterion of death has to deliver -- the cases it must get right (persistent vegetative state, locked-in, anencephaly, the ventilator-dependent brain-dead body, the controlled and uncontrolled circulatory-death donor, the patient who's awake but brainstem-dead-looking for other reasons, and whatever else you think belongs on the list), the distinctions it must not collapse (being dead vs. being dying vs. being beyond saving vs. being a person vs. being an organism), and the results from physiology, biology, or law that it cannot contradict -- with the specific case or consideration behind each requirement, not just the label. Then tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are really just the prejudices of one camp, whether any subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable (that's the part I most want -- if the list itself is a result, say so), and which single requirement does the most to knock out the most popular contenders. A table is fine for the last part. Assume the purpose is blunt: a criterion that could actually be written into statute and used at the bedside, not one that's merely philosophically elegant.
