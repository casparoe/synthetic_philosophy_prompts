Draft notes for an essay, "Assumptions Don't Have to Be True, But They Do Have to Work":

The thesis: Milton Friedman's famous methodological claim — that a theory is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions and not by the realism of its assumptions — is roughly right as a slogan but wrong as a methodology, because it treats "the assumptions of a model" as a single kind of thing when they actually do at least three distinct jobs.

The proposed taxonomy, which is the part I most want stress-tested:
1. Load-bearing fictions: assumptions that are false, known to be false, and whose only job is to make the analysis tractable — frictionless planes, perfectly rational agents, infinite populations.
2. Placeholders: assumptions that are false now but flagged as things to be relaxed or replaced later — a constant transmission rate, a fixed taste parameter.
3. Constitutive claims: assumptions doing explanatory or causal work that the modeler would defend as approximately true — that a particular feedback loop drives the outcome.

The argument: Friedman's defense only reaches type 1. For type 2 the unrealism is a research agenda rather than a license, since the assumption is on the books precisely to be given up. For type 3, an unrealistic assumption is a genuine defect, because the point of the model is to represent the mechanism, and a false mechanism is a false model however well it predicts.

An illustration I invented, so as not to lean on a stock example: a model of why commuters on a given metro line bunch into clusters. Model A assumes riders time their arrivals optimally and predicts bunching; Model B assumes riders copy the last person they saw board and predicts the same bunching. Identical predictions, but the assumptions play different roles — A's rationality assumption is a load-bearing fiction, B's copying rule is offered as a causal claim. The F-twist, as I read it, says we cannot distinguish the two; I say we can and should.

A second point I want checked: the "as if" move. When we say a billiard player plays as if he solved the equations, we are not saying anything about the player; we are saying the model is a predictive instrument. On that reading the assumption is not a claim about the world at all but a stipulation. Friedman seems to run these together, talking as though the assumption were a false claim about the world that we merely tolerate, when often it is better read as no claim about the world. I suspect that equivocation is the weakest joint in the view.

What I'd like from you: (a) an accuracy audit — have I characterized the position fairly, or have I set up a strawman or overstated a modest thesis? (b) whether the three-way taxonomy is exhaustive and whether the categories are genuinely distinct, or whether they collapse into one another under pressure; (c) whether the "as if" objection really damages the position or merely reframes it; and (d) whether any defensible version of the F-twist survives, perhaps one that separates the goal of prediction from the goal of explanation. Rank your objections by how damaging they are, and tell me what the position's own defenders would concede rather than only what its critics would say.
