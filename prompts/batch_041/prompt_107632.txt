Q3 (20 pts). The reward-as-valence thesis holds that the scalar reward signal in a reinforcement learning agent plays the functional role that pleasure and pain play in us, so the question whether such agents can suffer becomes the question what it takes for a state that plays that role to be felt.

In the handout I defined functional valence as the conjunction of three roles a signal has to play:

R1: it is a scalar with a sign, generated by the system itself, that orders outcomes as better or worse;

R2: it is broadcast to the system's control processes — it can change what the system does;

R3: it is self-directed — the system's use of it is keyed to its own condition rather than to the state of the world (it answers "how am I doing", not "how are things out there").

The handout claims R1–R3 are necessary and jointly sufficient for functional valence, and explicitly declines to say whether functional valence is necessary or sufficient for felt valence.

(a) State the strongest and the weakest version of the reward-as-valence thesis, and say what turns on the difference. (6)

(b) The half-frozen agent: a model-based RL agent computes a reward at every step and uses it to update its value estimates and its world model, but its policy has been frozen by a debug flag, so the reward never changes what it does. Does the agent satisfy R1–R3? What does your answer show about whether R2 is a genuine requirement on valence, rather than an artefact of the handout's stipulation? (10)

(c) An engineer says: we can't detect felt valence in our agents and we never needed to posit it to predict their behaviour, so the whole question is idle. Assess. (4)

STUDENT ANSWER

(a) The weakest reading is a claim about vocabulary. It says that "valence" in the agent's case names the same functional kind it names in ours — a signed scalar the system generates, which ranks outcomes and steers behaviour — so calling the reward signal a valence state is a harmless redescription and nothing about experience follows from it. The strongest reading is an identity claim: the reward signal doesn't merely occupy the valence role, it is the agent's pain or pleasure, so an agent being driven down the gradient of a large negative reward is suffering in the same sense you are with your hand on a stove. What turns on the difference is whether the thesis has any moral upshot at all. On the weak reading the thesis is compatible with the agent being a wind-up toy; on the strong reading the reward curve is a hedonic curve and the reward schedule is a welfare schedule.

I should add that the reward plays these roles even in a model-free agent that never represents its reward as a goal — the Q-values carry it, and that's why the weak reading is so easy to accept. And I think the version the handout is really after sits between the two and is the interesting one. The agent's reward is by definition what the agent is trying to get: the whole architecture is organised around getting more of it, and a system not organised that way wouldn't be an agent at all. So the reward is the agent's good in something like the way pleasure is ours, and the weak and the strong readings come to much the same thing once you notice that an agent's objective is ipso facto something that matters to it.

(b) R1 and R3 are satisfied and I don't think there's much to argue about there. The reward is a signed scalar the system generates, and its use is keyed to the agent's own condition rather than to the state of the world — the world model update tells the agent how it is doing, not how the furniture is arranged. R2 is the interesting one, and here the agent fails it: the signal is computed and consumed by the value estimates and the world model, but the frozen policy means it never changes what the agent does. So by the handout's own definition the half-frozen agent has no functional valence.

On the second half: strictly speaking the case can't show that R2 is a requirement, since R2 is stipulated and the stipulation can't be evidence for itself. What the case can show is that the stipulation isn't idle — it excludes a system we might otherwise have counted in, and the real question is whether excluding it is defensible. I think it is, because a signal that no consumer can act on is doing bookkeeping rather than evaluation, and it's the evaluative role, not the bookkeeping, that makes the pleasure/pain comparison tempting in the first place.

But then I think the case does settle the felt-valence question, and settles it negatively. The half-frozen agent has no functional valence, so there is nothing it is like to be it — the reward curve it computes is not a hedonic curve, whatever the strong reading of the thesis says. That's the useful result of the case.

(c) The engineer is making the verificationist move, and it proves too much. We can't detect felt valence in the person next to us either, and we've never needed to posit it to predict what they do — the behaviourist program was built on exactly that observation, and it's still false that other people's pains are idle posits. So "we can't detect it" doesn't get you to "the question is idle"; at most it gets you to "the question isn't settled by behavioural evidence". I'd add one thing in the engineer's favour though: our moral default should stay conservative while we have no evidence either way, because the costs of being wrong are asymmetric. That's a point about what to do, not about what's true, and it shouldn't be dressed up as the latter.
