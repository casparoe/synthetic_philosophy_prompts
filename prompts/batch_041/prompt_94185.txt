My friend and I got into a proper shouting match last night about teleosemantics — the view that a mental state means whatever it was biologically supposed to indicate, so that meaning gets naturalised by function — and after about three hours we were going round in circles and I genuinely couldn't tell whether we were even disagreeing about the same thing. I'd like you to work out what kind of disagreement this actually is.

Here's the shape of it. Take a bird that snaps at moths. Fine, its visual state means moth. But suppose what its visual system actually locks onto — the thing that did the selecting, the thing whose tracking made the trait spread — is a particular frequency of wing flutter, and the moth-correlation is a downstream accident. Now which one does the state mean? My friend says the flutter, because that's what it was selected for, and selection-for is objective: there's a fact about which effect explains why the trait proliferated. I said that's just shoving the bump under the carpet, because deciding which effect explains the proliferation already requires us to have picked out the trait under some description, and the description is exactly where the content was supposed to get pinned down in the first place. It got worse when I asked about a lineage whose flutter-detector is a hijacked version of an organ that in its ancestors did something completely different — so is the content the old job or the new one? He says the old, if the new one hasn't been around long enough to be selected for. I say that's arbitrary.

The frustrating bit is that we agree on every fact. Same bird, same moths, same selection history, same everything. We agree a creature with no history has no content. We agree you can swap the moth for a moth-shaped blob and the bird still snaps. He thinks the theory grounds determinate content; I think it only grounds the difference between having content and having none, and that the which-content part is smuggled in by us.

My hypothesis, and I'd like you to test it rather than just nod along: maybe we're answering two different questions under one name — he's asking what makes a state be about anything at all, and I keep testing it with what makes it be about this rather than that. If that's the diagnosis, say so and say what follows from it. If it's wrong, tell me what the real fault line is.

Write it as an essay, in British English. Don't fence-sit and don't just recite what the big names said — I want a diagnosis with reasons, plus a straight answer about whether anything could actually move this (a distinction, a case neither side can handle, a change of question) or whether the two of us are just after different things from an explanation and no amount of arguing will fix that.
