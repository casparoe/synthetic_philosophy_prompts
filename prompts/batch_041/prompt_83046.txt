I want a credence, with its derivation, on a specific claim about Kant's universalizability test, rather than a general appraisal of Kantian ethics.

The claim is this: there exists a single, principled specification of the test — one way of identifying the relevant maxim, one way of reading what a contradiction is — that (a) is applied uniformly rather than adjusted case by case, (b) returns the verdicts Kant treats as correct in the four examples he makes decisive (the false promise for money, the suicide from self-love, the neglect of one's talents, the refusal to help others in need), and (c) collapses into neither vacuity, in which every maxim passes, nor absurdity, in which maxims we are confident are permissible fail.

Give me your probability that this claim is true. If you find it ambiguous, say which disambiguation you are pricing rather than averaging over readings; if you think it lacks a truth value, say so and price the nearest well-formed claim instead.

Then decompose. I want a rough probability and a line of justification for each of at least these terms:

First, maxim specification: that the maxim can be pinned at the level of description Kant's verdicts require, without the tester being free to gerrymander the description in whichever direction suits the answer he wants — so that the false promise fails and the other three cases pass for the same reason rather than through separate repairs.

Second, the reading of contradiction: how much rides on which of the following is adopted — (i) the universalized maxim cannot be thought at all, because the description of the action becomes incoherent; (ii) the universalized maxim would destroy the practice or institution on which the action depends; (iii) the universalized maxim would defeat the very purpose the agent is acting on; (iv) there is no contradiction in any of these senses and the test is doing something other than what it advertises.

Third, the four cases themselves: whether they really come out as advertised — the suicide case is the usual trouble — and whether the division between contradiction in conception and contradiction in will marks a genuine distinction or is a patch fitted to yield exactly the perfect/imperfect duty division Kant wants.

Fourth, the ambition of the test: whether it is meant to be a decision procedure at all, or only a criterion of rightness with no algorithm attached, and if the latter, whether the claim I asked you to price is even the interesting one.
