My partner and I have a running argument about which video-game characters matter morally, and she has developed extremely firm and weirdly specific opinions about it, and I genuinely can't tell what rule she's actually running. Her verdicts so far: a stompable mook in a platformer that exists purely to be jumped on — doesn't matter. A generic merchant NPC with a fixed two-line script — doesn't matter. A companion character with a full written arc who can die and get mourned by other characters in the story — matters. A cute animal sidekick that's purely a game mechanic, follows you around, no interiority ever shown — doesn't matter, even though she freely admits it's adorable. A character who exists only as text in a parser-based game, no portrait, no voice acting, but pages and pages of first-person interior monologue — matters. A villain who's clearly set up to be hated but gets a sympathetic backstory late in the game — matters. And her own custom avatar after like 300 hours in one save file — doesn't matter, even though she's honestly quite attached to it. She's also said outright that cuteness and the player's personal attachment are basically irrelevant to the whole question, which I think is doing a lot of work here.

The new case that's tripping us up is a small indie game she just finished. You play the last member of an extinct species, stranded alone on an empty, slowly dying planet. The game gives this creature a vivid, continuous first-person inner life — you see and feel everything through it, it gets bored, it grieves, it remembers — but there is literally no other character in the game, nothing in the fiction ever relates to it, and the game is completely neutral about whether you let it die or keep it going; no one in the story ever treats its fate as a wrong or as something anyone owes it. Do you think she'll say this creature matters morally? Give me your best guess and your reasons, tell me which rule she's most likely running (a "the fiction represents a genuine inner life" rule, a "the fiction embeds it in a web of normative relations and treats it as wrongable" rule, or something else entirely), and name the case that would best tell those rules apart.

And a bonus, because it's related: a studio is now shipping NPCs driven by a language model, so they improvise dialogue, seem to remember you across sessions, and occasionally act distressed — but the game never claims they have inner lives and no character in the story ever treats them as wrongable. Would she say those matter? I half-suspect she'd have a totally different gut reaction to those two cases, and I want to know which of them exposes the real rule shes running.
