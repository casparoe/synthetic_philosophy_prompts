Two colleagues of mine have spent weeks arguing about an application before our city council and have asked me to referee, which I can't. A company wants a licence to sell a novelty food cultured from the cells of a paid, consenting adult donor: safe, no animals killed, no donor harmed in any ordinary sense, and repellent to nearly everyone who has heard of it. It has become obvious to me that the two of them are relitigating the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism in modern dress. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, rather than telling me what you would decide.

Priya: The council should refuse the licence — not because eating the stuff harms anyone, but because a market in human flesh as food erodes a shared norm, that human bodies are not provisions, and that norm is a common good. Norms of this kind have thresholds: a practice can be rare and still alter what everyone treats as thinkable, so the absence of measurable harm to any individual is exactly what one should expect to observe while the loss is going on.

Tom: That is the argument legal moralism always makes, and it reduces to one of two things. Either it is distress at knowing that others are doing something one disapproves of — not a harm, and no ground for prohibition — or it is an empirical prediction of social decay, of the sort that has been used to justify persecuting consenting adults and that has a poor record. And if the norm is as robust as she says, a novelty food cannot tip it; if a novelty food can tip it, it was doing less work than she claims. She also owes me a baseline: we permit the sale of plasma and hair, we permit commercial surrogacy in some places, and human tissue is used in medicine, so she cannot mean the commodification of the body as such. Her remaining line is majority disgust, which is not a reason.

Priya: He is answering a causal claim with evidence about consequences, but mine is constitutive, not causal. I am not predicting crime or breakdown. I am saying that a community which permits this is no longer a community that holds human flesh to be categorically not food, and that this is a change in what the community is. If so, demanding evidence of downstream harm begs the question, and the loss falls on the community as such rather than on any individual — which is why he will not find an aggrieved party to point at. His baseline point cuts the other way too: he treats whatever we happen to permit at present as the neutral starting point, and every argument for a line drawn elsewhere as the imposition of disgust.

Tom: Then who has standing to complain? If the answer is the community, that is either a fiction masking the majority's preference or a real collective good — and if it is a real good, she owes an account of how a good can be destroyed while no one's interests are set back. He added that the council's real choice is not between licensing and criminalising: the state could simply decline to license, advertise or subsidise the product, leaving the practice unendorsed rather than forbidden, and he denies that neutrality obliges the council to facilitate what it declines to endorse.

Adjudicate. Which of these four moves is right, which mistaken, and where are they talking past each other? I am particularly interested in whether the causal/constitutive distinction is a real one or whether the constitutive claim collapses into the causal one; who bears the burden of proof on the baseline question; whether the no-facilitation middle option is stable or collapses into one of the two positions; and what both of them are missing. Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify each rating briefly.
