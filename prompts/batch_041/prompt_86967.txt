I've been convinced that akrasia is a misnomer. On the view I now hold, there is no distinct failure of will, only a conflict between the preferences an agent holds at different times: what we call weakness of will is the familiar reversal produced by steep discounting of future goods, so that at the moment of action the agent does what she most wants then. On this picture akrasia is continuous with the whole family of present-bias effects rather than a defect of the will, and it is largely a matter of whether the agent has access to precommitment.

What should count against this? Rank the defeaters by how much they should move a committed holder of the view, and separate those she would concede from those she would dismiss. I am especially interested in cases the temporal-preference machinery seems unable to touch: an agent whose preferences never reverse — who has wanted the same thing all along, wants it now, and still fails to act — and akratic action that is neither more pleasant nor less effortful than the action it displaces, such as effortful self-sabotage with no immediate payoff, or cases where the akratic act is the better one, as when someone acts against a judgment she has reason to distrust. I also want the view treated as an empirical hypothesis: what does it predict that a time-consistent agent with state-dependent preferences and noisy choice would not, and would its defenders count the uneven success of precommitment as evidence against them? Finally, can "what she most wants at the moment of action" be identified independently of what she does? If not, has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a defect in a view of this kind?
