I have been trying to reconcile two things that I keep reading about separately, and every attempt to set them side by side leaves me confused. The first is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: any rule that takes rankings from voters, allows them to rank at least three options in any way they please, and never gives a voter a reason to misreport their ranking, must turn out to be dictatorial — one person's ranking settles the outcome. The second is the family of Groves mechanisms, best known through the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves rule, which are described as the exact opposite: there, each participant's best move is supposed to be to state their true valuation, whatever everyone else does.

Please compare these two results and explain how both can hold at once. I want to know precisely which assumption each one leans on — what the Groves setting allows that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite setting rules out, and whether the difference is really just the presence of money, or something deeper, about whether preferences are ordinal or cardinal, or about what counts as an outcome in the first place. Then I would like your judgement on whether the second result genuinely escapes the first or merely relocates the problem: once transfers are in play, is sincere reporting still impossible in some residual sense, or has the theorem's force been fully bought back? Be concrete about where the escape route runs, and tell me honestly whether the tension I feel is real or whether it comes from using one phrase, strategy-proof, for two different requirements.
