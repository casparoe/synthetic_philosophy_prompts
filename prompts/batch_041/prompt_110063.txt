Right, so I've been chewing on indirect normativity for a while now — the family of views where instead of saying what's good you say "do whatever the ideal version of us would converge on" — and I want you to make the arguement I half-believe and can't quite finish: that the whole family collapses. Not the usual objection that you can't say who counts as the idealized agent; I know that one and I'm bored of it. The version I want is that every idealization has to be specified in terms of something, and whatever does the specifying is the real theory of value, so the idealization is either decoration or smuggled content. The crux for me is what happens between the two idealizations people usually keep seperate. One: idealize the information and the reasoning, leave the ends alone — now the procedure is hostage to whatever ends we happen to have, and a species that delighted in pointless suffering gets the procedure's blessing. Two: idealize the ends as well — now the answer was built in and the deliberation is theater. My suspicion is there's no stable middle. The obvious candidate, idealizing coherence of the ends but not their content, looks formal, but coherence constraints on a preference set already rule things in and out, so it may already be doing the second horn's work under another name. Argue it out properly: show that any attempt to idealize in between collapses into one of the two horns — or, if you think there's a genuine middle, say exactly what it constrains and why that isn't a direct theory of value with extra steps. Give me at least one concrete case where the horns pull apart, with the verdict each one hands down.
