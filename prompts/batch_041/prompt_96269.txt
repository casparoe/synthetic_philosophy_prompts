Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on satisficing and bounded rationality lately (Simon, Gigerenzers stuff, the whole fast-and-frugal line). I run hiring at a small company, about forty people, we do five or six hires a year, and a while back I switched us from keep interviewing until we find the best person to make the first offer to anyone who clears a written bar we set in advance. Its worked much better and nobody misses the old way.

What I actually want from you is the most important implications of all that theory for what we're doing — and I mean the ones that really bite, not the good-enough-beats-perfect line everyone repeats. Two things I keep running into that I'd like you to take seriously instead of waving at.

First, our bar doesn't stay where we put it. When the market is hot it drifts up to roughly whatever our competitors are paying, and when the market is cold we end up hiring people we'd have turned down a year earlier. So the aspiration level looks like it's being set by other people's behaviour rather than by our own preferences. Does any of this literature have anything to say about satisficing when the threshold is set by a competition rather than by the agent?

Second, a friend of mine did an econ PhD and insists satisficing isn't a real alternative at all — it's just maximizing with a search cost bolted on, so the whole idea is either trivially true or trivially false depending on how you write the model. Is he right? And if he is, what's actually left of satisficing as a distinct claim?

One more thing I can't work out: our search costs us time and costs us candidates who take other offers, but it doesn't cost us money. Does that matter, or does any kind of search cost do the same work?

Go as deep as you think the material will support. And tell me how confident you are in what you say overall, and flag the single claim you're least sure about.
