I'm taking a module this term on consent in law and medicine, and before the seminar I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my own position so that others can press me on it. I'd rather you audit the set of commitments I've ended up with than argue for any one of them, because I'm fairly sure they don't all pull in the same direction and I can't see where the fault lines run. Here is where I've landed.

(a) Consent is valid only where the consenter is competent, adequately informed and acting voluntarily. I treat these as three distinct necessary conditions rather than as one fused requirement.

(b) On information: what invalidates consent is deception about a fact that a reasonable person in the consenter's position would regard as material to the decision. Silence is not deception, and a consenter's failure to ask about something she could have discovered does not by itself undermine her consent.

(c) On voluntariness: I hold the baseline view. A choice counts as coerced only where someone has deliberately made the alternatives worse than they would otherwise have been. Hardship that is nobody's doing — scarcity, illness, bad luck — narrows the options, but it does not on its own invalidate the consent given under it.

(d) On competence: I don't believe there is a single threshold. How much a person must understand before her consent counts should scale with the stakes, the complexity and the reversibility of what she is consenting to.

(e) The case our seminar invented, which I keep returning to. A walker is cut off by a rising tide on a causeway. The only person nearby with a boat is a ferryman, and he offers to carry her across in exchange for her late mother's engagement ring, which she is wearing. He did not cause the tide and he has no particular need of the ring. She hands it over and climbs in. My verdict is that she did not validly consent to that bargain — it does not bind her, and he has no claim on the ring — even though I am not comfortable saying he coerced her.

(f) Underneath all of this, I think consent is a mental state rather than a performance. What makes consent consent is the attitude of the person giving it, not the fact that a bystander would reasonably have taken her to be consenting.

(g) I take the point of the whole apparatus to be protecting people from having their choices overridden, and I think it matters most for those with the fewest alternatives.

What I want from you is an audit. Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look as though they are, and how severe is each real tension? Where a pair does conflict, tell me what would have to give, what the cheapest repair would cost me, and whether the repair leaves the rest of the set intact. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and be explicit about which single commitment is doing the most damage across the whole set — including any damage that only shows up when two of the commitments are combined rather than taken one at a time.

I'd like the bottom line in a form I can drop straight into my notes: end with one final line beginning Verdict: that names the tension I should treat as most urgent and the one commitment I should revise to resolve it.
