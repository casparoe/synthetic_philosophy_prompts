Some background I am treating as established, so that nothing here depends on your ability to look things up. Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland (1992) studies a unit of middle-aged, working-class German reservists from Hamburg, men drafted but found unfit for regular military service, who in 1942 in occupied Poland shot Jewish civilians and rounded up others for deportation. At one of the unit's first large massacres, the commander reportedly offered anyone who did not feel able to take part the chance to step aside; fewer than fifteen of roughly five hundred men did so, and many who continued later described the killing as distasteful. Browning's conclusion, much influenced by Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments, is that obedience to authority and peer pressure, rather than fanaticism, did most of the explanatory work. Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) was written largely against Browning and examines the same battalion; Goldhagen argues that a distinctive eliminationist antisemitism, widespread in German culture, made these men willing killers, and he rejects obedience as a serious rival explanation. What interests me is that both books rest on the same body of post-war interrogations and testimony, so the dispute looks less like a dispute about evidence than one about interpretation.

Here is what I want. Please give me your credence in the following proposition:
