I'm a few weeks into a policy role at a mid-sized AI lab, and last week I sat through a two-hour meeting on a single proposal. Two colleagues, Nadia and Tomas, did most of the talking and neither of them moved. I have to vote on the proposal next week and I want to know which parts of their arguments are actually load-bearing.

The proposal is to hold our next frontier model back for roughly four months after it is trained, in order to run a fuller evaluation and red-teaming suite, with a standing commitment not to release if the model crosses specified thresholds on dangerous-capability evaluations. The model is a generation beyond anything we have shipped. The two labs generally reckoned to be ahead of us on the last two generations have both narrowed their lead to a few months, and at least one of them is widely thought to be less cautious about deployment than we are.

Roughly, the exchange went like this.

(1) Tomas: The four months would be paid by us and by nobody else. The capability is coming either way; the only thing our decision changes is whether the version that lands first carries our guardrails or theirs. Holding back does not remove the risk, it reassigns it to someone who will take it less seriously. He called this the competitiveness constraint and insisted it was not an excuse but a description: a lab that unilaterally imposes an alignment tax on itself at the margin where the race is decided has simply chosen to lose the race, and losing the race is itself the harm.

(2) Nadia: That is the inevitability argument, and it proves far too much — the same reasoning would license any harmful act that some willing competitor would perform anyway. Her main objection, though, was that the comparison is wrong. Tomas is comparing our model with guardrails against their model without; the real comparison is both models four months earlier against both models four months later. Shipping first hands the rival a target and a proof of feasibility, so our release accelerates their next generation, whereas the delay buys time in which governance and safety research advance, and those do not run at capability speed. She added that Tomas was treating the rival's schedule as a fixed fact when it is partly a response to ours.

(3) Tomas: If their schedule is responsive to ours, it is responsive to our delay as well — and they will read a delay as an opening, not as a signal. What a rival copies is capability, not caution. Worse, the argument that we should ship first in order to be the ones setting the norms concedes the norm it claims to oppose: you cannot establish a practice of restraint by declining to restrain. And caution is a public good here. Our evaluation methods and findings get published; their weights do not. So a cautious lab subsidises the caution of precisely the labs that are racing it. If the safety measures really are a tax on capability, then selection favours whoever does not pay it, and the plan to stay at the frontier and set the norms from there is a bet on winning a race while carrying a handicap.

(4) Nadia: That is an argument for changing the payoff structure, not for capitulating to it — compute thresholds, licensing, liability that internalises the harm, procurement conditions. Unilateral restraint is often what makes coordination possible in the first place: it is a costly signal, it demonstrates that the thing can be done, and it moves the default in a negotiation that otherwise has no baseline. Norms also travel by routes other than the frontier — hiring, contracts, supply chains, customers who write safety requirements into their own procurement.

I do not know which of them is right, and I suspect they were arguing past each other at least some of the time. Please adjudicate the four moves in order, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and saying briefly why. Then give me the overall verdict: who is more right, and what both of them are missing.

Give it as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: items 1 to 4 for the four moves in order, and item 5 for the overall verdict and the shared blind spot. Write in British English.
