I want a case analysis of the Montreal Protocol regime through the lens of the theory of the commons, not a summary of the treaty or its history, which I already know. Take the stratospheric ozone layer as the resource, and the actors as the states that negotiated, the firms that produced chlorofluorocarbons and their substitutes, and the scientific bodies that supplied the causal claims. The following facts are stipulated, so do not spend words establishing them: the Rowland–Molina hypothesis of the mid-1970s that CFCs could catalytically destroy stratospheric ozone; the detection in the mid-1980s of severe Antarctic ozone depletion by the British Antarctic Survey; the 1985 Vienna Convention, which contained no binding controls; the 1987 Montreal Protocol with its initial fifty per cent reduction and subsequent tightening; the 1990 London Amendment, with its Multilateral Fund for incremental costs and its grace period for developing-country parties; the trade restrictions the Protocol imposes on non-parties; the major producers' eventual endorsement of a phase-out; the availability of substitute chemicals; and the later extension of the regime to further substances.

Spend the answer on the mapping. In particular:

1. Is the ozone layer a public good, a common-pool resource, or neither? Be precise about the two dimensions — rivalry in consumption and excludability — and say which feature the theory should actually be modelling: the depletion of a sink, or the underprovision of abatement. Note where the original tragedy-of-the-commons framing blurs the resource from the good, and whether that blurring does any damage in this case.

2. Map the collective-action structure: who are the appropriators, who are the providers, what exactly is the public bad, where the incentives to free-ride sit, and what the standard prediction gets right about the pre-1987 standoff.

3. Ostrom's design principles for long-enduring commons institutions: which does Montreal satisfy, which does it violate, and which simply do not fit a treaty among sovereign states rather than a village institution with graduated sanctions and cheap conflict resolution? Is the fit genuine or is the framework being stretched to cover a case it was not built for?

4. The epistemic-community account associated with Haas: the claim that a transnational network of scientists sharing causal beliefs and a common policy project drove the regime. Does it explain anything an interest-based account cannot — why actors converged on a phase-out before substitutes were fully proven, or why the Antarctic finding changed the politics when earlier warnings had not? Or is there a deflationary interest-based story that survives it?

5. The trade measures against non-parties: do they manufacture excludability and thereby convert a public good into something closer to a club good? If so, does that solve the free-rider problem or dissolve it by changing the game?

6. The counterfactual contrast with climate. Montreal is routinely called a benign problem — few producers, concentrated and cheap abatement, a dramatic and visible harm, ready substitutes — against the malign structure of climate. Does the benign/malign distinction do explanatory work, or is it a post hoc redescription of the outcome? What would it predict for high-seas fisheries or antibiotic resistance?

7. A verdict running in both directions: what the case confirms in the theory, what it is an anomaly for, and what it suggests the theory never anticipated — for instance, that the problem was resolved by technological substitution rather than by restraining appropriation, so that the commons frame may be the wrong frame altogether.

Be disciplined about the mapping and honest where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Write in British English.
