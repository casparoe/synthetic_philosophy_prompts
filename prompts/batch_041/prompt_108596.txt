I run a ninety-minute session in a science-and-society workshop for about twenty PhD students and postdocs (mixed biology, public health, and policy), and I want an activity that makes them feel the unilateralist's curse from the inside — the point at which a group of people who mostly agree that something should not happen watches it happen anyway, not because anyone is reckless but because only one person has to move. I want them to run into it before I name it; the term should not appear until the debrief. The scenario I'd like to seed is deliberately fictional, so nobody can short-circuit it with real-world priors: twenty independent labs have each arrived at the same enabling result, a self-spreading soil microbe that degrades a common industrial pollutant. Publishing the method is one click, and it is entirely within each lab's discretion. If it works as intended it is a large good; if the organism spreads outside the intended niche, the damage is serious and slow to detect. Assume for the purposes of the fiction that every lab wants the same thing — the general good — and that no one is tempted to publish out of malice.

Design me the activity: rules, materials, a way to encode each participant's private numbers without revealing the distribution, the sequence of rounds, and how the facilitator tallies. I have in mind something like this. Each person gets a sealed card carrying two numbers — their honest estimate of the chance of a serious adverse outcome, and the largest such chance at which they would still judge publication worth it, given what their lab stands to lose by being scooped (that second number varies across cards) — and each acts as that lab director would. Start with a small round (three labs, everyone else privately predicting the outcome) and then a full round of twenty, so they can watch restraint hold at small scale and collapse at large scale. Then a round with open deliberation and an attempt to agree a binding norm, and finally a test of whether the norm survives contact with one participant whose card has changed overnight and now makes publishing personally compelling. Before the first tally, have everyone privately write down the probability they think the capability will end up public, and collect those slips. No real money should be needed. Give me a printable facilitator's sheet with the deck's numbers, the minute-by-minute timeline, and the tally arithmetic — and note how to check the deck in advance so that the trigger reliably fires at N = 20 without firing at N = 3. Tell me, too, what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it: people talking out of band despite the sealed procedure, participants who refuse to play a lab director who publishes, the risk that the trigger never fires and the demonstration falls flat, and the risk that the whole thing collapses into an argument about whether the fictional microbe is plausible.

For the debrief, I want them to see the structure rather than hear a summary of it: that the group's behaviour is set by the maximum of the private judgments rather than their average; that the chance the capability goes public climbs toward certainty as the number of labs rises even when each lab's own chance of publishing is small; and that there are two separate engines here — heterogeneous thresholds, where the most permissive card decides for everyone, and the preemption argument, where even a cautious actor publishes because restraint does not reduce the chance of publication, only their share of it. Then have them run the mirror case: the same structure with a beneficial action that any one lab could take, where the failure is that nobody volunteers, so that they can see the problem is not that people are too bold but that the arrangement selects the tail of the distribution. Show them the conformity-style fix — act only as you would want every actor in a relevantly similar position to act, each with their own numbers — explain why it turns a question about me into a question about us, and why it is fragile: it needs common knowledge, it is defeated by the thought that my case is different, and it does nothing when the actors' values genuinely diverge. I would also like an honest paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply myself: that it bakes in shared values, so it cannot show where the curse ends and ordinary disagreement begins; that it treats the private estimates as independent draws when in reality they are correlated by shared literature, shared funders, and shared hype; that one-click publication is a fiction standing in for slower, monitored, partly reversible processes; and that the participants will feel structural inevitability rather than horror, so the motivation to adopt the fix has to come from me.
