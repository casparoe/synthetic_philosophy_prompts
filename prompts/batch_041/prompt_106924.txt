I'm workshopping a short paper on supererogation for a reading group and would value a careful round of comments before I commit to the argument. The paper's central claim is that the standard framing of supererogation is incomplete in a way that matters. On the usual view, supererogation is a relation between an act and a moral standard: the act exceeds what the standard demands, is therefore not required, and earns praise on that basis. I want to argue that this is really a two-place relation doing the work of a three-place one, and that any defensible account needs a third relatum — the agent's baseline, understood as the level of sacrifice that this particular agent is already disposed to absorb without its registering as a cost. Once the baseline is in place, three things follow that I think are worth taking seriously. First, the old puzzle about supererogation — if the act is morally better than its alternatives, why isn't it required? — dissolves rather than gets solved, because "required" is fixed to the baseline and not to the impersonal standard, so there is no tension left to explain away. Second, a ratchet: each supererogatory act raises the agent's baseline, so sustained heroism gradually converts into routine duty, and what was once beyond the call becomes merely the call. Third, and this is the part I'm least sure about, the account makes supererogation partly opaque to outsiders, since baselines aren't observable — two people who each donate a kidney may differ in whether the act is supererogatory for them, and no third party can tell which is which.

I can see at least three ways this might come apart, and I'd rather hear them from someone else. The view might covertly change the subject, replacing a claim about acts with a claim about character, in which case it isn't an account of supererogation at all but a relabeling of virtue. The ratchet might be an artifact of the baseline metaphor rather than a real feature of moral life — perhaps baselines decay, or perhaps they only rise for acts that were genuinely costly in the first place. And the whole thing may collapse into subjectivism about duty, which would be a reductio, since then nothing is required of anyone except relative to whatever they happen to be disposed to give; I take the familiar objection that a life of maximal moral sacrifice is not an admirable life to be a symptom of this problem rather than a separate worry. I'd like your comments on whether the three-place analysis is coherent on its own terms, whether the ratchet is genuine or illusory, whether the subjectivism charge lands, and what counterexample you'd press hardest against it.

Please give me exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
