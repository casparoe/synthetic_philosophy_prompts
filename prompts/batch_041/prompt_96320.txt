I'm building materials for a third-year seminar on the philosophy of time, and for the session on special relativity I want to assign a short story instead of yet another expository reading — students switch off when its all A-series/B-series jargon, and fiction sneaks the puzzle in through the back door. So I'd like you to write me one, maybe 1200–1500 words. Here's the philosophical content it has to carry, in case it isn't at your fingertips: there's a famous argument (usually credited to Rietdijk and Putnam, though the label doesn't matter) that runs roughly like this. Special relativity tells us simultaneity is frame-relative — two events that one observer counts as happening at the same time can be counted by another observer as happening at different times, and neither is making a mistake. Now take some event in what I'd call my future, say a supernova in a distant galaxy. Because simultaneity is relative, there's some observer for whom that explosion is happening right now, and another for whom it's already over. If we grant the modest premise that whatever is happening right now for somebody is real, it looks like my future is already as real as my present — and by the same route, so is my past. The conclusion people draw is that time doesn't flow; the whole thing, past, present, future, is just there, like a landscape you can walk around in. That's what the story has to dramatize rather than lecture about. And I'd like it to also touch a distinction my students always miss: that "the future is already real" and "the future is already settled, fated, couldn't have gone otherwise" are not the same claim — a block universe could in principle contain genuinely chancy or open events. So one character should push on that, and I don't want the story to resolve it.

For the setup, here's roughly what I have in mind (change details freely, just keep the shape). Two people, somewhere unglamorous — a bus station, a hospital canteen, a wake, wherever — who were once close and haven't spoken in years. One of them has recently lost someone and has latched onto the block-universe picture as a kind of consolation: the dead aren't gone, they're just elsewhere in the block. The other is a stubborn presentist who thinks that's a sentimental abuse of physics, and that the past really is gone, full stop. Let them talk, and let them be a bit petty about it, the way people actually are. Somewhere in the middle I want the relativity of simultaneity doing real work in the conversation — a concrete case where two observers genuinely disagree about whether some distant event has happened yet, with neither of them confused, and it should be clear this needs no time machine and no exotic technology, just two people in relative motion. Then near the end the presentist should make the sharper objection: fine, suppose other times are real, but that gives you no reason to think what happens in them couldn't have happened otherwise — being real isn't the same as being inevitable, and you've smuggled the second thing in under cover of the first. Nobody wins. End on a line of dialogue that leaves the puzzle open, not on a tidy summary of the positions.

No citations or named papers inside the story, obviously — it's fiction, they should sound like people, not a syllabus, and I don't want it reading like a physics lecture with a plot stapled on. Oh and if you can work in a small physical detail that keeps the thing grounded — weather, an object someone keeps fidgeting with, whatever — that'd help; my students respond to that stuff.
