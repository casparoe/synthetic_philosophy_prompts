So I'm in this little philosophy reading group that meets in the back of a cafe on Thursdays, and last week two of the regulars, Ravi and Mira, went at each other for like forty minutes over the knowability paradox (the old Fitch result — roughly, if every truth can be known, then every truth is known), while the rest of us just sat their nursing cold coffees. I've been turning it over since and I honestly can't tell who had the better of it. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Ravi: the proof is airtight, so the principle "every truth can be known" is simply false. Take any truth that nobody in fact knows — say that the number of grains of sand on some particular stretch of beach is odd, and no one knows it. That's a truth. If it can be known, then it's possible that someone knows it. But part of what's true is that nobody knows it; so the possible knower would have to know that nobody knows it and know it, at the same time. Contradiction. So there are truths that can't be known, full stop, and that kills any view that ties truth to knowability. (2) Mira: the whole thing turns on one step — going from "someone knows the conjunction: [the number is odd] and [nobody knows the number is odd]" to "someone knows that nobody knows the number is odd." That's the assumption that knowledge distributes over "and": that knowing A-and-B always comes with knowing A and knowing B separately. Deny that and the contradiction never gets off the ground. So the paradox doesn't refute knowability; it refutes knowability-plus-that-distribution-assumption. (3) Ravi: but distribution isn't an extra assumption you can just drop, it's what "and" means. If I know that it's raining and the streets are wet, then it's simply true that I know it's raining and I know the streets are wet. There's no such thing as knowing a conjunction while not knowing its conjuncts — that doesn't describe any possible mind. So that escape hatch is empty. (4) Mira: fine, grant distribution. Then the culprit is applying the knowability principle to a proposition that itself talks about knowledge. The principle was only ever meant for ordinary facts about the world — that this beach has an odd number of grains, that water boils at such-and-such. Feed it a proposition containing "nobody knows" and of course you get nonsense; garbage in, garbage out. Restrict the principle to knowledge-free propositions and the paradox evaporates. (5) Ravi: that's just moving the goalposts. And it's worse than ad hoc — it makes the principle trivial, since the interesting antirealist claim was supposed to be that even truths about what is or isn't known are knowable. You've saved the slogan by gutting it. (6) Mira: no — the honest version isn't "knowledge-free propositions," it's "propositions that aren't ruled out by the very act of knowing them." Any truth that can be known without contradiction is knowable; that's the only version anyone should have defended in the first place, and it survives the paradox untouched. (7) Ravi: and that version is circular. "Not ruled out by the act of knowing it" just means "can be known." So your principle says every truth that can be known can be known. Nobody ever doubted that. You've rescued the letter and lost the whole point.

Can you referee this move by move, (1) through (7)? For each, rate it right, partly right, or wrong, and say briefly why — and then tell me where the real disagreement lives, because I suspect they're talking past each other somewhere but I can't find it. I especially can't tell whether Mira's last move (6) is a genuine repair or the dodge Ravi says it is, and whether his (5) actually answers her (4). Seperate the good points from the bad ones for me, and don't just split the difference — commit. End with a final line that begins "Verdict:" and states in a short phrase who came out ahead overall — Ravi, Mira, or neither (and if neither, whose points were more central).
