I'm a final-year law student writing a long essay on the counter-majoritarian difficulty, and I keep meeting a comparison that I suspect is doing far more argumentative work than anyone admits. It runs roughly: judging is umpiring. A nominee for Chief Justice, at his confirmation hearings in 2005, put it that way — the judge calls the pitches, he does not throw them — and a classmate of mine who takes a robustly originalist line now uses the picture to argue that a court which discovers new rights is simply an umpire inventing a strike zone to suit himself. The constitution is the rulebook; the legislature and the executive play the game; the judge applies a rule he did not write to facts he did not choose, and ought to confine himself to that. I would like you to audit the analogy rather than either endorse or rubbish it. What maps onto what — give me the correspondences explicitly, a table is welcome — and where does the mapping break? I am interested in which features of umpiring get quietly imported into judging, and in whether the restraint conclusion my classmate draws can actually be carried by the comparison or is merely decorated by it.

My own suspicion, which you should test rather than accept, is that the analogy is shakiest precisely where the restraint argument needs it most. The strike zone is not a fixed feature of the world that the umpire merely reports: the official rules define it by reference to the batter's own stance, so a batter can shrink it by crouching; the league has repeatedly moved its upper and lower limits, at least once with the avowed aim of rebalancing the contest between pitchers and hitters; a plate umpire can be appealed to a colleague on a checked swing; and the league is now moving towards an automated ball-strike system, which has required restating the zone as a band defined by a fraction of the batter's listed height. None of that troubles a rulebook that somebody owns, amends and enforces, but the constitution is owned by nobody, has no commissioner, and the court is in the last instance the body that says what it means — including whether some other branch has overstepped it. So the comparison may be presupposing the very determinacy of the rules that it is wheeled in to establish.

Tell me which disanalogies bear on the intended conclusion — that judges should apply law they did not make — and which are merely colourful; whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along; and whether there is a better analogy for the same job, one that survives contact with the hard cases rather than flattering the restraint side. I do not need reading lists or citations; I want the argument, not the bibliography. And please be candid about the limits of the exercise: if the comparison is too thin to be audited in the way I am asking, or if I have got the baseball facts wrong, or am attacking a caricature of what restraint theorists actually mean, say so plainly instead of working within my framing. Where the answer turns on a contested reading of the analogy rather than on anything settled, mark it as such — I would rather have a calibrated answer that flags its weak points than a tidy one.
