Construct a thought experiment to order, on a topic in Augustine's ethics of lying.

Specification. The scenario must contain exactly one lie, spoken aloud, and it must fall simultaneously under two different rungs of the eight-fold ordering of lies that Augustine sets out in his treatises on lying. Specifically, it must be at once (a) a lie that benefits the speaker or a second party at the expense of a third, and (b) a lie told to shield someone from sexual violation — the kind Augustine places at the bottom of his ladder as least grave. The two must be genuinely co-present, not one after the other: the same utterance, in the same breath, does both jobs. Constraints: three characters only; the third party's loss must be real but modest (a sum of money, a missed opportunity, a spoiled plan), while the sexual harm being averted must be serious; no physical violence occurs within the scene; the lie is spoken, not merely implied or omitted; and the whole setup fits in one paragraph. The point of the case is to isolate whether Augustine's ordering ranks acts by the good the liar intends or by the harm the lie does, so do not build the case by announcing which rung it lands on — let the placement follow from the structure.

Then, in order:

1. State the lie's position in the ordering, and set out the criterion that generates the ordering — what makes one lie graver than another for Augustine. Say plainly whether the third party's modest loss moves the lie up the ladder or is outweighed by the aim of averting the graver harm, and why.

2. Give Augustine's own verdict on the case. Be careful to separate three claims: that the act is a lie; that it is a sin; and that it is the least grave kind of sin. Say whether he permits it, and note any place where he wavers or where the two treatises pull apart. If you are not sure how far he wavers, say so rather than smoothing it over.

3. Give the verdict a defender of a graded-permissibility reading would extract from the same materials — the reading on which the bottom rungs are tolerable when they prevent something worse — and identify exactly which premise in Augustine licenses it.

4. Give the verdict a defender of mental reservation would give: that the utterance is not a lie at all, because the speaker does not intend to signify what the hearer takes him to signify. Say precisely which feature of your case that defender must exploit, and whether your setup leaves the feature available.

5. Identify the single feature of the case doing the most work. Say what happens to each of the three verdicts if you remove it.

6. Say how an Augustinian rigorist would attack the case — not the verdicts, the case.

State your confidence in each substantive claim, and single out the one point you are least sure of. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in no more than twelve words, the rung of the ordering the case occupies and whether Augustine permits the lie.
