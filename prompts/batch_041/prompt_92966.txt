I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to explain to a colleague why our team has spent two years and a great deal of money rebuilding a payments service that everyone privately agrees is a dead end. The way I've come to see it, we're playing a game: two players take turns raising a bid on a one-dollar prize; the highest bid wins the dollar, and the runner-up still has to pay what they bid. My colleague says that's just the sunk cost fallacy dressed up, and that any sensible person walks away at sixty cents. I think he's wrong, and that the interesting part is that you don't need any irrational attachment to money already spent in order to keep bidding — from where the bidder is standing, the next bid genuinely looks like the better option, and the one who stops is the one who eats a certain loss. Write a short story, around a thousand words, about a team in exactly that position. I want the reader to feel the pull of each next bid as a live and defensible choice rather than an obvious blunder, and to be able to tell apart the three things keeping it going: sentiment about what has already been spent, the forward-looking trap built into the choice actually on offer, and the fact that everyone has publicly backed the earlier bids. Include a moment where someone offers the team a way out that turns out to be a way back in. Don't have a character stop and explain the game; let the decisions and the dialogue carry it.
