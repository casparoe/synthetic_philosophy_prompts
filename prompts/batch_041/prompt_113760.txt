I cover science and medicine for a regional daily, with a fairly long leash when a story turns toward ideas, and for the past four months I've been the reporter on the aftermath of a chemical release at a plant that makes industrial solvents — roughly two hundred people went to the hospital, most of them from a neighborhood that is majority Spanish-speaking. The company's response has turned into its own story, and I'm the one who has to write it. Here is the sequence, because the details are what I can't get past. Ninety minutes after the release, a company spokesperson stood outside the plant gate and read a statement drafted by outside counsel and the communications director: it said the company "deeply regrets the harm to our neighbors," said it "takes full responsibility," and ended with a line the insurer insisted on, stating that the statement was not an admission of liability. Two days later the company mailed a letter to every household within a mile, signed by the plant manager, which said the company "apologizes for the disruption to your lives." The Spanish version of that letter, translated by a contractor and never reviewed by anyone in communications, uses a phrase three of my sources tell me lands closer to "we feel bad about the trouble" — not the same thing the English says. When I asked the CEO about the gap, he said, "I've already apologized, and I meant every word of it," and the families I've spent time with say that isn't true, that no one from the company has ever looked them in the eye and said it, and that an apology with no face and no name attached is not an apology. The company's lawyer then told me, on background, that the spokesperson's statement "wasn't cleared language" and shouldn't be treated as the company's position — even though the CEO has since endorsed it twice.

My editor wants a piece that gets past the usual anatomy-of-a-good-apology listicle, the one with five steps and a note about naming what you did wrong. I want something harder. I want to know what kind of thing these statements actually are, and whether the answer shifts when the speaker is a corporation rather than a person, when the words are read aloud by someone who didn't write them, when two official versions of the same sentence say different things, when a single paragraph both accepts and disclaims responsibility, and when the people on the receiving end refuse to accept the words at all. I keep catching myself on a smaller question too: is "we apologize" something the company does in the saying of it — so that it either happens or doesn't, depending on conditions I could list in advance — or is it merely a report of an inner state that I have no way to verify and probably shouldn't write about as though I can? A colleague on the copy desk, who did a philosophy degree before this, says I can't have it both ways: either the words do something or they don't. That doesn't feel right, but I can't articulate why, and I suspect the honest answer is that the two halves of the story — what the company did with its words, and what the families are willing to grant — are being run together in my own draft without my noticing.

So what I'd like from you is help thinking this through well enough to do two concrete things: decide how to describe what the company did, in plain news prose, without either overclaiming that an apology was given or sneering that it wasn't; and know what to put to the lawyer, the communications director, and the families, so that I'm asking about the right joints rather than collecting everyone's opinion about sincerity. The cases I care most about are the failures — a statement that is issued but never taken up, one that is sincerely meant but so hedged that it can't land, one that is impeccably worded and hollow, and one spoken by someone whose authority to speak for the entity is exactly what's in dispute. And I'd like to know whether "an apology" and "being forgiven" are even the same kind of thing, since my sources use the two interchangeably and I have a strong hunch they aren't.
