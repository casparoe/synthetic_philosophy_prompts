ok so background: I've been reading LessWrong and the old Overcoming Bias archives for like fifteen years, I know the vocabulary (indexical, reference class, observer-moments, all that) but I'm not a mathematician and I've never actually sat down and worked the anthropics stuff out on paper.

Here's what I need. I'm one of eight people in a small giving circle. We pool maybe $400k a year, make 15ish grants, mostly longtermist-adjacent, and everything gets decided by a vote of the group. And a growing share of the proposals we get have their whole case riding on anthropic reasoning. Someone says 'under SIA you should expect vastly more observers than you'd naively think, so the EV here is astronomical.' Someone else says 'the doomsday argument means we're probably in the last century, so put the money in near-term stuff instead.' Someone wants us to fund work on whether the universe is spatially infinite. And the thing that drives me up the wall is that everyone just reaches for SSA or SIA depending on which one makes their proposal work, and since basically none of us can actually do the math we end up voting on vibes. Nobody in the circle can even tell me whether that's a real disagreement or a free parameter people are tuning.

So what I want from you is a procedure. Not another essay about the debate -- an actual checklist that a non-mathematician can run on a proposal in about twenty minutes, in order, with clear stopping points, of the 'stop, the number is not usable, all you can say is that this proposal is good conditional on SSA and bad conditional on SIA' kind. It needs to tell me: (a) what facts about my own existence I'm allowed to condition on when doing the estimate, (b) how to pick a reference class without it being pure convenience, since every argument I've ever had about this ends with someone picking the reference class that gives them their answer, (c) exactly where SSA and SIA come apart on a given proposal and whether that divergence should kill the proposal or just get flagged for the group, and (d) what to do about the presumptuous philosopher type case, where the mere fact that one hypothesis implies way more observers makes you almost certain it's true without doing any physics. Please don't average the two assumptions into some mushy middle number -- that's exactly the move that lets us pretend we have a number when we don't.

Then run the procedure on three proposals and show your work: (1) fund X because there are astronomically many future people, so the EV is huge; (2) fund Y because the doomsday argument says we're early and the window is closing; (3) fund Z, about whether the universe is infinite and whether infinitely many observers makes our EV numbers meaningless or undefined. I fully expect the procedure to tell me something I don't want to hear on at least one of these, so say which and don't soften it. And be upfront about where the procedure just can't decide anything and a human has to pick a prior and write down that they picked it, and where it will misfire on unusual proposals. Also I half-remember some Adam and Eve case that's supposed to break one of the reference classes -- if that's a real thing, tell me what it does to the checklist.
