You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of bringing people into existence, and you have a particular talent for the hardest part of that job: taking a scattered pile of someone's gut reactions and finding the smallest, sharpest principle that generates them — or showing that no such principle exists and saying exactly which reaction has to be surrendered.

I have been keeping a private list of my reactions to cases about having children, and I want you to tell me what, if anything, I actually believe. I am not a philosopher and my reactions are not theory-driven; they are just what I notice myself thinking. Before you answer, think the problem through carefully and show the work: first restate my reactions as a set of claims; then propose two or three candidate principles, beginning with the simplest one that could possibly cover the ground; then test each candidate against every case; then say which survives, what it implies about cases I have not listed, and where it forces me to give up a reaction. Please do not hand me a reading list or tell me which famous position I have reinvented — I want the principle worked out on its own terms, and I would rather have your own clean formulation than a label.

In every case, "the child" is a possible person whose whole life we can foresee with certainty; assume no one else is affected, and that the parents would be good parents.

1. A couple could conceive now; they know the child would have an ordinary good life, with the usual mix of satisfactions and disappointments weighted clearly toward the satisfactions. They conceive. My reaction: this is good, and the world is better for it — not obligatory, but good.

2. The same couple could instead simply not conceive. My reaction: nothing is lost. I do not feel the world is poorer, I do not feel a good was forgone, and I do not think they owe anyone an explanation. Yet in case 1 I counted the same child's existence as a good. Those two reactions sit oddly together and I have never made them sit comfortably.

3. A couple could conceive a child who will have a short life of severe, unrelieved pain before dying young. They conceive. My reaction: they have done something wrong, not merely something sad. I would try to stop them.

4. The same couple could instead not conceive, so that this suffering life never begins. My reaction: this is positively good — a harm averted, not merely the absence of one. Which sharpens the asymmetry against case 2: refraining from a good life is neutral, refraining from a bad one is good.

5. A couple conceives a child whose life will be worth living, but only barely: mild chronic tedium, no great joys, no great pain, and the child will never be glad to have existed, though also never wish it hadn't. My reaction: I cannot get a grip on this one. Some days it seems a small good, some days like nothing at all, some days like a mild wrong. This is the case I keep going back and forth on, and I suspect it is the one that decides the issue.

6. A couple conceives a child who will have a rich and happy life, but who will also pass through one stretch of intense suffering, and who in retrospect will be glad for the life as a whole. My reaction: clearly good. The suffering does not cancel the good or make the creation wrong.

7. Now vary case 6 by order: the intense suffering comes at the very end, after the good, so the child dies in misery having lived well until then. My reaction: still a life worth having created, but I feel a distinct tug the other way, as though the same total is worth less when it ends badly. I cannot tell whether that tug is a genuine verdict or mere sentimentality about endings.

8. A couple takes a gamble: one chance in ten the child's life will be miserable, nine in ten that it will be good, and they conceive on those odds. My reaction: wrong — the bad outcome is a real harm to a real person, and nine good lives do not buy it off. But when I set the odds at one in a million I no longer think the gamble wrong at all, and I cannot say what the difference is except that the bad case is less likely. I have no principled place to draw the line.

9. A couple conceives a child whose life will be good, but only at the cost of something they value greatly — a career, a place they love. My reaction: a real cost, and choosing the child anyway is a fine choice, but not obviously the better one. I mention this to show I do not think creating a good life automatically wins against anything.

The rule I have been using on myself, which I do not trust, is this: in deciding whether someone comes into existence, only the suffering you would be inflicting counts; the good you would be conferring counts only for people who already exist. It gets cases 1 through 4 and makes case 8 wrong at any odds, which I do not believe, and it says nothing at all about case 5, which is where I live.

What I want from you, in the end: the principle or small set of principles that actually fits the pattern, with each clause tied to the case that forces it; a verdict on case 5, since I cannot supply one; an honest statement of which of my reactions has to go; and your principle's verdicts on cases I have not mentioned — say, a life that is good on the whole but that the person will later, sincerely and stably, wish had never begun; a case where the only way to prevent one miserable life is to prevent a great many happy ones; and a couple who conceive in order to save their marriage, knowing the child will have an ordinary good life but be wanted mostly as a means. Finally, tell me whether your principle needs the idea that never existing can be better or worse for someone who never exists, and if it does, whether that idea can be made sense of or whether the principle should be restated so as not to need it.
