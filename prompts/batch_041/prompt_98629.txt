So I write about science and ideas for a general-audience outlet, and I've been circling a puzzle I'd like to hand off as a proper essay assignment. Its the old Frege thing about identity statements — you know, how Hesperus is Hesperus feels like a dead end while Hesperus is Phosphorus was a genuine discovery, even though the two sentences seem to say the exact same thing about the exact same object. Frege's own answer was that there's more to a name than the thing it points at, some sense or mode of presentation, and ever since, people have been fighting over whether that extra something lives in the language, in the mind, or nowhere at all, with the informativeness smuggled in by pragmatics instead.

What I want is an essay that actually picks a side and defends it: is the difference in cognitive significance between the trivial identity and the informative one a matter of meaning — part of what the sentences semantically express — or is it a matter of the psychology and pragmatics of the people using them, so that the two sentences really do mean the same thing and only our mental files differ?

To keep this from being a rerun of the usual star-and-planet examples, I want you to test whatever view you land on against a case I've been chewing on. Imagine a woman who reads two different books about the same reclusive inventor, under two different names, and builds up two whole seperate pictures of him — different quirks, different anecdotes, she'd swear they're two different people. Years later she finds out the two names pick out one and the same man. Her realization that they're the same guy has real cognitive oomph; it reorganizes everything she thought she knew. So: does that oomph attach to the identity statement itself, so that its meaning differs from the meaning of the boring self-identity? Or is it just about the reshuffling inside her head, with the proposition itself staying flat and trivial? Use the case to pressure-test your verdict — if your view says it's just psychology, explain what the semantics is doing there; if your view says it's semantics, explain what exactly the extra meaning is and where it comes from.

Couple of ground rules. No citations, no reading list, no "as Frege argues in such-and-such" — I want the argument, not the bibliography, and I'd rather you build the thing from scratch than lean on famous names. Reach a real conclusion; don't hedge into "it's complicated." And write it in American English, please.
