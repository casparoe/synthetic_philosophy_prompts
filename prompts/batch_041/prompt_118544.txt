I am the methods officer for a state environmental and public-health agency. Our advisory committees must commit, for each decision, to a single quantitative model; we cannot publish a decision as "somewhere between the models." Twice a month a committee faces rival models that fit the same body of evidence about equally well but disagree about the decision-relevant quantity, and the members have no training in the philosophy of science, so they settle it by seniority. I need a standing decision procedure I can hand them as a one-page annex, built on what the underdetermination literature actually establishes and honest about what it does not.

The procedure should run in order, say what counts as an adequate answer at each step, and keep these distinctions alive: underdetermination that more data would dissolve versus one that any feasible study would leave standing; rivals that are equivalent in general versus rivals that are equivalent only with respect to the quantity we have to decide; and the holism problem that any model can be rescued by adjusting its auxiliaries, so "the evidence favors X" is always relative to a package someone chose. At each step it must mark whether that step is evidential or a judgment the committee is making -- how much to hedge, which error is worse -- and say who has to sign off when it is a judgment.

Then run it on two cases: two transmission models of the same respiratory outbreak that fit the same case data but disagree about the benefit of closing schools; and two dose-response models behind a contaminant limit, one with a threshold and one without, where I suspect the procedure will tell us we cannot defend the stricter limit on evidential grounds alone. Close by telling me where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot decide. Separate what is settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
