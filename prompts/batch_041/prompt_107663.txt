For my final paper in a metaphysics seminar on fiction and virtual objects, I want to argue that this debate has been proceeding in the wrong order: we keep getting proposals about what virtual objects are and whether virtual harms are real, without any prior agreement about what such a proposal would even have to accomplish. Before I commit to my own account, I need the specification itself. Please write the desiderata for a theory of virtual objects and virtual harms: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

I want the list to be adequate for a specific purpose, so please keep that in view: I need a principled basis for saying whether virtual harms deserve the same moral and legal standing as their real-world counterparts, a lesser standing, or none at all. Requirements that don't bear on that question can be left off.

Work from the following cases and pressures, which are the ones that make me distrust the standard accounts. First, a virtual object can be duplicated exactly and at no cost, and a world can be rolled back or reset; say what that does to any criterion of objecthood and identity over time. Second, a virtual object's continued existence depends on a company's servers, a license agreement, and a business model, and can be ended by a decision its users have no part in. Third, grief for a virtual companion, or for a pet in a game whose world has been deleted along with a save file: real distress, apparently directed at something that some accounts say doesn't exist. Fourth, theft of an item bought with real money, and harassment of an avatar that the user experiences as happening to them. Fifth, users who report bodily presence, startle, and lingering distress, and users for whom the virtual world is the more real one.

Then separate the genuine desiderata from the prejudices of a particular camp, flag any pair of requirements I cannot have jointly, since I suspect the list itself is where the real result lies, and tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidates. If it fits, score the main contenders briefly against the list — the pretense or make-believe approach, the view that virtual objects are abstract artifacts created by their makers, and the view that they are simply ordinary digital objects with causal powers — but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard. I am a student, not a specialist: describe any position you invoke in your own words rather than relying on names, and skip citations and reading lists entirely.
