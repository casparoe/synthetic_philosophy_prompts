I'm in a grad seminar on Quine right now and ive been having this ongoing argument with a friend of mine in the physics department, and its actually a decent test case for the whole analytic/synthetic business, so I want to hand it to you. Here's the setup as I understand it (flag it if I've got any of the details mangled, but I think this is right). For most of the last century the kilogram was just whatever the platinum-iridium cylinder kept outside Paris weighed -- the international prototype -- so 'that cylinder has a mass of exactly one kilogram' wasn't something you could ever discover to be false, it was a stipulation. The kelvin was nailed to the triple point of water at exactly 273.16 K; the mole was pinned to the number of atoms in 12 grams of carbon-12, so 'the molar mass of carbon-12 is exactly 12 g/mol' was true by definition; and the second had already been defined in 1967 by a caesium transition frequency. The metre was the interesting one: in 1960 it was defined by a number of wavelengths of a krypton-86 line, and then in 1983 they switched to defining it as the distance light travels in a fixed fraction of a second, which fixed the speed of light at 299 792 458 m/s and turned 'light travels at 299 792 458 m/s' from a physical postulate into a definition. Then in November 2018 the CGPM (effective May 2019) fixed exact numerical values for the Planck constant (6.62607015×10^-34 J s), the elementary charge (1.602176634×10^-19 C), the Boltzmann constant (1.380649×10^-23 J/K) and the Avogadro constant (6.02214076×10^23 per mole), and let the kilogram, ampere, kelvin and mole fall out of those. So a whole batch of statements changed status at once: the prototype is now a measured object with an uncertainty attached rather than the standard, the triple point of water is no longer exactly 273.16 K, the molar mass of carbon-12 is now only approximately 12 g/mol, and the dalton was left defined off carbon-12, which is where a lot of the grumbling came from.

What I want is for you to treat that whole sequence -- 1960, 1983, 2019 -- as a case study for the analytic/synthetic distinction and for Quine's attack on it. Work out which statements in the old system were analytic in any serious sense and which were merely treated as definitional for convenience, which ones changed status in 2019, and whether anything in the world changed when they did (my instinct is nothing did, which is either exactly the point or a problem, depending). Then run it the other direction, because that's the part I can't settle. Does the episode vindicate Quine, on the grounds that the supposedly unrevisable definitions turned out to be revisable and the choice of what to hold fixed looks like a pragmatic whole-theory decision -- the metrologists went to absurd lengths to make sure no number anybody had ever measured would visibly jump, which is precisely the kind of conservatism Quine says governs revision -- or does it cut against him, since the people doing this seem to have a perfectly clear, unmysterious grip on which statements are stipulations and which are empirical claims about the world, which sounds more Carnap than Quine, with the redefinition looking like a deliberate change of linguistic framework rather than a discovery? And here's the wrinkle I really can't shake: if the metre is now defined as how far light gets in a given time, isn't the definition quietly assuming that light moves at the same speed in every direction -- the very thing the old conventionality-of-simultaneity crowd said you can't test at all, since any test of the one-way speed has to presuppose a synchrony convention? If that's right, then the SI has converted a convention into a truth by definition, and I'd like you to say what that does to the analytic/synthetic distinction rather than just noting it and moving on. I know the standard exposition of the two dogmas, so please don't give me that; I want the mapping worked out in both directions, including an honest account of where Quine's framework has nothing useful to say about what metrologists are actually doing when they do this.
