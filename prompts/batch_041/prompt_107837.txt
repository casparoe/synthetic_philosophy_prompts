Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on aggregation in ethics and social choice, and the audience is not the one I wrote for. Roughly half the room are moral philosophers in a Kantian or constructivist vein: they are already suspicious of interpersonal aggregation and will be reading me to see whether my view actually blocks it or merely relabels it. The other half are formal: two decision theorists who work on representation theorems for social welfare orderings, an economist who works on population axiology, and a game theorist. There is also one historian of philosophy who will want to know what conception of a person my view presupposes, and one medical ethicist who will keep returning to cases. Nobody in the room works on the torture-versus-dust-specks puzzle itself, though all of them know the shape of it: one person's prolonged agony weighed against an astronomically large number of momentary annoyances spread across many people.

My position, briefly. I defend a two-tier axiology. Below the threshold, harms and benefits aggregate in the ordinary additive way and the numbers do all the work. Above it, they do none: a harm is agency-destroying when it destroys the capacities a person needs in order to form, revise, and pursue a conception of her own life over time, and agency-destroying harms are lexically worse than any quantity of non-agency-destroying harms, however large that quantity. The lexical tier is triggered not only by harms that actually destroy agency but by any act that imposes a real probability of one; and I argue that here the probability does not matter, because the tier is fixed by the kind of thing at stake rather than by the odds. My argument for the tier is not an intuition pump. It is that agency-destroying harms are incommensurable with inconveniences, since they remove the very capacity in virtue of which a person can trade goods against one another for herself, so treating the two as commensurable is a category error.

For each objection give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so that I am not defending ground I cannot hold, and which single objection, if I lose it, takes the paper down. Flag any objection on which the two halves of the room will agree, since those are the ones that will hurt. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
