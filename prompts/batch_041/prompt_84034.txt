I run a small prediction-market desk: six analysts, a bankroll that doubles as our operating budget, and between ten and forty new positions a week on questions that resolve anywhere from an hour to a year out. We size positions with a crude version of the Kelly rule plus instinct, and I have concluded that this is worse than having no rule at all. The same nominal edge has produced a four per cent stake on a by-election question and a thirty per cent stake on a central-bank question in the same afternoon, and nothing explained the difference except that the second analyst sounded more certain.

Please write the sizing procedure we should adopt.

It must be a procedure, not an essay: a short sequence of questions answered in order, each with a stated sufficiency condition, an explicit stopping rule that returns a stake of zero, and a terminal rule that returns a recommended stake as a fraction of the bankroll together with a one-line justification an analyst who has never heard of expected log wealth can check. Two analysts given the same inputs should arrive at the same number.

It has to handle four things our current practice handles by shrugging.

1. The edge is estimated, not known. Our probabilities come from a model and from an analyst's judgment, and both are wrong by amounts we could roughly bound. Say how the stake should shrink when the estimate is soft, and whether taking half of what the formula recommends is a principled correction or a superstition. Say also whether the fact that someone is willing to take the other side of our bet should count as evidence against our estimate.

2. Positions are not independent. On election night we may hold eight questions that all resolve the same way. Say what the procedure does about that, and whether the right response is to shrink each stake or to size the group as a single bet.

3. Most of our questions are one-offs; we will never get to run the same bet a hundred times. One colleague argues that this guts the whole apparatus, because the case for maximizing the expected log of the bankroll rests on the same multiplicative process running long enough for the typical outcome to pull away from the average, and a single bet has no typical outcome, only the one we get. Another colleague says this is a distinction without a difference. The procedure has to come down somewhere: say what it is entitled to assume, what it should do when the assumption fails, and what about our situation would settle the question.

4. Our bankroll is also our operating budget, and losing most of it means losing the sponsor and shutting down. Ruin, then, is not a point at the far end of a curve but a cliff at a known height. Say where the procedure gets that height, and what it does differently as the stake approaches it.

The objective must be stated rather than assumed. Say plainly which quantity the procedure maximizes — expected bankroll, expected log bankroll, or the growth rate of the bankroll along the sequence we actually live through — and whether that choice is a fact about our situation or a decision about what we want. If it is a decision, mark it as one and hand it to me instead of burying it inside a formula.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases and show the arithmetic: a liquid market where our model says 0.55 and the market says 0.50, repeated dozens of times a month; a single question resolving after the season where we say 0.65 against a market of 0.50 and where the stake the formula wants would put us near the cliff; and a cluster of six questions that are not independent. At least one of the three must come out with a recommendation I will not like, and you should say so rather than softening it.

Close with a short, unhedged account of what the procedure cannot do, where an analyst will be tempted to use it in place of judgment, and which of its steps are arithmetic wearing the costume of principle — so that we can cut the decoration instead of letting it lend false authority to the rest. The analyst running it has no measure theory, and the whole thing must be runnable in twenty minutes with numbers we can plausibly estimate. Say what to do when the analyst has only a range, or nothing.
