I've been doing this meditation thing for about four months now — twenty minutes a day, extremely boring — and the strangest side effect is that I keep catching myself being wrong about what I'm feeling. Not dramatically. I'll be sitting there sure I'm calm, then notice my jaw is clenched and my stomach is doing something, and have to admit the calm was more like a story I was telling myself. Or I'll think I'm annoyed about a text message and then realize I'm actually anxious about work. Which sent me down a rabbit hole about whether you can be wrong about your own current mental states, and I ended up reading about the anti-luminosity argument (Williamson, I think it's in Knowledge and Its Limits, the 2000 book — I'm going off a half-remembered podcast so correct me if I've got that wrong). I get the basic shape of it: imagine a long slow series of experiences going from clearly cold to clearly hot in tiny steps, each one indistinguishable from the one before; somewhere in there you cross from cold to not-cold, and since you can't tell adjacent cases apart, whatever belief you form near the boundary could easily have been false, and knowledge can't be right by luck, so no belief about your own temperature is guaranteed safe. Fine. But there's two things I actually want explained, and they're what I keep failing to sort out on my own.

First, my friend Sam thinks the whole thing is a trick about grain. He says of course I can be wrong about whether I'm at 37.2 or 37.4 units of felt coldness, but I can't be wrong about whether I'm cold at all, or in pain at all — those are chunky states, and chunky states stay luminous even if fine-grained ones don't. So the argument only kills a version of luminosity nobody wanted anyway. Is he right? I keep feeling like the same boundary problem just reappears at the chunky level — that last step where you're still sort of cold — but I honestly can't tell whether that's a real objection or me just repeating the original argument in a louder voice. And if chunky states do get rescued, I want to know what's doing the rescuing: a buffer of neutral in-between cases, vagueness, the fact that cold is a threshold concept, something else?

Second, Sam also says this is all just the KK principle in a trench coat — same argument, different target. KK says if you know something you know that you know it; the anti-luminosity thing says you can know something without being in a position to know what state you're in; so KK dies as a corollary and we should stop treating them as two separate puzzles. That doesn't sit right with me, because KK is about knowledge iterating and luminosity is about states being knowable, and those seem like claims that could fail independently — but I can't articulate why, or say whether one actually does imply the other, or what extra premise you'd need to get from the cold-to-hot case to KK. So: can you lay out the anti-luminosity argument properly first, then take Sam's two claims one at a time, the grain rescue and the KK-in-a-trench-coat line, and tell me which parts of each are right and where he's definately smuggling something in? I'd rather have the machinery than a verdict, and if the honest answer to either is that it's a live dispute, just say so instead of pretending it's settled.
