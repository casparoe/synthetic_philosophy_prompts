I spent thirty-five years as a chemist and only after retiring did I start reading philosophy properly, so forgive me if I'm reinventing wheels that got invented in 1740. Here's the thing I've landed on, mostly via Hofstadter — the idea that a self isn't a thing but a pattern, a self-referential loop that a brain runs, and that the same pattern can be running, in part, in other brains too. Which means my wife has a low-resolution copy of me running in her, my old students have fragments, and when I die the pattern doesn't just stop, it degrades slowly as the copies degrade. So there's a real sense, not a merely sentimental one, in which some of me persists in other people. I find this genuinely consoling and I've started to suspect that's exactly why I believe it, which is not a good reason.

What I want from you is the case against it, and I want it sorted by how much it should actually move me rather than by how clever it sounds. What would count against this view? Which intuitions, thought experiments, or empirical findings (split brains, dementia, the way people misremember the dead into someone they'd never have recognized, whatever else you've got) push back hardest, and which are just the standard grumbling that any pattern-based theory of mind has to live with anyway? Some of this seems to me obviously true and boring — of course people carry traces of each other, of course I'm changed by my friends — so the interesting question is where the view goes beyond the boring version and thereby risks something. Does it make any prediction at all, or has it been quietly stretched (copies can be partial, degraded, distributed, unconscious, whatever) until nothing could falsify it? If so, is that fatal or is it just the price of a metaphysical view, the way 'the self is a pattern' was always going to be?

And tell me honestly which parts a defender like me would have to concede as counting against us, versus which we'd shrug off — because I can't tell anymore whether my willingness to shrug is conviction or motivated reasoning. Also, if you think the whole framing is confused (I have a nagging feeling that 'how much of me is in my wife' might be a category error rather than a hard question), say so and tell me what the better-posed version of my view would be.
