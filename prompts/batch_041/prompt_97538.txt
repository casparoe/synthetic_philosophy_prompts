I'm a philosophy student in a seminar on medieval Jewish philosophy, and we've just been through Maimonides' account of the sacrificial commandments in the Guide of the Perplexed. His reading, as I understand it, is that animal sacrifice was never commanded for its own sake: it was a concession to a people who lived in a world where every mode of worship took the form of an offering, so the Law took an entrenched practice and redirected it toward God instead of abolishing it outright and asking for something the people could not yet bear. I have to give a short presentation next week and I want to open with a joke. Please write me one that turns on the content of that view rather than on generic religion humor: the punchline should hinge on the gap between what the sacrifices were for and what the people performing them thought they were for, or on the oddity of a command that is simultaneously binding and, on this reading, not really wanted. It should be funny to someone who actually knows the Guide, and it should not depend on any citation, quotation, or appeal to a specific passage.
