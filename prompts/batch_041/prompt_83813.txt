I'm a features writer — I do long pieces mostly about care work, aging and disability, the economics of who looks after whom — and I've somehow ended up in a reading group with a bunch of grad students who've made me actually write my position on care ethics down instead of gesturing at it over drinks. So here's where I've landed, and I want an audit of whether it hangs together, because I honestly can't tell anymore. (1) Care is the fundamental moral phenomenon; principles and rules are at best summaries of caring practice, useful for teaching but not the ground of anything. (2) The capacity to care is a developmental achievement rather than a given — people who were never properly cared for often grow up unable to care, and that's a fact about what happened to them, not a defect of will. (3) Distance doesn't thin out obligation: a stranger's hunger and my own kid's hunger make the same kind of claim on me, and where I do less for the stranger that's a fact about what I can do, not about what I owe. (4) Care ethics is a political theory before it's a personal one — whether anyone can care or be cared for depends on wages, on immigration rules, on who's expected to do the work for free, so the real subject is who a society lets be dependent. (5) I don't think the principle that ought implies can survives contact with care; you can be at fault for failing to care even if, through no fault of your own, you were incapable of it. And (6), a verdict I'm actually quite sure about, which is why its been nagging at me: the hospital administrator who designs a discharge policy that quietly shifts the burden of care onto unpaid family members, mostly women, is doing something wrong even if she never meets any of them and is unfailingly decent to everyone she does meet.

Where I need help is seperating the things that actually pull against each other from the ones that only look like they do. I suspect at least one of these pairs is a fake problem caused by me using the word care in two different registers, and I'd rather be told which is which than have everything hedged. Then rank the real ones by how much they cost me: what exactly has to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what I lose by making it. Please be upfront about which of these commitments are basically settled among people who work on care ethics and which are live disagreements — don't sell me a contested reading as the consensus, I'm a journalist and I'll end up quoting someone.
