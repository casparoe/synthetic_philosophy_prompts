I built the following argument for a graduate seminar and now suspect it proves far too much, yet every premise still looks true to me and I cannot find the joint that gives. I would like you to diagnose it: name the step you would reject — or, if you would keep them all, say so and explain what the conclusion then commits me to — explain why that step looked plausible in the first place, and price the alternatives, i.e. what I have to give up if I take your route rather than some other. Do not simply tell me the conclusion is distasteful; tell me where the reasoning breaks or where it holds and the price is paid.

The argument concerns what is often called the gender-equality paradox: the finding that in countries scoring highest on measures of women's legal, educational, and economic equality — the Nordic countries and other wealthy liberal democracies, typically — men and women are more concentrated in gender-typical fields than in poorer, less equal countries, with engineering and physics skewing male and care work and primary education skewing female. The same pattern appears in self-reported personality traits, and the share of women among STEM graduates falls in the most equal countries even where overall STEM participation is high for both sexes. The question I care about is what this shows about nature, culture, and justice.

(1) Across countries, the more equal men and women are in legal rights, education, and access to paid work, the more they diverge in the fields they choose to study and enter.

(2) If a divergence in chosen fields were produced by inequality — by legal barriers, discrimination, or the expectation that certain work is not for women — then reducing that inequality and equalizing opportunity should reduce the divergence.

(3) Reducing it does not reduce the divergence; the divergence is largest precisely where equality is greatest.

(4) Therefore the divergence is not produced by inequality. It is produced by something that survives equality, namely differences between men and women in interest and aptitude.

(5) What is produced by such differences is natural, and natural differences in interest cannot and should not be engineered away by policy.

(6) Therefore the gender-typical career choices of egalitarian societies are natural, and programs meant to change them are both futile and unjustified.

The conclusion I cannot accept is (6), and (5) also sits uneasily with me, but I trust the cross-national data in (1) and I am drawn to the inference in (4), so I would rather not give either of those up. Where does this go wrong, if it does — and if I have to choose, what exactly am I paying?
