You are a philosopher of cognitive science with a specialism in the timing of conscious experience and in the methodology of adjudicating between rival mechanistic explanations of a single behavioural report. Take your time with what follows, and begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you list every rule you can think of that would fit the record described below, including ones you expect to discard. Start by setting out in your own words the two hypotheses that Daniel Dennett distinguished under the labels Orwellian and Stalinesque: one on which the content a subject reports was assembled before it was ever experienced, so that the subject never had the unedited version; one on which the subject did have the unedited version and the memory of it was subsequently rewritten. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, and I do not need references. I am asking you to reason from a pattern of verdicts to a rule, and from the rule to a prediction.

A colleague of mine in the same department works on this and has a track record I cannot make sense of. Her verdicts, as I would summarise them:

- Colour phi, in which two lights of different colours are flashed a few degrees apart in quick succession and subjects report the first light travelling across to the second and changing colour as it travels, although the second colour only arrives later: she says the colour change and the motion are settled before the content is ever experienced, so the subject never saw the first light as green and stationary.
- Filling in across the blind spot, and across artificially induced scotomata: she says the gap is never experienced.
- Backward masking, where a letter shown briefly is rendered unreportable by a pattern that follows it: she says the subject did experience the letter, and that what is degraded is the later reconstruction.
- Libet-style studies in which subjects report the moment at which they decided to move: she says there is no fact of the matter.
- Change blindness: she says there is no fact of the matter.

Next month she must give a verdict on a case that is new to both of us. A briefly presented scene contains a small region with a sharp discontinuity. A texture that is absent from the scene is reported by subjects as continuing through that region, in the ordinary way. The experimenters, however, can decode the true discontinuity from the recorded neural activity, and they can do so on the very trials on which subjects report the continuous texture: the signal tracks what was actually there rather than what was reported. The discontinuity also leaves a measurable effect on a speeded response that subjects cannot deliberately control. So this looks like a filling-in phenomenon of the first kind, except that there is now independent evidence about the state that the report does not reflect.

What will she say about it? I care much less about the verdict itself than about the following. First, what are the candidate rules that fit her whole track record, including the ones that fit it just as well as my preferred reading of her? Second, what does each of those rules predict about the new case, which of them do you think she is actually running, and why? Third, if the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so plainly and tell me which further experiment would separate the rules that remain. Be explicit about which of her earlier verdicts each candidate rule explains well and which it strains to accommodate.

Lay out the competing rules before you commit to any of them, and give me a best guess with your reasons rather than a menu. Where you find yourself importing a general position about the timing of experience that the track record does not actually support, flag it as an assumption rather than a finding.
