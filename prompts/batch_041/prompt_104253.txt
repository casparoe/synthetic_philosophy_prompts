I teach a final-year undergraduate module on game theory and the evolution of cooperation, and I am setting the exam. I want a single essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the classic iterated-dilemma tournaments and the celebrated properties of tit-for-tat. The crux I want to test is whether students grasp that iterating the prisoner's dilemma does not so much solve the dilemma as replace it with a different game; that the folk theorem tells us cooperation can be sustained rather than that it will be; and that once moves can be misperceived, the logic of reciprocation starts to work against itself. Please write, in British English, a response in three named sections: first, "The question", of about 120 words, phrased so that a student who has merely memorised the standard results is exposed; second, "Model answer", of about 700 words, which takes a definite line rather than surveying the literature; and third, "Marking scheme", worth 25 marks, which names the specific moves a first-class answer must make, the marks each is worth, and the specific errors — for instance, treating the repeated game as simply the same dilemma played many times, or reading the folk theorem as a prediction of cooperation — that should cost marks.
