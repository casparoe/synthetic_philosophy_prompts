My friend Mira and I spent most of last night arguing over two tables of numbers, and by the end neither of us could remember what the other had conceded. I would like you to work through the exchange as it actually went, claim by claim, rather than writing a general essay on the topic. I have tried to set out both sides at full strength, and I have deliberately not said which of us I think came out ahead, because I changed my mind at least twice during the argument.

The first table is from a retrospective review of two regimens for the same infection, at the hospital where I trained. Severity was recorded by the attending physician on admission, before any treatment was given.

Regimen A: mild, 150 of 200 recovered (75%); moderate, 40 of 100 (40%); severe, 10 of 100 (10%); overall, 200 of 400 (50%).

Regimen B: mild, 39 of 50 (78%); moderate, 18 of 40 (45%); severe, 6 of 50 (12%); overall, 63 of 140 (45%).

(1) Mira: A is the better regimen. Overall it saves half its patients, against B's forty-five per cent. Subgroups can be sliced until they say anything; what the hospital observed is what counts.

(2) Me: B is better. It leads in every severity band. A's overall edge exists only because A was given mostly to mild patients — half of A's patients were mild, against roughly a third of B's. The pooled figure is an artifact of who got what, and the comparison within bands is the one that answers the question. If B wins in every band, then B wins, whatever the mix.

(3) Mira: That assumes severity is the only thing that differs between the two groups, and that the difference is a distortion to be corrected rather than a fact about how the regimens are actually used. If the question is what my ward can expect next year, and the patients and the prescribing habits stay as they are, then fifty per cent is the honest answer for A. You are answering a different question and calling mine a mistake.

(4) Me: The reason to care about a regimen is that it can be chosen. Once the choice is real — for the patient in front of me, or for a policy that assigns treatment — what matters is what the drug does, and the pooled comparison is confounded by indication: the sicker patients were steered to B, and being sick is what kills them. A reversal of this kind is the standard fingerprint of confounding, so B is the answer.

(5) Mira: "Fingerprint" is doing a great deal of work. A reversal is neither necessary nor sufficient for confounding. Confounding can be present with no reversal at all: if it is mild, the pooled number is biased without changing sign. And a reversal can be manufactured with no confounding whatever, by slicing on a variable that the treatment itself affects, or on a variable with two independent causes. You cannot read "confounded" off the table. You are importing a causal story; if you may import one, so may I.

(6) Me: Then I will import it openly. In this cohort, assignment was the attending physician's judgment of severity, made before treatment, and nothing else about the patients differed systematically between the arms. Severity is therefore a pre-treatment common cause, and conditioning on it is exactly right. My rule was never "disaggregate whenever the numbers reverse"; it is "disaggregate on pre-treatment variables that the treatment could not have influenced."

(7) Mira: That rule is more modest than what you said in round two, and it does not rescue the sure-thing move. Here is a second table from the same review: a randomized comparison of a drug, X, against placebo. Hospitalized during the trial: placebo, 45 of 150 (30%); X, 14 of 50 (28%). Not hospitalized: placebo, 35 of 50 (70%); X, 102 of 150 (68%). Overall: placebo, 80 of 200 (40%); X, 116 of 200 (58%).

Same shape, opposite lesson. X wins overall and loses in both strata, and here the overall figure is the trustworthy one, because assignment was random and the strata are defined by something that happened after the drug was given. So "trust the band-by-band comparison" is not a rule; it is a coincidence in your first table. No one would call this confounding. And someone will say the whole business is Simpson's paradox — a name that explains nothing, since the arithmetic is identical in the two tables and the conclusions are opposite. What decides is the causal story, and the causal story is not in the numbers.

(8) Me: That cuts both ways. If the reversal is not evidence for my reading, it is not evidence against it either. In the first table the causal story is known, and it favors B.

Please adjudicate. For each numbered move, give a verdict on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification, and where a move is half right, say which half. Then address these specifically: Is my round-two inference — that the pooled figure is an artifact — sound as stated, or does it claim more than the data support? Is Mira's round-three point about which question is being asked a genuine objection or a dodge? Is her round-five claim about necessity and sufficiency correct, and does it bear on the first table? Is my round-six rule a fair statement of my position or a retreat? Is her use of the second table legitimate, and what does that table show about the stratum variable that neither of us said? Who is closer to right about the first table, and on what grounds? And what did we both miss — in particular, anything neither of us raised that would change how the first table should be read? Do not give me the standard verdict on Simpson's paradox; assess the arguments as they were actually made.
