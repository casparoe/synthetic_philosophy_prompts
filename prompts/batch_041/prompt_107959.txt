I sit on the character-and-fitness panel of a state bar's board of law examiners. Our job is to interview applicants whose records raise a question — an old conviction, a finding of academic dishonesty, undisclosed debts, a run of jobs abandoned without explanation — and to recommend admission, conditional admission, or denial. We sit monthly; each panelist carries six to eight files; each applicant gets forty-five minutes and a vote in the same session; the chair signs the recommendation and the full board ratifies it. Our written reasons are disclosed to the applicant and are appealable, so a recommendation that amounts to disliking the applicant is a real liability for us. Treat the substantive standard as ours to state; I am not asking what any particular jurisdiction's rules currently provide.

I want to replace our internal guidance with an actual decision procedure, because I have come to think that most of what the panel does is an unlicensed inference from conduct to character. The folk theory we run on — that a piece of conduct reveals a stable trait, and the trait predicts later conduct — is precisely the theory that the situationist research program in psychology and the philosophical debate over virtue ethics have been undermining for decades. I cannot simply abolish the panel; someone has to make these calls, and a panel that shrugged and admitted everyone would not be doing its job either.

So write the procedure. Start from what virtue ethics actually claims about character, habituation, and practical wisdom, and from what the situationist critique actually establishes as opposed to what it merely insinuates, and then do the translation work: which distinctions have to survive the compression into something a non-philosopher can run, which can be dropped, and where the procedure will misfire. I want the questions in the order a panelist should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns a no, and the points at which it must hand the decision back to a person because what remains is a question of values rather than of fact. I want a test that separates a trait ascription doing explanatory work from one that merely restates the conduct, and something a panelist can do with a single episode where there is no pattern to read. And I want the procedure to say what to do about local traits — the applicant who is scrupulous in one setting and careless in another — because I suspect that is most of what we actually see, and our current guidance has nothing to say about it.

Then run the procedure on three files. First, an applicant who in her second year of law school plagiarized a seminar paper during a term in which she was working nights and caring for an ill parent, and who has since practiced four years without complaint. Second, an applicant with a spotless record — strong references, no discipline, no debts — whose entire history consists of situations in which nothing was at stake. Third, the file where I expect the procedure to tell me something I do not want to hear; choose it yourself, but it should be a case the panel would decide on the basis of the applicant's character in a way the procedure cannot license. For each, give the recommendation the procedure yields, the written reason a panelist could actually defend, and where you had to make the procedure's silence explicit rather than paper over it. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do, and what a panelist should do when the answer it yields is one the panel will not accept.
