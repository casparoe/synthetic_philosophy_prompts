I'm a novelist -- literary sci-fi, mostly -- and I've been stuck for weeks on a structural problem in my new book, which is probably why I let two friends turn a quiet drinks night into a three-hour fight about it. The setup: my protagonist, Mara, exists in three adjacent timelines. In one she's a trauma surgeon in Boston, married, miserable; in another she's a cellist in Vienna, broke, happier; in a third she dies at nineteen in a car crash. The twist is that the surgeon and the cellist both have vivid memories of the other's life -- not as fantasy, as memory, first-person, with the same childhood, same scar on her left knee, same habit of biting her lip when she lies. I wanted the reader to feel that these are the same woman in different worlds, but also that they're seperate people with seperate fates. My friend Dev, who teaches philosophy part-time, says that's just counterpart theory and I should stop fighting it: on the Lewisian picture there's no literal transworld identity, each Mara is a distinct individual, and the relation between them is similarity -- a counterpart relation -- which can be non-transitive, one-to-many, and context-dependent, so the surgeon's counterpart can be the cellist while the cellist's counterpart is someone else entirely, and that's not a contradiction. My other friend Rosa, who writes criticism and has read more Kripke than Dev thinks, says that's a bait-and-switch: if the cellist is only a counterpart, then the surgeon isn't remembering her own life, she's remembering someone else's, and alot of the emotional engine dies; if it's really her life, then we need transworld identity, the same Mara in both worlds, and then we have to say which properties are essential to her -- her body, her origin, her memories, her name -- because otherwise "Mara" is just a label we're slapping on two different people.

Dev said identity across worlds is no more mysterious than identity across time, and we already accept that the Mara of ten years ago is the same person as the Mara now even though almost everything about her has changed. Rosa said that's exactly the problem: if personal identity over time is already a loose, similarity-based, practical relation, then calling it "identity" across worlds doesn't give you the metaphysical sameness the story needs, it just gives you more of the same looseness, and the counterpart theorist wins by equivocation. Dev said she's begging the question by assuming there's a fact of the matter about "same person" that the story has to track, and that maybe the right response is to make the ambiguity the point. Rosa said fine, but then my ending -- where the surgeon sacrifices her career to save the cellist's hands in a surgery that can only happen because their timelines briefly overlap -- is either incoherent or it secretly assumes identity. Dev said the surgeon can care about her counterpart without identity, the way you can care about a sibling or a version of yourself you might have been; Rosa said that's not grief, that's just empathy with a stranger who happens to look like you, and if I want the reader to feel the loss as a loss of self, I need the stronger relation. They both looked at me. I honestly couldn't referee.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step -- Dev, Rosa, or neither -- and then say what each of them is missing about how counterpart theory and transworld identity actually relate to personal identity, memory, and narrative? I don't need citations or reading lists, just the adjudication.
