So I need help writing a short speech, and it's kind of a strange one, so bear with me.

Eleven years ago there was a flash flood in the town I grew up in. I was nineteen and I was out on the wrong side of the creek, up on the top rail of a flooded fence, and maybe two hundred yards the other way, on the roof of a station wagon that was still above water, there was a family — two parents and their two kids, the little one was six. The volunteer rescue boat could reach one spot and then the road would be under water, not both. The guy driving, Dale, had been doing it for thirty years, and when the rulebook ran out he had this habit: he flipped a quarter. It came up for me. They pulled me off the fence, and by the time anybody could have gotten around, the water was over the wagon. All four of them drowned.

The town does a little thing by the creek every year on the anniversary, and this year they've asked me to say a few words, I guess because I'm the one who's still here. Front row is the family's people — the mother's sister, the grandfather, the aunt who raised the older boy. Behind them are the crew, half of whom I've known my whole life and a few of whom have told me straight out that the quarter was a cop-out, that Dale should have gone for the four, because four. And the company is rewriting its policy this year on calls where you can get to one or the other but not both, so whatever I say is going to get read as a position on that. I dont want to turn a memorial into a policy meeting, but I also don't want to say nothing.

Here's the part I actually need help with. There's an argument I read in college, some ethics paper, I couldn't tell you who wrote it or what it was called, but it stuck. It said that when you can save either one person or several, the several don't have a stronger claim just because there are more of them. Each person has one claim, and claims don't stack like that — five people aren't one person times five. The number of them is a fact about the world, not a reason. And the upshot was that if you can't save everybody, the honest thing is to give everyone an equal shot — a coin, a lottery, something that doesn't decide by arithmetic — and then live with what comes up. Which sounds insane said out loud, and I remember the paper knew that, and the objection it had to get past is the one I can't answer: if you flip a coin, aren't you saying four dead is as acceptable as one dead? That's what the grandfather is going to be thinking. Honestly it's what I think at 3am, alot.

So: write me the speech. Five or six minutes, call it 850 words, American English, no jokes. The argument has to be the spine of it, not a paragraph I drop in and move past.

Things I want it to do, and things I don't want it to do:

- Hold onto a distinction I think is the whole thing: "every life matters the same" is not the same claim as "everybody's claim on me was the same size." I want it to say the second thing without ever sounding like it's denying the first.
- Face the coin question head-on. If there's no clean answer, let it not have a clean answer. I would rather these people hear "I don't know" than get a tidy ending.
- Don't let me confess, don't let me ask anyone for forgiveness, don't let me say I shouldn't be here, and don't let me call myself lucky. All four of those are ways of making it about me.
- Don't use the word "triage" and don't use the word "utilitarian." The crew will hear the first as an insult to their training and the families will hear the second as me calling the crew monsters.
- No quotes, no authors, no titles, no books. It can gesture at "a paper I read once" — nothing anybody could check, because I'd get it wrong.
- It has to land for the grandfather and the aunt and the lady from the hardware store who's never read a word of philosophy, and it can't be condescending to the two people in the back who have (Dale's daughter teaches philosophy at the community college, she'll be there).
- Start with something small and specific — the fence, the quarter — not with a thesis. No epigraphs.
- If it can quietly be a word about the policy without ever mentioning the policy, do that. But it can't be the point.
- Don't make me out to be brave or humble, and don't end on "and that's how I learned to live every day," or anything like it.
- If a line would hurt the families, leave it out, even if it's true.
