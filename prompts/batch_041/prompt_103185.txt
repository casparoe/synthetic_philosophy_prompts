For the past several terms I have taught a graduate seminar on Marx and the philosophy of technology, and each year the same popular book on automation gets cited back to me as authoritative. Its treatment of Marx runs about as follows:

"Marx is the true origin of the automation debate. In the Grundrisse notebooks he notices that as machinery advances, real wealth comes to depend less on the labour time directly expended and more on the general state of science and technology — what he calls the general intellect — and he concludes that once things have gone that far, labour time can no longer be the measure of value, so that the capitalist mode of production has abolished its own foundation and must give way to a society of free time. In Capital he adds that machinery degrades the worker, prolongs the working day and swells the reserve army of the unemployed. And since Marx holds that the productive forces inevitably outgrow the relations of production, the arrival of socialism can be read off the advance of technology itself, with no need for politics. This is why he sympathized with the machine-breakers; and it is why today's arguments about automation, basic income and a post-work society are all, at bottom, footnotes to Marx."

I have my suspicions about this passage, but my time has gone into Marx's economics rather than into the exegetical fine points, and I am unwilling to correct my students on the strength of a hunch. Please audit it for me. Go claim by claim — or sentence by sentence, if that is cleaner — and sort the claims into three piles: what a careful reader of Marx would simply accept; what is a defensible simplification that a sympathetic expositor might be allowed; and what is a misrepresentation. In the third pile I would especially like you to flag three recurring species of error: places where two things Marx takes pains to keep apart have been fused; places where a diagnosis of a tendency internal to capital has been quietly converted into a prediction of its supersession; and places where a phrase of Marx's has been handed over to a later school of interpretation that reads its own programme back into him. Where you find a misrepresentation, say what a defender of Marx would object to and whether the objection is itself sound, rather than merely noting that partisans would complain.

Two further things. First, the book's larger argument is that Marx either foresaw or failed to foresee our present automation predicament, and that the question of his accuracy can be settled by comparing his text with current trends; tell me whether that framing survives once the passage has been corrected, or whether the corrections leave the argument standing. Second, give me a corrected version of the two or three sentences you judge most misleading — a fair statement of the position, in the same register as the original — so that I can put something accurate in front of the seminar without assigning the whole book.
