I have no academic background in philosophy or in design, but for the last few months I have been reading about a Viennese social scientist and philosopher named Otto Neurath, who from the mid-1920s onward tried to build a genuinely pictorial language. As I understand it, the idea was a system of standardized, abstracted pictograms and charts — repeated identical figures rather than differently sized ones, so that a reader could count instead of eyeballing areas — meant to convey social and economic facts to people who shared no common written language, and to do it in a way that ordinary prose supposedly could not: without the distortions, the vagueness and the metaphysical baggage of words. He ran a museum of society and economy for a workers' movement in Vienna, worked with an artist who drew the symbols and a so-called transformer whose job was to turn raw statistics into sketches for a chart, liked to say that it is better to remember a simplified picture than to forget an accurate figure, and after being forced out of Austria in 1934 kept the project going in exile under a new name. What actually grips me is that he himself appears to have admitted the thing could never be a full language — he described it as something like a language-like technique, a helper for speech rather than a replacement for it. I keep wanting to know exactly where the wall was.

So I would like you to write a short story that puts that wall at the centre of its plot rather than in the background. Set it in a fictional but period-flavoured version of such a project: a municipal statistics office or an international institute for visual education in a port city in the mid-1930s, with a director who is a true believer, a small team of draftsmen cutting symbols out of linoleum, a sponsor who wants charts that will travel abroad and be understood by foreigners, and one junior employee whose whole job is translating tables of numbers into pictures. The plot should turn on a piece of information the pictures seem unable to hold — say the difference between a zero and an unknown, or something that did not happen (a housing scheme that was proposed and never built, an epidemic that was averted), or the claim that one thing caused another, or a figure everyone privately knows to be wrong. Please do not let the characters settle the matter by delivering speeches at the end; I want the difficulty to surface in scenes, in arguments over one particular chart, in what the drawings can and cannot be made to say, and in whatever the team ends up smuggling in from ordinary writing in order to say it. You may invent any people and events you like, nothing has to correspond to real history, and you should not need to cite anything — but the story should be faithful to the kind of problem that this project actually ran into, and it should work as fiction rather than as a disguised essay.
