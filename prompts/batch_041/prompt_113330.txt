**Question (20 points).** A family of proposals for making advanced AI systems safer aims to penalize side effects. On these proposals, the system is scored on how far the trajectory it actually produces departs from a baseline trajectory — typically what would have happened had the system done nothing, or had it followed some default policy — and it is penalized for the departure. One influential version of the idea, relative reachability, measures the extent to which the agent's ability to achieve its goals has been reduced relative to the baseline, and penalizes actions that make some of the agent's possible goals harder to reach. The attraction of the family is that it appears to require no specification of what the designers value: the measure is supposed to be neutral among values, so that an agent can be made cautious without first solving the problem of specifying the good.

Assess this claim. Can avoiding side effects be defined without a full specification of value? In your answer, distinguish the sense in which such a measure is value-neutral from the sense in which it is not, explain what the measure can and cannot be expected to accomplish, and say whether it can serve as a hard safety constraint.

**Student's answer.** The proposal is best understood against the alternative of writing down a complete objective function and then constraining the agent to respect it. An impact measure does not attempt that. It asks how far the world has moved from a baseline — normally the trajectory that would have occurred had the agent done nothing — and penalizes movement that the task does not require. Relative reachability measures the extent to which the agent's ability to achieve its goals has been reduced: actions that make some of the agent's possible goals harder to reach are penalized, relative to the baseline. This is an important advance, because it shows that one can define a penalty for disruption without first listing what is valuable. Because the measure is defined in terms of the agent's own goals rather than a substantive ranking of outcomes, it is agent-neutral: the same measure applies to any agent, whatever its goals, and no agent's goals are privileged over any other's. It can therefore be adopted by designers who disagree about value.

The choice of baseline has an obvious cost. An agent that does nothing scores perfectly on the measure while accomplishing nothing, so the measure has to be traded off against task performance rather than treated as an absolute constraint. The baseline itself is unproblematic, since we can simply let the system idle and observe what happens. A more serious point is that the measure cannot substitute for a specification of value. It tells the agent to preserve its own options; it does not tell the agent which options matter. An agent that preserves its reachability while ignoring the welfare of others may have a low impact score and still be doing something seriously wrong, because the measure registers only effects on the agent's own goal-achieving capacity. The choice to count only the agent's own reachability, and the choice of the inaction baseline, are themselves substantive normative choices.

Even so, I think the measure can do real safety work. Because a low-impact agent is penalized for changing the world, it will not seize resources or resist shutdown: both are large departures from the inaction baseline, and so both are ruled out by the measure itself. This is why low-impact measures are often described as a route to corrigibility. The remaining questions are engineering questions: how to compute the measure accurately, and how to weight it against task performance.

The measure is therefore value-neutral in the sense that matters most for safety: it can be specified and implemented without deciding what is good.
