I'm in a weird spot with my PhD advisor and I need help modelling her before my next supervision meeting. She works on premodern societies and has strong, idiosyncratic views about when it's ok to say a society 'collapsed' — she's basically allergic to the word and keeps insisting it hides four or five completely seperate claims. Her verdicts so far, as best I can reconstruct them from three years of seminar rants: the eastern Mediterranean Bronze Age around 1200 BC is a real collapse, and she doesn't care much about the Sea Peoples question — what matters to her is that the palace economies, Linear B and the long-distance trade system all went and didn't come back at anything like that level of complexity for centuries; the Classic Maya lowlands are *not* a collapse in her sense, even though she'll happily grant the demographic and political catastrophe was enormous, because for her the northern centres and the Postclassic show the same tradition continuing, so it's a reconfiguration with a horrifying body count; Easter Island is where she gets genuinely annoyed, because she thinks the ecocide story is mostly a projection of Western anxieties and that what actually happened is a society losing its monument-building elite and then carrying on, which is again not collapse; the western Roman Empire she treats as the collapse of a state but not of a society, pointing at continuity in North Africa and Gaul and saying the empire was a political shell that got swapped out, not a civilization that ended; Norse Greenland she calls an unambiguous collapse — the settlements are gone, the colony is gone, there's nobody left to reconfigure anything; and she flatly refuses to call the Spanish conquest of the Andes or of Mexico a collapse, since for her that's destruction from outside, a different category entirely, and lumping it in with endogenous breakdown is exactly the sloppiness she's trying to avoid. I can usually guess her, but I've stopped being able to, and I suspect her list is doing more work than she thinks.

Two cases are coming up that I genuinely can't predict her on. First, the Ancestral Puebloan world — Chaco largely emptied by the twelfth century, Mesa Verde and the surrounding cliff towns abandoned by around 1300, no state anywhere in the picture, and the descendants very much still around in the Rio Grande pueblos and at Hopi and Zuni. Second, the Soviet Union in 1991: an empire, an official ideology and a command economy all gone in about four years, while the language, the literature, the bureaucracy and nearly all the people stayed put. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of history and a conceptual historian here — someone who thinks about what our categories of social breakdown are actually tracking. Please start by brainstorming the small set of rules that would generate her existing verdicts, then work through: which of those rules are genuinely distinct from each other and which are the same rule in different clothes; what each surviving rule predicts for Chaco/Mesa Verde and for 1991; which rule you think she's actually running, and why that one rather than its rivals; and which single further case would be the cleanest test between the candidates that are left. I care less about getting *her* right than about the structure underneath, so if two of her verdicts turn out to be in tension, say so plainly, and if the honest answer is that the list is underdetermined and she'll just do whatever her priors say on the day, tell me that instead — and then sketch what a better formulation would look like. Think carefully and lay out the candidate rules before you commit to any of them; I don't want a survey of the collapse literature, I want the rules spelled out and the predictions actually made.
