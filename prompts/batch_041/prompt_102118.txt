I'm working through Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France and have gotten stuck on a passage that is rarely quoted, because it concerns a squabble over London societies rather than the French constitution itself. Burke is discussing the Constitutional Society, which he says circulated books at its members' expense, and then he writes:

"The institution of this society appears to be of a charitable and so far of a laudable nature; it was intended for the circulation, at the expense of the members, of many books which few others would be at the expense of buying, and which might lie on the hands of the booksellers, to the great loss of an useful body of men. Whether the books, so charitably circulated, were ever as charitably read is more than I know. Possibly several of them have been exported to France and, like goods not in request here, may with you have found a market. I have heard much talk of the lights to be drawn from books that are sent from hence. What improvements they have had in their passage (as it is said some liquors are meliorated by crossing the sea) I cannot tell; but I never heard a man of common judgment or the least degree of information speak a word in praise of the greater part of the publications circulated by that society, nor have their proceedings been accounted, except by some of themselves, as of any serious consequence."

I would like help with three things, and I would prefer a close reading to a summary of what the Reflections is "about."

First, the rhetoric. What is the tone of "charitable" and of "as charitably read" — is the joke at the expense of the society, of the books, of the French, or of the very idea that a book can do political work? What is the commercial metaphor doing: books as stock that "might lie on the hands of the booksellers," books as goods not in request at home that happen to find a market abroad? Is Burke deliberately redescribing intellectual influence as the dumping of surplus inventory, and if so, what does that redescription accomplish that a straightforward dismissal would not?

Second, the puzzle this creates for the standard readings of Burke. He is usually treated as a founding witness for the view that the Revolution was made by men of letters and a philosophical cabal — that doctrine did the work. Yet here the vehicle of that doctrine is remaindered rubbish whose circulation he treats as a non-event, and the "lights" drawn from books become a shipment whose improvement in transit he cannot verify. If books are just goods, and their effect on France is a joke, what has become of the thesis that ideas caused the Revolution? Does the passage commit him to something closer to an interest-based or class-based explanation, on which the ideas are epiphenomenal and what matters is who holds property and credit; or is he merely refusing to grant his opponents the dignity of having had a doctrine at all? Say which reading the passage actually supports, and where the text resists both.

Third, the possibility that the joke cuts against Burke himself. He is writing, at great length, a book meant to change minds about France. If exported books do not meliorate, why should his own? Is there a coherent Burkean answer here — something about the difference between writing addressed to a settled constitution and writing addressed to a dissolving one, or about opinion as cause versus opinion as symptom — or does he simply help himself to an exemption?

I would also like the distinction between "lights" and "goods" pressed. The Enlightenment trope of light implies something that propagates by its own nature and cannot be stopped by distance or by custom, whereas a commodity's fate depends on demand. Which of the two does Burke think ideas are, and does he think the Revolution shows what happens when a society treats the first as though it were the second, or the reverse?

Keep the answer close to the passage. I do not need a general account of Burke's thought, and I do not need citations or further reading. Where the passage is genuinely ambiguous, say so and give the competing construals rather than collapsing them into a single position.
