I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on distributive justice and have been unable to settle an argument that a student reconstructed for me last term. I would be grateful for your diagnosis. I would rather keep the levelling-down objection than accept the conclusion the argument reaches, but I want to know what that preference costs.

By way of setup: an outcome is a distribution of welfare across persons. Egalitarianism treats inequality between persons as intrinsically bad, so that a more equal outcome is, other things equal, better. Prioritarianism treats a gain in welfare as mattering more the worse off its recipient is; equivalently, the value of an outcome is the sum of welfare levels weighted by factors that decline as the level rises. The levelling-down objection is standardly pressed against egalitarianism: because egalitarianism treats inequality as intrinsically bad, it must count an outcome in which the better off are lowered to the level of the worse off, with nobody gaining, as in one respect an improvement — worse for one person, better for nobody, yet better. Prioritarianism is generally thought to escape this objection, since priority to the worse off supplies no reason to lower anyone when no one is helped.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) A view escapes the levelling-down objection only if it may rank the levelled-down outcome as worse than the unequal one.
(2) The only ground available for that ranking is that the levelled-down outcome is worse for the person lowered and better for no one — call this principle (P).
(3) (P) is a special case of a broader principle: an outcome is better than another only if it is better for at least one person — call this (B). If it is better for no one, it is not better.
(4) (B) is unrestricted in scope: it ranges over any two outcomes, including outcomes containing different people.
(5) Now compare W1, which contains a single person, Anna, at welfare level 20, with W2, which contains Anna at 20 and Bob at 20, Bob's life being well worth living and Anna wholly unaffected. W2 is not better for Anna. Nor is it better for Bob, since Bob does not exist in W1, and one cannot compare how well off a person is across a world in which that person has no existence.
(6) By (B), then, W2 is not better than W1; by parity, W1 is not better than W2; so, assuming the two are comparable, they are equally good.
(7) The same reasoning iterates: a world containing one flourishing person is exactly as good as a world containing that person together with any finite number of further flourishing people.
(8) That is absurd — call it the Sterility Result.
(9) So (B) is false.
(10) So the ground identified in (2) is unavailable, and the prioritarian is left merely asserting her weighting function against the egalitarian's, which is the point at issue.
(11) So prioritarianism does not escape the levelling-down objection; the objection stands against any view that will not rank a levelled-down outcome as worse.

Please tell me which step to reject, why it looked acceptable, and what each available rejection would cost. I want to know what each rejection leaves standing: whether the prioritarian keeps her escape, and whether the levelling-down objection retains any force against egalitarianism, which is where it is usually deployed.
