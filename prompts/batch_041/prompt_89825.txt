Hey, so I'm a science journalist — I write a column on ideas, and for the summer I'm doing a series where instead of the usual 1,200-word explainer I commision a short story that actually dramatizes a concept instead of spelling it out. The good robot fiction does this: the ethics bleed out of the plot, nobody stops to lecture you. That's the vibe I'm after.

The concept I'm stuck on is the St. Petersburg paradox. Quick version so we're on the same page: you flip a fair coin until it comes up heads; if heads first shows on flip n, you win 2 to the n. The expected payout is infinite — 1 + 1 + 1 + ... forever — but almost nobody will pay more than a few bucks to play. So the math says the bet is worth everything and the gut says it's worth pocket change.

Here's the thing though: I don't want the stock casino version, everyone's seen that one. I want you to build the story around a twist I've been turning over. The house doesn't run the game for one player at a time. It runs it for a whole room at once, and they all win or lose on the same sequence of flips — every player in the room is riding the identical coin. The owner's entire business model rests on a comforting thought: he collects a pile of entry fees up front, and over enough players the law of large numbers smooths the payouts into something predictable. I want the story to live in the gap between that assumption and what actually happens when the outcomes aren't independent at all, when one unlucky streak takes out everybody at the same time.

Some things I'd like:
- Let a character be the one who spots the flaw, but don't have them deliver a seminar about it — let the realization land through the plot, a spreadsheet, a late-night argument in the back office, whatever.
- The paradox should be load-bearing. If you swapped the game out for any old gambling scene the story should fall apart.
- Don't end with a tidy moral about expected utility. I'd rather it end on the human cost of trusting an average.
- 1,500 to 2,500 words, third person, and honestly feel free to make the owner a bit of a true believer — the most interesting version is one who isn't stupid, just certain.

Also — no need to cite anything or gesture at the literature, I just want the fiction. Make it feel like it could happen in a real town with a real license and a real regulator who definately should have asked more questions.
