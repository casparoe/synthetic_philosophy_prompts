For a graduate seminar in the philosophy of science I have ninety minutes on Neurath's picture language — the Vienna Method, later Isotype, with its fixed quantity per pictogram, greater quantities shown by more identical figures rather than one larger figure, and no perspective — which my students will know, if at all, only as the ancestor of the infographic. I want the session organized around a tension I have come to think is the whole point of the topic and cannot yet stage: Neurath the holist and anti-foundationalist, for whom no statement is secure and every claim is answerable to the rest of the system, is also the man who spent his last decades designing a notation meant to be read off at a glance by readers of any language — though he himself, tellingly, would call it only a language-like technique rather than a language. Tell me how to teach that tension: what has to be in place before it can even be felt, and in what order the pieces depend on one another; which single chart should carry the session, and what it must have and lack to do so; and the misconceptions to expect, with exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. I anticipate "pictures are more natural than words" and "this is data visualization avant la lettre," but I am more worried that they will read him as simply contradicting himself, or will fold him into Wittgenstein's picture theory of the proposition, which I want kept distinct. Give me one closing question or task that would show whether they have grasped the tension rather than the vocabulary, and say what I should refuse to cover in ninety minutes.
