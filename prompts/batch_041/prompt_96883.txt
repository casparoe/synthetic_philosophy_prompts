Here's the situation. The frequentist vs Bayesian fight has been going for like a century and it feels totally stalled to me. Same math, same data, nobody moves. And lately the replication crisis has gotten dragged into it — Bayesians say, look, p-values are the problem; frequentists say, no, you just did bad frequentist stats, and anyway your priors are doing the same fudge with less accountability; and around and around.

But here's the specific thing that made me think the whole debate might be misframed. In my reporting I keep hitting the stopping-rule thing. You run a study, you collect data until p < .05, you land on p = .04, and a frequentist referee says the p-value is meaningless because you would have stopped at some other point if the data had come out differently — the answer depends on outcomes that never happened. Then a Bayesian says, no, the evidence in the data you actually got is exactly the same either way; your stopping plan shouldn't matter at all. And they just talk past each other forever. Both sides agree the data are identical, so it's not a factual fight about what happened.

So my hypothesis — and please treat it as a hypothesis to test, not something to nod along with — is that this isn't really a fight about probability meaning long-run frequency vs degree of belief. I think it might be a disagreement about what an inference is even allowed to depend on: whether the sample space, the stuff you didn't observe, is part of the evidence or not. And maybe underneath that, a disagreement about what science is supposed to deliver — guaranteed error rates over the long run vs coherent updating of belief given a model you already accept.

What I want from you: diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Verbal, substantive, two sides answering different questions under one name, or bedrock? Pin down exactly where the two sides' actual claims diverge, using the stopping-rule case concretely rather than in the abstract. Then the honest part — given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? A theorem, a distinction, an experiment (like, do we have any real evidence that one framework's methods replicate better in practice?), or is this one of those things where no amount of argument will ever settle it and we should stop expecting it to?
