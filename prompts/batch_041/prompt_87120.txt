I am a novelist at work on a book set in a mill town in the 1890s, and in the course of getting the period right I have been reading in historical materialism. A friend whose Marxism is more orthodox than mine has left me with a difficulty I cannot resolve. His position is that the economic base determines the superstructure, and that this leaves no room for the superstructure to act back upon the base: the revival meeting, the temperance league, and the nationalist newspaper in my town are, on the strict view, mere reflections of the relations of production, effects that cause nothing in turn. He concedes that Engels, late in life, wrote letters denying that the economic factor is the sole cause, but he regards those letters as a retreat from the doctrine rather than a clarification of it. If he is right, then a novel that lets a preacher's sermons change what the mill hands do is not merely historically naive but conceptually confused, since it grants causal power to ideas and so lapses into idealism. Yet the whole plot turns on precisely that: a sermon that moves people to act. So I would like to know whether historical materialism is in fact committed to the superstructure's having no reciprocal effect on the base, and if it is, what a novelist is to do. Must I treat my characters' beliefs as symptoms rather than causes, or is there a defensible reading on which ideas can be real causes without my having abandoned materialism? Please answer as directly as you can, and tell me how confident you are in your reading and which part of it you are least sure of.
