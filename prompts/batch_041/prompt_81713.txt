I'm partway through a final paper for a seminar on the archaeology of complex societies, and I've run into a conceptual snag that I'd like you to help me work through rather than resolve by pointing me toward more reading.

The course treats three episodes as paradigm cases of societal collapse: the breakdown of the palatial systems of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean and Near East, the Terminal Classic abandonment of the great southern Maya lowland centres, and the transformation of Rapa Nui once the monumental statue-building era ended. In each case I can specify what came to an end -- the palaces and their redistributive administrations, the institution of divine kingship together with the monuments and calendars that served it, the statue-building tradition itself -- but in none of the three can I say that a population ended or a culture vanished, and in two of them the descendants are very much present, with living languages and ongoing claims on the sites. The same museums and heritage institutions that narrate these collapses also exhibit those descendants as continuous with the people whose civilization is said to have collapsed. That coexistence looks to me like more than a rhetorical slip, and I want to know what the practice of calling these episodes collapses is committed to.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory. Does calling an episode a collapse presuppose a picture of a society as a bounded, integrated system with a normal mode of functioning from which it can fall away -- and if so, how is that baseline fixed without begging the question, given that the archaeological record gives us windows rather than a continuous film? Does it presuppose that the loss of central institutions is what collapse consists in, and therefore that the relevant standpoint is the elite's or the institution's rather than the ordinary inhabitant's? If the same decades can be a catastrophe for a palace scribe and something close to a liberation for a village farmer, is collapse an observer-relative category rather than a factual one, and does that make the concept useless or merely evaluative?

I have a half-formed criterion of my own that I'd like you to assess and, where you can, sharpen or break. A society collapses, in the interesting sense, not when its institutions change but when it loses the capacity to choose what comes next -- when the change is something that happens to it rather than something it does. That seems to fit the Late Bronze Age and Rapa Nui better than criteria based on complexity or population size, and it lets me say that a society can reorganize itself radically without collapsing, while a much smaller shock counts as a collapse if it forecloses the options. But I suspect it smuggles in a notion of collective agency these societies may never have had, that it makes collapse a matter of degree rather than of kind, and that a population which survives entirely intact but with no options left would come out as a collapse on my criterion even though nothing about its way of life was lost. Tell me whether the criterion can be repaired, and what it costs to repair it.

Finally, say what follows for the practice itself. If the presuppositions are roughly what I've described, should the museums and textbooks drop the word? Would replacing collapse with transformation or reorganization lose something we need -- our ability to say that something was genuinely destroyed rather than merely rearranged, or to assign responsibility for it? And where the practice is underdetermined, tell me which rival accounts of society it is compatible with, so that I can see which parts of the story are load-bearing and which are just convention.
