I am setting the final examination for an upper-level seminar in political philosophy whose last third covers distributive justice, the family, and equality of opportunity. I want a single long essay question on inheritance, dynastic wealth, and equal opportunity, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Students have ninety minutes for the question and see only the question itself, so all case material must be stated inside it. Assume no internet access, and do not let the question or the model answer depend on named authors, named doctrines, or specific empirical findings: the point is to test reasoning about a case, not recall of a literature. Do not ask for citations, readings, or sources of any kind.

The question should turn on a legislative choice between two designs for a tax on wealth transfers. Set them out in your own words, along these lines.

Design A, a receipts tax. Every person pays tax on the running lifetime total of gifts and inheritances they receive. Marginal rates rise with that running total; a first slice is exempt; the tax is owed by the recipient rather than by the estate of the deceased. Because the exemption attaches to the recipient, a family with four children can pass on four exemptions.

Design B, a receipts tax with a lineage premium. As in Design A, except that the rate charged on a particular asset also rises with the number of times that asset has already passed by gift or inheritance. Wealth that has already been inherited twice is taxed more heavily on its third transfer than newly created wealth of equal value given for the first time, even where the two recipients are otherwise identically placed.

The question must make candidates do at least the following: judge which design, if either, a principle of equal opportunity supports, and say what work the principle is being asked to do in each case; consider whether the lineage premium of Design B can be given a rationale that is about opportunity at all, and if it cannot, say what kind of value would have to carry the justification and what that implies for anyone who wants to defend the scheme as an egalitarian measure; and bring out what Design A's treatment of the family, namely the per-recipient exemption and the fact that the donor's act of giving goes untaxed, reveals about whether the operative ideal is equality of opportunity or something adjacent to it. A strong answer will also have to say something about the family's claim to partiality and about whether the operative ideal is comparative, and it should not treat the matter as settled by the familiar question of whether inheritances are earned.

Then write a model answer of roughly 650 words, such as a strong candidate could produce in the time, and a marking scheme for it. The marking scheme should list the specific moves a top script must make, assign marks to each, total them to 20, and give for every point a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. At least one required move should force the candidate to confront the strongest objection to whichever design they favour. End with an explicit statement of the line between an A and a B script: the two or three moves that must be present for a script to reach the top band. The model answer should contain no citations and should refer to positions by description rather than by name.
