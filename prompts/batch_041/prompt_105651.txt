I'm a high school senior writing a paper on hard choices, and I've built an argument that I can't seem to break but that I'm fairly sure proves far too much. I'd like you to tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it would cost.

Some choices are hard because the options seem to be values that can't be weighed on a single scale. This summer I can either stay home and care for my grandmother as she declines, or take an unpaid internship in another city that could shape the rest of my life. The two options look incommensurable to me: neither is plainly better than the other, and they aren't equal either, because there is no common measure on which to rank them.

(1) If two options are incommensurable, then no comparison of their value can rank one above the other.
(2) If no comparison can rank them, then no reason can favor either one, since any reason that favored one would just be a ranking in disguise.
(3) A choice is rational only if some reason favors the option chosen.
(4) So when two options are incommensurable, no choice between them is rational.
(5) If no choice is rational, then every choice is on a par: it is no more reasonable to protect what I love than to throw it away, and no one can be faulted for choosing the trivial over the precious.
(6) But we do fault and praise people for just these choices. We think someone who discards something precious for something slight has done wrong, and someone who gives up a great deal for something greater may be admirable.
(7) So either nothing is ever genuinely incommensurable, or incommensurability makes no difference to how we should choose.

Where does this go wrong? I would rather give up (1) than (6), but I want to know what I'd be paying. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write an email — and in American English.
