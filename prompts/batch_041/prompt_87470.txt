I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, midway through a chapter on the timing of experience, and the section I keep rewriting is the one on the color phi phenomenon. I have the paradigm and Dennett's use of it in front of me, so I don't need them described back to me: a spot is flashed at one location, a differently colored spot is flashed at a nearby location a fraction of a second later, and within a certain range of intervals and separations subjects report a single spot that travels between the two positions and changes color partway across — that is, the change is reported as occurring before the second spot's color could have been registered. Dennett's claim is that our explanatory instincts here come in exactly two shapes, and that they are not so much rival hypotheses as two ways of describing the same brain events from either side of a boundary whose existence he denies: either a memory that was briefly accurate is retrospectively rewritten, or the discordant information is reconciled before it ever reaches experience, so that nothing is ever experienced wrongly. What I want is not an exposition of that argument — I can recite it — but a case analysis of the phenomenon and of the long disagreement it has generated, run in both directions and disciplined about what is actually at stake.

So fix the mapping before anything else. Who or what occupies the role of the reviser in each story, what precisely is the explanandum — the subject's report, the subject's experience, or the timing of a content — and what would count as evidence bearing on it from the outside? Then say what the multiple drafts picture genuinely explains about the case and what it merely accommodates after the fact: why the reported change lands where it does along the path rather than at either endpoint, why the effect has the dependence on interval and separation that it has, and why the subject's inability to distinguish the two stories from the inside is supposed to be a point in the theory's favor rather than a confession that the case cannot discriminate between them. I want to know where the theory is silent, not where it can be made to speak.

Here is the part I most want pressure on. I can distinguish two readings of the idea that an experience was revised, and they seem to me to cut across Dennett's dichotomy rather than to reproduce it. On the first, the content is fixed late but includes its own temporal index, so the experience really does present the change as having happened mid-flight; on the second, the content is fixed early and what is late is only the subject's account of when things happened, so there is no moment of experience that misplaces anything. Tell me whether the color phi paradigm can in principle separate these, and if it cannot, whether the reason is the one Dennett gives for the emptiness of his own distinction or a different and weaker one that leaves his conclusion under-supported. Then turn the analysis around: if the case cannot discriminate, is it evidence for multiple drafts at all, or only something the model can redescribe once it has been stated — and could a more traditional account of temporal experience accommodate the same data just as comfortably? A friend in my cohort who works on predictive processing insists the puzzle simply dissolves once one stops asking when a percept was assembled; I suspect that is Dennett's point restated in other vocabulary, and I would like to be told whether that suspicion is fair or whether the two positions come apart on this case. Please give me the argument rather than the literature: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I cannot check anything right now, and what I need is the reasoning and an honest verdict on how much the color phi phenomenon can actually carry.
