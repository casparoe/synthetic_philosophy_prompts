I'm teaching a seminar on time in literature and next week is Borges. Give me a reading of "The Garden of Forking Paths" through the philosophy of time -- McTaggart's A and B series, presentism vs eternalism, and the standard branching-time semantics where there's one actual history and a tree of unrealised possibilities hanging off it. The story doesn't fit that last one: Ts'ui Pên's novel seems to need every branch to be equally real, which is a much stranger claim and maybe not coherent at all. It also sits badly with Borges's own essay where he argues that time is unreal and that succession is a confusion we project onto a series of independent states -- if there's no time there's nothing to branch. And then there's the third strand, his early fondness for Nietzsche's eternal return, which gives you cycles rather than branches. Three incompatible pictures from one writer. Tell me which one the story actually dramatises, whether any reconciliation is available, what the story adds that the metaphysics can't (I suspect the consolations is the point -- that every choice gets made and nobody's possibility is wasted), and where a philosopher of time should say the story is cheating. It's grad students, so don't slow down. Also say how confident you are in the reading and single out the bit you're least sure of.
