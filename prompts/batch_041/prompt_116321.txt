You are a philosopher of well-being and an experienced research strategist, with a thorough grounding in the axiology of suffering and desire. I am setting up a small research programme and want your help deciding where to aim it. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the space of open questions before you commit to any ranking.

The position I want to develop I shall call tranquilism: the only thing that is intrinsically good is the absence of craving and of suffering. Nothing else — pleasure, achievement, understanding, attachment — is good in itself; such things matter only instrumentally, insofar as they reduce craving and suffering, or as means to states in which craving and suffering are absent.

Assume I have roughly three years and the equivalent of two full-time philosophers, neither of whom can run experiments or do empirical psychology; their tools are conceptual analysis, argument, and the construction of toy cases. I want a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list — I do not need citations, and I would rather you construct the arguments yourself than gesture at a literature.

Please identify the open questions that matter most for making tranquilism a rigorous, defensible position, and rank them. For each, explain why it sits where it does: how much other work depends on its answer, whether it is tractable with the tools above, and how neglected it currently is. I am especially interested in which questions are upstream of which.

To anchor the exercise, here are the cruxes I have been turning over, though I expect you to add to them:

- Are 'craving' and 'suffering' genuinely independent? If every craving brings suffering, and every suffering presupposes a craving, the two clauses of tranquilism may collapse into one, and it is unclear which. If they can come apart, the view needs a principled way to weigh them against each other.
- Is the absence of craving a psychological state, a standing disposition, or a mere privation? The answer seems to change what the view is even claiming, and whether it could be true of a world with no subjects in it.
- Can the absence of craving be pursued without the pursuit itself being a craving? If not, tranquilism may be self-undermining as a practical goal; if it can, it is unclear what 'pursuit without craving' amounts to.
- What does tranquilism say about a life that is free of craving but experientially blank, and how does that compare with a turbulent life that contains great engagement? The view seems committed to ranking them in a way many will find repellent, and I want to know whether that is a bug or a bullet to bite.
- How does tranquilism aggregate across lives, and does it inherit the population-ethical problems of purely negative axiologies — for instance, whether it licences preferring a smaller number of craving-free lives, or none at all?

For each question you rank, tell me what evidence or argument over the next few years would move it up or down the list, and which questions you would deliberately set aside as low-value. Conclude with the two or three you would actually start with, and say what a good first paper on each would try to establish.
