I am a novelist, about a third of the way into a book whose spine is a citizens' assembly convened to settle a handful of constitutional questions that are logically tangled up with one another, and I have reached the point where I need the machinery to be honest rather than merely atmospheric. Before I decide what my assembly actually does on the page, I want the specification rather than any particular procedure. Please act as an expert in social choice theory and formal epistemology; before you write anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming the candidate requirements, throwing out the ones that turn out to be merely the preferences of one camp, and only then commit to a list. What I want is the desiderata that any adequate theory of collective judgment must meet — what such a theory has to deliver, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, and which formal results it cannot contradict — with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. No citations, no reading lists, no bibliography; I want the reasoning, not the scholarship, and a table is welcome if it keeps the list legible. Please write in American English.

The cases are where I need the most care, because a theory that handles only the textbook example is no use to me. It must get right the familiar three-member court in which a majority affirms each of two premises and a majority denies the conclusion those premises jointly entail, and it must tell me whether the court's verdict is the premise-based one, the conclusion-based one, or something else — and on what ground. It must also handle a case I have invented for the book, in which the assembly votes on whether to add a further proposition to its own agenda, knowing that the answer changes which majority is decisive; the procedure's output here is partly a fact about the procedure itself, and I want to know whether that is a genuine constraint on a theory or a mere artifact of majority rule. It should say what happens when a majority endorses each item in a package of positions that everyone associates with one option, while a majority also prefers the rival option once the package is presented as a single choice; when the "committee" is itself composed of committees whose internal verdicts are the inputs; when members abstain, report degrees of confidence, or sincerely say they do not know; when they disagree about which propositions are premises and which are conclusions, so that "premise-based" and "conclusion-based" are not even well defined for the group; and when members vote strategically rather than sincerely, so that the procedure's outputs under sincere and strategic behavior diverge.

Then I want the boundary work, which is the part I most want to see. Say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the commitments of a particular camp — collective consistency versus collective completeness, anonymity, neutrality, independence, responsiveness to individual opinion, deductive closure, immunity to agenda manipulation, strategy-proofness, the demand that the collective judgment be a function of the individual judgments rather than of something else — and name the pairs or triples you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, explaining why. There is an impossibility result in this area holding that universal domain, collective consistency and completeness, anonymity, and systematicity (independence plus neutrality) cannot all hold at once, and I want to know exactly which of those you would surrender first and what surrendering it costs; likewise, tell me what to make of the fact that majority voting over a connected agenda can make the outcome depend on the order in which propositions are put. Tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories. If you can, close with a few sentences each on how a premise-based approach, a conclusion-based approach, and a consistency-seeking approach that minimizes distance from the individual judgments fare against the list — but keep the weight on the criteria, since the criteria are the deliverable and the scoring is only a sanity check. If any of my cases turn out not to be genuine constraints on a theory, say so plainly and tell me why.
