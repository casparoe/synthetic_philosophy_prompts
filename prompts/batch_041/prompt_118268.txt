I am in the middle of an art-history project for my EPQ, and a friend and I constructed the following argument after visiting a gallery that had hung very good copies of two paintings while the originals were out on loan. I can follow every step from inside it, and I cannot accept where it ends, so I would like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

(1) A painting's aesthetic value — its value as something to be looked at — is entirely a matter of the experience it affords to a suitably informed and sensitive viewer.
(2) If two objects afford exactly the same experience to every such viewer, then they have the same aesthetic value.
(3) A perfect forgery is possible in principle: an object that no viewer, however expert, and no instrument, however fine, could distinguish from the original by looking at it, or by touching it, or by examining it in any other way open to a viewer.
(4) Therefore a perfect forgery has exactly the same aesthetic value as the original it copies.
(5) If it has the same aesthetic value, then nothing of aesthetic value is lost when a gallery hangs the forgery and stores the original away, or destroys it.
(6) Therefore there is no aesthetic reason to prefer the original to the perfect forgery, and the discovery that a work is a forgery is never a discovery about the work's value, only about the painter's honesty.
(7) But a gallery that quietly destroyed its Vermeers and hung perfect forgeries in their place would have done something wrong with the paintings; the loss would be a loss in the art, not merely a loss of information about who painted what.

I should most like to keep (1), because I do think the value of a painting is the value of looking at it, and I should also like to keep (7), because I believe it. Somewhere between them something has to give.

Two further things I should like you to bring in if they help you decide. First, the difference between what a thing is worth as an experience and what it is worth as an achievement: a forgery might reproduce the first without reproducing the second, but then (1) is carrying a great deal of weight, and I want to know how much. Second, the contrast with music — nobody calls a faithful performance of a score a forgery, and nobody thinks a score loses its value when a different orchestra plays it — so perhaps the argument shows only that paintings differ from music in a way we ought to state rather than assume.

Finally, does it matter that a perfect forgery may be impossible in fact even if it is possible in principle? If the argument needs (3) to hold of real cases rather than merely possible ones, please say so.
