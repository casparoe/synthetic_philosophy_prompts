I write a column on science and ideas, and I am drafting a piece on the distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances. The physical stance predicts a system by treating it as matter in motion; the design stance predicts it by treating it as organized for a purpose; the intentional stance predicts it by attributing beliefs and desires. I want the column to argue for a definite position rather than survey the debate. Please make the following argument: intentional-stance patterns can be thickly real, not merely thinly real. Define thin realism as the claim that a pattern is real only relative to the predictive interests and cognitive limits of the agent using it, and thick realism as the claim that a pattern would remain indispensable even if those interests and limits were radically different. Then argue that at least some belief-desire patterns are thickly real, and that the physical stance cannot in principle replace them without explanatory loss. In making the argument, develop your own criterion for when a higher-level pattern earns thick realism. Consider compression, counterfactual robustness, interventionist support, multiple realizability, and the possibility of an explanatory remainder that physical description cannot capture. Test the criterion on a concrete case of your own construction: a system whose behavior can be predicted both physically and intentionally, but where the intentional prediction seems to add something. Address the strongest objection—that thick realism for the intentional stance proves too much, since it would make almost any useful predictive scheme thickly real—and reply to it. Finally, explain what this implies for the physical stance: is it the fundamental description of reality, or is it one stance among others, with its own domain of thickly real patterns? The argument should be self-contained, should not rely on citations or a literature review, and should reach a clear verdict.
