Okay, so I'm a political reporter — statehouse stuff mostly, plus a weekly column where I try to smuggle in ideas from academic philosophy that never make it into the horse-race coverage. There's a body of work I keep half-remembering, judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma, and before I try to write about it I want it restated in the vocabulary I actually think in, which is: whip counts, caucuses, floor votes, platform planks, amendment votes vs. final passage, coalition agreements, endorsement ballots, "where does the party stand on X" stories. So take the core of that debate and translate it into that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. I know how a roll call works, I know what a plank fight at a convention looks like, I know a caucus can vote yes on two things and end up committed to a third that none of its members would accept on its own. Don't dumb it down, and don't let the translation quietly import assumptions from my world that aren't actually in the original.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which bits come through cleanly, which come through only with a distortion I should flag to readers, and which have no counterpart at all in how I think about politics. Two things I'm suspicious about. First, in my world a group that holds contradictory positions is just a coalition with factions, or a leader who's bad at messaging — I've never needed the notion that the group itself has inconsistent beliefs that don't trace back to any member. Does that idea survive the translation, or does it turn out to be an artifact of how philosophers set the problem up? Second, the difference between everyone voting on each plank separately versus everyone voting on the platform as a whole, and how those two can come apart — is that a real distinction in my vocabulary, or am I just redescribing the difference between an amendment vote and a final passage vote? And where something genuinely has no home in my vocabulary, tell me straight whether that's political reporting missing something or the philosophical claim being thinner than it sounds.
