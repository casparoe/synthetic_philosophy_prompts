Design a matched pair of cases that isolates outcome luck from every other kind of luck, and then audit what three positions in the moral-luck debate must say about it. Reason entirely from the materials supplied here; I do not want a survey of who said what, and no citations or reading lists.

Three terms, fixed for this exercise. Call the control principle the thesis that an agent is morally assessable only for what lies within that agent's control, from which it follows that two agents who differ solely in how the world happened to turn out are equally blameworthy. Call outcome-directed response the apology, repair, restitution or acknowledgement owed because a harm has actually occurred, as distinct from the degree of blameworthiness of whoever caused it; some theorists hold that responses of this second sort may vary with outcomes even where blameworthiness does not. Call the attributable outcome variance of a pair of cases the share of the difference between the two outcomes that is produced by the agents' own acts rather than by surrounding circumstances. I want a pair in which this quantity is as close to zero as you can make it while the two agents are still doing something.

The pair must satisfy all of the following:

- One ordinary setting, available today: a kitchen, a clinic, a workshop, a stretch of road. No trolleys, no transplant dilemmas, no invented technology, no villains.
- Two agents qualitatively identical up to the moment of action: same information, capacities, reasons-responsiveness, character, social position, act performed, and foreseen risk.
- The divergence in outcome must be a low-probability deviation, so that ex ante expected harm is equal across the two cases.
- The divergence must not be traceable to an avoidable omission, a neglected precaution, an unheeded warning, a gamble knowingly taken, or a difference in skill. If a reader can repair the case by observing that one of them should have checked something, the design has failed.
- No third party whose intervention explains the difference.
- Nothing differs between the two cases except the outcome itself.
- Avoid the well-worn shape in which an agent negligently fails to do something and is then judged by the resulting crash; the case must not turn on fault that becomes visible only in hindsight.
- The scenario itself should run to no more than three short paragraphs.

Then deliver the audit, in this order:

1. State the pair, and mark the single variable that differs.
2. Give the verdict of each of three positions: a strict control principle; a view on which the unlucky agent owes a distinctive first-personal regret that is not blame, of the kind that attaches to having done a thing rather than to having been at fault (the phenomenon often labelled agent-regret); and a view on which institutional responses — compensation, sanction, public accounting — may justifiably track outcomes even where blameworthiness does not. For each, say what the divergence in the case does and does not license.
3. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, then demonstrate this by deleting it: give the modified pair and show that the verdicts converge.
4. State the attributable outcome variance of your pair, showing your reasoning about which variation belongs to the agents and which to the world.
5. State what the case is for, and what would count as cheating in constructing it — in particular, whether the divergence in verdicts is generated by the stipulated commitments of each position or merely asserted by the framing.
6. Steelman the defender of the control principle: give the strongest line of attack on your case, and say what its author must concede.

Aim at a case in which the temptation to distinguish the two agents is strong while the specification leaves nothing to distinguish them by. If your pair collapses into a story about negligence, rebuild it before answering.
