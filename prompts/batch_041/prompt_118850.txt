I have been asked to circulate a short position statement to a formal epistemology reading group, and before I send it round I want to know whether it is a coherent package or a set of views I hold for incompatible reasons. I am not after a survey of the debates about Dutch books; I want an audit of this particular bundle — which commitments genuinely pull against which, which pairs only appear to conflict, and, for every genuine conflict, which commitment is doing the least work and what the cheapest repair would cost me. I have a suspicion that not everything here hangs together, though I cannot tell whether the apparent conflicts are real ones.

Here is what I hold.

1. Incoherence in credence is a rational defect, and what makes it a defect is that an incoherent credence function exposes its bearer to a Dutch book: a finite collection of bets, each of which she will regard as fair or favourable, whose combined payoff is negative in every state of the world.

2. I take the Dutch book criterion to be extensionally adequate and not merely sufficient. If a credence function cannot be Dutch-booked in the sense of (1), that is for me a strong prima facie case that it is rationally permissible. The criterion is meant to be my account of what is wrong with incoherent credences, not a convenient test that happens to catch some of them.

3. I think of (1) and (2) as the belief-side instance of a general no-arbitrage principle. Coherent credences stand to the probability axioms roughly as arbitrage-free prices stand to the conditions on admissible price systems: the axioms are what the absence of a riskless profit imposes on the agent's own evaluations. I would like to know whether this analogy is illuminating or a cheat, and I am prepared to have it used against me.

4. Rational credences are countably additive, not merely finitely additive.

5. Rational credences are sharp. A rational agent has a single real-valued credence for each proposition, and imprecise credences are not a permissible rational state but a failure to have formed a credence at all.

6. Learning is conditionalization, and what establishes it is the diachronic argument: a bookie who knows an agent's updating rule can arrange bets, offered at different times and each acceptable to her at the time it is offered, that leave her poorer in every state of the world unless her rule is conditionalization.

7. When I ask whether a sure-loss argument convicts an agent of irrationality rather than merely describing her betting behaviour, the test I apply is single-time commitment. To convict her, the argument must show that at one time she is committed to a set of evaluations that jointly guarantee her a loss. An argument that shows only that she would be exploited if she were willing to take a succession of bets at different times, when at the later times she need have no reason to take them, shows at most that she has an unusual attitude towards her own future preferences.

8. I am not a behaviourist about credence. Credences are real states that a person can have whether or not a bookie ever appears, and someone can be irrational in her credences even if she never gambles and cares nothing for money.

9. One concrete verdict, to fix the abstractions: I hold that there cannot be a coherent credence function assigning the same credence to each of countably many mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive propositions. A fair lottery over the natural numbers, each ticket equally likely and some ticket certain to win, strikes me not as a coherent state but as an impossibility.

Where two of these are independently motivated and cannot be derived from a common source, that is exactly what I want flagged; I would rather carry fewer premises than carry an idle one.

Please do the following, in order. First, identify the pairs that genuinely conflict and rank them by severity, rather than treating them all as equally bad. Second, for each, say what the least costly repair is, what that repair costs, and whether it forces me to give up something I plainly want to keep. Third, identify any pair that only appears to conflict and say what dissolves it. Fourth, if two of the conflicts turn out to be symptoms of a single underlying problem, say so instead of counting them twice. Finally, tell me which single revision would resolve the most conflicts at once, and whether adopting it leaves any other commitment in the bundle unsupported.
