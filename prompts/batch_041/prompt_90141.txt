I work in the communications office of a mid-sized logistics firm, and our new Chief People Officer has lately become an enthusiast of a philosophical position I gather is called eliminative materialism — the claim, associated in her telling with Paul Churchland and Stephen Stich, that the beliefs, desires, intentions and other states we ordinarily attribute to one another do not really exist, and that the vocabulary of folk psychology is a degenerating theoretical framework which a mature cognitive neuroscience will eventually discard. She has decided that the company's internal documents must stop presupposing it, and she has asked me to draft the all-staff announcement.

Please write that memo for me: roughly 600–700 words, dated, with a subject line, addressed to all employees, and laid out in numbered clauses. The voice is a confident HR executive who has read just enough philosophy and is unshakably serene about having understood it; the tone is deadpan throughout, the document never acknowledges that it is amusing, and it never turns into an argument against the position — it is a working policy document, not a rebuttal. The humour has to come from getting the doctrine right, so build the memo out of the genuine consequences of eliminativism rather than out of generic corporate absurdity. I would like it to exploit the following in particular: first, that the memo's own sentences are supposed to be true and its demands are supposed to be obeyed, so the document keeps groping for precisely the idioms it has just abolished — let it notice this and explain it away in the most soothing terms available; second, that the difference between saying beliefs do not exist and saying they are merely convenient predictive fictions should be thoroughly muddled, with the company quietly adopting whichever reading suits each clause; third, that ordinary workplace operations — performance reviews, disciplinary hearings, exit interviews, a dispute over whether someone meant to send a file to the wrong client — should be followed out with bureaucratic literalness; and fourth, that at least one dissenting voice, say a note from the legal team or from an employee with a philosophy degree, should be reproduced in the memo and answered in a way that is worse than the objection.
