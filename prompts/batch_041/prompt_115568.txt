I've been reading around nuclear deterrence and brinkmanship and theres a problem I cant shake. Deterrence is supposed to be a causal claim — the threat stopped someone from doing something — but the only cases we can see clearly are ones where it either failed or was never seriously tested, and the cases where it supposedly worked look from the outside exactly like the cases where the threat was irrelevant and nothing was going to happen anyway. So here's the reframing I want to try out. Instead of asking whether a threat changed the adversary's decision, ask whether the adversary's behavior would have been the same with the threat removed, and then notice that in the nuclear case that counterfactual isn't just hard to observe, it's unobservable by construction. My suspicion is that this turns deterrence into something other than an explanation of the adversary's behavior: it becomes a commitment device aimed mainly at allies and domestic audiences, a way of making your own future conduct predictable rather than a way of changing someone else's mind. Threats are then doing their real work on your own side of the table.

Tell me the most important implications of taking that reframing seriously. Give me exactly five, as a numbered list, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no wrap-up at the end. Keep it concrete — I care about what follows for alliance guarantees, for how we should read the historical record of non-use, for doctrine choices, and for the ethics of building a strategy on a claim nobody can check. No citations, I'll add those myself, and don't send me off to read anyone; just reason it through.
