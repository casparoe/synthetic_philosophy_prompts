I'm putting together a case study for a seminar on distributive justice, and I want to work through the September 11th Victim Compensation Fund as if it were a real-world attempt to implement luck egalitarianism. The basic shape of the episode is familiar to me: Congress created the fund in late 2001, Kenneth Feinberg was appointed special master, claimants received an individualized award in exchange for waiving their right to sue the airlines, and the awards were built up from an estimate of each victim's projected lifetime earnings, adjusted for the number of dependents and, as I understand it, reduced by other sources of recovery such as life insurance and pension death benefits. The result was that the family of a young, highly paid financial professional with children received far more than the family of an older, low-wage worker with none, and the fund's defenders called this a serious attempt to put people back where they would have been, while its critics called it a way of letting the arbitrariness of the labor market price a human life. I don't need the background; what I need is the analysis, and I want it run in both directions, from the theory onto the case and from the case back onto the theory.

First, the theory onto the case. Set out the luck-egalitarian reading properly: the brute-luck versus option-luck distinction, the question of the correct currency of equality, and the requirement that distributions be sensitive to responsibility. Then tell me where the fund's design maps onto that structure and where it does not. I am particularly interested in two things. One is the offset for other sources of recovery: if a family's award was reduced by the life insurance or pension benefits the victim had arranged, is that offset coherent on luck-egalitarian terms, or does it smuggle in option-luck reasoning that the theory cannot license, given that these beneficiaries were selected by catastrophe rather than by imprudence? The other is the choice of currency: the fund compensated in dollars calibrated to market earnings, which luck egalitarians themselves regard as morally arbitrary, so I want to know whether the fund is best described as equalizing anything at all or merely restoring the status quo ante. Bring the standard objections to luck egalitarianism to bear on the case rather than in the abstract — that the view licenses humiliating and intrusive judgments about whose misfortune was their own fault, that it expresses pity rather than respect, and that it abandons the imprudent — and say which of these the fund actually instantiates and which are beside the point when the beneficiaries were chosen by an atrocity.

Second, the case onto the theory. What does this episode show about luck egalitarianism that the usual hypothetical insurance markets and unlucky-gambler cases do not? In particular: whether compensating for one instance of brute luck by restoring a distribution that is itself the product of brute luck is anything the view can endorse; whether the fund's voluntary character — claimants gave up their tort claims in exchange for an award, under conditions nobody would call a fair bargain — turns the episode into a case the theory has no tools for; and whether the theory yields any determinate answer to what the fund should have done instead, whether that is a flat per-victim payment, compensation in some currency other than money, or nothing at all. Finish by saying which features of the episode luck egalitarianism illuminates, which it misdescribes, and whether the case should count as a confirmation of the view, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated. Please write in American English.
