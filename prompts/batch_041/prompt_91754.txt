I am a second-year PhD student and have to submit a short essay (roughly 4,000 words) to a departmental work-in-progress seminar on the question whether philosophy makes progress. I have a draft whose central argument I believe is good, but which I suspect may be either a re-description of a familiar position in fresh vocabulary or quietly circular, and I would like your comments before I rewrite it. A friend of mine in the history of science keeps telling me that the whole genre is exhausted, which is one reason I want an outside view. The thesis is this: philosophy does make progress, but neither of the two standard pictures captures it. The accumulative picture — that philosophy banks results the way chemistry does — is false, and so is the pessimist picture that philosophy merely swaps one vocabulary for another without getting anywhere. What actually happens, I argue, is that philosophy progresses by transmitting obstacles. A field has progressed when the people working in it can give a better account than their predecessors could of why the predecessors were stuck: not of what the answer was, but of what made the question hard. The mark of this is what I call difficulty atrophy — a difficulty that once gripped everyone simply stops being tempting, so that reconstructing why anyone ever felt its force takes an effort of historical imagination.

I then distinguish two ways in which a philosophical question can be retired. The first is dissolution, where the question is shown to rest on a presupposition that cannot be made good. The second is relocation, where the question is replaced by a sharper one that does the same work but admits of a determinate answer. My claim is that relocation is the dominant mode, and that philosophers persistently mistake relocation for dissolution: we tell ourselves a question has been shown to be confused when in fact it has only been exchanged for a better one. That mistake, I argue, is the main engine of the 'no progress' pessimism, because it makes each generation believe it has finally cleared the ground while the old difficulty is still standing there under a new name. The part I am least confident about is whether the account is circular. A pessimist can reply that any school whatsoever can narrate why its predecessors were confused, and that a criterion which fashionable nonsense satisfies as easily as real work is no criterion at all. My proposed fix is a constraint: the explanation of the earlier impasse may not lean on premises that are themselves the live point of contention within the successor's own framework — it must be an explanation that would have compelled assent from the very people it is about, granted only what they already accepted. I cannot tell whether this constraint does any work or merely relocates the difficulty one step back.

Could you comment on the argument as it stands? I would particularly like to know: whether the obstacle account is genuinely distinct from the familiar positions or merely a restatement of one of them in new words, and if the latter, which one; whether the constraint I propose escapes the circularity charge or only postpones it, and what a version that really escaped it would look like; whether dissolution and relocation are exhaustive, or whether there is a third mode of retirement I have overlooked, with an example; and one episode in the history of philosophy that the account handles well together with one where it plainly fails. I am not asking for references — I shall add those myself — I want the test cases from whatever you can reconstruct on your own. Finally, tell me which single claim, if false, would do the most damage to the essay, and whether the distinction between difficulty atrophy and mere forgetting can be held. I would far rather be told that the essay is wrong-headed than be flattered about the prose, so please be blunt about the argument and treat the writing as a secondary matter. Please write your comments in British English.
