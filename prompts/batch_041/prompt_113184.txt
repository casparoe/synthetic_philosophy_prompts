I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of behavioural public policy, and I sit as the student representative on my university's student wellbeing strategy group. At the last meeting, two members — Nadia, a behavioural scientist seconded to the university's behavioural insights team, and Callum, a philosopher in my department — fell into a sustained argument about the proposed redesign of the student counselling service. I have to vote on that redesign at the next meeting, I stayed out of it at the time, and I genuinely don't know who had the better of it. I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange as it actually happened, not to give me general remarks about nudging.

The background, briefly. Previously, a student requesting counselling filled in a form and was triaged into whatever the service offered, usually a one-to-one appointment roughly three weeks out. Under the proposal, the booking page presents as its pre-selected option a group wellbeing workshop running within forty-eight hours, with a link lower down the page offering one-to-one instead, still about three weeks out. No option is forbidden and no fee changes. The service's stated case is that the workshops work as well as one-to-one for most presentations, that they clear the waiting list, that they cost less to run, and that the pilot was well received.

Callum's opening move was that the proposal bundles two quite different things — a genuine service improvement, namely a workshop within two days, and a presentational trick, namely the pre-selection — with the result that anyone who objects to the trick is made to look as though they are objecting to the improvement. He added that the default points at the cheaper option, so the "we are helping you" story is doing cover for a throughput decision; on his view, a nudge has to be justified by the chooser's own good as she sees it, and that is not what is motivating the service here.

Nadia replied that motive is the wrong place to look. What matters is whether the design helps students get what they want while leaving them free to refuse. The pilot found that when students were shown both options with their waiting times and with no pre-selection, a majority chose the workshop. So for most students the default merely spares them a decision they would have made anyway; it also frees one-to-one capacity for the minority who need it; and it makes the alternative visible in a way the old form never did, since under the old system students simply took whatever triage assigned them.

Callum's second point was that if most students would choose the workshop anyway, the default can only be doing work among those who would have chosen one-to-one and did not scroll far enough — so the entire effect of the intervention is concentrated on people whose own preference, had they looked, was the opposite. That, he said, is precisely the group you should be least confident about overriding. And the mechanism gives the game away: the design succeeds only while students do not deliberate, and stops working the moment they do. He also cited the pilot's finding that the students who took the workshop under the new system were less satisfied afterwards than the average, which he said is exactly what you would expect if you are capturing people who wanted something else.

Nadia answered that the counterfactual of the attentive chooser does not exist. Someone must decide what the page shows first, and the old system had its own default: whatever triage assigned. Put one-to-one at the top and you steer the other way, wasting the capacity that gets people seen in two days. Force an explicit choice and you add friction at the moment when a distressed student is least able to absorb it, and some of them will end up with nothing at all. As for the satisfaction figures, they are confounded by waiting time: the workshop group was seen in two days and the comparison group in three weeks, so the difference tells you nothing about whether people got what they wanted.

Callum's final point concerned the students in what he called the residual group, and then disclosure. The proposal mentions the pre-selection in a footnote and the service will publish the design. If disclosure is what makes the design acceptable, then the design would be acceptable even if no student ever read the footnote, which seems wrong; if disclosure is not what makes it acceptable, then the footnote is a ritual. What matters morally, he said, is whether the design engages the student's own judgement or substitutes for it — and on Nadia's own account it works by substituting. He also complained that Nadia had defended the aggregate and had said nothing about what is owed to the students who, by her own description, are getting something other than what they wanted.

Please go through the exchange turn by turn. For each turn, say what is right in it, what is wrong or overstated, and rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken through to clearly right, with a brief justification each time. Separate the empirical claims from the normative ones, and flag anywhere a claim is being used to do work it cannot do — in particular, say whether the pilot figures settle anything either way. Then tell me what both of them are missing, whether this dispute is really about paternalism at all or whether the surviving objection is about something else entirely, and, if you had to come down on one side, who has the better of the argument overall. I have to vote on Thursday, so I would rather have a verdict than a survey.
