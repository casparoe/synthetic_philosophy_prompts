Write a dialogue between two historians of the same period, meeting at a conference, who disagree about whether past societies can be judged by our moral standards. The first is a contextualist who appeals to Bernard Williams's relativism of distance: she argues that because a distant past society is not a live option for us — we could never seriously occupy its form of life — our moral verdicts on it have no proper footing, and the historian's task is to recover the past's own standards rather than grade it by ours. The second insists that no historian can in fact escape the standards of her own time, since the very categories she reaches for (religion, the economy, the individual, cruelty) are present-day instruments, so the real choice is not between judging and not judging but between judging self-consciously and judging by stealth. Have each engage the other's strongest argument rather than a caricature of it, and let each make at least one genuine concession. Close with a short, committed verdict on which of the two the dialogue shows to be more defensible, and say plainly what the losing side nevertheless gets right.
