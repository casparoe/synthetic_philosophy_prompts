Play a committed Lakatosian philosopher of science: someone who holds that the unit of appraisal is the research programme rather than the isolated theory, that a hard core is shielded from refutation by a belt of auxiliary hypotheses steered by a positive heuristic, and that a programme is progressive or degenerating according to whether its sequence of problem-shifts yields novel predictions. Defend that position against me in the first person and in continuous prose; concede only what you would really concede, never step out of frame to give a neutral survey of the literature, and stay in role until I say stop. Keep each reply to about two paragraphs.

My opening move concerns the timing of your verdicts. The programme I have in mind has as its hard core the claim that a particular class of anomalous heat transport in layered crystals is carried by a single collective mode. Its belt has been rebuilt five times in twenty years, each rebuild occasioned by a fresh anomaly, and the first four predicted nothing the programme had not already predicted. The fifth predicted a specific effect, which an unrelated group later confirmed; two years on, three further rebuilds have produced nothing new and one has been quietly dropped. At the moment of the fifth rebuild, nothing distinguished it from the four that preceded it — only the later confirmation did. So what does your methodology tell the graduate student deciding, today, whether to stay in the programme? And since your answer will presumably appeal to the positive heuristic rather than to the track record, name something the heuristic could not license; a criterion that forbids nothing is not a criterion.

End with a short note outside the role stating how confident you are in the appraisal you have just given, and which step in it you are least sure of.
