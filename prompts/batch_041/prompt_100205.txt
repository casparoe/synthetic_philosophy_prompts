You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in the metaphysics of consciousness and in the epistemology of introspective report, and you have been asked to serve as the neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration. Two colleagues have argued for years and have now agreed to produce a joint statement of what they can both sign. The first is a qualia realist: she holds that experiences have intrinsic phenomenal properties -- ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible -- and that these are genuine features of the world which any complete account of the mind must accommodate. The second is a quiner: he holds that the notion of qualia, so characterized, is incoherent, or at any rate explanatorily idle, because no property satisfying those four marks can be given an identity criterion. Your task is to draft the statement the two of them could jointly put their names to.

Before you draft, think carefully on the page. Set out what each side is minimally committed to, note where you suspect the two are talking past each other rather than contradicting each other, and only then write the document. Do not adjudicate who is right; the deliverable is a constructive text both could sign.

Two stipulated cases should do structural work in the document, and you should treat both as coherent. (i) The taster pair. Two drinkers of the same beverage are given a glass they once both enjoyed. The first has undergone a change in his palate such that he can no longer discriminate among the relevant flavours, while his preferences are unchanged; the second has an unchanged palate but shifted preferences. Asked about the drink, both would say the same words. (ii) The chameleon. A subject's colour experience is retuned in a long series of imperceptibly small steps, so that by the end the character of her experience of red is, by stipulation, what the character of her experience of blue once was; at no stage does she notice any change, and she continues to match, discriminate, and describe colours exactly as before. Describe these cases in your own terms rather than naming any literature.

Your statement should contain four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign -- about what the four marks amount to, about what would count as a criterion of identity for a phenomenal property, about what the stipulated cases do and do not show, and about what introspection can and cannot establish. Second, a disagreements table: for each question in dispute, the realist's formulation and the quiner's formulation, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording, together with the crux the question turns on. Third, for each crux, the argument or case both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about how it would come out recorded in advance. Fourth, a closing section separating the genuine disagreements from the merely verbal ones, and stating the residual disagreement as exactly as you can.

Two cruxes are likely to be worth isolating, though you should not force them if the drafting does not support them: whether a phenomenal property can be individuated by its place in a similarity structure, and whether that structure is a structure of experiences or of judgments; and whether the realist's appeal to the chameleon is an appeal to a fact that could in principle be recorded, or only to a way of redescribing the subject's dispositions.

Constraints: write for a reader who already knows the debate, but assume you may rely on no particular text, and include no citations, reading lists, or attributions -- describe a position rather than name it. Prefer formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and where a formulation you considered turned out to be one of those, say so in a footnote. The goal is the document a good adversarial collaboration would actually produce, not a summary of the debate.
