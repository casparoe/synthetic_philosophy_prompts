I sit in on a fortnightly political-economy reading group, and last week we spent the session on the old socialist calculation debate — the dispute between Mises and Hayek on one side and Lange on the other over whether a centrally planned economy could allocate resources rationally. What was meant to be a discussion turned into a long argument between two members, Nadia and Owen, and I was the only person in the room who never said anything, largely because I could see that each of them was making at least one move I had no answer to. I should say that I am not asking you to survey the topic or to tell me that the issues are subtle and both sides have a point; I want you to referee the argument as it was actually conducted, move by move, and to be decisive where a move deserves it.

It went roughly as follows. (1) Nadia opened by saying the whole controversy is a period piece: on her reading, the objection pressed by Mises and Hayek was an objection about information processing, since a modern economy contains millions of distinct goods and rational allocation would therefore require solving millions of simultaneous equations, which was simply not feasible with the calculating technology available in the interwar period. Cheap computation, modern optimisation and machine learning have dissolved that constraint, so whatever the calculation problem once was, it is no longer an impossibility argument. (2) Owen replied that she has hold of the wrong objection: Hayek's point was never that the equations are too numerous, but that the knowledge they would have to encode is dispersed, largely tacit, and in many cases does not exist in any centralised form at all, being embedded in particular people's familiarity with particular circumstances and elicited only by the process of market exchange itself. No quantity of computing power can gather knowledge that nobody holds in the first place. (3) Nadia said that even granting this, Lange's answer to Mises still stands — a central planning board need not solve anything in one shot, it can adjust prices by trial and error, raising them where there are shortages and lowering them where there are surpluses, much as a Walrasian auctioneer does, while instructing managers to produce at minimum cost and to follow the resulting prices. Owen answered that this concedes his point rather than meeting it, because trial-and-error adjustment is parasitic on the very thing it is meant to replace: it needs some independent signal telling the board what to try next, and in a real market that signal is profit and loss, with the prospect of loss and exit doing the disciplining work. (4) Nadia shifted ground and said that the technology has changed the facts in a way Lange could not have anticipated — her employer, a large logistics firm, centrally allocates thousands of resources a day across a continent without any internal prices at all, using forecasting and optimisation, and does so better than any decentralised arrangement the firm has tried; if a single organisation can plan on that scale, the burden of proof shifts to anyone claiming that planning is impossible in principle. (5) Owen said the firm is a bad analogy, because it plans inside a market: it takes the prices of its inputs and the prices its customers will pay as given, and those external prices are what tell it what to optimise for, so strip them away and the objective function has nothing to anchor it. He added that where platforms do set prices themselves they remain disciplined by competition and the threat of entry, so the market has not been abolished, only relocated. (6) Nadia replied that the tacit-knowledge story is empirically weaker now than when the debate was conducted, because so much of the dispersed knowledge Hayek described is captured in digital traces — purchases, searches, location and sensor data — which a central system can simply read off without asking anyone; and that in any case the incentives problem, however serious, is a different problem from the calculation problem, and it is the latter she is defending.

Please go through moves (1) to (6) in order and, for each, say whether it is right, partly right or wrong, and why, with your ratings doing real work rather than being hedged into the middle. I am particularly interested in three things: which of the two readings of the original debate — Nadia's, that it was a limit on information processing, or Owen's, that it was a limit on the availability and elicitation of knowledge — is the more defensible; whether the logistics-firm example is a genuine counterexample or an equivocation, and if an equivocation, exactly where the equivocation lies; and whether a purely computational advance could ever by itself settle the question at issue, or whether it is the wrong kind of answer to the right question. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing — the consideration neither raised that ought to decide the matter, if there is one — and say who is more right overall, and on what. Please write in British English.
