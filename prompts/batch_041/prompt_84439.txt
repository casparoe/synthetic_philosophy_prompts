I volunteer at a small city museum, where I have been asked to prepare an exhibit about housing and work in our city. Our visitors speak a dozen languages, our translation budget is almost nothing, and the trustee who oversees the exhibit has proposed that we drop sentences altogether and present everything in pictures: little figures of people for counts, houses for housing, coins for money. He argues that pictures need no translating because they show what they mean, that a drawing of five houses beside a drawing of two houses speaks for itself, and that our earlier exhibits used words only out of habit. A colleague half agrees with him: she says that between the wars a group in Vienna built a whole pictorial statistics for readers who could not follow a newspaper, and that serious people took it seriously — though she also says that the man who ran it insisted it was only ever meant to help written language along, never to replace it.

I want the exhibit to convey six things: that evictions in our city fell last year; that most of those evicted were renters rather than owners; that the housing authority is not allowed to evict anyone between November and March; that no new social housing was built at all in the 1990s; that if the recent trend continues the waiting list will double by 2035; and that nobody actually knows how long the waiting list is, since the authority stopped counting in 2019. The first two I can manage. The rest turn into a muddle the moment I try to draw them. I can draw a family carrying its things out of a door, but how do I draw that this must not happen in winter, or that nothing was built, or that something has not happened yet, or that we are only guessing?

The trustee says the fault is my drawing and that a competent illustrator could render any of it; he has also suggested that where the numbers are large we simply draw the houses larger, which my designer friend says is worse than useless, since a house drawn twice as tall looks four times as big. She says the trouble with the whole plan is deeper: a picture can only show how something looks, and everything about absence, permission, the future, or our own ignorance has to be put in by the viewer, so that a picture-only exhibit would quietly tell different visitors different things. I suspect they are each partly right, but I cannot tell which of my six items are genuinely beyond pictures and which are merely hard to draw well. Nor am I sure what it would mean for a picture to be read correctly at all: if a visitor looks at my five houses and my two houses and concludes that the city has seven houses, whose mistake is that?

What I would like is advice on how to think this through, and a verdict I can act on. Should I insist on pictures alone, or accept some words? If words, which of the six items need them, and why those rather than the others? And is there any way to decide in advance what a picture can carry by itself and what it cannot, or does that question have no answer except trying it on visitors? I do not need a reading list; I need to understand the underlying issue well enough to defend whatever I decide to the trustee.
