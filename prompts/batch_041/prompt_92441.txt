I am not a philosopher and have no university education, but for the past four years I have chaired the verification desk of a small amateur astronomy group that reports transient events — possible novae, supernovae, satellite glints, meteor trails — to a wider volunteer network. We are all unpaid enthusiasts, we work from home with our own telescopes and cameras, and when a dispute arises we cannot consult a library or search the internet; we have only the images, the logs and each other. About five times a week two members of the desk reach opposite conclusions about the same observation: one says the data support a real event, the other says it is an artefact or a misreading. Sometimes they have examined exactly the same frames and records; sometimes one has extra private observations from their own instrument; sometimes one has a much better record of past calls than the other. I have been reading about the disagreement between conciliationism and steadfastness in peer disagreement, and I think I understand the broad shape of the debate, but the examples always involve philosophers with identical evidence and equal competence, which is almost never our situation. I need something I can actually run at the desk on a Tuesday night.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure that our duty chair can follow when two members disagree. It should be a numbered sequence of tests, with a one-page scoresheet or form, and it must say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step. It should begin by determining whether the two people are genuine epistemic peers for this particular claim — same relevant competence, same evidence, independence from a common source, no stake in the outcome — and if they are not peers, it should say how to weight the disagreement using our calibration ledger, in which every member records a confidence score before the outcome is known and we track how often their eighty-per-cent calls turn out right. It should include a test for whether the two judgements are genuinely independent or merely two readings of the same corrupted pipeline, a rule for what to do when one party has private evidence the other cannot inspect, and a rule for when the correct response is to suspend judgement rather than choose a side. It should have a clear stop rule that says do not report, and a marked point at which the procedure hands the decision to a named person because the remaining question is not about the evidence but about how much risk of a false alert or a missed discovery we are willing to accept. Please mark the steps that are judgement calls dressed up as arithmetic, and be honest about which parts of the procedure a small group of volunteers is likely to get wrong.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: first, two equally calibrated members who have looked at the same images and reached opposite readings; second, a highly calibrated member who disagrees with a novice, where the novice has a private observation from a different instrument; third, a case in which I am one of the disagreeing parties and the procedure tells me to reduce my confidence or defer, even though I feel certain. For each case, give the procedure's output and the reasoning, including any point where it has to stop and hand the matter to a person. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle and where it will misfire. I do not want a reading list or a survey of the literature; I want the procedure itself, written so that someone with no philosophical training can use it at the desk.
