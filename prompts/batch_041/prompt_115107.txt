I write novels — the kind in which people say things that change their lives: vows, resignations, confessions, threats, curses, the reading of a will, the moment someone is given a name or has it taken away. I make decisions of this kind several times a chapter, and the problem is craft-level but I think it is really conceptual. When I draft a scene that turns on such a line, I cannot reliably tell whether the line actually does the thing in the world of the book or merely sounds as though it does. My editor has twice caught me building a climax on words that, on inspection, could not have bound anyone to anything; I noticed the second time only because a reader asked why a character was ruined by something that was not, in fact, a promise.

I would like a decision procedure I can run at my desk, mid-draft, on a single line of dialogue or a single document in a manuscript. Ten minutes, no theory on my part, no philosophy library.

Three distinctions I half-remember from an undergraduate course and have probably mangled. Use them if they are sound, replace them if they are not, and tell me where I have got them wrong:

- Some failures of these acts are cases where the act never came off at all; others are cases where it came off perfectly and is simply hollow — the promise made with no intention of keeping it. The two have different dramatic consequences, and I keep treating them as one thing.
- Some of these acts need an institution standing behind them (a court, a church, a guild, a rank); others need only the mutual recognition of the parties in the room.
- Some require the other party to take them up; others take effect whether or not anyone registers them.

What I want from the procedure: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure stops and returns a no, meaning that this line cannot do that work and must be rewritten or reassigned; and the point at which it hands the decision back to me because the question concerns my invented world's conventions rather than language — together with what I would then have had to establish earlier in the book for the answer to hold.

Then run it on three scenes from the current draft:

1. A woman resigns a chairmanship by speaking the words aloud in an empty committee room. She means them, the words are the right ones, and the only witness is a cleaner who does not understand the language.
2. A dying man extracts a promise from the narrator, who intends at that moment to break it. I need this promise to be void so that the narrator remains free; I suspect I am wrong about that.
3. In an invented society, a betrothal is spoken in a form that a religious reformer altered two generations earlier, so that half the community recognizes it and half does not.

For the third, the procedure should tell me what I owe the reader by way of preparation. Please include, rather than smooth over, the case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like.

Finally: say how confident you are in each part of what you give me, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
