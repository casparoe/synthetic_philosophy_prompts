I'm in a small reading group that's been chewing over the unilateralist's curse — the idea that when lots of people independently decide whether to do something, the mere fact that someone does it is evidence the thing is less good than that person thinks, so you tend to get over-action. We got stuck on a variant I came up with, which I've been calling doxastic unilateralism: same structure, but the 'action' is forming a belief. Suppose a bunch of inquirers each look at the evidence and adopt P if their private credence passes some threshold. The fact that you're at the threshold and others aren't might itself be evidence that you're on the optimistic tail, so your belief is less safe than it looks. I've been thinking about this alot and want to make a tier list of strategies for handling it, because I'm writing a short thing on it and need to know which moves are actually load-bearing. Here are the ones I've come up with, though feel free to add one if you think I've missed something obvious: (1) precommit to a higher evidential threshold when you know many others are inquiring independently; (2) ignore the fact that others are inquiring, on the grounds that their inquiry isn't evidence about P unless you know their evidence; (3) treat your own threshold as a random draw and shrink your credence towards the group's base rate; (4) publicly announce your inquiry before you look, so others can condition on your expected threshold; (5) adopt a policy of only believing P if you'd still believe it after learning that most other inquirers rejected it; (6) use a 'curse-corrected' threshold that rises with the number of independent inquirers but not with the correlation between their evidence; (7) refuse to form any belief about P and stay agnostic until a single trusted inquirer reports; (8) split the difference by believing P only in the weak sense of assigning it a higher credence than before, without full belief.
