Aristotle treats exchange, in the first book of the Politics and the fifth book of the Nicomachean Ethics, as arising from need: people trade because they lack what others have, need is the ultimate measure of value, and exchange therefore requires some common standard that renders unlike things comparable. Money supplies that standard, but only by convention: it is not valuable by nature, it is a token or pledge standing in for need, its worth can be altered or destroyed by law, and it does not always measure the same thing. Acquisition that serves the household is natural and bounded by what the household requires; acquisition whose end is money itself is unnatural and unlimited; and lending at interest is the most unnatural case of all, since there the medium appears to generate more of itself without any transfer of goods. Aristotle even reports, and then dismisses as beside the point, a scheme in which a philosopher cornered the market in olive presses after foreseeing a good harvest. Take this as the theory.

Now the episode. In January 2021, a heavily shorted American brick-and-mortar video game retailer became the target of a coordinated buying campaign by retail investors organized on a public internet forum. The share price rose more than tenfold within weeks, imposing heavy losses on the hedge funds that had bet against the company; several brokerages then restricted purchases of the stock, and the price collapsed. Take these facts as given.

Analyze the episode through the theory. What does Aristotle's account say the retail buyers, the short sellers, and the brokers were each doing, and what would it have told them to do instead? I already grant the familiar charge that speculation can be socially destructive; what I want is the mapping. Say precisely which features of the episode the theory captures -- the disappearance of need and use from the transaction, the share treated as a mere vehicle for more money, a pursuit with no limit internal to it -- and then which features it cannot describe at all: short selling is a sale of something the seller does not own; the trade paid only because buyers expected other buyers to keep buying; and the decisive interventions came from private intermediaries who set the rules of the game. Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.

Then reverse the lens. Does the episode confirm Aristotle's suspicion that acquisition detached from need is corrupting and self-undermining, or does it expose a gap in his account -- that a standard of need cannot distinguish productive finance from a squeeze, that he assumes a market already embedded in a well-ordered political community, that he has no vocabulary for speculation as a wager on other people's expectations?

Close by saying what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict on how well the theory handles the case, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
