Novelist here, not a philosopher, so pardon the vocabulary. I've got a scene I need to build, and I'd rather build it to spec than borrow one of the usual examples, so here's the spec.

I need a case where a character has two separate ways of keeping track of one and the same person — by face and by voice, say, or by handwriting and by face — and she's competent with both: she can pick him out either way without any trouble. She just never linked the two. Then something puts them together, it lands as news, and it changes what she does next.

The constraint that does the work, I think: neither of her two ways of tracking him can be backed by a description. She can't tell you a single thing about him that would single him out — she recognizes the face and she recognizes the voice, thats it. So no hidden descriptions, no unique scars or job titles, no "the man who did X," no names that secretly mean something. Also no twins, no amnesia, no disguises, no false identities — it's genuinely one person and she simply hadn't put the files together. Realistic enough to sit in a novel about ordinary people, and keep each half short.

Then, in plain terms: does what she learns count as learning a fact? Give me the Fregean answer (the sense-and-reference crowd) and the Millian answer (names and guises are just handles on the object, nothing more), and tell me whether the Millian can still fall back on "she learned which object was at issue" — my hunch is no, since she could already pick him out both ways, but correct me if I'm off. Then say which single feature of my setup is doing the real work, what happens to the case if I let one of the two guises carry a description, and what the Millian's best move is to defuse it.

American English, please. And if this can't be built the way I'm asking, don't paper over it — say so and give me the closest thing that works.
