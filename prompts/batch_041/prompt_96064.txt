I teach the vagueness unit in a second-year metaphysics course, and the thing my students reliably fail at isn't the paradox itself — they can all write the sorites argument down in two lines — it's that they treat epistemicism, supervaluationism and degree theory as three flavours of the same shrug, and can't say which of them they actually believe. So I'd like you to build me a decision tree that takes a student from the handful of intuitions everybody shares (one grain can't turn a heap into a non-heap; someone who is plainly bald doesn't stop being bald because one more hair fell out; there is no sharp line anywhere) to a specific, named position about what vagueness is. A tree, please, not a list of labels with glosses: every internal node should be a question the student can answer by saying what they'd say about a concrete case, and every leaf should carry the name of a position, one sentence giving its central commitment, and one sentence naming the case or objection that makes it hardest to hold.

Build the tree out of the logical space first, and locate the names in it afterwards. The questions I want it to turn on: whether the tolerance premise of the argument expresses something constitutive of the predicate's meaning or merely a habit of ours about when it is safe to apply it; whether the vagueness is supposed to be in the predicate, in the world, in our knowledge, or in our psychology of categorisation; whether there is a cut-off — knowable, unknowable, or shifting with context — or no cut-off at all; what the student thinks is the right thing to do in a forced march, where every item in the series has to be sorted; and whether the borderline region itself has a sharp edge, so that the student also has to answer for second-order borderline cases, i.e. whether it can be borderline whether something is borderline. Include a node that turns on whether the argument is put as a single universally quantified premise or as a long chain of conditionals, since I suspect one or two of the leaves only exist for one of those formulations. For cases, use the heap of sand, a strip of ever-so-slightly different shades of red, a height series in one-centimetre steps, and a version of each in which the student is forced to sort every item rather than allowed to abstain. Leaves I want to see, whatever you end up calling them: a sharp but unknowable cut-off; a predicate whose meaning is fixed by a range of admissible precisifications, with borderline cases neither true nor false; the variant on that which calls them both true and false; truth on a continuum of degrees; extension that shifts with context so that the premise means something different at each step; indeterminacy that is in the world rather than in us; the deflationary view that these words are simply approximations and that the puzzle trades on a false picture of what they are for; the view that the phenomenon is psychological — prototype effects and graded similarity — rather than semantic; the view that what the tolerance premise really captures is a norm of assertion rather than a truth-condition; and the view that the fault is in classical logic rather than in the semantics. Where your tree leaves a combination of answers unoccupied, say so and diagnose the vacancy — is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended? — and where two of my required leaves come apart on no case you can find, flag them as a pair your tree cannot separate instead of pretending to split them. If two different paths lead to the same leaf, say that too; I'd rather have a lattice honestly labelled than a tree with invented forks.

Aim for roughly a dozen nodes and seven to twelve leaves, in whatever format is clearest for a handout — indented outline, text flowchart, whatever suits. Please don't hedge the thing into uselessness: where a branch calls for a judgement about which reading of a position is the more defensible one, make the call. And close with two or three sentences saying which leaf you would actually defend and the single strongest reason to doubt it, so that the sheet ends with a position rather than a menu.
