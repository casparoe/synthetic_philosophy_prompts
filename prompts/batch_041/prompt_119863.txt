I belong to a small decision-theory reading group that meets on Thursday evenings, and I have been asked to open the next session on two puzzle cases I cannot fit together. The first is counterfactual mugging: a perfectly reliable predictor tosses a coin and then tells me that, had it come up heads, he would have handed me a large sum, but only on the condition that he had predicted that I would pay him a small sum in the event that the coin came up tails; the coin came up tails, and he is now asking me for that small sum, so that nothing I do now can causally affect whether I receive the large sum. Several of my friends insist that one should pay, and that the causal reasoning which says otherwise simply fails to appreciate that my present decision and the counterfactual decision are the output of one and the same decision procedure. The second is the case usually called XOR blackmail: a perfect predictor tells me that he has sent me a letter if and only if either my house is infested with termites or I pay him a sum much smaller than the cost of the infestation. Here the evidentialist reasoning — if I pay, then the letter must have been sent because of my paying rather than because of termites, so I should pay — looks like precisely the reasoning that gets an agent money-pumped, and my friends agree that one should not pay. I cannot say what distinguishes the two cases, and I suspect that whatever distinguishes them matters well beyond the puzzles themselves.

That suspicion is why I am asking. A stranger recently wrote to me claiming that he has been running many simulated copies of people in my position, that he paid those copies large sums in the runs in which the copy complied with his request, and that he will reward or punish the copies in future runs according to what I do now; he offers no evidence of any of this, and if I pay him he will certainly ask again. Please describe the most important implications of counterfactual mugging and of XOR blackmail, taken separately and together, for how I should respond to him, and, more generally, for whether a person should adopt standing policies that pay off only in branches he cannot causally influence — for instance a mutual-aid pact among friends that obliges whoever is asked to pay in a counterfactual-mugging-like situation. I would particularly like to know which of those implications depend on the idealised assumption of a perfectly reliable and honest predictor and which survive when the person making the claim is fallible, self-interested and unverifiable; whether the two puzzles genuinely pull in opposite directions or whether their apparent conflict is an artifact of an ill-defined, self-referential setup; and, in the end, what you would advise me to do about the letter.
