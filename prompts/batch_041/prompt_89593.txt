I'm teaching a small upper-level seminar on rational choice this term -- mostly philosophy majors, plus two econ students who keep trying to reduce everything to a utility function -- and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question built around the Death in Damascus case, the one where Death informs you he'll be coming for you and has never once been wrong about what a person will do, you can stay put or run to the other city, and either way he turns up in whichever city you chose, so there's no safe option, and yet each city somehow looks better when you're standing in the other one. My fear is that my students will just recite the labels ("causal decision theory says run, evidential decision theory says it doesn't matter") and stop, which misses the entire point. What I actually want them chewing on is why the case is supposed to be a problem for causal decision theory in particular -- whether its advice here is even stable enough to count as advice, i.e. whether it's the sort of recommendation you could still endorse after finding yourself having acted on it -- and why the move they'd make on Newcomb's problem (take both boxes, the contents are already fixed and your choice doesn't cause them) can't just be transplanted. I'd also like them to say something about what evidential decision theory does with the case, and whether "both options are fatal, so it doesn't matter" is a real answer or a shrug.

So to stop them pattern-matching, I want the question to include a variant I've been kicking around with a colleague. Suppose the trip out of the city is a genuine hassle -- you have to sell your belongings, the camel ride is miserable, whatever -- so fleeing costs you something, call it c, while staying costs nothing. Ask them what EDT says about that version and what CDT says, and whether either recommendation passes the test above. I'm half-convinced the cost actually sharpens the whole thing, because it forces EDT to give a definite answer instead of shrugging, but leave room for a student to disagree with me about that. I'm also curious whether anyone will notice that the CDT agent's arithmetic in the original case implies a survival probability that can't possibly be right given what Death told you -- if a student spots that, I want to be able to give credit for it. Oh, and one more small part I'd like tacked on: ask them which steps of their reasoning depend on Death being infallible rather than merely very good, and what happens to each theory if his predictions are only, say, ninety percent reliable.

Deliverable: it's a 90-minute in-class final and this will be one of three questions, so it should be answerable in about 25 minutes and I'm giving it 20 marks. Please write (1) the question itself, self-contained -- they can't look anything up and I don't want the answer to hinge on knowing who wrote about the case or when; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words; and (3) a marking scheme that names the specific moves a top answer has to make, what each is worth out of 20, and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. Prose, definately no multiple choice.
