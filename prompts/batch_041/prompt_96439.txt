Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether any good is genuinely off the market, and I came away certain that I was right and unable to say why. His position is the clean one: wherever two adults who understand the terms can say yes to a price, the price is doing useful work — moving the thing toward whoever wants it most — and the unease that certain transactions provoke is evidence about our stomachs rather than about the goods themselves. Mine is the muddy one: I keep wanting to say that some things are not merely misallocated by a price but altered by it, and whenever I try to spell that out I hear myself defending a taboo instead of an argument. I want to see his view stated from the inside, at full strength, by someone with no incentive to be coy.

Please write a letter to shareholders, about 700 words, in the deadpan register of a confident annual report, with a title, from the chief executive of a company that runs a legal market in human kidneys.

A few requirements.

The company's signature product is not cash: donors are compensated in transferable "priority credits," redeemable for preferential access to a future organ, and the credits trade on a secondary market. The letter should present this as a matter of commercial taste, and should never notice what it concedes.

The letter must handle each of the following as an ordinary matter of business, without naming any of them as an objection and without conceding an inch: that what is bought is the donor's time, risk and inconvenience rather than her organ; that the price may be paid less by the buyer than by the seller's circumstances; that paying donors may reduce the number who give for nothing; that a shortage of organs is a failure of price rather than of supply; that a single regulated buyer setting one price may be better or worse for the poorest seller than no market at all; and that a price may corrupt a good rather than merely reallocate it.

Include one quotation from an opposing bioethicist. The critic must be invented — do not put words in the mouth of any real person — and the quotation should be the strongest argument in the piece, which the chief executive reports as a misunderstanding.

No citations or references to real scholarship. Someone who has followed the commodification debate should find the position internally consistent rather than a straw man, and someone who has not should still be able to tell what is being satirized. Keep the chief executive reasonable, fluent and unbothered; the discomfort, if there is any, should come from his competence rather than from any doubt of his own.
