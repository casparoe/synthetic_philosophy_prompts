The argument I keep meeting runs like this: a computer simulation of a storm wets no one, however fine-grained it is, so a program that reproduces the functional organisation of a human brain would be an elaborate accounting of a mind rather than a mind — hence no sentience and no moral status. The same person also insists that a simulated computation is a computation, that a program which adds is adding. Please reconcile those two claims or show that they cannot both be held. Then audit the storm analogy rather than dismiss it: set out the correspondence (storm, simulation, wetness, substrate, program, phenomenal state), separate the disanalogies that bear on the moral-status conclusion from those that are idle, and say whether the conclusion survives them. I am also uneasy that the analogy assumes there is a determinate fact about which computation a given physical system implements, when it may be that almost any system can be described as implementing almost any program; if that is so, say what becomes of the analogy. If a better analogy makes the anti-simulationist point, supply it; if none does, explain why the point resists analogical statement. Use British English throughout, and finish with a single line of the form \answer{...}, the braces containing a short phrase that states your verdict and the one feature it turns on.
