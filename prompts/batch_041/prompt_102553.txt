I have come to accept the standard deflationary response to Skolem's paradox, and I want you to tell me what would count against it. The response I have in mind runs roughly as follows: the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem delivers a countable model of set theory, but 'uncountable', as the model uses it, means only that there is no bijection between the relevant set and the model's natural numbers inside the model; since the model is countable from the outside, such a bijection does exist in the wider universe, and the apparent contradiction dissolves once we keep the object language and the metalanguage firmly apart. On this view the paradox is no antinomy at all but a lesson about the expressive limits of first-order languages and the danger of confusing a model's internal notions with our own.

I want a serious inventory of the pressure points. Which results, arguments, or possible cases would genuinely threaten the dissolution, and which would a committed defender simply absorb? In particular:

- How much does the resolution depend on choosing a transitive, well-founded model? If consistency alone gives us only a countable model that may be ill-founded, and whose 'natural numbers' may therefore be non-standard, does the clean story survive, or does it need restating? Is the appeal to transitivity an assumption the view cannot do without, and if so, is that a hidden cost?
- The dissolution treats countability as relative while, presumably, treating membership and finitude as not relative. What marks the line between notions that are absolute across models and notions that are not, and could the same reasoning that relativises countability be pressed further — to power sets, to the ordinals, or to set-theoretic truth generally? Where exactly does the argument stop, and on what principle?
- Does the deflationary line commit its holder to any claim about the intended interpretation of set theory? If our own 'uncountable' is supposed to be the privileged one, what makes it privileged, and would anything be lost if we admitted there is no fact of the matter about absolute cardinality? Does the paradox, taken this way, lend any support to a multiverse-style picture in which there is no single intended universe, or would that be an extra premise smuggled in?
- Is there a version of the paradox that survives the object/metalanguage distinction — for instance once the reasoning is embedded in a richer language, or once we move to a second-order formulation with full semantics, where Löwenheim–Skolem fails? If the paradox is averted only by staying first-order, is that a point in favour of the dissolution or merely a change of subject?

Rank these from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which of them the defender of the dissolution should concede and which they can fairly dismiss, and be honest about whether the position risks anything at all or has instead been insulated by qualifications. Say also what would change your own verdict: which single consideration would lead you to abandon the deflationary line, and where your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
