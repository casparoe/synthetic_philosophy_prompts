I'm enrolled in a graduate seminar on social ontology, and I've been stuck for two weeks on a distinction that everyone in the room treats as obvious but that I cannot get to do any real work. The distinction is between two ways of explaining why a local convention could bind a visitor. On the first account, a convention is nothing but a coordination equilibrium: everyone expects everyone else to do a certain thing, deviations degrade an expectation that is already serving everyone, and whatever obligation the visitor has is exhausted by the duty not to foul a working scheme. On the second account, a convention is a claim that a community holds over its own shared space, so that conforming is a form of recognition owed to the community as such — owed even when the visitor's nonconformity would damage nothing and no one would notice. My friend, who is writing on this, thinks the gap between the two accounts is stark and shows up immediately in the humdrum cases of tipping, queueing, and jaywalking, and she has challenged me to say which account handles each case better. I would like you to compare the two accounts by running all three cases through both of them.

Take them one at a time and be concrete. For tipping, consider both the ordinary visitor who underpays against local custom and the visitor who conspicuously overpays; for queueing, consider the tourist who cuts a line because he did not know there was a line, and the local who cuts a line because he knows no one will enforce it; for jaywalking, consider the pedestrian who crosses against the signal when the street is empty and when the street is full of children. For each case, say what each account predicts about whether there is an obligation at all, whom it is owed to, and what the visitor is permitted to do when the local custom strikes him as wrong or merely silly. Then tell me whether the two accounts are genuinely distinct, or whether the first quietly collapses into the second as soon as we ask what damage to a coordination scheme really amounts to — my friend insists they come apart, and I want to know whether she is right.

Finally, I want a verdict, not a survey. Which of the two accounts, if either, gives the better answer to the question whether everyday conventions are moral obligations at all, and which gives the better answer to the question whether a visitor must follow local ones? Say plainly where each account is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and tell me which of the three cases is the hardest for each. Please write in American English.
