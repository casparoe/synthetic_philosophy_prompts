Six months ago I agreed to take on the cataloguing of a small local history collection — a few thousand items from a valley whose economy was coal for about a century and has been something else since — and the work has become personal in a way I did not anticipate. While sorting a box of overseers' papers I went looking for my own great-grandmother and found that the whole documentary trace of her life amounts to a line in a household schedule (the census form her employer filled in), an entry in a removal order (the paperwork for sending a pauper back to the parish that was liable for her), and her name in the margin of a letter written by somebody else about somebody else. She is in the record only as a by-product of other people's business. I have stopped being able to treat that as a gap I simply work around, and I want to think it through properly. What I need is not an essay but an instrument.

Please act as an archivist-historian with a firm grounding in the philosophy of history and in critical work on record-keeping and power — someone equally at home with appraisal, arrangement and description and with the conceptual questions about what a record is and what it is for. Think carefully before you write, and work in stages: first brainstorm, for your own use, the distinct questions on which positions about archival silence genuinely divide; then build the instrument; then test it against my cases and repair whatever it fails on. You should be able to build this without leaning on attributions to particular authors, and where a position has no standard name, say so rather than inventing a school.

The deliverable is a taxonomy of positions on archival silence, laid out as a tree: every branch node is a question, every leaf is a position. Then:

- Build the questions first and place the positions afterwards. A reader should be able to answer every node from their own practice without knowing where it leads. No node may be a dressed-up version of 'do you think archives are political?'
- Somewhere on the tree, put to work rather than merely list the difference between four kinds of silence: a record never made; a record made and later destroyed; a record made, still held, but never described, so that in practice nobody can find it; and a record made, described, and never read. One of my cases is exactly the third kind. Let the tree decide whether these are four phenomena or one.
- Also on the tree: whether the remedy for a silence is more evidence (recovery, oral history, reading against the grain) or a changed relation to the absence itself; whether the wrong is epistemic — we misdescribe the past — or something other than epistemic; and whether an archivist's obligation runs to the dead, to the living community, or to the record.
- Include at least one leaf holding that the demand for inclusion is itself part of the problem, and at least one holding that the four silences are not four but one, with the reason stated.
- List every combination of answers the tree permits that no position occupies, and diagnose each briefly: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unoccupied?
- Flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to separate them.
- Each leaf carries the name of the position (or 'unnamed'), one sentence on what it holds, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The cases the instrument has to handle, at least one node apiece — and please make the reader answer the case, not recognise it as an illustration afterwards:

(a) a workhouse minute book that records every decision taken about a named pauper but records the pauper's own words only when they were read back at a disciplinary hearing;
(b) a photographic album whose captions name three generations of the family that owned it and identify nobody else in the frame;
(c) a bundle of letters that survives only because the recipient was an official who filed his correspondence;
(d) a shipping company's crew lists, which record wages, ages and ports of engagement but not what languages anyone spoke;
(e) the undescribed backlog: a large share of a repository's holdings described only at collection level, so that individual items are not lost, not closed, merely unfindable;
(f) my great-grandmother.

Finally, give me a scoring key: for each leaf, the pattern of answers to the earlier nodes that lands someone there, in two or three lines, so that the tree doubles as a sorting quiz I can hand to the other volunteers.

Write in British English throughout — spellings, punctuation and idiom.
