The question:

"In a thought experiment sometimes called the careenium — I've seen it credited to Hofstadter, though people keep telling me it's Dennett and I always mix up those two — imagine an enormous number of tiny magnetic marbles, 'simms,' bouncing around on a frictionless surface. Simms sometimes stick together on contact and form clusters called 'simmballs,' which can themselves drift, collide and split, gaining and losing simms at their edges. Suppose a careenium big enough, taken as a whole, reliably behaves as if it has beliefs and makes decisions — in the plain functional sense, it is thinking. The simmball level is where the symbolic/meaningful roles live (one simmball is 'the one that stands for the threat,' and so on), while the simm level is the brute substrate. Does the symbolic level push the substrate around, or is the simm-level story the only one that is causally real? Address head-on the worry that, since every simm's motion is fully fixed by the micro-mechanics, the simmball-level story must be causally idle — a mere redescription of what the simms were doing anyway."

And here's the student's answer:

"The careenium looks like it forces a choice, but the choice is false, because the two descriptions aren't rivals. The simm story and the simmball story describe the same goings-on at two levels, and there's no need to say one of them 'really' does the pushing while the other tags along. When a simmball drifts left, that just is a large number of simms doing what they do; no extra event has to be added on top of the simms' motions. So the worry that the simmball level is causally idle rests on a mistake — it assumes that for the simmball level to be causal it would have to introduce some force not already present in the micro-story, and no causal claim has to be like that.

That said, the two stories aren't on a par. The micro-story is in principle complete — you could in principle derive everything from the simm mechanics — but it's useless for explaining or predicting the careenium's behaviour. If you want to know why it ducked, you don't trace simms, you cite the simmball that represented the threat. Explanations are interest-relative, and the simmball level is the level at which the interesting generalisations and counterfactuals hold. The traffic-jam analogy makes this vivid: a jam is caused by rush hour, not by any particular car's engine, even though the jam is nothing but cars.

The mistake would be to think the simmball level is a further entity with its own causal powers, a kind of downward causation where the macro level reaches down and shoves the micro. It isn't that. The macro level is irreducible in the sense that you can't get the simmball explanations out of the simm story in any manageable way, but it isn't a separate layer of causation. So the honest answer is that the symbolic level is a description we find indispensable — a convenient gloss that lets us navigate a system whose micro-details we could never track — rather than something the world contains over and above the simms. If I had to say who pushes whom: both, but only in the deflationary sense that the two stories are two ways of telling the same causal tale, and the simmball story is the one worth telling."
