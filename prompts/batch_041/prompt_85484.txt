I teach a seminar on the moral status of fictional and virtual beings, and I want a sustained reading of Shadow of the Colossus (the PlayStation 2 game and its later remake) that takes the game and the philosophy equally seriously. I am going to set out the philosophical scaffolding I want used, because it is not the standard framing and I do not want it quietly swapped for the standard one.

The standard framing asks whether a video-game character can be wronged, and answers no: the colossi have no welfare, no interests, no experience, and nothing is done to anyone. That answer is correct as far as it goes, and I want it stated rather than dodged. But it leaves two things unexplained, and those are what the reading should work on.

First, the two-ledger picture. Moral bookkeeping in a game can run on the ledger of the fiction, where ex hypothesi no one is wronged, or on the ledger of the player, where what is at stake is not a character's welfare but the player's own conduct: what she is willing to do, what she attends to, what she becomes in doing it. A good reading says which ledger the game actually draws on, and whether it can honestly draw on the second without smuggling the first back in.

Second, the possibility I care most about: acts that are wrong even though no one is wronged. If cruelty, desecration, or betrayal can be wrong in themselves — if a wrong can be constituted rather than suffered — then "do video-game characters matter morally?" may be the wrong question, and the right one may be whether there is a class of acts a person should not perform even in a world where nothing is at stake but herself. Games are unusually good laboratories for this, because they can make the player the sole locus of an act. Call it a complicity mechanic: the fiction marks an act as wrong and then requires it of the player in order to proceed, with no in-fiction excuse and no alternative.

Here are the beats I want the reading to rest on; if you recall the game differently, say so rather than papering over it. Wander comes to the Forbidden Land carrying a dead girl, Mono, and a disembodied voice tells him she can be restored if he destroys sixteen colossi. The colossi are ancient, monumental, and do not attack first; they fight only once he does, and most read as living creatures rather than antagonists. Each death releases a black substance that enters Wander, and he visibly degrades as the game proceeds. At the end he is transformed into a horned being, destroyed by the pursuing priests, and the girl wakes; the colossi turn out to have been the seals on what the voice was, so the player's success is also the catastrophe. The game offers no branch and no refusal.

What I want from you: a reading that uses the two-ledger picture and the idea of wrongless wrongs to say what the game dramatizes and what it merely assumes. Address specifically whether the complicity mechanic succeeds here, whether the game is addressing the player or the character, and who should be counted the author of the deed — the designer who made it necessary or the player who performed it. Say where the game resists this reading and the honest critic has to concede it, and finish by saying what the game adds to the debate that argument alone does not. Write roughly a thousand words, in continuous prose. Do not quote the game's text, do not cite or recommend literature, and do not lean on any particular theorist by name; if a point would require scholarship you cannot reconstruct, say so rather than inventing it.

End with a final line of exactly the form

Verdict: <LEDGER> — <one sentence>

where <LEDGER> is one of FICTION, PLAYER, BOTH, or NEITHER, and the sentence gives the bottom line of the reading.
