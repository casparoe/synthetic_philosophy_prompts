I would like a short story that dramatizes a problem in the philosophy of pleasure which is easy to state and unexpectedly hard to resolve — the heterogeneity problem. Since I want the story to stand on its own for a reader who has never encountered the debate, let me set the problem out as I would give it to a writer.

We apply one word, "pleasure," to experiences that appear to share no single phenomenal feature: the cool relief of a headache lifting, the taste of something sweet, the satisfaction of a proof that closes, the ease of a friend's company, the pleasure of scratching an itch, the pleasure of anticipating a holiday. Each is pleasant, yet it is hard to name what they have in common beyond the word. This creates trouble for any view that treats pleasure as a homogeneous quantity to be maximized — a hedonic calculus in the Benthamite style — because it appears to sum things that may not be commensurable. The two standard repairs are (i) that pleasure is a determinable admitting many heterogeneous determinates, held together by family resemblance rather than a common core, and (ii) that pleasure is not a felt quality at all but an attitude of liking directed at an experience; each repair has costs, and the story need not adjudicate between them.

Write a short story of roughly three thousand words set in an institution, laboratory, or society that has taken the hedonic calculus literally — somewhere pleasure is measured, aggregated, and administered as a matter of policy. I want the plot to turn on the heterogeneity problem rather than merely mention it. Specific requirements:

- Include at least one scene in which a character must make a real trade-off between two pleasures of different kinds and discovers that the comparison has no answer independent of a convention someone has imposed — and show what that convention costs.
- Include a character who proposes a common currency (a neural signature, a hedonic meter, a physiological proxy) and let the story test whether this dissolves the problem or merely relocates it.
- Avoid the standard set-pieces: no experience machine, no soma, no wireheading, no organ-harvesting trolley. Find your own image for the difficulty.
- Do not resolve the story with a lecture or a twist that makes the problem vanish. Let at most one character articulate the philosophical position in dialogue, and let the narrator stay neutral. A reader who has never heard of the debate should finish the story able to state the problem in their own words.

The deliverable is the story itself, with a title.
