Here's my opening move. Two players sit in seperate rooms with no communication, and each knows the other is a perfect reasoner running the same procedure. Each privately picks a whole number from 1 to 10. If the two numbers differ, each player gets a dollar; if they match, each gets nothing. Now, your act-level story says: ask what would happen if my twin picked whatever I pick, then pick the act that fares best on that assumption. But every act is worth zero on that assumption — pick any number and my twin picks it too, and we both walk away with nothing. So act-level superrationality either hands me advice that guarantees I earn zero or has nothing at all to say. Meanwhile, if you climb a level and say I should choose the policy that fares best given my twin runs the same policy, then I just randomize uniformly over 1 through 10 and clear ninety cents in expectation, and no deterministic act is even a contender. So which is it: does the view prescribe an act that guarantees failure, or does it quietly stop being about acts? And if it's the second, what distinguishes your position from the claim that rational agents simply play some Nash equilibrium of the policy game — which, last I checked, is neither unique nor obviously binding on anyone?
