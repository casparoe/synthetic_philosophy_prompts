I have no philosophical training; what follows is just what I've worked out on my own over a couple of years of reading and arguing with myself. I've ended up with a set of views about love, impartiality, and what makes an action defensible, and I have a nagging sense that they don't all fit together. I'd like an audit before I go any further.

(1) Morality is impartial at the root. Everyone's interests count equally, and nothing about the fact that someone is mine makes their interests count for more.

(2) I've always thought that a parent who rushes out of a meeting to get to a sick child, and who first runs through whether they are permitted to leave, has already lost something. The thought should be: my kid needs me. Nothing further.

(3) Love and friendship are partly constituted by partiality. Someone who would trade a friend for a stranger at the same cost does not have the kind of relationship I am talking about.

(4) What makes an action defensible, in the end, is that it could be defended to the people it affects — not out loud, but in principle. That is the test I actually use.

(5) A moral theory ought to return a verdict on every case, at least in principle, even where I would never manage to work it out in the moment.

And here is the case that set this off. If I could either spend the evening with my daughter or go to a meeting that would help twenty strangers, I think I should be with my daughter, and I do not think I owe those twenty anything I could say to them. I would go, and I would have nothing to offer them — and I don't think that makes it a mere excuse rather than the right thing to do.

Which of these can I hold together? Tell me which pairs are genuinely in conflict and how badly, and which only look like they are. For each real conflict, give me the cheapest revision and what it costs me. Rank the conflicts by severity, then commit: tell me which one has to go, and what should take its place.
