For a few years now I have been part of an intermittent argument about futarchy — the governance proposal, associated with Robin Hanson, of deciding policy by conditional prediction markets while settling the goal by vote — and the exchange has hardened into a ritual. The proponents advance two claims: that a conditional market on a proposition such as the one that national income will exceed a stated threshold if policy P is adopted aggregates dispersed information better than any committee or electorate; and that fixing the objective by vote keeps the mechanism normatively neutral with respect to ends. The critics, whose side I largely share, advance three, and I can never get a proponent to concede any of them. First, that thin, manipulable, and self-fulfilling markets will not reliably track the truth. Second, that the separation of values from beliefs is spurious, because the mechanism requires a single measurable objective, and the choice of that metric — income, welfare, some weighted index — already imports a contested theory of what matters, so the genuinely hard value question is not answered but relocated. Third, that even a perfectly accurate market would not confer legitimacy on the resulting decision, since authority must derive from something other than predictive success.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that these are not three objections to one thesis but responses to two different theses that share a name: a narrow mechanism, in which conditional markets merely inform a decision-maker who has already fixed an objective, and a broad political program, in which betting markets displace or subordinate democratic deliberation. If that is so, much of the dispute may be a category mistake, with each side defending or attacking the thesis the other is not advancing.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement verbal, turning on what is to count as a market or as a value; substantive, turning on facts about market accuracy and manipulation; a difference about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data; or a clash of bedrock normative commitments — the authority of deliberation against the authority of outcomes — that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, for instance the incentive-compatibility or the manipulability of conditional markets? Are the two sides simply answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a change of question — or whether the impasse should be expected to persist, and why. I want reasons drawn from what each side actually claims, not a survey of the literature.
