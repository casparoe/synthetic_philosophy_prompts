For a graduate seminar I teach on practical reason, a guest lecturer has circulated a handout arguing as follows. Just as a set of beliefs that violates logic is defective because a contradiction can be made to yield anything, a set of preferences that violates the expected-utility axioms is defective because a bookmaker can arrange things so that the agent pays out for certain; the money pump, on this view, stands to the axioms of expected utility as a proof of contradiction stands to the laws of logic, and intransitivity is therefore a real vulnerability rather than an aesthetic blemish — a bug, the handout says, not a style. From this the handout concludes that expected-utility maximization is the unique standard of instrumental rationality, not merely one respectable convention among others.

I suspect the conclusion is being carried by the analogy rather than by the argument. Please audit it for me. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the belief, the contradiction, the proof, and the defective agent — and then mark where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in whether the breaks that matter are the obvious ones (the pump is diachronic, it needs the agent to go along at each step, it ignores foresight, commitment devices, and transaction costs, and it needs a single agent to face all three pairwise trades in sequence) or something deeper about what a consistency requirement can demand of an agent whose preferences are never all exercised at once. Say which breaks are fatal to the conclusion as stated and which are mere complications, tell me whether any surviving version of the argument delivers uniqueness rather than bare permission, and if a better analogy is available, give me that too.
