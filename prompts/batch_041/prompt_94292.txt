I have been trying to settle what I think the boundary of a democratic people is, and I would rather do it the way I would with any other concept: put my verdicts on a set of cases on the table and ask what principle, if any, they express. I will use three levels of inclusion rather than two — a vote (full membership in the demos), a hearing (notice, a place at the table, reasons given, and perhaps a narrow veto over decisions that fall especially hard), and nothing at all.

My verdicts:

1. A woman who moved to the country at nineteen, has lived there twenty-two years, works, pays taxes, and never got around to naturalizing: she should have a vote. Her citizenship papers are a formality; her life is the country's.
2. Her brother, who emigrated at the same time, went home after three years, and has voted by post from abroad ever since while renting out the apartment he still owns: he should not have a vote.
3. Her nine-year-old daughter, born there, a citizen: no vote.
4. A forty-year-old man under full guardianship, born there, who cannot manage his own affairs: he should have a vote. His guardian should not vote in his place; he should vote.
5. Four hundred thousand guest workers on temporary contracts, subject to every law of the country, expected eventually to go home: a vote.
6. A foreign diplomat with immunity, resident for a decade: no vote.
7. The residents of a territory the country governs — its statutes, its courts, no representation in its legislature, no citizenship of it: a vote in that legislature, or whatever is equivalent to one.
8. Farmers across the border whose water the country's dam authority allocates, and whose crops depend on the allocation: not a vote — I do not think they belong to the demos — but more than nothing. Notice, a seat at the table, reasons.
9. A foreign consumer who pays more for the country's exports because of its trade policy: nothing at all.
10. People not yet born, who will live with a decision about long-lived waste: not a vote, and I keep going back and forth on whether they get a hearing, or a trustee with a veto over the irreversible decisions, or nothing.

The principle I would tentatively offer is this: you belong to the demos when the polity's ordinary functioning depends on your compliance, and you cannot leave without bearing a cost no one would call reasonable. On that principle, 1, 5 and 7 are in; 2, 6, 8 and 9 are out; and 3 and 4 are both in — which is not what I said about 3. That is the pair I cannot reconcile.

Tell me what principle actually gets these verdicts, or gets most of them. If it takes two principles, one for membership and one for the hearing, give me both, tell me which clause is doing the real work and which case each clause exists to save. If saving all my verdicts requires a clause that earns its keep nowhere except the cases it was built for, say so, and tell me which verdict to give up instead. Then take the repaired principle to a case I have not listed and tell me what it says; I would rather be surprised by an implication than flattered by a fit.

There is one further worry I want addressed head-on: does any such principle constrain who a demos may include, or does it only redescribe what an existing demos has already settled? If it is the latter, say so plainly. I would rather know that the boundary is a decision than keep hunting for the rule that makes it not one.

Finally, say how confident you are in the principle you land on, and identify the point in it you are least sure of.
