I'm a novelist, and I've been stuck for two weeks on a scene where a guy's jealousy makes him do something stupid, because I keep asking myself whether the jealousy is really doing anything or whether it's just the neurons and the jealousy is a passenger. I mentioned this at dinner and it turned into a whole thing between my partner (who did a philosophy PhD before he sensibly became a lawyer) and our friend who runs a lab and thinks philosophers are all frauds. They went at it for an hour and I could follow maybe sixty percent of it, and honestly the sixty percent I followed was the best fun I've had all year. So now I want a proper referee's report on the actual exchange, because I still don't know who to believe and I'd like to before I write the chapter.

Here's my reconstruction, and I've tried to be fair to both of them. (1) My partner said: once you've fixed the brain state, the arm moves — the neurons are sufficient on their own. If you also insist the jealousy did it, you've got two causes where one will do, and worse, the neural one already guaranteed the effect, so the jealousy can't be *the* cause too. Either the jealousy just is the brain state, in which case it's not a second cause, it's the same one under a different name, or it's something extra that does nothing at all. There's no third option. (2) Our friend said: that only works if you think a cause is something that guarantees its effect. That's a bad model of causation. Ask what would happen if the jealousy had been slightly different — milder, aimed at a different person — and the arm movement changes too, in ways the neural story doesn't capture at any level anyone actually cares about. Causes are difference-makers, and the same event can be a difference-maker under lots of true descriptions. Nothing competes, because competition only makes sense between causes that make the same difference at the same level. (3) My partner said: fine, that just renames the problem. Now you've got the neural description and the mental description tracking the same difference, and you owe me an account of which one *is* the cause, or you've given up on there being a fact of the matter. And the moment you ask which is the more fundamental difference-maker, the neural one wins, because you can intervene on it directly and the mental one only moves when the neural one does. That's a decent working definition of being carried along for the ride. (4) Our friend said: more fundamental isn't the same as more causal, otherwise nothing above particle physics causes anything and you'd have to say the economy doesn't cause recessions, which is absurd. And directness of intervention is an artifact of which tools we happen to have. Besides, the mental description has something the neural one lacks — it generalizes. Any brain state that produced that exact movement would be a different physical state in a different person, but "jealousy" picks out the thing that travels.

Then it got genuinely interesting and I lost the thread. (5) My partner said: careful, because your own principle cuts against you. If a cause has to be *proportional* to its effect, then in plenty of cases the very specific neural story is the proportional one and "jealousy" is just a vague label for a big disjunction. And there's a real line of thought in the causation literature — I think he said it's about specificity — that the more determinate difference-maker is often the *better* cause, that specificity is a virtue in a cause. So the mental doesn't automatically win the proportionality contest; it loses it whenever the details are what matter. (6) Our friend said: then your specificity principle says that when someone dies of poisoning, the poison didn't kill him, only the particular metabolic event did, and that the rock didn't break the window, only the molecules did. Specificity can't be the whole story, or all our ordinary causal talk is systematically false — and if you're willing to swallow that, you're not defending physicalism anymore, you're defending the view that nothing but microphysics causes anything, which is a much bigger pill than the one you were trying to take.
