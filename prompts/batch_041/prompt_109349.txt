We run a little philosophy-and-poetry night in my department and this month I've drawn Fitch's paradox of knowability, so I need a poem. Take on the role of a poet who knows the proof cold — the derivation from "every truth is knowable in principle" to "every truth is in fact known by somebody", which turns on that sneaky conjunction that says of some truth that it's true and that nobody knows it, and which can't be known without being false. Before you write a line, think out loud for a few sentences about what happens to that conjunction when someone finally knows it, then draft.

I want a pantoum: four-line stanzas, lines 2 and 4 of each stanza reappearing as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the final stanza looping back to close on the opening. The form has to do the philosophy rather than just decorate it — the words of a repeated line should stay put while the new context makes them say the stronger thing, so that a truth the poem began by saying nobody knows comes round again as a truth somebody knows. If that works, the reader walks the derivation without ever being told about it. Rhyme if it falls out naturally; don't force it.

Anchor the whole thing in one banal unobserved truth — the exact number of grains in a handful of sand, the angle of a particular moth's wing at a moment nobody was looking, that kind of thing — instead of talking about propositions in the abstract.

Dont name Fitch, dont use jargon like the knowability thesis, and no epigraphs or citations. Four or five stanzas is plenty. Someone who knows the paradox should find the structure right, and someone who doesn't should still come away with a poem about the impossibility of saying the one thing no one knows. British English throughout.
