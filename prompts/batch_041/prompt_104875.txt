I am at work on a novel whose central character is a lender in a small commercial town, a man of some reading and considerable self-regard, and I have given him a speech in which he defends his trade on Aristotelian grounds. Before I can write the scene honestly I need to know whether the grounds I have given him are the ones Aristotle actually supplies, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether I am misreading the text or whether the text is divided against itself. I have no Greek and no wish to acquire any; what I want is help with the argument.

As I understand the relevant stretch of the first book of the Politics: Aristotle separates two kinds of acquisition, one belonging to the management of a household, which is bounded by the needs of that household, and another that he treats as a later and lesser development, which has no natural limit and takes money itself rather than the goods money buys as its end. Retail trade falls under the second and is therefore not natural, though he grants that exchange arose naturally enough out of barter and out of the need to bring in what a household lacked. He remarks that every possession has two uses, as a shoe may be worn or may be traded for something else, and he treats the second use as legitimate in a manner but not as the use proper to the thing. Money, he says, exists by agreement rather than by nature, and yet it also serves as the measure of need and the medium of exchange. Then, near the end, he singles out lending at interest as the most hated form of moneymaking and the one with the best reason to be hated, on the ground that it makes a gain out of money itself rather than out of the natural use of money, and he adds something to the effect that money was instituted for exchange and not for breeding.

What I cannot make cohere is this. If exchange is natural in origin, and if trading a thing is one of its real uses, then the merchant who buys cheap and sells dear is doing something Aristotle seems at least half to allow. The lender, by contrast, exchanges nothing; he sells the use of the money for a period. Is that a third use, or the same second use stretched over time? Aristotle calls it the worst case rather than merely a further case, and I want to know what principle makes it worse. Is it that the gain is unlimited where the borrower's need was limited? Is it that money, being conventional, cannot have a proper use that could be violated? Or is it that usury turns the measure back into a commodity, so that the very thing meant to render goods commensurable becomes the object of an appetite it was supposed to restrain?

Please tell me, in plain terms and without assuming I have read the commentators: first, what the distinction between the two kinds of acquisition really amounts to when stated carefully, and where I am overreading it; second, whether the two-uses-of-a-shoe remark is doing any work in the usury argument or is a separate point I have wrongly fused to it; third, whether the conventional status of money creates a difficulty for calling any use of it natural or unnatural; and fourth, what the strongest version of the anti-usury argument would be and where its weakest joint lies. I would also like to know whether the account of exchange as growing out of need sits in tension with the condemnation of retail trade, or whether that is a tension I am importing. My lender must not be a straw man; if his Aristotle is a bad Aristotle, I would rather know now and give him a better one.
