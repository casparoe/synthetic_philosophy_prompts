So I've been going down a rabbit hole on the 1995 Quebec referrendum and the weird constitutional aftershocks — the No side squeaking it out around 50.6 to 49.4, the federal government refusing to just take yes for an answer and kicking the whole thing over to the Supreme Court, the 1998 advisory opinion saying Quebec couldn't unilaterally walk out but that a clear majority answering a clear question would trigger a duty to negotiate, and then the Clarity Act a couple of years later trying to spell out what even makes a question clear. I want to run that whole episode through the remedial-right-only theory of secession — the view that a group has a right to break away only when it's been on the receiving end of serious injustice (the classic colonial cases, systematic denial of rights, that sort of thing), and not simply because a majority of its members want out. On the face of it Quebec was never oppressed in that sense: prosperous, its own functioning government, language rights, seats in the federal parliament. So a strict remedial-right-only theorist should say there was no right to secede at all, and that the referendum, however close, was a moral non-starter. But the Court didn't say that. It said there's no unilateral right, yet a clear democratic expression would obligate the rest of the country to come to the table. So what I want is a careful mapping: who's the supposed rightholder here, what would the injustice actually have to be for the theory to even switch on, is the clear-question / clear-majority standard doing any genuine principled work or is it just the federal side quietly reserving the right to define the terms, and does that negotiation duty smuggle a plebiscitary or primary-right view in through the back door. Then flip it around: what does the episode tell us about the theory itself? Does it show remedial-right-only can't cope with real secession politics, or does it actually vindicate it by keeping the trigger out of the hands of a bare majority? And be upfront about the stuff the theory just doesn't speak to.
