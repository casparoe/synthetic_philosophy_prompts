I'm running a 75-minute session on modal realism for second-year undergrads who've had a bit of modal logic but no metaphysics, and I want a teaching plan, not an explanation of the view. What I keep getting wrong is the order. They've met possible worlds as harmless bookkeeping devices in semantics, so the whole difficulty is making them feel the step from "worlds are just useful posits / ways things could be" to "worlds are concrete universes of the same kind as this one," and then making the price of that step feel real rather than absurd. They'll also reach straight for "a world is just a maximal description of how things could be" and I want the sequence to make them see what that costs, without turning into a survey of alternatives. Plan it: what has to come first and what depends on what, which single example should carry the whole thing and what it must have and lack (and why the obvious sci-fi "alternate universe" example, or "a world where I had toast instead", wont do the work), where the indexical treatment of "actual" comes in — the way "now" is supposed to be indexical on a B-theory of time, or "here" on a map — and at which point that analogy breaks down and stops being helpful. Then the misconceptions they'll predictably form (my guesses: a possible world is a distant place you could in principle get to; if everything happens somewhere then nothing matters or everything is permitted; "concrete" means physical; actuality is a special intrinsic property some worlds have and ours happens to have) with exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off, one closing question that would show whether they've actually got the view, and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say so out loud.

Give it to me as JSON with these fields: sequence (array of {step, idea, depends_on, why_here}), carrying_example ({example, must_have, must_lack, why_not_the_obvious_one}), misconceptions (array of {misconception, where_in_sequence, how_to_head_it_off}), check_for_understanding ({question, what_a_good_answer_looks_like, what_a_bad_one_looks_like}), and leave_out (array of {thing, why}).
