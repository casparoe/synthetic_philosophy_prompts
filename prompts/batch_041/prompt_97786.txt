My sisters wedding is in three weeks and I'm giving the toast, and I've got myself into a strange spot with it.

Background, so you know where I'm coming from: I'm not academic at all, never went past first year of uni, but I've been chewing on this argument I read about by some philosopher (I want to say Williams? might be mixing him up with someone). The setup is a man whose wife is drowning, and he swims out and saves her. Fine, no controversy there. But the philosopher says — if, before he dives in, the man thinks about how she's his wife and this is what a husband does, or thinks about how saving her will do the most good, then he's had one thought too many. The point being that the love is supposed to be the whole reason, and the second you tack a justification onto it you've stepped outside it and turned into a little moral accountant instead of a husband. I think there's something really true in that and I want the toast to actually pull it off, not just name-drop it.

Heres why thats hard: a wedding toast is basically a machine for giving reasons. Everyone in the room expects me to list why this marriage is a good idea — she's kind, they balance each other out, it makes sense, all of it. And I don't want to do any of that. My sister is a philosopher and about half the room is her department, so they'll clock it instantly if I either wuss out or turn it into a seminar, and the other half is our aunts and uncles who just want to cry into their wine and have no idea what I'm on about.

The bit I can't solve, and I want the speech to solve it: her fiance actually asked her once, early on, 'why do you love me,' and she genuinely couldn't answer, and it almost sank them for a whole month. So the idea that you're not supposed to have reasons isn't a cute abstraction for them, it's the actual fault line in the relationship. I also half think the whole no-reasons thing is wrong — surely when things get hard you'd want a reason or two to hold onto — but I want the toast to play it straight anyway, no hedging, no winking at the audience about it.

So: write the toast, about four minutes, warm, a little funny, and it has to land for both halves of the room without me standing there explaining the drowning story out loud like a textbook. And I mean it about the reasons — not one line in it should give a reason why she should marry him. If you can only get one thing right, get that one right.
