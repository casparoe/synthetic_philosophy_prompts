I am a novelist, and for several years I have been circling a problem that I have only lately learned the philosophers have a name for: transformative experience — experience that is at once epistemically transformative, in that no description can tell you what it will be like until you have lived it, and personally transformative, in that it revises what you value and who you take yourself to be. The standard cases are becoming a parent, converting to a faith, going to war, or committing to a life's work. I would add the case that actually preoccupies me: a character offered a cure for a lifelong condition that would also dissolve the sensibility she has built her identity upon, so that she must choose between two selves who cannot both be consulted.

Please restate the main positions in the philosophical debate over whether such choices can be rational at all — and, if they can, how one is supposed to reason about them — in the vocabulary of my own craft, and then tell me what does not survive the translation.

Treat the vocabulary of narrative craft as the target framework, and assume I am fluent in it: character arc; want versus need; the lie the character believes; the turning point; scene and sequel; free indirect discourse; the distinction between the change a character undergoes and the change a reader undergoes; the fact that a plot is a machine for forcing irreversible decisions; and the special problem of the reread, where the reader who returns to page one is no longer the reader who began it. Translate each philosophical position into these terms as faithfully as you can, using my cure-or-sensibility case as the running example. I should flag one private distinction of my own that I want you to test against the literature: I separate a first-order transformation, in which the character changes, from a second-order transformation, in which the reader's own values are revised by the book, and I suspect the philosophers have a counterpart to the first and nothing at all for the second. Tell me whether that suspicion holds.

Then the residue report. Which claims come across cleanly? Which require distortion, and what is the nature of the distortion? Which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? And where something is lost, tell me whether the loss indicts the framework I work in or instead exposes an unclarity in the philosophers' formulation — whether, in other words, the novel is missing a concept, or the philosophy was never as precise as it sounded.
