I am a novelist, and next month I am to lead a session at a writers' residency — a mixed room of novelists and poets, all sharp readers with no formal training in philosophy — on Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. I have been living with the idea for three years while writing a novel whose narrator is a disgraced Leibniz scholar, and I have found that I cannot present it to my fellow writers without either sounding like Pangloss or making it seem a pious shrug at suffering. What I want from you is not an explanation of the doctrine but a plan for how I should explain it: the order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the conclusion arrives as a consequence rather than a slogan, and a clear account of what depends on what. What has to be in place before the claim can be heard as a claim rather than a joke? Which single example should carry the whole ninety minutes — I am tempted by the obvious ones, a earthquake that kills thousands or Voltaire's satire of the optimist, so tell me whether either can bear the weight, and if neither can, what the carrying example must have and must lack? And tell me where the standard of comparison enters the explanation, because my own persistent trouble is that I can never say what this world is being measured against.

The misconceptions I expect are these, though I may be wrong about their order: that best means every particular thing in the world is the best it could be; that Leibniz either denies that evil exists or calls it good; that best means the most pleasure or the least pain; and that possible means whatever I can imagine rather than whatever can be jointly realized. I keep bumping into the word compossible in my notes and suspect it is the hinge of the whole thing, but I do not know how to stage it so that it lands rather than merely gets defined. A philosopher friend also tells me that Leibniz thought the best world maximizes variety together with order, and that he likens its evils to shadows that make a painting more beautiful — I am drawn to this but do not trust myself to use it without turning it into a platitude. Tell me which of my expected misconceptions should be headed off at which point in the sequence, and which ones the audience will form only after I have said something true.

Finally, give me a check for understanding — one question I can put to the room at the end that would show whether the claim has actually landed rather than merely been admired — and tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level, and say so out loud. I would rather the session end with the audience holding a precise puzzle than with them holding a comfortable sentiment, and I suspect the difference lies entirely in the order of the ideas.
