So I run a philosophy-in-the-pub thing — about forty regulars, we meet Tuesdays in the back room of the King's Head. Last month we voted to kick out a guy who'd been coming for two years. Dave. He never said anything illegal, that's the thing, and some of his arguments were honestly good. But he'd worked out that if he kept insisting we debate whether two of our members deserved to be in the room at all, we'd spend every session on that instead of whatever we'd come for, and people just stopped showing up. I was the one who pushed hardest to get rid of him, and I'm also the one who invited him in the first place two years ago, so this is sitting badly with me.

Now I have to give the opening five minutes at our fifth-anniversary night, Friday after next. Forty people, and the room's split: maybe ten of them read philosophy and will know all the standard material about tolerating the intolerant and will be sitting there waiting to see if I just parrot it back; the rest are ordinary people who come becuase they like arguing about big questions with a pint and will glaze over if I turn it into a seminar. Two of our founding members quit over the vote and are (I think) coming back for this one. I don't know how that's going to feel.

What I want to build the speech around isn't the usual stuff. It's something I worked out on a long drive and I'm fairly sure it's right: I'd been confusing two different things — tolerating what someone *believes* and tolerating the *moves* they make in a conversation. A group can be totally open to any belief, anything anyone actually thinks, however awful, and still refuse certain moves: refusing to let a claim be examined at all, treating the other people in the room as not really participants, making the argument itself impossible to have. The beliefs are unlimited; the moves aren't. And I think that isn't a loophole in the openness, it's the thing that makes the openness real instead of a suicide pact. I also want to be honest that the line between "a move I disagree with" and "a move that breaks the game" is not obvious, that we'll get it wrong sometimes, and that a room which never has to make that call is a room that's already stopped having arguments.

Write me the speech. Five minutes spoken, so around 700–800 words. It has to do the philosophical work through what actually happened with Dave and the Tuesday nights, not by citing anyone — you can gesture once at the old line about tolerating the intolerant if it really helps, but don't lean on it and don't name-drop a reading list at people. It has to be warm and a bit funny, and it has to make the ten who know this stuff feel like I've said something they hadn't quite put that way, while the other thirty just follow the story. And don't let me off the hook about Dave — I don't want to be the hero of my own speech.
