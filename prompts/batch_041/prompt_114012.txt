I am a program officer at a small regional foundation. We make about forty grants a year, each between $15,000 and $60,000, and I am responsible for recommending a slate to a board that has to sign off. The decisions repeat every quarter, the stakes are moderate but real, and I have roughly two days per slate, no capacity to commission external reviews, and a fixed pot of money. I keep being told that I should be satisficing rather than optimizing, but every version I encounter collapses into the advice to pick something good enough, which is useless when the board asks why we did not fund the stronger proposal that arrived a week later. Please turn the theory of bounded rationality and satisficing into a decision procedure I can actually run on individual proposals and on a slate.

I want the procedure to respect several distinctions that I have found useful but that popular accounts tend to blur. First, satisficing is not merely optimizing with a lower bar; it has a different structure. An optimizer needs a well-defined option set and a complete ranking, whereas a satisficer needs a search rule and a stopping rule, plus an aspiration level that can adapt as search proceeds. Second, the search rule and the stopping rule are separate decisions: where to look next is not the same question as when to stop. Third, there is a difference between satisficing as a descriptive claim about how people actually decide under limits and satisficing as a normative recommendation for what to do when search is costly. Fourth, bounded rationality includes temporal, cognitive, and institutional constraints, not only missing information. Please build the procedure around those distinctions, and do not let it quietly turn into a scoring rubric that ranks every proposal, because that is the optimizing behavior I am trying to avoid.

The procedure should be something I can run in a few hours with a pen and a folder of proposals. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then apply it to three cases: a proposal that meets the threshold but is in a fashionable area where we already have three grants; a proposal that falls just below the threshold but comes from a region we have never funded and where the need is severe; and a proposal that arrives after the slate is set but looks stronger than anything on it. Include one case where the procedure tells me to stop and not fund the stronger late proposal, which I will not like. End by saying what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Write as if you have no web access; do not provide citations, reading lists, or references.
