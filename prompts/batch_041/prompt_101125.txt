I have been trying to settle whether "better than," as I actually apply it to lives, is transitive, and I keep producing a set of verdicts that will not line up. I would like you to tell me what principle, or small family of principles, captures the pattern, what it implies about cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts, if any, I have to give up.

Each case compares two possible lives. I describe a life by its quality (how good each year of it is, on a scale where 1 is a life barely worth living and 10 is as good as a life can go) and its length in years. I assume a year at quality 1 is still worth living, though only barely.

Here are my verdicts. First, a life of 11 years at quality 9.9 is better than one of 10 years at quality 10: the extra year is worth the sliver of quality lost. Second, 12 years at quality 9.8 is better than 11 years at quality 9.9, for the same reason. Third, I can run this reasoning all the way down a ladder — 13 years at 9.7, 15 at 9.5, and so on — and at every rung I judge the longer life better than the one above it, ending with a life of 100 years at quality 2, which I also judge better than the rung just above it. But fourth, I judge 10 years at quality 10 to be better than 100 years at quality 2. Since the first three verdicts chain by transitivity to "100 years at quality 2 is better than 10 years at quality 10," and the fourth denies exactly that, something has to give.

The pair I find hardest to reconcile, and cannot explain even to myself: I judge 30 years at quality 5 better than 10 years at quality 10, but I judge 35 years at quality 4 worse than 10 years at quality 10. Somewhere between quality 5 and quality 4, extra length stops making up for lost height, and I cannot say what fixes that boundary or why it sits there rather than elsewhere.

My tentative principle is that a life is better the greater its total welfare, where total welfare is the sum over its years of that year's quality. Please test it against the verdicts above, tell me where it breaks and how you would repair it, and if it cannot be repaired, offer the simplest principle that saves as many of my verdicts as possible.

Then: which single verdict does your principle force me to abandon, and what is the cost of abandoning it? What does your principle say about cases I have not listed — for instance, a life that is long and mildly good versus a short one with a single great stretch, or a life containing some neutral years (quality 0, neither worth living nor not), or a life with a few years of real suffering mixed in? And is the right lesson here that "better than" is not transitive, that these lives are sometimes simply not comparable, or that one of my verdicts is just a mistake? Please write in American English.
