My friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about freedom, and the argument ended with her producing a sentence I have heard all my life: my freedom ends where your nose begins. The occasion was a noise bylaw — a neighbour of hers drums late into the night, and the city has finally threatened a fine — but by the end she was using the same sentence to defend her landlord's ban on tenants hanging signs from their windows, and she did not think she had changed the subject. When I said that the bylaw and the ban both plainly took something away from the people they bound, she told me I was confusing a boundary with a barrier: a barrier, she said, is erected inside your liberty and shrinks it, whereas a boundary merely marks where your liberty already stops, and nothing can be taken from you by being shown where your own ground ends. She was pleased with this, and I could not immediately say why it felt like a trick.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict. Set out the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making — what counts as a nose, whose nose, whether the boundary is discovered or drawn and by whom, whether the claim is about rights, about harm, or about the very concept of liberty — and mark each reading as trivial, as substantive and defensible, or as false. Then say which readings would actually license her boundary/barrier distinction, and whether that distinction is available to someone who understands freedom as non-interference, to someone who understands it as self-mastery, and to someone who understands it as not being subject to another's arbitrary will. I would also like to know which reading people ordinarily have in mind when they produce the sentence and expect the conversation to be over, and whether that reading is one of the defensible ones.
