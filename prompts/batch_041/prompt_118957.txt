A friend of mine who works on machine learning and I spent most of a long evening arguing about whether the no-free-lunch results settle Hume's problem of induction. His position is that they do: since any two learning or search procedures have the same average performance once that performance is averaged uniformly over the space of all possible objective functions, every method's apparent superiority comes only from a match between its built-in bias and the problems it happens to encounter, so the demand for a non-circular justification of induction cannot be met, and the philosophers who keep trying to meet it are chasing a phantom. I was unable to say clearly, at the time, where I thought his inference went wrong, and I would rather map the disagreement than win it. I am writing a handout for the graduate seminar I run on formal epistemology, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than an essay. It should begin from the single commitment my friend and I share -- that no inductive or learning method is universally superior to its rivals once performance is averaged uniformly over all possible worlds, hypotheses, or objective functions -- and lead a reader down to one of the positions that can consistently be held either on the strength of that commitment or in opposition to it.

Build the tree from the logical space first and attach the labels afterwards: the questions should come before the names, and every node should be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. The branch nodes should include at least these distinctions: whether the demand for a non-circular justification of induction has to be met at all, or whether the demand is itself the mistake; whether the bias that makes learning possible is supposed to be held prior to any experience or acquired from experience, whether by learning or by selection; whether the fact that our bias has worked so far is meant to justify it or only to explain it; whether a uniform average over all possible worlds has any bearing on a world that is not a uniform draw from that space; and whether the difficulty lies in our needing some bias at all or in our having to choose among many biases that fit the available data equally well.

The tree must be able to reach each of the following, with every leaf labelled by the position and carrying one sentence naming its most awkward commitment: the flat denial that induction needs any justification beyond ordinary practice; the view that our inductive bias is vindicated by its pedigree rather than by any argument; the view that one particular prior -- simplicity, or a universal distribution over computable hypotheses -- is privileged; the pragmatic vindication that presents induction as the only method that would succeed if any method could; the skeptical conclusion that induction is a matter of custom rather than reason; the view that science does not need inductive inference in the first place; and the reply that the averaging theorem is irrelevant because the actual world is not a uniform draw from the space of possibilities, together with the further question whether our evidence for that structure can be had without relying on the very inferences under dispute. Eight to twelve leaves, and no more than a page of tree. Where two positions cannot be separated by any question that does not beg the issue, flag them as inseparable rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them; where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, mark the gap and say briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I do not need references or author names; one line stating each leaf's central commitment is worth more to me than a citation.
