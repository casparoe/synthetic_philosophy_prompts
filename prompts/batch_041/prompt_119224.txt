I spent my working life in a laboratory and have come to philosophy late, which means I still find the standard moves of the subject surprising rather than second nature. Lately I have been trying to understand the money-pump argument and its diachronic cousin. The synchronic version, as I understand it, runs like this: an agent whose preferences over three options run in a cycle — A preferred to B, B preferred to C, and C preferred to A — can be induced to pay a small fee to trade C for B, then B for A, then A for C, landing back where she started but poorer; if she will accept each trade on its own merits, the trader can drain her indefinitely, and the argument takes this to show that such preferences are irrational. The diachronic version is less tidy. There the agent's preferences may be perfectly transitive at every moment, but they shift across moments, so what it is rational to choose now is regretted later, and the trader's profit comes not from any cycle within a single ranking but from the agent's inability to bind her future selves.
