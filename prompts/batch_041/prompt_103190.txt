I'm putting together the take-home final for an upper-level seminar on teleology and functions. The unit I want to test is the free-floating rationale idea -- reasons that are genuinely there in a selection process even though no one entertains them -- and I want one essay question that students can't get through by defining the phrase back at me. So I want to hand them three short cases and make them commit to a criterion.

The three cases, roughly: (a) a plant that, when a caterpillar chews it, releases a volatile that attracts parasitic wasps which kill the caterpillar -- we say the plant "recruits bodyguards," but nothing in the plant represents the wasp or the goal; (b) a structure that's a sheer byproduct of how the organism develops, no selection story behind it at all, but that happens to look like it's there for something; (c) a trait where two quite different rationales fit the evidence equally well and nothing in the biology picks between them.

The question should ask, for each case, whether there's a free-floating rationale at all, what criterion they're using to say so, and -- the part I actually care about -- whether that criterion survives case (c). If two descriptions are equally good, is the free-floating rationale a fact about the organism or a fact about us? I also want them to say whether the notion does any explanatory work that plain natural-selection talk doesn't, or whether it's just a redescription of the same facts in mentalistic language.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer -- name the specific moves each band has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. Total 20 marks, 90 minutes.
