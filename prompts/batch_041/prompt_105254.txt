Two colleagues of mine — both careful people, neither a bully — spent most of a reading group arguing about the principle of indifference, and they asked me afterward to referee. I have my own leanings, but I will keep them out of what follows; what I want from you is a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made, not a declaration of who won overall. I will give you the two cases they worked from, then their exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

The first case. A machine stamps square tiles. We are told only that the side length of a tile lies somewhere between 1 and 2 centimeters, and nothing further about how the machine behaves. What is the probability that the side length is less than 1.5 cm? One route: the side length is uniformly distributed over [1, 2], so the probability is 1/2. Another route: the area is uniformly distributed over [1, 4] square centimeters — by the same reasoning, applied to the area rather than the side — and "side less than 1.5" is the very same event as "area less than 2.25," so the probability is (2.25 − 1)/(4 − 1), which is 5/12. One event, one principle, two answers.

The second case is the classical one about a chord drawn at random in a circle: what is the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? If we generate the chord by picking two points uniformly and independently on the circumference, the answer is 1/3. If we generate it by fixing a direction and picking the chord's midpoint uniformly along the radius in that direction, the answer is 1/2. If we generate it by picking the midpoint uniformly from the disk, the answer is 1/4.

Now the exchange. I have numbered the moves so that you can rate them individually.

(1) A: The tile case is a contradiction, not a curiosity. Both computations invoke indifference, both are equally well motivated, and they disagree. A principle that licenses contradictory conclusions from the same data is not a principle; it should be abandoned outright, and the chord case only reinforces this, since three equally natural symmetries give three different answers.

(2) B: That is too quick. Indifference is not a license to assign a uniform distribution to whichever quantity you happen to name. It applies only when the description of the setup itself puts the possible values on a par. In the tile case nothing in the setup singles out side length or area as the quantity that is "equally possible," so the setup is simply underspecified and neither answer is licensed. The lesson is about missing information, not inconsistency.

(3) A: Suppose I grant that the setup is underspecified. That does not rescue the principle. If we must already know, from the setup, which quantity is uniform, then we have been handed the distribution, and indifference adds nothing to what we were given. And where the setup does supply a symmetry — the circle is rotationally symmetric, and everyone agrees on that — the different symmetries still yield 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4. So even symmetry plus indifference fails to determine an answer; you must first choose which symmetry is the relevant one, and that choice is exactly the arbitrary step the principle was supposed to eliminate.

(4) B: The rotational symmetry of the circle is not the symmetry that matters. What matters is the symmetry of the procedure that generates the chord. If chords are produced by dropping a point uniformly on the circumference and joining it to another such point, then 1/3 is the correct answer for that procedure. If they are produced by taking a uniform midpoint in the disk, then 1/4 is correct for that procedure. The paradox dissolves once the generating mechanism is fixed: the original question is underspecified, not contradictory. The right generalization is to choose the distribution that is invariant under the group of transformations the procedure itself leaves open — and that is not an arbitrary choice, because it is read off the procedure.

(5) A: Then either the principle is doing no work, or it is doing illegitimate work. If the procedure already fixes the answer, I can compute it without ever invoking indifference, so the principle is redundant. If the procedure does not fix it, indifference tempts us to posit a symmetry the world need not have. Real manufacturing processes have no exact rotational or translational invariance; the tile machine has a calibration drift and a wear pattern. A principle that is sound only where it is redundant, and tempting only where it is unsound, is not worth keeping.

(6) B: That last line is a slogan, not an argument. Indifference, or its group-invariant version, was never meant to conjure knowledge out of nothing. It is meant to encode the absence of relevant information; and in the symmetric cases it is a theorem that the invariant distribution is the unique one that adds no information beyond the stated constraints. Where there is no symmetry it returns no verdict, and that is the honest result. Its job is not to answer every question but to tell us when a question cannot be answered from what we have.

Please proceed as follows. First, rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken through defensible-but-incomplete to clearly right, and justify each rating in a sentence or two. Second, for each move, state precisely what its author is right about, even where the move is flawed. Third, identify what both A and B are missing — in particular, whether they are even disagreeing about the same thing at moves (1) and (3), and whether "the setup is underspecified" and "the principle is contradictory" are the same diagnosis or two different ones. Finally, say what you take the correct role of the principle of indifference to be, and whether the invariant-distribution fix that B proposes at move (4) is genuinely a rescue of the principle, a replacement for it, or a restatement of something already contained in the procedures being described.
