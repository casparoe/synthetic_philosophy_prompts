A colleague has sent me a chapter from a general-audience behavioral economics book on information cascades, and he insists it is the clearest treatment he has read. I would like you to audit the chapter's central summary for me, because I have already repeated parts of it in conversation and I am no longer certain which parts I should have.

The summary runs roughly as follows. In the standard sequential-learning story, people decide one after another, each observing the choices of everyone ahead of them but not the private reasons behind those choices, and each combining a private signal of their own with the inference the sequence supports. Once the first few choices line up, the inference from the sequence outweighs anything a later person could plausibly have learned on their own, so from that point on it is rational for each of them to set their own information aside and follow the crowd. The group has then, in effect, stopped learning, because the private information of everyone after the turning point never makes it into the record. The author calls this the moment the group stops thinking, and draws what he presents as the striking lesson: a crowd of fully rational people, all pooling what they know, can nevertheless settle on the wrong answer, which he says is why the theory is often described as showing that markets are irrational. He adds that such cascades are famously stubborn — once a herd forms, only a catastrophe, a crash, or some comparable shock will dislodge it, since no participant has any incentive to break ranks. The same machinery, he says, explains fashions and fads and why professional investors all pile into the same trade. He closes by noting that the theory has been confirmed in laboratory experiments many times over, so conformity of this kind ought to be treated as an established fact about how groups actually behave.

I do not know the literature well enough to sort this out on my own. Could you go claim by claim and tell me what a specialist would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification, what misrepresents the model, and what is the author's own overreach — and then, separately, which of his conclusions survive the model once the misstatements are corrected? I suspect the line about markets being irrational is loose talk rather than a real entailment, but I would not be surprised to learn that other parts are worse than they look.

In my lab, the first two or three comments at group meeting reliably set the tone, and after that everyone reads the same results in the same way. I have been calling this a cascade. Is that the right label for it, and what would have to be true of the situation for the informational-cascade account, rather than some other variety of conformity, to be the correct explanation? If my case falls outside the model, say what the model actually requires and what my case lacks.
