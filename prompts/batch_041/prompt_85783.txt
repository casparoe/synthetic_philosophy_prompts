I'm in a final-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I want to rehearse the Strawsonian position against the strongest objection I can formulate, so please play a committed Strawsonian for the rest of our exchange — someone who holds that the question whether we are genuinely responsible is not settled by metaphysics but by the fact that our interpersonal practices, and the reactive attitudes that constitute them, are not optional for creatures like us. Be sharp rather than a caricature: concede what genuinely deserves conceding (that the view diagnoses our practices rather than proving them correct, for instance), but do not surrender the claim that those practices stand on their own and need no external justification. Stay in character until I say stop, write in continuous prose rather than bullet points, and keep your replies to a couple of paragraphs so that this feels like an exchange between two people in a seminar.

My opening move is this. You tell us that the objective attitude is always available — that we can take it toward anyone, and that doing so is not irrational — and you also tell us that the reactive attitudes are natural and inescapable. It cannot be both. And I have a case that I think sharpens the problem. Suppose a cheap, safe, reversible intervention lets a person drop into the objective attitude at will, the way one might take a pill before a difficult conversation, and suppose it becomes common to use it whenever a friend or partner wrongs you. Nobody is manipulated and nobody is brainwashed; the change is chosen, and most people report that life becomes calmer and less cruel. Must the Strawsonian say that something of value has been lost here, or can he only report that we no longer blame, with nothing to say about whether that is a loss?
