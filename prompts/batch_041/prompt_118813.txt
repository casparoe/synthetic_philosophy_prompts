I'm rebuilding my epistemology seminar and I need a new case for the bootstrapping unit. The ones I've been using are tired, half the room has seen them already, and there's alot riding on this unit since the closure material comes right after it.

Setup, so we're on the same page. A subject forms a belief with some method M, then turns around and uses that very belief — the one M produced — as part of her evidence that M is reliable. Do that over and over and it starts to look like you can know your own methods are reliable without ever checking them against anything outside themselves. Toy version, off the top of my head: she glances at a fuel gauge, comes to believe the tank is full, notices that the gauge reads full and that the tank is in fact full, and on that basis concludes the gauge is reliable. Most people's gut says that's no good. But then you're stuck with the worry that knowledge of our own methods' reliability comes either too cheap or not at all, and neither of those can be right.

What I want from you: a case of the same shape — the reliability claim is supported by a belief the method itself produced, and she has no independent corroboration of the method's reliability available to her — but where the verdict should come out the other way. She knows. Constraints: ordinary life, one paragraph, no brains in vats, no fake barns, no lotteries, and not a fuel gauge and not a clock or a watch checked against itself. Make the method memory, testimony, or induction rather than perception, and make the reliability claim a genuine contingent claim about the world rather than something true by definition. And dont cheat by smuggling in outside evidence — the whole point is that all she has are the method's own outputs.

Then do the work. State the belief, the method, the bootstrapping step, and the reliability conclusion. Say why someone who thinks bootstrapping never yields knowledge has to deny knowledge here, and why the other side says she's got it. Then tell me which feature of your case is actually doing the work, and what happens when you flip that feature — do you get the standard bad case back, or something else entirely? Finish by playing the opponent: what's the cheapest principle a no-bootstrapping person could appeal to that blocks your case, and does that same principle also block cases of knowledge we obviously want to keep? If it does, say so, and say whether that's their problem or mine. No citations needed, I know the literature.
