I am a graduate student in an interdisciplinary seminar on measurement and governance, and I keep running into a slogan that functions as a conversation-stopper: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” The last time was in a discussion about a proposal to tie a portion of departmental funding to a composite research quality index; a colleague cited the slogan and concluded that the proposal was self-defeating, because any index we adopt will be gamed and will stop tracking what it is supposed to track. In another session, someone used the same slogan to argue that benchmark-based evaluation of machine-learning models is doomed, since developers will optimize the benchmark rather than the capability the benchmark is meant to proxy. I suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs in these conversations, and that some of those jobs are trivial, some are empirical and contingent, some are close to tautologies, and some may be false. Please act as a philosopher of science and measurement theorist with expertise in incentive design, social statistics, and machine-learning evaluation. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming all the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making before you organize them.

I want a menu of readings, not a verdict on the slogan as a whole. For each reading, give the precise thesis in plain language; say what it is committed to about the causal mechanism, the stability of the measure-target relationship, the meaning of a “good measure,” the role of intentional gaming versus selection effects, the difference between measurement and incentive, and the possibility that the target construct itself changes when it is operationalized; and then give your verdict on whether that reading is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a substantive but overstated or false claim, or a normative claim dressed up as an empirical one. Then tell me which readings would actually support my colleague’s conclusion that we should not adopt the index or the benchmark, and which would not. Finally, tell me what the slogan leaves out that matters: cases where targeting a measure improves it, cases where the measure was never a good proxy to begin with, and cases where the real problem is not gaming but the fact that the target itself is contestable. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need the conceptual anatomy I can bring to the next seminar.
