I write about science and ideas, and I teach a five-session evening seminar on political theory for working reporters, at a journalism school where I hold an adjunct appointment. The exercise below is the final written assignment in that seminar, marked A through F. I would like you to grade it.

The session it follows was on the boundary problem. The seminar's formulation was that any procedure for deciding who may participate in a decision seems to presuppose an answer to who may participate in deciding who may participate: a demos cannot constitute itself without already being constituted. We settled on three principles for fixing the demos, and one rule for conflicts among them. These are the whole of what the students were given.

The Subjection Principle. The demos comprises everyone the decision binds: those whose conduct it authoritatively directs, those on whom it imposes enforceable obligations, and those to whom it grants enforceable permissions.

The Affected-Interests Principle. The demos comprises everyone whose basic interests — health, livelihood, physical security — are significantly affected by the decision, whether or not the decision binds them.

The Contestability Principle. A person or body excluded by the boundary may claim membership, but only on the strength of an interest that is determinate and non-speculative at the time of the decision; claims resting on remote or merely modelled possibilities do not trigger the principle.

The Conflict Rule. The demos is the set fixed by the Subjection Principle. Those who qualify only under the Affected-Interests Principle are entitled to a hearing — to submit evidence and to be answered — but not to vote. Where, however, the interest at stake is basic and the decision is irreversible on a human timescale, the affected party is added to the demos and votes.

The question I set was this:

"The Republic of Aldera has licensed Vantor Minerals, a consortium registered in the neighbouring state of Brend, to discharge treated tailings from its Aldera mine into the Vela River, which crosses the border into Brend. The licence binds Vantor to a set of operating conditions and permits it to discharge; it also imposes a levy on the residents of Aldera, collected through a surcharge on their water bills. The tailings are expected to accumulate in the delta sediments on the Brendish side of the border, where three fishing towns draw their livelihood from the estuary; the scientists' best estimate is a substantial decline in fish stocks over the next ten years, and the accumulation is expected to be effectively irreversible on a human timescale. Aldera's parliament holds the licensing power, and only Aldera's citizens may vote in its elections. The Ocean Commons Trust, an NGO registered in a third country, has petitioned for membership in the licensing decision, arguing that the discharge may, on some modelled scenarios, contribute to a decline in global fish stocks over the next fifty years. Using the three principles and the Conflict Rule from the course materials, determine who belongs to the demos for this decision, and say which parties are entitled to vote and which only to a hearing. Explain the reasoning at each step."

One student's answer is reproduced in full below.

"The boundary problem is that a demos cannot draw its own boundary, because the drawing is itself a decision that would have to be authorised by a demos. The three principles are competing answers to the question of whose claim to participate is valid.

Subjection comes first, because the Conflict Rule makes it the baseline rather than one consideration among several. Vantor is bound by the licence: the operating conditions direct its conduct, and the licence grants it an enforceable permission to discharge. That Vantor is registered in Brend makes no difference; subjection turns on being bound, not on nationality. The residents of Aldera are likewise bound, since the levy is an enforceable obligation on them. The demos therefore contains Vantor and the residents of Aldera.

The Affected-Interests Principle picks out a different set. The three fishing towns in Brend draw their livelihood from the estuary, so their basic interest is significantly affected, and the harm is expected to be irreversible. They are not bound by the licence — nothing in it directs their conduct — but they plainly have a stake in it.

The Contestability Principle is where the case becomes interesting. Its trigger is that the claimant shows the decision affects an interest of theirs, and the Trust does: the health of the global fishery is a genuine interest, and the Trust is a competent claimant able to articulate it. So the principle admits it to the demos. A principle that let a boundary be defended by declaring every claimant remote would not be a principle at all; the burden should fall on those who draw the line.

The three principles give three different answers, so the Conflict Rule has to arbitrate. The demos is fixed by subjection, so it contains Vantor and Aldera's residents. The fishing towns qualify only under the Affected-Interests Principle, but their interest is basic and the accumulation is irreversible on a human timescale, so they are added to the demos and vote. The Trust, admitted under the Contestability Principle, is entitled to be heard.

The upshot is that the case shows the framework to be a version of the Affected-Interests Principle after all: once the question is who is affected, and anyone who can say they are affected is admitted, subjection does no independent work and the demos simply expands to fit the stakes. That is why the boundary problem admits of no neutral solution — any boundary Aldera's parliament draws will be drawn by a body that the excluded cannot vote in — and the framework's real service is to make the exclusion visible."
