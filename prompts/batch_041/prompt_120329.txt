I teach a second-year undergraduate module in social and political philosophy, mixed philosophy and PPE students, 24 of them, one 90-minute session in a flat seminar room with movable chairs and no computers. I want a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel the force of something they will otherwise assume they already understand: that what is usually called the tragedy of the commons is not one problem but a family of structurally different ones, and that which member of the family you are in is fixed by how individual contributions aggregate, not by how selfish the participants are.

The design should exploit a contrast the standard treatment skips. The same people, with the same preferences and roughly the same stakes, can be trapped in three quite different ways depending on the aggregation technology governing the good: first, summation, where what matters is the total, so the individually rational move is to hold back and let others carry it; second, weakest-link, where the good is only as large as the smallest contribution, so the rational move is to wait and see whether anyone else moves first; third, best-shot or threshold, where the good arrives only if one contributor, or enough of them, reaches a fixed level, so the rational move is to hope that someone else is the one who does it. Something like a volunteer's dilemma sits inside the third case. In the second and third, the binding constraint is strategic uncertainty — not being able to see whether anyone else has already acted — rather than greed.

So: design an activity with at least four rounds for the same 24 people, with the same nominal stakes and the same payoff magnitudes, in which the aggregation rule, and where relevant the information each player has about others' moves and endowments, is the only thing that changes. Give me the rules, materials I can print or improvise from paper cups and tokens, a points scheme needing no real money, roles if the structure needs them, and a timeline that fits 90 minutes including the debrief. Participants must not be told that the changing rule is the point; the discovery should come from comparing their own behaviour across rounds, ideally from a scoreboard they fill in themselves. At least one round should make the obvious remedy look inadequate — talking things through should help in one structure and not in another, and the debrief should say why.

Then the debrief itself: map each round onto the structure it instantiates, and make the class separate two kinds of explanation for what they just did — motivational (greed, myopia, warm glow, reciprocity) and structural (aggregation rule, threshold, information). I want them to see that the structural explanation is doing the real work, because the same people failed in one round and cooperated in another without their preferences changing. Be explicit about what the activity cannot do: it cannot reproduce the slow emergence of norms, the reputational and monitoring dynamics of a real commons, or the ecological feedback that makes over-extraction irreversible, and the facilitator has to supply those in words.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where the rules will be too opaque or too transparent, which students will treat the points as meaningless, what happens if someone guesses the twist in round two — and how to handle each case. Include a one-page facilitator's sheet with the actual payoff numbers and a check that the intended equilibrium is the one that will really show up.

Finally, be candid about the weakest part of the design: the point where it leans hardest on an empirical generalisation about how people behave in games of this kind, and what classroom observation or experimental result would lead you to redesign that round. Write in British English.
