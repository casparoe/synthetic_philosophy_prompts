My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether British rule in India can be assessed the way a business is assessed — whether you can draw up a single ledger, put the railways and the schools and the civil service on one side, put the famines and the deindustrialization and the revenue drain on the other, and read a verdict off the bottom line. He holds that the ledger is the only honest way to argue it; I suspect that the decision to frame the whole thing as a ledger is itself where the dishonesty gets in. I want to watch the ledger approach taken at its own word and then pushed until it embarrasses itself.

Write a mock annual report for the British Raj — the kind a listed company publishes for its shareholders — presented as a business, with a chair's letter, financial highlights, a risk-factors section, and a brief set of notes to the accounts. The comedy has to come from getting the accounting right rather than from inventing fresh horrors: take the real features of imperial rule and render them as revenue lines, capital expenditure, depreciation, goodwill, and shareholder distributions, so that the form quietly performs the moral work on its own. Keep the register deadpan and corporate from beginning to end, and never wink at the reader.

The parts I care most about are the ones that actually divide people who argue about this. In the notes to the accounts, include a note on the counterfactual baseline — what India without the Raj is meant to have been, and why the auditors were forced to assume one — and a note explaining the discount rate applied to costs that fall on people who are never shareholders. Add a risk-factors entry on the possibility that the whole enterprise was net negative, written in the same voice as a risk about commodity prices. Let the chair's letter make the familiar move of weighing a benefit that accrued to the public in general against a cost that fell on particular people, without ever acknowledging that this is what it is doing.

About 650 words, and legible both to someone who knows this historiography and to someone who doesn't. Begin directly with the report itself — no preface, no commentary, no restating of this request.
