A colleague of mine defends what she calls the continuity thesis: the coherence argument for expected utility maximization is the Dutch book argument for probabilism with the objects swapped out. On her account, coherent credences are those that no set of bets can turn into a sure loss; coherent preferences are those that no sequence of trades can turn into a sure loss, that is, into a money pump; and in each case a representation theorem tells you what the exploit-free agent's attitudes must look like — a probability function on the one side, a complete, transitive, expected-utility-representable ordering on the other. She leans on the parallel in a talk about machine objectives. Any system we deploy, she argues, must have a complete preference ordering over outcomes, because an agent that cannot compare two options is a money pump waiting to happen, exactly as an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability calculus is a book waiting to be made. On her view, incomplete or partly conflicting objectives are an engineering nuisance rather than a conceptual obstacle: the parallel to credences is supposed to show that the demand for completeness is not an artefact of one particular decision theory but a general requirement of coherence.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I cannot yet say where it fails cleanly. I would like the audit.

1. Set out the correspondence in as much detail as it will support: what plays the role of the betting odds, the bookie, the book, the sure-loss guarantee, the axioms, the representation theorem, and the exploit.

2. Then the breakdowns. I am especially interested in four:
   (a) whether credences are answerable to something — truth, or accuracy — that preferences are not, and whether the exploit argument needs that answerability in order to get from "avoidable sure loss" to "irrational";
   (b) whether the book is synchronic while the pump is diachronic, and what that difference does once the agent can foresee the whole sequence — including whether an agent that adopts a plan and then sticks to it genuinely escapes the pump, and whether "escapes" here means "is immune" or merely "is a different kind of agent with a different rationality";
   (c) whether the pump bites on indifference rather than on incompleteness, and if it does, whether the parallel can still mark any difference between the incomplete agent and the complete one;
   (d) whether the existence of a rival defence of probabilism with no obvious preference-side analogue — the thought that credences can be scored for accuracy and incoherent ones are accuracy-dominated by coherent ones — means that these were never the same argument in the first place.

3. Separate the disanalogies that matter for her use of the parallel, namely licensing a design requirement for machine objectives, from those that are merely picturesque, and say whether her conclusion survives once the ones that matter are on the table. If it does not, say what weaker conclusion the analogy can still carry, and what a better analogy would be.
