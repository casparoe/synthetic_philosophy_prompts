I'm halfway through a term paper on whether dreams are experiences, and I've built an argument I can't get out of. My supervisor says it "proves too much," which I'm 90% sure is supervisor for "I don't want to argue with this." I'd like your read on it.

Background, briefly, since I'm not asking you to have the literature at your fingertips. Malcolm's line, which he takes from Wittgenstein, is that a dream can't exist independently of the waking impression — the memory you come out of sleep with, the account you give, whatever you say when someone shakes you awake mid-dream. There's no criterion for the identity of the bear in my dream, or for how long the dream lasted, or for whether the dream-bear is the same bear as the one last night, other than the story I tell. So there's nothing to be made of what it was really like in there, apart from the report. Dennett, if I've got him right (I've read him once, probably badly), thinks that way of setting the question up is off: asking what I really experienced at 3 a.m. smuggles in a picture of a dream-narrative playing out in real time during sleep, and once you drop the picture the question doesn't get a No, it gets dissolved — you stop asking it.

Here's my reconstruction. I've definately turned the Malcolm move into a general principle, and I think that's where the trouble is, but I can't see the flaw.

(1) If I dreamt I was chased by a bear, everything there is to know about the dream is fixed by the waking report: the memory I come out of sleep with, what I say, how I flinch when you wake me.

(2) Suppose instead there were a further fact — a fact about what I actually experienced at 3 a.m., over and above the report. Then it would be possible in principle for that fact to obtain and leave no trace at all. I forget the dream completely, nobody wakes me, nothing about my later life differs. From every point of view, including mine, the world is exactly as it is now.

(3) A difference that makes no difference anywhere, ever, even in principle, is not a difference. So there is no fact of the matter about whether I had an experience at 3 a.m.

(4) If there's no fact of the matter about whether I had an experience, then I didn't have one. You can't have an experience without it being true that you had it.

(5) So dreams are not experiences. I dreamt I was chased by a bear is a story I'm disposed to produce on waking, not a report of an experience.

(6) And nothing in the argument turns on the episode being a dream — all I used was that a dream can be wholly forgotten and leave no trace. So the same reasoning covers any experience that is forgotten or leaves no mark. That's the proves-too-much bit: it would mean an experience I had and forgot never happened, and it would mean there's nothing it's like to be an infant, or a dog, or someone under anesthesia, not because of anything about their minds but just because they can't file a report. It would also mean the question gets settled a priori, which seems wrong given that people woken out of REM sleep usually have something to say about what they were just doing.

If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than give up the whole approach, but I want to know what I'm paying.

So: which step do I drop, why did it look fine until now, and what does dropping it cost me? I can't even tell whether (6) is a reductio of (1), of (2), or of (3).
