I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and I have to hand in a proposal for my seminar paper by the end of the month; the paper itself is due at the end of the semester and I'm aiming at about 7,000 words. The seminar is on moral philosophy and everyday life, and it's cross-listed with the law school, so at least one reader will want to know whether any of this bears on how the law treats custom. My half-formed idea came out of an argument I had with a friend who had just come back from a year in Osaka: she'd got out of the habit of tipping and had stopped doing it at home too, and I couldn't tell her confidently whether she'd done anything wrong, or whether whatever she'd done was different in kind from cutting a queue or crossing against the light on an empty street. What bothers me about the usual framing is that all three cases get lumped together as "conventions" and then sorted by the single question of whether conventions can generate obligations at all. I think the interesting variation lies elsewhere. Consider three features. First, what the norm is doing: a queue norm looks like a pure coordination device, in the sense that any stable rule about who goes next would serve about as well, whereas a tipping norm looks more like the completion of a price — the posted price isn't the whole price, and failing to tip doesn't merely disappoint an expectation, it moves money from one person to another. Crossing against the light, on the face of it, is neither: it's a rule about who bears a risk, and violating it on an empty street seems to move that risk onto no one. Second, whether non-compliance is legible to the person affected: a server learns about a missing tip only after the meal is over, when nothing can be renegotiated, while a queue-jumper is seen in the act and can be answered on the spot. Third — and this is the part I can't find treated in the way I want — whether compliance sets a precedent: whether the deviation is something others can observe, copy, and later cite.

My suspicion is that once you separate these features, the question about the obligation and the question about the visitor come apart, and the answer comes out asymmetric in a way that neither a straight coordination account nor a straight fairness account can capture. Some norms may bind the visitor more tightly than the local — I think tipping may be like that, since a regular's non-tip reads as a message about the server and a visitor's as a message about the visitor — while others may bind the local more tightly than the visitor, since a local's crossing on a red sets a precedent that a tourist's doesn't. I want you to turn this into a proper proposal for the seminar paper: state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, say why the existing positions (coordination-based, fairness-based, harm-based, and the line that these norms aren't obligations at all) leave it genuinely open rather than merely unresolved, and set out the method — what I would actually do, month by month, over a semester. I'd like the method to include some kind of matrix in which the three norms are scored on the features above, a search for cases where the features come apart and the accounts give opposite verdicts, and a run at norms that are themselves criticizable, where the local practice looks like it shifts an obligation off the party who should bear it. Say what a real result would look like and what would count as the project failing — I'd rather know now if the whole thing collapses into a restatement of the standard views. Be honest about scope for one semester, and feel free to flag anything that has to be cut and marked as a limitation; I'd rather have one paragraph on the legal question, clearly labelled as unfinished, than a second project bolted on. Don't give me a bibliography or citations — I'll add those myself, and I'd rather you refer to positions and arguments by description than guess at who said what.
