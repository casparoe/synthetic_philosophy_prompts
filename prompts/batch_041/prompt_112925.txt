I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and the social sciences, and I'm working on a feature about that moment when people quietly stop believing their own individual actions matter -- the why-bother-voting, why-bother-not-buying-the-burger thing. I've had this argument alot lately. One friend says buying a factory-farmed burger can't be wrong because supermarkets order in batches and one more purchase just doesn't kill another animal; another says voting is pointless because her district is safe by forty thousand votes and, anyway, if she'd stayed home her roommate would have cast an identical ballot; a third says it's all deflection and the only real lever is policy. I want to build a reader-facing decision tree for the piece -- something a person can walk through with their own cases and land on a position they can then argue for. So put on your hat as a philosopher who works on collective action, causation and moral responsibility, and take your time with this: start by brainstorming, separately, the axes you think this debate actually turns on, and only then draw the tree.

Every branch node should be a question phrased in plain language, so a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to -- no nodes like "are you a rule-consequentialist?". The distinctions I most want the questions to pull apart are: whether the individual act makes any difference at all to whether the harm happens, versus an imperceptible difference, versus a difference only in aggregate with other people's acts; whether the harm is threshold-shaped (nothing happens until enough people act, and then a discrete bad thing) or smooth; whether the thing that matters is the act's causal contribution, the agent's participation in a practice she endorses, the agent's character and integrity, or the fairness of everyone doing their bit; and whether the right response to individual inefficacy is to relocate the obligation onto institutions and policy. The tree needs leaves for at least these positions: the one where the individual act is simply permissible and the real duty is political; the expected-consequences reply; the view that each tiny imperceptible contribution is still a wrong; the threshold or pivotal-act view; the rule or fair-share view; the complicity view, where you're answerable for participating in a collective wrong regardless of your causal contribution; the virtue and integrity view; and the one where nothing at all is wrong with the individual act. Name the leaves wherever a name exists, mark them "unnamed" where nobody defends the combination, and say in a sentence whether the empty branches are empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Four cases have to show up somewhere in the tree, since they're the ones my readers will recognise: (1) the burger from a farm that kills in fixed batches, where one more purchase really doesn't add an animal; (2) a vote in a district your side wins by a landslide, in the version where your friend would have cast an identical ballot if you'd stayed home; (3) a flight you're told is carbon-neutral because an eccentric donor has already committed to fund exactly enough carbon removal to cover whatever you emit; (4) a case where a thousand participants trigger the harm, there are 1,001 of you, and you know for certain that you aren't the one who tips it over. Finally, flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate -- I suspect some of the participation-based views blur into each other -- and please don't pad it with citations or a reading list, I can't use those in a magazine piece; plain descriptions are better.
