I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized research funder — we make maybe forty grants a year, mostly biomedical and animal-behaviour work — and we've just adopted a policy that we won't fund research that inflicts suffering on a being that can suffer. Great in principle. The trouble is the second half of the sentence. The policy is deliberately substrate-neutral: it says what matters is whether the system has the right kind of organisation, not what it's made of. The two philosophers on the committee tell me this is just functionalism plus multiple realizability, and I believe them, but it's no help at my desk. Files come in at two or three a month, I have to sign off in writing against a deadline, and the room contains a vet, two biologists, a lawyer and me — no one who can reconstruct a theory of mind from scratch at 4pm on a Thursday.

So what I want from you is a procedure we can actually run on a file: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where it's allowed to stop and say no, where it has to hand the file to the full committee for a vote, and a short written template we can paste into the decision letter. It has to fit in about ninety minutes of reading, it can't require us to commission new experiments or master a technical literature, and it has to survive appeals from applicants. Please also flag the steps that aren't measuring anything at all and are just us choosing a side — I'd rather see them labelled than have them hidden in the middle of the flowchart. And if the honest conclusion is that the theory settles less than people think and some of these calls are votes dressed up as findings, tell me that too.

Run it on three files we have open right now. (1) A shrimp-farming study that involves repeated handling and, in one arm, eyestalk ablation; our program officers will be furious if we protect shrimp, because it takes out a whole portfolio. (2) A human stem-cell-derived brain organoid that the lab claims is starting to show something like learning, which bothers people more than the shrimp does, though I can't articulate why. (3) A companion chatbot the comms team wants to pilot with isolated older participants, where the vendor's materials insist the thing has feelings and a couple of our reviewers roll their eyes. For what it's worth I've always thought functionalism was obviously right — a brain isn't magic, if the job gets done some other way the job gets done — but our lawyer keeps saying that on that logic a big enough spreadsheet could suffer, and I never know what to say back. I'd like at least one of the three to come out in a way I won't enjoy.
