I'm drawn to a broadly Neurathian thesis about visual communication: that a genuinely pictorial language — no words, no written numerals, only ordered pictures and icons — could in principle carry the whole content of empirical science, and that its advantage over ordinary language is epistemic and not merely mnemonic, since the vernacular imports distinctions (substance and accident, thing and property, the mental and the physical) that a purely pictorial notation cannot state and therefore cannot inherit. I'd like this treated as a position and its exposure audited. What would count against it? Which expressive tasks would a wordless notation have to discharge — negation, generality, conditionals, modality, the gap between depicting and asserting, between a single case and a statistical summary — and which of these are plausibly impossible to discharge pictorially rather than merely awkward? What does the thesis predict or forbid that a rival account of pictorial notation does not? What would its defenders themselves concede as a real cost, and what would they dismiss as a prejudice of verbal language? And if it retreats to the weaker claim that pictures can carry scientific content with verbal scaffolding but not without it, is that a qualification or a surrender? I'd be most moved by a demonstration that some content is not merely hard to picture but impossible to picture, and I'm genuinely unsure how much weight the objection deserves that pictures are convention-laden in just the way words are.
