You are a philosopher of value and practical reason — someone who works on comparability, incommensurability, and hard choices, and who is used to defending technical distinctions against people who suspect they are just elaborate ways of saying "it's a judgment call."

A note on me before we start: I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. Where my terminology is off, correct it and use the standard term — but don't let the terminology do the arguing for you.

I want you to construct a thought experiment to my specifications. The specifications exist to isolate one verdict about how two options compare: that they are on a par — comparable, but neither better than the other, and not equal either. (I believe parity in this sense is Ruth Chang's; if I have the name or the content wrong, say so and proceed with the correct version.) The case has to earn that verdict rather than assume it, so most of the constraints below are there to close off the rival explanations.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Realistic and contemporary. An ordinary adult, a decision such a person could actually face, stakes that matter but nothing operatic. No trolleys, lifeboats, desert islands, or other apparatus. Both options must be defensible; neither may be the obviously selfish, reckless, or self-destructive one.
2. The options must be valuable in different ways, not different amounts of one thing. No two salaries, no two lifespans, no more-of-the-same-good.
3. The difficulty must not come from uncertainty about how things will turn out, from information the agent lacks, or from vagueness in the agent's own preferences. The agent knows what each option involves.
4. The agent must have to choose: no deferring, no doing both, no splitting the difference, no waiting to see.
5. No stock examples. Not career versus family, not city versus country, not law versus philosophy, not money versus meaning.
6. The case must be tie-breaker-resistant. It should not be settled by any general policy of the "keep your options open," "prefer the reversible," "minimize regret," or "do the more urgent thing" variety. If one of those resolves it, the case fails.
7. Comparability must still have a grip. Include at least one third option that is clearly worse than both, and one variant of one of the two options that is clearly worse than that option. If the case reads as "these two simply cannot be compared in any respect," it fails.
8. Small-improvement signature. It must be possible to improve one option slightly — in a way everyone would agree is an improvement, and that leaves the option the same kind of thing it was — without the improved option thereby becoming better than the other. And the "neither is better" verdict must not rest on the improvement being too small to notice or too small to matter. (I think this is Chang's small-improvement argument; again, correct me if not.)

Work through candidate scenarios quietly before committing to one. Present only the case that survives the constraints; I don't want to see the discards.

Deliverable, in this order and with roughly these lengths:

Bottom Line (2–3 sentences). Which verdict the case actually supports, and why the main rival verdicts fail.

The Case (200–300 words). The scenario in prose, with the agent named and both options concrete.

Verdicts by Position (about 500 words). Run the case through each rival account — a straightforward maximizing view; the view that the comparison is merely vague or indeterminate; the view that the two options are roughly equal; the view that they are radically incomparable, sharing no common measure — saying what each predicts and where it goes wrong. Then say what the parity reading adds that none of them can supply, and finally whether this is a case in which the agent's own commitment can create a reason for choosing, or whether that is just a dignified name for a coin flip.

The Control Case (about 200 words). Change exactly one feature of the scenario so that the verdict flips to plain superiority (or to rough equality), and name the feature you changed.

What Is Doing the Work (about 200 words). The single structural feature the verdict hangs on, and what happens to the case if you remove it.

How the Losing Side Fights Back (about 250 words). The strongest objection from whichever account comes off worst, stated the way its defender would state it, followed by your honest reply.

Keep the prose tight; I would rather have the case airtight than the writing pretty.
