I'm a third-year cog-sci student and my philosophy of mind seminar just spent two weeks on the color phi phenominon — the one where a blue dot and a red dot get flashed in succession and you see a single dot travel and change color mid-flight, with the change reported as happening before the second dot is even up. We read it through Dennett's Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction (memory rewritten after the fact vs. the experience fabricated in advance), and the line the prof pushed was that there's no fact of the matter which one is going on. I've argued myself onto something like the other side: I think there's no fact about the content — it gets filled in either way — but there is a fact about the timestamp, i.e. when that content was experienced, and color phi is Stalinesque in the sense that the filled-in content is stamped earlier than the process that produced it. What would count against that? I want the real defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward — observations, lab findings, thought experiments — and especially any argument that the timestamp is as constructed as the content, or that a specious present with no internal order makes the whole question ill-posed. Our seminar also floated a two-channel test, some fast motor or pupil response that indexes timing independently of the verbal report; would that actually adjudicate the Orwellian/Stalinesque question or is it just another draft? Be straight with me here: if I've reinvented a distinction that's already been shown empty, or the timestamp/content split doesn't even hold up, say so plainly instead of humoring me — the thing I'd personally find most damaging is a clean case where timing and content dissociate, so tell me whether such a case is even possible.
