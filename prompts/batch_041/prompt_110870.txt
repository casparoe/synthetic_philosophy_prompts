In a development economics reading group I sit in on, the working definition of institutions is that they are the rules of the game and that firms, parties, unions and the like are the players. The person who introduced that definition used it to carry a fairly strong conclusion: that institutions are the fundamental cause of the wealth and poverty of nations, and that because rules are written by someone and can be rewritten, we already know both why nations differ and what to do about it, while appeals to geography or culture are at best background conditions. Before I accept the conclusion I want the analogy audited on its own terms.

Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the rules, the players, the umpire, the field, the score, the league, and the fact that the game is played repeatedly — and then say where the mapping fails. I am particularly interested in the features the analogy quietly imports from the source: that the rules are written down, complete, consistent, and fixed for the duration; that some party is authorized to enforce them and can do so from outside the game; that the players are constituted independently of the rules, so that the same players could take the field under a different rulebook; and that the payoffs of a game are given to the players rather than produced by their play. Set against that the alternative conception on which institutions are not rules at all but self-enforcing equilibria sustained by shared beliefs and by the expectations each participant has of the others, with no umpire anywhere — and say which parts of the analogy, and which parts of the conclusion, survive the shift.

Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use to which the analogy was put from those that are merely decorative. In particular: is the causal-inference problem in this literature — the unavailability of the counterfactual in which the same players face a different rulebook — a general limitation on learning causes from history, or is it a specific consequence of institutions being self-enforcing rather than decreed? Does geography correspond to anything in the game at all, or is it smuggled in as the field and the weather precisely so that it can be demoted to background? And does the analogy itself manufacture the contrast between institutions and culture, by making rules the sort of thing that can be written down and swapped while leaving everything unwritten to be classified as culture? Finish by saying whether the conclusion the definition was used to support survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if it does not, what analogy would do the work better — one that preserves the coordination and multiple-equilibria features without smuggling in a legislator — and what that replacement would license.
