You are a philosopher who works on decision theory and on the ethics of large-scale philanthropy, and you have drafted policy for grant-making bodies before. Think carefully before you write: begin by spending a few lines, for your own use, mapping the space of rules a funder could adopt to keep tiny probabilities from dominating its decisions (credence floors, caps on the ratio of payoff to probability, requirements that the case survive worst-case or model-uncertainty analysis, dominance and portfolio criteria, lexicographic priorities, and the like), and note how each of those can be gamed. Then write the memo itself as the deliverable.

Setting: I chair the grant committee of a mid-sized private foundation. Our charter directs us to give each cycle's budget to whatever we judge to have the greatest expected impact per dollar, and our reviewers score proposals by multiplying an estimated effect size by an estimated probability of success. We vote a week from Tuesday, and I need a short written opinion to circulate beforehand.

Three finalists are competing for the same $2 million. The first is a well-measured service-delivery program: high confidence, narrow uncertainty, an expected value around 10,000 on our internal scale, which counts a life-year saved as one unit. The second is a speculative research program whose entire case rests on a chain of the applicant's own estimates: a probability on the order of one in a trillion of an outcome the reviewers would have to score at something like 10^20 life-years, which makes its expected value roughly 10^8 and the largest in the pile by four orders of magnitude. The third is a research program the reviewers score at a few thousand units of expected benefit but also, by the same method, at an expected loss of about 10^8, because they put a one-in-a-million chance on a misuse catastrophe worth 10^14 life-years lost. The second and third cases are exact mirrors of each other, and that is the knot I need untied.

Half the committee says the arithmetic is the arithmetic: our charter says expected impact, and a rule that throws away small probabilities is precisely the failure of nerve the charter exists to prevent. The other half wants to bar any proposal whose case depends on a probability below some floor, and cannot see why a tiny probability of catastrophe should be treated any differently from a tiny probability of enormous good. Whatever I circulate has to be something a majority can actually adopt, so it must survive both factions.

In the memo, please: recommend a concrete disposition for each of the three proposals, with reasons their advocates could respond to; state the rule we should adopt for future cycles and give me the wording I could paste into our guidelines, saying explicitly how it handles the negative case; confront the strongest objection to your rule, namely that any floor or cap is either arbitrary or empty, since a large enough payoff can defeat any threshold; tell me plainly what your rule costs us if the fanatics turn out to be right; and close with a short section that states the losing side's position in terms its own adherents would accept. Assume we will make grants for many years, not just this once. I want the argument, not the literature: no citations, no reading lists.
