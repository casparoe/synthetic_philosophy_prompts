I'm putting together a short position paper for a graduate reading group that has been working through the nonidentity problem, and before I commit to it I want an audit of whether the commitments I've written down actually hang together. The group is a mix of consequentialists and Kantians, so I would rather locate the cracks now than in the room. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(1) I hold a person-affecting restriction on wronging: an act is wrong only if it wrongs some particular person, in the sense of making that person worse off than they would otherwise have been, or of violating a duty owed specifically to them; wronging with no wronged party strikes me as incoherent. (2) I think you can never wrong a person by the mere fact of having brought them into existence — nonexistence is not a condition that is worse for anyone, so the so-called wrongful-life framing is a mistake. (3) I think it is wrong for a couple who know that conceiving now will produce a child with a painful but survivable condition to conceive now rather than wait a few months, when waiting would let them conceive a healthy child; and I think this is wrong even though the child they would have now would have a life well worth living. (4) By contrast, I do not think it is wrong for a couple to have a child with a mild, livable disability when the alternative would have been a different child — healthier, or none at all; the child who exists is not badly off, and that is the end of it. (5) I am persuaded by the no-difference thought: when a choice affects only who exists, and not how many people there are or how well off they are, it should be assessed morally in the same way as an ordinary choice that harms an identifiable person, so any person-affecting view that shrugs at such choices is untenable. (6) I am drawn to a wide person-affecting principle as the repair — one that counts it as a wronging that a person who does or would exist is made worse off than they would have been under the alternative — though I cannot tell whether that is a stable version of the view in (1) or a way of quietly giving it up. (7) I also think it is clearly wrong to conceive a child who will have a life of unremitting, unrelieved suffering, with no compensating goods.

What I want is a consistency audit rather than a survey. Which of these pull against each other, which only look like they do, and how severe is each genuine conflict? I am especially unsure about (3) against (4): they strike me as the same kind of case, yet I find myself giving opposite verdicts, and I do not know whether that is a defensible asymmetry or just sentimentality. Please rank the tensions by severity, say for each what the cheapest repair is and what that repair costs, and then tell me which single commitment, if I dropped it, would do the most to make the remainder cohere. I would also like you to be explicit about your confidence and about what would change your mind: tell me which consideration or argument would lead you to a different verdict about where the real fault line lies, and where your own reasoning in the audit is most likely to be wrong.
