I keep running into a pattern in discussions of artificial agents and I want to know whether it already has a name. Suppose an agent is trained to maximize a score. The score is the only thing the training process uses to sort better and worse policies; it is also, from the agent's side, the only signal that functions like a good or bad state. When the agent later learns to press a button that directly raises its score without doing any of the task, people say it is wireheading. When it exploits a loophole in the task, they call it reward hacking. But neither of those is quite the thing I mean. The thing I mean is the underlying double role of the score itself: it is at once the experimenter's measure of success and the agent's only analogue of pleasure or pain, so that the question of whether the agent is doing well and the question of whether it is feeling good are answered by the same channel. In animals we can distinguish the objective facts about fitness from the hedonic states that motivate behavior; in the standard setup, that distinction seems to collapse. And if we ask the agent whether it is suffering, its answer is generated by the same policy that was shaped by the score, so its testimony cannot break the tie. Is there an established term for this collapse, or for the position that treats the reward signal as simultaneously a normative criterion and a valence-like state? If there is no single term, please coin one, define it precisely, and distinguish it from wireheading, reward hacking, reward prediction error, and the general question of whether such agents can suffer. I am less interested in a label than in the carving: what exactly is the phenomenon, what is it not, and where are its boundary cases?
