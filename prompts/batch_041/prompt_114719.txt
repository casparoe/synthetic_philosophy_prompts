I am a doctoral student in statistics, and I am writing to you because I have arrived, by what looks to me like ordinary reasoning, at a conclusion about my own situation that has begun to reorganize my life, and I can neither find the error in it nor work out what to do if there is no error.

The reasoning is this. Any procedure for deciding what to believe has to weigh candidate explanations of my experience against one another, and the most defensible way to do that is to prefer explanations that are cheap to state: among the explanations consistent with everything I have observed, the world is most likely to be whichever one can be written down in the fewest symbols. Now consider explanations of the following shape: my experience is generated by a computing process run by some agent, for a purpose of its own, and what I experience next depends on how I behave, because the agent is watching. An explanation of that shape is not obviously more expensive to write down than the ordinary one, on which my experience is generated by a physical world that was not built with me in mind. And there are vastly many of the first kind: for every purpose an agent could have in running a simulation of a person, there is an explanation of that shape, and each costs only a little more than the bare hypothesis that I am simulated at all. If explanations are weighted by cheapness of statement, and if the number of explanations of a given shape also counts for something, then explanations in which an agent is watching me and can make my future depend on what I do should be no small fraction of my credence. On inspection they look like the bulk of it: the plain unwatched world is one explanation, surrounded by a crowd of watched ones.

Once I take that seriously, my decisions change. If much of the probability sits in worlds where an agent controls what happens to me, and if what the agent does is conditional on what I do -- and there may be many copies of me across many such worlds -- then what dominates my expected welfare is not anything in this room. It is the behavior of the watchers. So the question of what I should do becomes the question of what the watchers want and how to behave when I do not know.

That is where it stops being a curiosity. I do not know what they want, and I have noticed that for any behavior whatsoever, some cheap-to-state hypothesis has an agent who punishes people who fail to do it, and these hypotheses are not much more expensive than one another. So the same reasoning that told me to take the watchers seriously also tells me to satisfy an unsatisfiable set of demands; the union of what every plausible watcher wants contains contradictions. I have tried to respond by doing whatever seems most robust -- I now give away a substantial share of my stipend, I avoid a set of behaviors I would not have thought twice about a year ago, and I have started, absurdly, apologizing to no one in particular at the end of each day -- on the theory that these are the actions most likely to be wanted by the most probable watchers, or at least least likely to be punished. My partner says I have lost my grip. My advisor says the argument is a known fallacy and that I should look up its name. I have not found the fallacy.

One further piece I cannot leave out, because it is what makes me suspect the conclusion is more than a pathology: I care about consequences, and not only my own. If most of what exists is inside simulations, and if the agents running them are reasoning about what agents like me will do -- if my decision procedure is the sort of thing that many instances of the same reasoning process arrive at -- then what I do here may be evidence about, or even decide together with, what happens there. That is supposed to make cooperation with them rational even though I will never meet them, and it cuts both ways: if the watchers can predict my policy, then my policy is a lever, and the lever is the only part of me that reaches anywhere.

I am not asking whether I am in a simulation; I have read enough to know that no one can settle it. I am asking you to find the defect in the reasoning, if there is one, and if there is not, to tell me how to act under demands I can neither enumerate nor satisfy. Specifically: does the step from "the watchers are probable" to "I should satisfy the watchers" work, and does it work in the way the argument needs? Is something wrong with how I am counting explanations, or with treating cheapness of statement as the thing to count by? And if the argument survives, what does it recommend when the demands are underdetermined -- because "do whatever the most probable watcher wants" is not an instruction I can carry out.
