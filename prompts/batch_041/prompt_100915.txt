I am assembling a teaching handout for a graduate seminar on measurement and incentive design, and I want it built as a diagnostic instrument rather than as an essay. The topic is the cluster of claims usually filed under Goodhart's law — the thought that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure — together with the narrower phenomenon of specification gaming.

Please construct a decision tree that takes a reader from a described case of metric failure to a named diagnosis of what is doing the work. The tree must be organised around mechanisms rather than labels:

1. Each internal node should be a question that a reader can answer about a concrete case without already knowing which diagnosis it leads to. No node may be phrased as "is this the causal variety?" or "is this the adversarial kind?".
2. The leaves must include at least the following diagnoses, each named and each given a one-sentence statement of the mechanism:
   - regressional: the correlation was inflated by selection, since the cases entering the target group were chosen on the very variable being predicted, so the apparent fit was never there to be preserved;
   - extremal: the metric is adequate near the centre of its distribution and noisy in the tails, and optimisation deliberately drives cases into the tails;
   - causal: the metric is a cause as well as an effect of the goal, so intervening on it moves the goal itself rather than tracking it;
   - adversarial: agents who want the reading to change locate and exploit the gap between what is measured and what is meant;
   - one leaf for the case in which the metric is functioning as intended and the goal was misdescribed;
   - one leaf for the case in which the "law" is doing no explanatory work because the failure is definitional.

For each internal node, give at least one concrete case that the node sorts — drawn from medicine, education, industrial planning, platform design, or machine-learning evaluation — and at least one case that plausibly triggers two or more leaves at once, naming which and explaining why. Two cases must appear somewhere in the tree: a drug that reliably suppresses a biomarker and nonetheless worsens the outcome the biomarker was meant to predict, and a waiting-time target that is met by holding patients in ambulances rather than in the department. State which leaf each lands on and, for the first, why it is not the adversarial leaf.

The handout should also contain:

- a short account of which pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, and why (coincidence of symptoms, absence of a discriminating case, or something else);
- the order in which you would ask the questions if the reader's aim is to avoid misdiagnosis, and the different order you would use if the aim is instead to find the quickest fix;
- a final table mapping each leaf to the remedy it licenses and the remedy it rules out — for example, collect more data, use several metrics at once, stop measuring, keep the target unstable, audit for gaming — with one sentence on why the remedy that suits one leaf is a mistake for the leaf it most resembles;
- a closing paragraph on which combinations of answers are unoccupied among positions actually defended, and whether each is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Aim for a tree of roughly twelve to eighteen nodes, with a named leaf at the end of every path. Assume the reader is a graduate student who understands what a proxy measure and a perverse incentive are but has not read the technical literature, so every diagnosis must be explained from the case rather than from its name. Do not supply references or reading lists; the handout must stand on the cases alone.
