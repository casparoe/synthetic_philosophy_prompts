I'm presenting a paper at a workshop on the foundations of welfare economics, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion that follows it.

The thesis I'm defending: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the result that if the social ordering and each individual's ordering both satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern expected-utility axioms and the social ordering respects the Pareto principle, then social value is a weighted sum of the individuals' cardinal utilities — is routinely cited as a proof that rational social evaluation must be utilitarian. It is not. It is a conditional, and its conclusion is only as strong as two gaps the theorem leaves open. The first is the comparability gap: summing cardinal utilities presupposes a common unit, and the theorem is silent on where that unit comes from. The second is the weighting gap: nothing in the theorem fixes the weights, so the step from "a weighted sum" to "utilitarianism" needs an independent premise that the weights are equal. That premise is supposed to come from Harsanyi's separate impartial-observer argument, and I argue the argument fails because it simply assumes that impartiality is an equal-probability lottery over lives. The two arguments are therefore not a pincer movement on utilitarianism but a single circle, and the habit of reading them as independent is what hides the circle.

The room splits cleanly. Roughly half are formal social choice theorists and welfare economists who know the measurability and interpersonal-comparability literature cold; they will press me on exactly which informational assumptions I am granting and on whether my two "gaps" are real or artifacts of stating the theorem loosely. The other half are moral philosophers — a Rawlsian and two committed prioritarians — who think the veil-of-ignorance move is rigged from the start, will not be moved by formal bookkeeping, and will want to argue about the separateness of persons.

Prepare me for the discussion, in four sections. "Ranked objections": a short ranked list, one line each, of the eight or so objections I will actually get, in the order they are likely to come, each marked for how much damage it does. "Social choice side" and "Philosophers' side": roughly a page each, every objection phrased the way that half of the room would put it, followed by a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. "Concessions": a paragraph on what to give away in the first two minutes to buy credibility. "The question I am hoping nobody asks": the one I most dread, and how I would handle it if it comes.
