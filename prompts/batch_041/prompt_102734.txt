I'm doing an MA in practical ethics and I've been slowly talked round to a position I used to find frankly repugnant, which is that moral trade — the thing where two people who disagree morally each give up something they care about less in exchange for the other giving up something they care about more, so each ends up closer to their own ideal — isn't just permissible but is something we probably have a pro tanto duty to do a lot more of. My mates think I've lost it, and honestly the bit I find most convincing is the bit they hate most: that it doesn't matter a jot if the person you're trading with holds views you find monstrous, and that the trade can be anonymous, indirect, and run through some kind of clearing house that sets an exchange rate between causes (so many units of animal-welfare concession to one unit of immigration-policy concession, or whatever). Lately I've been chewing over a few extensions that seem to follow and that I can't decide if they're features or reductios — trades where the commodity isn't an action but your advocacy or your vote, trades where one side is a group you're not a member of and can't really speak for, trades across time where you concede now and they concede later and someone has to hold the promise, and trades where your counterparty is simply mistaken about the facts and would give you a much worse deal if they knew what you know.

What I want is the strongest honest case against the whole thing, but done properly — not a list of things that make me feel icky, but the observations, arguments, intuitions and formal results that should actually move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. I'd especially like the defeaters that a sympathetic defender of moral trade would themselves concede rather than only the ones critics throw at it, and an honest verdict on whether the view has been quietly insulated into unfalsifiability by all the caveats it's accumulated (sincerity, no coercion, informed consent, no one's rights get violated, both sides better off ex ante) such that it never actually gets to endorse a real trade — and if that's what's happened, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just what any sensible view looks like once it's grown up. Tell me too what the position predicts that its rivals don't, in particular the rival that says moral commitments just aren't the sort of thing that can be traded at all, and whether the clearing-house exchange-rate machinery covertly assumes some interpersonal comparability of moral value that, if it doesn't hold, sinks the entire project rather than just making the arithmetic hard. And I'd be curious whether the erosion objection — that a world where everything is tradeable corrodes the norm that some things aren't for sale, and that the corroding is itself a moral cost the trade doesn't price in — is a genuine defeater or just a side-constraint that a clever defender can absorb. I'll say up front what I'd personally find most persuasive: I don't think I'd be moved by anything that just reasserts that some values are sacred, but I think I would be moved by a case where the trade works exactly as advertised and the world still comes out worse, or where the machinery turns out to be doing something under the table that the parties never agreed to.
