OK so I need help prepping for a talk I agreed to give at our town's science cafe next month and I'm starting to think I've made a big mistake. Background: I'm not an academic, I just got obsessed with the "what is life" question after reading about those nematode worms that got revived from Siberian permafrost after tens of thousands of years, and I've been annoying my friends about it ever since. My thesis, which I'm now going to have to defend in front of actual people, is that "alive" isn't a fact about the world the way "contains carbon" is -- it's a line we draw, and when people argue about whether viruses are alive they're not disagreeing about a fact, they're disagreeing about what the word is for. But I don't want to just say "it's all made up," because I do think the word does real work, I just think the work is different depending on who's asking. So I'm going to walk through a pile of borderline cases: viruses obviously, but also the giant viruses that apparently get infected by even smaller viruses, prions, viroids, plasmids and those jumping genes that just copy themselves around the genome, HeLa cells (a human cell line that's been dividing in labs since the fifties -- the cells are definately alive, but is the *thing* alive, and is it even a person's cells anymore?), and mitochondria, which used to be free-living bacteria and now can't survive on their own. Plus the reverse cases -- tardigrades and those worms, which are basically dead for years and then aren't. I also want to bring up the idea that maybe the real test should be "does it evolve" instead of "does it metabolize," and the weird case of a beaker of chemicals that starts making more of itself with no cells at all.

The audience is what's scaring me. It's a science cafe so it's whoever shows up, but I know a few regulars: a retired microbiologist who will not let the metabolism thing go, two high school bio teachers who teach the official "characteristics of living things" checklist and will want to know what they're supposed to tell their students, a philosophy grad student home for the summer who I'm pretty sure is a realist about natural kinds and will call my view "deflationary" or worse, and a guy from the local astronomy club who keeps bringing up life on Europa and thinks the whole point of the definition is to have a test that works on a space probe. Could you write me a prep sheet for the Q&A -- the objections I'll actually get, phrased the way each of these people would actually phrase them, ordered by how much damage they'd do to me, with a short reply for each (like two minutes out loud) and a note on where my reply is weakest? And tell me up front what I should just concede before anybody even asks, and the one question I'm dreading that I should have an answer ready for even though I really hope nobody asks it. I don't need a reading list or anything like that, I just need to not get destroyed in front of my neighbors.
