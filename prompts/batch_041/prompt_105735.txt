I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to two decades, and the topic I keep circling back to is personal identity — specifically what actually matters in survival, as opposed to what merely preserves a label. I've accumulated a set of gut verdicts on the thought experiments, and they don't obviously add up to anything. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, they express, and which of them I should abandon.

Here are my verdicts, stated as plainly as I can manage.

Destructive teletransportation: if a scanner reads me, the original is destroyed, and a body on Mars wakes up with my memories and dispositions, I think I survive. I'd sign up for it, and not merely because a duplicate would carry on my projects — I think the Mars body is me. If it were tortured afterward, that is bad for me.

Non-destructive copying: if instead the scanner leaves me intact and also builds the Mars body, I think the Mars person is not me. I feel about him roughly the way I'd feel about an identical twin who shares all my memories — real concern, but not the concern I have for my own future. If he is tortured after I die, I don't think that is bad for me, whereas in the destructive case it plainly is.

Brain transplant: if my brain is transplanted into a new body, that is me. If only one hemisphere is transplanted and the other discarded, I still think it's me, though I'd say the result is dimmer or thinner than the original.

Fission: if my brain is divided and both halves are transplanted into two bodies that both wake up, I don't think I'm either one. I think I've become two people, and that this is not death — it's survival twice over. This is the case I can't square with the rest.

Gradual versus sudden replacement: if my neurons are swapped one at a time, over years, for silicon chips doing the same job, I think I survive the whole way through. If every neuron is swapped in a single operation, I think I don't — what wakes up is a copy.

Psychic overwrite: if my memories and character are gradually overwritten with someone else's, so that at the end there is a person who remembers nothing of being me and shares none of my traits, I think I've died, even though the body never stopped breathing.

Delayed destruction: here is a case I've constructed and genuinely don't have a verdict on. The scanner reads me and builds the Mars body; the original — me — is not destroyed, but placed in a reversible coma for a year and then woken. The Mars body wakes immediately. Does the Mars body's being me depend on my being destroyed? My destructive verdict says yes, my copy verdict says no. And if, at the moment of waking on Mars, I'm told the original will be killed in an hour rather than now, does that change the answer?

My tentative principle, which I'd like you to test and repair: what matters in survival is the preservation of a pattern — the information-bearing organization of my psychology and the dispositions that flow from it — carried forward through a causal process linking the earlier being to the later one, where the process need not be physical and the pattern need not be realized in the same matter. This makes the destructive case survival, the non-destructive copy not-me (there are now two bearers of the pattern, and the relation carried-forward-from picks out the original), gradual replacement survival, and fission the sticking point, since the pattern is carried forward to two beings at once and the principle says nothing about branching.

What I want from you: state as precisely as you can the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best fits these verdicts; name the cases that motivate each clause; say which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up, and why I should prefer that to a messier principle that saves all of them; then apply it to cases I haven't given. At minimum: a case where the causal chain runs through a third party who memorizes everything about me and builds the copy from that memory rather than from a direct scan; a case where the copy is built from a scan taken years before my death, so the copy is experientially younger than I am at death; and a case where I am copied, the original lives on, and over decades both original and copy drift until neither much resembles the person who was scanned. I don't want a reading list — I want the principle. And I'd rather have an honest statement of where your principle yields a verdict I'll find counterintuitive than a principle quietly tailored so that every case comes out the way I already feel about it.
