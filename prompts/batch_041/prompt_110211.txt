So I'm about 200 pages into a novel and I've hit a snag I can't think my way out of. My protagonist is a decision theorist — the sort who reads LessWrong at 3am and has strong opinions about acausal trade — and there's a scene where she gets mugged. Only the mugger doesn't want her wallet. He says he's from a far-future civilization, and if she just says yes out loud, he'll run a simulation containing 10^100 happy people. If she stays quiet, nothing happens. So she's definately supposed to say yes, right? The expected value is astronomically positive no matter how tiny the odds.

Here's where I'm stuck. My understanding is that this whole Pascal's mugging thing is basically a proof that expected utility maximization is incoherent — a formal reductio showing that any agent who maximizes expected utility can be talked into handing over anything, so the whole framework collapses. So I wrote the scene where she sees this and concludes the only sane move is to throw out expected utility altogether and trust her gut. My beta reader, who's a philosophy grad student, says I've got it backwards, and that "ditch EU, trust your gut" is just about the opposite of the standard reaction to the argument. Is she right? I need to know what the mugging actually does and doesn't establish, because the ending of my book hinges on it, and I don't want the climax to rest on a misreading.

Also, practically speaking — what should she do in the scene? I need her to actually make a choice, and it's a wierd thing to have to dramatize.
