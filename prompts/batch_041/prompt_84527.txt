Ok this is a slightly weird one but I think you'll see why I'm stuck. I teach philosophy at a small liberal arts college (about 1800 students, the kind of place where the entire faculty walks in the commencement procession), and this year it's my turn to give the commencement adress. Eight minutes, outdoors under a tent, roughly 250 graduates and behind them a couple thousand parents, grandparents and little siblings who are hot and proud and have never read a word of Adam Smith. Except in the third row, where my whole department will be sitting, plus the eleven seniors who took my Smith seminar last fall, and they will absolutely notice if I turn Smith into a motivational speaker. What I want is for the impartial spectator to be the actual spine of the speech, not the opening quotation before I get around to the real advice. I want the talk to be, in some sense, an instance of the thing it's about: I'm standing up there trying to get two thousand people to feel what I feel about these kids, trimming myself down to what the room can follow, and Smith has an account of exactly that, of sympathy as a correspondence you have to work for rather than something that just happens. So I want at least one honest moment in the middle where the speech turns that mirror on itself — me admitting what I'm doing up there while I do it. But it can't become a lecture about Smith. My test is my uncle Ray, who will be there, who has never read a page of moral philosophy, and who will tell me at lunch if it was boring.

Three things I care about. First, don't quote Smith, not one line in quotation marks. I don't trust that anything I could produce from memory is word for word right, and I'm not standing in front of a tent full of people attributing sentences to a dead man that he maybe never wrote — the philosophers in row three would know, and it would be a bad joke in a speech about caring more about being praiseworthy than about being praised. Paraphrase, or just state the idea and keep moving. Second, the bit of Smith I keep circling isn't the invisible hand thing everyone's uncle already knows, but the passage about the poor man's son — the kid who looks around, finds his father's cottage too small, admires the condition of the rich, works himself into the ground for the palaces and the retinue and the machines, and only in the weariness of old age sees what he has been chasing for what it is. And I don't want the standard money-doesn't-buy-happiness commencement move with it, because that isn't quite his point: the illusion about the rich isn't just an error he's warning us off, it's a deception nature practices on us and (this is how I read him, anyway) the very thing that keeps the industry of mankind in motion, so he can't simply tell us to see through it. I want the speech to sit with that tension rather than resolving it into a fortune cookie. Third, the awkward part: this year the college is giving an honorary degree to a billionaire alum, payments company, his name going on the new science building, and the president has gently suggested the address should acknowledge the occasion. Which is funny, since the whole ceremony is a machine for doing precisely what Smith is most suspicious of — admiring the rich and the great, and teaching 250 twenty-two-year-olds that the way to be loved is to be admired. I need to be gracious about the man in the front row for thirty seconds without saying anything I don't mean, and then make the case for the other thing: that what's worth having is being lovely and not merely being loved, and that the gap between those two is the whole speech.

So write it. It should sound like a person talking, not an essay with a podium, and it should be about the length I actually have — call it 1,000 to 1,100 words. When you get to the imagined spectator, don't let it collapse into conscience, or God, or what would your mother think; those are the three wrong versions and I don't want any of them in there, because the point is that this spectator has nothing at stake in you either way, and if the speech has to get that across it should do it by example rather than by definition. And end it so the parents who've never read Smith walk out with something they can say to their kid over lunch, without me having told them a lie to get there.
