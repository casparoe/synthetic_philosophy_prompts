I chair the forecast-evaluation working group at a public hazard-forecasting service. Each of our forecasters submits a probability for every event on a fixed list — that a river exceeds flood stage in a given sector within 72 hours, that a heat threshold is crossed in a given district, and so on. We score those probabilities, publish a monthly leaderboard, and publish a single pooled house number that regional authorities use in their planning. At the quarterly meeting I have to circulate a written opinion on three proposals that have been put to the group, and I would like you to draft it.

Our current practice, for context. We score with the Brier score, a bounded penalty on the gap between the probability a forecaster assigned and what actually happened, and we take the house number to be the arithmetic mean of the members' probabilities. Two episodes last month have pushed this to a head. One member gave 0.02 to an event that then occurred; under our metric that confident miss cost him almost nothing, and the authority that relies on us wrote to say that the metric rewards fence-sitting. Another member gave 0.45 to an event that did not occur and fell several places. Neither episode by itself tells us which rule is right; both tell us that the choice of rule is doing real work.

The three proposals before us are these. First, replace the Brier score with the logarithmic score, which is unbounded, so that a confident miss is penalized without limit. Second, replace the raw score altogether with a skill score measured against a baseline — and the baseline the proposers have in mind is not climatology but our own office's forecasts over the previous five years, on the argument that what the authority is buying is improvement rather than accuracy as such. Third, compute the house number by averaging log-odds rather than probabilities, on the ground that a member's updating and the group's pooling should commute: on this rule it does not matter whether we update first and then pool or pool first and then update, whereas on the arithmetic mean it does.

Two notes that have been circulated worry me more than the proposals themselves, and I want the memo to confront them honestly rather than around them. One member argues that the entire exercise rests on the premise that accuracy is the only epistemic good — the premise of what is called accuracy-first epistemology — and that if the premise is false, if informativeness, or the ability to explain one's number, or decision-relevance, is a separate good that the metric ignores, then we are optimizing a quantity the authority never asked for. Another notes that a scoring rule of the kind we are considering is strictly proper, meaning that a forecaster does best, by her own lights, by reporting her genuine credence — and that the flip side of this is that a forecaster's own credences always look optimal to her: no score can tell her that her priors are wrong, only that she departed from them. If that is right, the memo has to say what a metric can still discipline, and to tell the authority plainly what they are and are not buying.

Please draft the memo with the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths.

- Recommendation (about 120 words). The bottom line on each of the three proposals, stated first and without hedging.
- Background (about 200 words). Our current practice, the two episodes, and what the authority has asked for.
- The case for the proposals (about 350 words).
- The case against (about 350 words), giving the strongest version of the objection rather than a token one, including the point that any baseline encodes a prior.
- What the metric can and cannot discipline (about 400 words). My answer to the two notes above, and what follows from it for the three proposals.
- Implementation (about 150 words). What we adopt, what we tell the authority, what we commit to revisiting, and a date for revisiting it.

No citations or reading list, please; I want the argument, not the literature. Write it as a document I can circulate on Monday.
