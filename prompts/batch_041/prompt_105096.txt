I'm putting together a handout for a first-year criminal justice theory module and I want a 'which theory of punishment do you actually hold?' decision tree, not another list of names to memorise. Build it from the logical space rather than the textbook labels: the first split should be whether the justification looks backward (something was done) or forward (something will be prevented), and after that each node should be one of the questions that actually pulls the positions apart — whether desert is a necessary condition for punishment at all, whether it's sufficient on its own or only a ceiling, whether the justifying reasons have to be agent-neutral or may be agent-relative (does it matter who gets deterred?), whether the offender's own good counts as a reason to punish them, and whether the state's aim is to communicate censure or to repair a relationship. Phrase every node as a question a student can answer without knowing which label sits at the end of the branch, and test each node with a concrete case — at least these three: the wrongdoer who's already reformed and harmless but whose punishment would calm a riot, the wrongdoer who can't be deterred by anything, and the corporate offender that can only be fined. Leaves should include pure deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, denunciation/communication, restorative justice, negative retributivism, positive retributivism, and a hybrid or two, each carrying the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody really defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate, and mark which answer combinations come out empty and why — incoherent, unattractive, or just never defended.
