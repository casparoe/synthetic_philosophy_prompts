So this is a slightly odd request, and I'm not totally sure it's answerable, but here goes. I'm chairing a two-day workshop next month on the welfare of farmed insects -- crickets, mealworms, black soldier fly larvae, that kind of thing -- and I have to give the opening remarks. The room is genuinely split and its my job to set a tone that keeps both halves in the room. One half is philosophers and animal-welfare people, several of whom think the whole enterprise is either obviously serious or obviously a category error, depending on the day and the person. The other half is the industry side: engineers who run the climate-controlled rearing rooms, a couple of entomologists who mostly want to talk about growth rates, and two founders who are visibly nervous that I'm going to spend ten minutes subtly implying they run slaughterhouses. We also had a bad month locally -- a rearing facility had a heat-control failure and lost something like nine million crickets overnight, and the local paper ran a piece about it that was, honestly, all over the place on whether that was a tragedy or just a broken thermostat.

What I want from you is those opening remarks, maybe six to eight minutes spoken. Here's the thing I keep getting stuck on, and it's where I'd like the speech to actually live: I don't believe sentience is a switch, and I doubt anyone in that room believes it either, but the moment you say it comes in degrees you've made a rod for your own back, because the next question is always "degrees of what, and where does it stop." So I want the remarks to take the gradation seriously as the organizing idea -- a scale that starts at something like a pig, comes down through a shrimp, down through a fruit fly, and then keeps going past the point where anyone's comfortable, past whatever you'd guess is the simplest nervous system that could feel anything, and out the other side to things that sense and respond to their surroundings but that nobody would call unhappy. I want the industry people to feel their work is being taken seriously rather than indicted, and I want the philosophers to feel I haven't just smoothed everything into "it's complicated." And I'd rather the speech end by handing the room a genuinely open question they can fight about at the coffee break than by resolving it for them. Please don't lean on citations or names -- it's remarks, not a lecture, and if you name-drop a bunch of papers it'll die in the room.
