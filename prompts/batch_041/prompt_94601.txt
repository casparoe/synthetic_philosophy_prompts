I teach the second half of a 300-level seminar in applied ethics whose last three weeks are devoted to the moral weight of suffering, and I am the only grader for the midterm, so I would like a careful second opinion on one student's paper before I hand it back. The exam was closed-book; the students had read Toby Ord's essay "Why I'm Not a Negative Utilitarian" and Andreas Mogensen's paper on the weight of suffering, and in class we built up the view we called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU) as follows. There is a threshold depth of suffering such that suffering at or beyond that depth cannot be compensated by any amount of happiness, however great, while suffering below that depth remains commensurable with happiness in the ordinary way, so that a mild pain can be outweighed by a sufficient amount of pleasure. The exam question was: "(a) State LTNU and distinguish it from two neighboring views: unthresholded lexical negative utilitarianism, on which no amount of happiness outweighs any suffering at all, however mild, and a totalism that merely assigns suffering a large but finite negative weight. (b) Explain what the threshold is supposed to do about the pinprick objection, and what it does, if anything, about the world-exploder objection -- the charge that a view which gives suffering lexical priority must approve of painlessly extinguishing all sentient life, since extinction prevents all future suffering. (c) Consider two worlds. World A: a large population, most of whose members live with deep, unrelieved suffering, though each of them also experiences some happiness. World B: a smaller population, all of whose members have lives worth living, with only mild and passing pains and a great deal of happiness. What does LTNU imply about the comparison, and how? Then: is the threshold arbitrary? State the strongest version of the arbitrariness objection you can, and say whether LTNU can meet it."

The student wrote: "(a) LTNU says that there is a threshold depth of suffering. For suffering below that depth, suffering and happiness are commensurable, and enough happiness can outweigh a mild pain; this is what lets the view escape the pinprick objection. For suffering at or beyond the threshold, the lexical claim applies: such suffering is infinitely disvaluable relative to happiness, so no amount of happiness, however great, can compensate for it. Because worlds containing suffering beyond the threshold are all infinitely bad, LTNU can deliver only a partial ordering of them. The view is thus distinct both from unthresholded lexical negative utilitarianism, which applies the priority of suffering at every depth, and from a totalism that assigns suffering a large but finite negative weight. (b) The threshold is what disarms the pinprick objection, since the lexical priority is confined to deep suffering and ordinary trade-offs remain available for mild pains. The same restriction disarms the world-exploder objection. That objection has force only against a view on which any suffering whatsoever is a reason to end everything; under LTNU, worlds whose members' suffering stays below the threshold are governed by the ordinary calculus, so nothing licenses destroying them, and the view therefore does not carry the implication that made unthresholded negative utilitarianism unacceptable. (c) LTNU implies that World A is worse than World B. Once suffering of the relevant depth is present, happiness cannot be weighed against it, so the comparison turns on how much suffering each world contains, and A contains far more of it than B does. This is the verdict a threshold view is designed to deliver. The threshold itself is not arbitrary: it is fixed by the point at which suffering stops being tolerable and a life stops being worth living, which is a fact about the lives being compared rather than a line drawn for convenience."
