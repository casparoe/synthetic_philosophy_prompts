Hi — I'm a novelist (three books, mid-list, you havent heard of me) and for the last year I've sat on the grants panel of a small foundation that hands out money to writers. It's basically buying people time. Six of us, two meetings a year, about £60k to give away, forty applications, one afternoon to decide. There's no rubric. One panelist thinks we should give everything to whoever's worst off; another thinks we should fund the best book, full stop; the rest of us improvise, and there's alot of quiet resentment afterwards. A friend who teaches philosophy says the thing I keep circling is prioritarianism and the thing that makes me uneasy is called the leveling-down objection. Maybe. I don't know if that's the right label for it.

Can you write me a decision procedure we can actually run? Take the role of a professional philosopher who works on distributive justice and who has also had to make real funding decisions with real money and no time. Please brainstorm for a bit first — talk me through what the view actually commits you to, and where it's tempting to cheat — and then give me the procedure itself. It has to be usable by six writers with no training, in an afternoon, without anybody having to say "axiology."

It needs to have the questions in order, what a good-enough answer to each one looks like, the places where we're allowed to just stop and say no, and the places where the procedure has to hand it to a person because it's a values question and not a facts question. And it has to be built so that it can never fund something that leaves the worse-off no better off and the better-off worse off, just because it makes things more even. I've heard that argued for, and I half agreed, and that scared me. If you think that constraint is wrong, say so and say why — but the procedure has to take a position on it either way, and be honest that it's taking one. Also: we can't measure anyone's welfare. So which proxies does it use, and where do they break?

Then run it on three applications and show your work. (a) A famous critic wants a grant to write a book that will, in their words, cut three celebrated novelists down to size. It won't help any struggling writer at all, but it will make the field less top-heavy, and two panelists are thrilled. (b) A broke writer with no project, just rent, who would produce nothing. (c) A rich, famous novelist who wants to finish a book that will be a masterpiece, and has tacked on a mentoring scheme that gives twelve beginners a small boost. One of these should come out in a way I won't like. Tell me which, and don't soften it.

Finally: which steps are just judgment dressed up as procedure, and what can this thing not do at all?
