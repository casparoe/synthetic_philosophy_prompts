I'm a few years into a cog sci PhD and I've somehow ended up in the middle of the perception-versus-cognition fight, and at this point I've stopped believing the two sides are even disagreeing about the same thing. The shape of it, roughly: one camp says there's a big pile of results showing that what you believe, want, or were just primed with changes what you literally see -- ambiguous figures flipping depending on what you're looking for, wine rated as better when you're told it cost more, letters in a blurry word resolving into the word you're expecting, ambiguous faces sorted into different categories depending on what you were told about them a second earlier. The other camp goes through the pile one by one and says every case is explained by something boring: attention drifting, memory of the previous trial, the subject guessing what the experimenter wants and giving the number that fits, or a judgement being reported rather than a percept being had. Then both sides write papers saying the other side's cases aren't cases, and this has been going on since the modularity people first drew the line, so about forty years now.

Here's my hypothesis, tell me if it's right. I think the two camps are using different tests for whether a state counts as perceptual. One test is introspective: does the state have the content it seems to have, does it present itself as a fact about the world rather than a thought about the world, is it fine-grained and stubborn against the subject's own correction. The other test is causal-architectural: was the state produced by something sealed off from the subject's beliefs about the stimulus, or is it downstream of them. Those two tests come apart. A state can pass the first and fail the second -- an experience that looks perceptual but is driven by expectation -- and the reverse. If that's the fault line, then no experiment settles anything, because each side takes its own test as the thing to be explained and treats the other's as either beside the point or as evidence that the other side is confused. The penetration people are asking whether the causal story has belief in it; the no-penetration people are asking whether the experience has belief-shaped content in it, and answering the first question to the second.

But I don't know if that's the real fault line or if I've just redescribed "is this perception or judgement" in fancier words. And there might be a third thing doing the work: the two sides may disagree about how much top-down influence a system can take and still be worth calling perceptual at all -- one treating "perceptual" as a functional role, the other as a kind of vehicle -- in which case there's no fact of the matter about where the word stops applying, and part of the impasse is just terminological. Except that part of it looks substantive to me, because the reason anyone cares whether a state is perceptual is that perception is supposed to be the tribunal beliefs answer to, and a system that porous maybe can't play that role.

So: is my hypothesis right, half-right, or wrong? Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a mix, and if it's a mix, which bits are which? And what would count as progress given the diagnosis -- a distinction that lets one side's results count for the other, a decisive experiment (and if so, what would it have to look like, given the obvious ones have all been run), or an admission that the question is badly posed and should be swapped for a narrower one? I don't want a literature review, I want the diagnosis and your reasons for it.

Write it as continuous prose please -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just write it out like you'd write a letter.
