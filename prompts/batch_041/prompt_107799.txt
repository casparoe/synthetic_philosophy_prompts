I host a tiny podcast on cognitive science and philosophy of mind, mostly with people whose work hasn't quite hit the mainstream yet, and next weeks guest is a researcher who has been developing a fairly heterodox line on Bongard problems -- the classic ones with six little boxes on the left and six on the right, where you have to figure out what distinguishes left from right. Her claim is that almost everyone since the problems were popularized has treated them as a test of concept induction from examples, and that this is a category mistake. On her view, the problems are really testing something more like concept possession under a generative grammar of the grid: the solver doesn't infer a rule from scratch, she recognizes which of a small stock of drawing operations and spatial predicates could have produced the six left figures and not the six right ones. She also has this phrase, 'drawing affordance,' for the idea that the figures are artifacts of a hand that could have drawn them otherwise, and that this is what makes the left/right contrast meaningful rather than just a statistical split. She's giving a talk in our department next month and I want to interview her in a way that actually pushes on the view, not just lets her restate it.
