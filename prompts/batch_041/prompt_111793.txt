Write a joke about why promises bind. The humor should turn on the substance of the competing accounts rather than on the general oddity of philosophers, and it should land for someone who has read a bit in this area: the assurance or expectation view, on which breaking a promise wrongs the promisee by defeating an expectation the promisor deliberately created; the reliance view, on which the wrong consists in inducing reliance, the distinction contract theorists mark by contrasting expectation with reliance damages; the practice view, on which the obligation belongs to a shared practice and can't be assessed one act at a time; the convention-based account usually attributed to Hume, on which bindingness is an artifact of an existing social convention and is unintelligible apart from one; and the constitutive-rules account, on which the obligation follows from the meaning of the speech act itself, so that a promise nobody ever hears about binds anyway. A natural setup is a promise that is never discovered, so that its bindingness comes out differently depending on which account you hold, though you should feel free to invent a better one. Keep it short. After the joke, add a brief note on where it takes liberties with these positions or leans on a contested reading, separating what is a standard textbook gloss from what is genuinely disputed among people who know the literature, and don't let a contested characterization pass as the consensus. Begin with the joke itself, with no preface or commentary.
