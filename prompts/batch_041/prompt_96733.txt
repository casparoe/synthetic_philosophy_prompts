I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm at work on an essay that hands a contemporary problem to a thinker who died before it could be posed. The problem is what the effective-altruism literature calls a suffering risk, or s-risk: the possibility that the long-run future is not merely empty or foreclosed but positively awful — that misdirected artificial intelligence, or engineered minds, or an expanding civilization, could at some point bring into existence astronomical quantities of sentience whose lives are not worth living, and could do so in a way that is stable and hard to reverse. Nothing in John Stuart Mill's world resembled this, but almost everything in his ethics bears on it, and I want to know what he would have said.

Please reconstruct Mill's likely position, arguing as far as you can in his own idiom, on each of the following, and then tell me where the reconstruction is most likely to be wrong. Write it so that a reader who knows Mill but not the contemporary literature can follow the whole thing.

First, weight. Mill's hedonism is aggregate and, on its face, impartial: all pleasure and pain count, wherever and whenever they occur. Does that make the prevention of vast future suffering lexically prior to the pursuit of vast future happiness, or does he have grounds for preferring a secure and moderate present over a small chance of something enormous? Where exactly do his case for security, and his insistence that human beings are progressive creatures with permanent interests, cut?

Second, fanaticism. The standard objection is that a tiny probability of astronomical suffering licenses monstrous sacrifices in the present. Would Mill accept the implication, resist it, or deny that the comparison is well formed? Is there anything in his account of the higher pleasures — in the thought that some lives are qualitatively richer rather than merely more pleasant — that bears on whether a world containing enormous suffering can be weighed against one containing enormous happiness?

Third, scope. Whose suffering would count for him: non-human animals, minds built rather than born, minds whose experiences we have no name for? The harm principle is framed around the liberty of persons; does it have anything to say about the act of creating minds that suffer, or does his hedonism carry the whole burden?

Fourth, asymmetry. Much contemporary suffering-focused ethics treats suffering as bad in a way that the mere absence of pleasure is not. Mill is usually read as a symmetric hedonist. Which way would he go, and does his critique of Bentham's narrowness, in the essays on Bentham and Coleridge, push him toward asymmetry or away from it?

Fifth, nature. In his essay on nature, Mill argues that the ordinary course of things is neither a moral model nor a guide, and that human effort is right to improve on it. Does that meliorism extend to redesigning the conditions under which future minds come to exist, or does it stop at the boundary of the human?

Finish with the doctrine that does the most work in your reconstruction, the one Mill would have had to surrender, and the single fact about his writings that would most change your answer. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need the argument.
