I am an undergraduate in a seminar on emergence, reduction, and explanation in cognitive science, and in two weeks I owe a proposal for the term paper I will write over the six weeks after that (roughly five thousand words). The proposal has to state a question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the familiar positions on it do not settle it, set out the method and the order of the steps, say what would count as a result and what would count as the project failing, and be honest about what can realistically be done in the time available. I will attach the citations myself, so please do not supply a bibliography or a literature review; refer to positions and arguments in your own words and leave the references to me.

My half-formed idea starts from the careenium, the thought experiment in Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop. A large flat arena contains a very large number of tiny magnetic marbles — Hofstadter calls them simms — which careen about and collide. Because they are magnetic, they sometimes adhere into clusters, which he calls simmballs; the clusters persist, merge, split, and drift in patterns that can be described without mentioning any individual marble. The arena's walls change how bouncy they are in response to conditions outside the arena, so the drift of the simmballs carries information about the outside world, and the simmballs interact with one another in a way that looks like the manipulation of symbols. Hofstadter asks who is doing the pushing inside the careenium: the individual simms, each following local rules and encoding nothing, or the simmballs, which are the only things in the story that appear to stand for anything. The moral is meant to carry over to the brain — neurons below, symbols above.

I can feel the question sliding around, and I think that is the paper. My suspicion is that it trades on a conflation between three different claims: that simmball-level states make a difference to where individual simms go; that the simmball-level description is the one that carries the information about the world and licenses prediction; and that simmballs produce the simm motions in some thicker sense. I want to argue that only the second is defensible, and then to ask what feature of the careenium makes the conflation so hard to resist — and whether the brain has that feature, more of it, or less. If the seduction depends on a feature the brain lacks, that should change how we state the autonomy of the higher-level description.

Turn this into the proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, and make clear which claims about the careenium would settle it. Explain why the usual positions in the debate about higher-level causation leave it open, without assuming I have read any particular text. Set out the method: how I should specify the careenium precisely enough for it to serve as a controlled case, which notions of causation I should apply to it and in what order, and what perturbations of the model — for instance, a variant in which the simmball-level regularities are robust but make no difference to the simms — would discriminate among the candidate answers. Say what a successful outcome looks like, what would show the project had collapsed into an exercise in stipulation, and which parts are too ambitious for six weeks and should be cut or deferred. Finally, say why this is worth a term's work rather than a footnote.
