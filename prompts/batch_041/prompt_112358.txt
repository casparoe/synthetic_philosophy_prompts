I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on the epistemology of disagreement, and I've hit a wall that I suspect is not just my own confusion. In our reading group we've spent the better part of a year on conciliationism versus steadfastness, and the exchange has become ritualized. The conciliationist says: when someone you regard as an epistemic peer disagrees with you about p, you have higher-order evidence that you've made a mistake, and that evidence should pull your credence toward theirs; to ignore it is to privilege your own reasoning in a way you can't justify. The steadfaster says: the mere fact that someone equally intelligent and informed disagrees does not by itself give you a reason to change, because the disagreement may simply show that the other person has reasoned badly; what matters is the first-order evidence and the correctness of your reasoning, not the sociology of who believes what. We all know the standard objections—the self-undermining problem for conciliationism, the independence principle, the difficulty of identifying peers, the worry that steadfastness collapses into dogmatism—and yet no one's position has budged. The arguments seem to slide past each other, and I can't tell whether that's because the issue is genuinely hard or because the two sides aren't really answering the same question.

I've developed a hypothesis, but I don't trust it. It's that the debate is stalled because both sides accept a container picture of evidence—evidence as a body of propositions the agent possesses—and then disagree about whether the fact of peer disagreement is a new proposition to be added to the container. Conciliationists say yes; steadfasters say no, or say that it is in the container but lacks the weight the conciliationists assign it. If evidence is instead a relation between an agent and a proposition under a question—something like E is evidence for H for S in context C—then the two sides may be answering different questions: one about the strength of an existing evidential relation, the other about whether a new relation is created at all. That would make the impasse partly a question-substitution and partly a substantive disagreement about what evidence is. But I can also see a less flattering diagnosis: that peer is doing illicit work, and that conciliationism is nearly analytic if peer means equally likely to be right, while steadfastness is nearly analytic if peer means equally rational and equally informed. On that reading it's a verbal dispute dressed up as a substantive one. I've also wondered whether the whole exchange presupposes uniqueness—that a given body of evidence permits exactly one rational credence—and whether dropping that assumption would make conciliationism and steadfastness look like answers to different questions rather than rivals. I don't know how to choose between these diagnoses, or whether they're really distinct.

What I want from you is not a survey of the literature and not a verdict on who is right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement: is it substantive, verbal, a hidden empirical question, a clash of epistemic frameworks, or two different questions under one heading? Use what each side actually claims, as I've sketched it, and tell me what kind of progress is possible given your diagnosis—a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing at all. Please take a definite position on which diagnosis is correct and defend it. I'd rather you commit to a diagnosis and give your reasons than lay out five possibilities and leave the choice to me. If you think my container hypothesis is confused or that the verbal-dispute diagnosis is too quick, say so directly and explain why.
