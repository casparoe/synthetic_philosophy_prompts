I spent thirty-one years as a field entomologist — beetles, mostly — and I retired two years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy the way I used to read the literature: too much, too fast, no system. I've hit something I can't get past, and I want you to write me a proper essay about it. Put on your philosopher's hat for this one, the kind of philosopher who thinks hard about probability and about what it means to locate yourself in a world, and think it through slowly before you commit to anything. Brainstorm a little first if that helps. I'd rather have your careful reasoning than a tidy summary of a debate, and please don't bother with citations or a reading list — I'll never chase them down anyway.

Here's the problem. There seem to be two ways to handle the fact that I'm me rather than somebody else. The first says: treat yourself as though you'd been picked at random from the observers who actually exist, out of some group you've decided you belong to. The second says: treat yourself as though you'd been picked at random from all the observers who could possibly exist, so that a world containing more observers is a world more likely to be the one you're in, simply because it holds more slots for someone like you to occupy. A friend tells me the second is discussed in a book by Nick Bostrom called Anthropic Bias and that he actually argues against it — fine, but I want the arguments, not the scorecard.

Each view seems to get it's comeuppance. Against the second: imagine two possibilities, one in which the universe is modest and holds ten billion minds, one in which it's enormous and holds ten billion trillion. Nothing in my evidence distinguishes them. Yet if I'm a random draw from all possible observers, the enormous universe is overwhelmingly more likely, and I should expect to be one of its inhabitants rather than a human being at all. That conclusion feels like something has gone wrong, but I can't see where. Against the first: if I'm a random draw from all the humans who will ever live, then finding myself somewhere around the middle of the queue suggests the queue is nearly over — the doomsday argument. Which also feels like something has gone wrong.

And here's where my old field keeps intruding, and where I actually want your verdict. Suppose it turns out — and having spent my life around insects this is not an idle supposition for me — that the universe is stuffed with enormous numbers of very simple minds: not people, not even clever animals, but creatures with just enough going on to have an experience, and vastly more of them than there are of us. The second view seems to say I should now think I'm almost certainly one of them. The first view says the answer depends entirely on which group I chose to be a random member of, which starts to look like picking the group that hands you the answer you wanted. And then there's the case that definately ties me in knots: what if the number of observers depends on what I do, if I can press a button that makes more minds of my kind? Does either view give me a reason to press it, or a reason to care either way?

So: essay, please. Weigh both views honestly, then take a side and defend it — even if the side you take is that one of them survives only in a patched-up form, or that the whole way of posing the question is broken and something else should replace it. Reach a conclusion and justify it; don't leave me with a shrug. One thing though: write it as plain continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just the way you'd write a long letter to somebody who's genuinely interested.
