I'm a science journalist and I'm finishing a long feature on predictive processing / the free energy principle -- Friston, Andy Clark, the brain as an inference engine that's constantly guessing what's causing its input -- and I want to close the piece with a poem instead of one more summary paragraph. I've got a friday deadline and I keep failing to write the ending myself.

So: you're a poet who also actually knows this literature (not just the vibes), and I want a pantoum. The reason is that the form already seems to be the theory. In a pantoum, lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, so the same line keeps getting re-read in a new surrounding -- which is exactly the claim that the same bottom-up signal means different things under a different prior. And the way the last stanza has to fold the poem's opening line back in is the loop where the system ends up modelling itself. So let the recurring lines be what the system expects, and the fresh lines be what actually arrives and has to be squared with them.

Two things I really need it to engage, because they're the parts of my piece I don't want to shortchange. First, the dark room problem: if the whole game is minimizing prediction error, why doesn't the system just find a dark quiet room and sit there forever, where nothing ever surpises it? Second, the cousin of that worry: if a system can act on the world instead of updating its model, then one that's good at controlling its own inputs can keep a flattering delusion running indefinitely -- so what exactly is accuracy buying you? Take a side if you have one, or at least don't pretend these are settled.

Before you write, spend a few sentences thinking out loud about the mapping -- which formal feature carries which claim -- then give me the poem, 5 or 6 stanzas. A specialist should recognise the theory; my readers shouldn't need a glossary.
