I'm moderating the Q&A after next Thursday's seminar and I could use help with questions. The speaker is a psychologist who's spent basically her whole career as the most visible defender of the "the gender-equality paradox is telling us something about biology" line. You know the finding: the higher a country scores on the usual gender-equality indices, the *bigger* the sex differences tend to be — in the share of women among STEM graduates, in self-reported risk and altruism and trust, in measured personality traits. She's done this talk a hundred times and she's very smooth, so I need questions that don't let her just re-deliver it or wave at the literature.

Here's the stuff I've been chewing on, which I'd like the questions to be built out of — not questions *about* these points, but questions that make her do work:

- The indices are composites. They mix in political representation, health outcomes, etc., none of which have anything to do with the constraints a 17-year-old faces when picking a degree. So "egalitarian" might be doing no work in the correlation at all.
- The outcome is a *share* of graduates, not a rate per capita — and who ends up in the pool of graduates varies enormously across countries. In a country where few women get degrees, the ones who do may be unusually careerist and pick the secure high-earning option. So we might be comparing differently-selected samples.
- The substitution story: where STEM is one of the few routes to a decent income, choosing it is less about interest and more about security. So choice tracking interest more closely in rich countries is exactly what you'd expect — but that's a story about opportunity structure, not about what the preferences are made of.
- In the most egalitarian countries, what's left of the earnings gap is heavily concentrated in the years after a first child. That points at the interaction of care norms with job structures (long-hours premia, the ideal-worker norm), not at teenage subject choice.
- Differences in averages vs. differences in the tails. The policy fights are usually about the top 1% of some distribution, not the median, and those are separate claims.
- And the big one: even if some average difference in interests is partly innate, what follows for justice? Maybe nothing, because the injustice might live in how work and care are organised rather than in the preferences.

I want 8-10 questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — assume the standard move and write the follow-up that closes it off. At least one question on the index/measurement worry, one on the share-vs-rate/selection issue, one on the child penalty, one on means vs. tails, and one that forces her to say what evidence would actually make her drop the biological reading. Nothing that requires her to name papers or studies, and nothing as limp as "isn't this all very complicated." Also, her favorite dodge is to say it's all an empirical question and then not say which empirical question, so please include at least one follow-up that makes that dodge cost her something.
