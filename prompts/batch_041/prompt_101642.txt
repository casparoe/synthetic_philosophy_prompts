I am to deliver the eulogy at the memorial for a philosopher and teacher who spent his later years arguing that an I is not a thing but a pattern—one that can be distributed across other brains, so that after death there is no single surviving center of consciousness, only partial, overlapping, sometimes conflicting copies of him in the people who knew him. The room will be mixed: family and old friends who are not philosophers and who need genuine comfort; graduate students and colleagues who know the view and will notice any dilution; and a few people who knew him only slightly. Write the eulogy. It should not claim that he is watching over us, that he is in a better place, or that he lives on in our memories in the usual vague sense. Let the distributed-self idea do the structural work instead: the speech should locate whatever persistence he has in those partial copies, acknowledge that they are not him and that they will diverge, and still make that thought consoling rather than chilling. One twist to build in: the copies have no shared conductor, so the self that persists is more like a chorus that cannot hear itself than like a single voice. Roughly five minutes.
