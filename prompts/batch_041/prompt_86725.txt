I am a doctoral student in philosophy, working on the foundations of computer science, and I have been sitting in on a course in computational complexity while also maintaining a small scheduling tool for my research group, which is how the following question arose. The tool solves a combinatorial problem — I will not belabor the details; it involves fitting tasks into slots subject to a budget — and the algorithm I wrote for it is, on every input my users actually produce, effectively instantaneous: its running time scales with the magnitudes of the numbers appearing in the input rather than with how many numbers there are, and since those magnitudes are never enormous, it never bogs down. A friend who works in complexity theory pointed out that, strictly speaking, the algorithm is exponential, because the numbers are written in binary and the running time is exponential in the number of digits; had the same problem been posed with the numbers written in unary, the very same procedure would count as a polynomial algorithm. I follow the mechanics of the point, but it seems to expose something larger than a technicality: the same algorithm is excellent by every practical measure and poor by the official one, and what separates the two verdicts is not the problem or the algorithm but how the input is written down. Since then I have noticed the mirror image — problems whose best algorithms are provably polynomial in the length of the input, yet so heavy in their constant factors that nobody would ever run them — and I have begun to suspect that "hard" and "efficient" are doing two different jobs in these conversations, one of them relative to a chosen encoding and the other relative to what can actually be computed.

What I would like to know is whether there is an established name for the specific situation I first described — an algorithm that is polynomial in the values appearing in its input but not in the length of that input, so that its standing as a "good" algorithm depends entirely on how the numbers are represented — and, if so, precisely what that concept covers and what it excludes. I am just as interested in the broader pattern: is there a settled way of marking the difference between a hardness result that survives any reasonable encoding and one that is an artifact of writing the input in binary rather than in unary? I have a vague sense that complexity theorists have vocabulary here — that some hardness results are described as more robust than others — but I cannot reconstruct the terms, nor tell whether they name a formal notion, a family of results, or merely a habit of speech. In particular I would want to know whether the distinction attaches to problems, to encodings, or to the relation between the two, and whether it has a definition sharp enough to settle borderline cases.

Please do not simply supply a label. What I am after is the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, how it differs from ordinary worst-case hardness and from the general gap between theoretical and practical efficiency, and where its edges lie. If some part of what I have described has no established concept, say so plainly and propose the term and definition you think it needs.
