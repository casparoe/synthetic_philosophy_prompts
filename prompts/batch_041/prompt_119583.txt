I'm a novelist — four books in, the new one has a Spanish Jesuit in it who won't shut up about middle knowledge — and I've been roped into leading one session of a "theology for the perplexed" night at our local arts centre, which is basically fifteen people in a church hall with a whiteboard and a kettle. The topic I foolishly picked is Molinism, which I gather is named after some Jesuit called Molina, don't ask me which century. The audience has no philosophy or theology training: two lapsed Catholics with opinions, a retired chemistry teacher who will ask about determinsm in minute four, and my brother-in-law, who thinks the whole thing is a word game. Seventy-five minutes, and I want the last twenty for actual arguing, which I know is generous.

So don't explain Molinism to me — I can read. Plan how I should explain it. I keep tangling myself up on the three kinds of knowledge and I don't want to open with "there are three kinds" like a shopping list; I want the order the pieces have to go in so that the middle one arrives as the obvious fix for a problem they can already feel, plus what has to be nailed down before each step or the next one just floats. Give me the one carrying example — a single case, reused all the way through, that can take counterfactuals of freedom and land on "God could have actualized a different world" — and tell me what that example has to have (a real person, a real choice, a situation specific enough that it's about something) and what it must not have (any hint that the circumstances or the character make the choice inevitable, because then it's just a prediction and the whole point collapses; and nothing so arbitrary that the choice looks like a coin flip). My instinct is to use a scene from my own novel, so tell me whether that's a bad idea.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually come up, with where in the sequence I head each one off: that middle knowledge is just God guessing very well what I'd do; that if God knows the counterfactual then the poor guy couldn't have done otherwise; that this is Calvinism with better manners; and that if God knew all the counterfactuals, why didn't he just pick a world where everyone freely chooses the good — I know there's a name for whatever blocks that move, don't make me go look it up, just tell me whether it's worth putting in front of this crowd at all. And tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level. The one about what makes a counterfactual of freedom true in the first place is where I can feel myself going under, so I need a way to name it, park it, and move on without looking like I'm dodging. Finally, one question to put to the room near the end that would tell me whether they've actually got the structure rather than just enjoying the paradoxes.
