Ok so I have to write a seminar essay and I want to do it on the Moloch / multipolar trap thing — races to the bottom, arms races, the whole "nobody wants this outcome and yet here we are" cluster. My problem is I genuinely can't tell whether the framing is a real theoretical contribution or just a mood. So here's what I want: an essay that takes a position on whether "Moloch" picks out a genuine failure mode that our existing tools don't already cover — game theory, collective action problems, principal-agent stuff, regulatory arbitrage, evolutionary/selection stories about firms — and defends that position. Please don't end on "it's complicated." I need a verdict, even if it's a narrow one ("it earns its keep only in this restricted class of cases, and here's why the rest is just metaphor").

Here's the distinction I've been messing around with, and I want the essay to actually engage with it instead of sailing past it. I think there are at least three things people lump together under this heading:

(i) straight dominance traps — defecting is your best move whatever everyone else does, so the bad outcome is just what rational play looks like;

(ii) coordination traps — the good outcome is available and everyone prefers it, but there's no route there from here, or no way to keep it stable at scale once you get there;

(iii) the one I actually care about — cases where the bad dynamic persists even though nobody involved prefers it, and nobody would defect from a mutual stop if they could trust it, because the pressure isn't coming from any player's preference at all. It's coming from selection: whoever exits the race, or refuses to run it, gets outcompeted, replaced, or squeezed out, and what's being selected for is a strategy, not anybody's aim. The agents are more like carriers than choosers.

What I can't settle, and want the essay to settle: is (iii) really a separate phenomenon, or is it just (ii) with a bigger cast — because once you count potential entrants, outside investors, and the option to exit as moves in the game, does the "selection" talk collapse back into ordinary equilibrium reasoning? If it does collapse, then I think the Moloch framing is doing mostly rhetorical work and I should say so, and say what it does to our thinking that's bad. My suspicion is that it makes structural outcomes feel like weather — something that just happens to us — and quietly lets whoever benefits off the hook. But I want to be argued out of that if the argument's there.

If it doesn't collapse, then I want to know exactly what kind of explanation (iii) is: is it an empirical claim that could come out false? Does it need a selector, and if the selector is just "the market" or "the culture," is invoking something like Moloch a legitimate explanatory move or a category error dressed up as a proper noun? And does any of it change if the selection is economic or cultural rather than biological?

Use one or two concrete cases to do the work, and keep them small and ordinary. I keep thinking about how every lab in my old department ended up buying the same overpriced machine, because the one lab that didn't was read as not serious — and about the herbicide treadmill, where each farmer would prefer a world where nobody sprays but the one who skips a season gets wiped out. And I want the essay to say something about responsibility in case (iii): if no one chose it and no one prefers it, is blame even the right category, or is "nobody in particular did this" the actual point?

Just don't let it turn into a literature tour or a pile of examples with no thesis. Argue for something. I'd much rather read a wrong verdict defended properly than a balanced survey that dont commit to anything.
