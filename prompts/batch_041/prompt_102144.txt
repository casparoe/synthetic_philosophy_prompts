Two of my mates in the department have been circling each other for two years over Dutch book arguments, and I've somehow been volunteered to knock their positions into an actual adverserial-collaboration document. I want you to draft it.

The two camps, roughly. Camp one — call them the betting-pragmatists — holds that a sure-loss Dutch book against you really does establish that your credences have to obey the probability axioms: being exploitable by a bookie who can drain you whatever happens just *is* a failure of rationality, and that's the whole content of probabilism as a norm on credence. Camp two — the ones who think the argument is a non-starter on its own — hold that all the construction shows is something practical or prudential, and that to squeeze an epistemic norm out of it you have to smuggle in extra premises: that the agent cares about money, that her credences equal the odds she'd actually bet at, that avoiding sure loss is bad for its own sake rather than just something a bookie likes. They've been talking past each other for ages, so I want the version they could both put their names to.

What I need in it:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign — about what the bookie construction does and doesn't show, about what a sure-loss book is, about the formalism, about which parts of the disagreement are actually agreed. These should be meaty, not platitudes.
2. The residual disagreements, each written up in wording that its own side would accept for itself — so neither formulation is a strawman of the other.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling things would actually move at least one side. My guess is one is whether "she'd bet at those odds" is a definitional claim about credence or a substantive one, and another is whether sure-loss is intrinsically irrational or only instrumentally bad — but don't just take those, work out what the real ones are.
4. For each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Please make one of those cases concrete: an agent whose credences over a simple partition don't add up — say she's at 0.6 on A and 0.5 on not-A — and a bookie who offers her a package of bets. Both sides presumably accept the bookie can lock in a sure profit; the point is what each predicts that shows about her credences, and what each says it *doesn't* show.
5. Two side-issues I'd like handled rather than dodged. First, the diachronic case — the sure-loss argument for conditionalising on new evidence, and whether it inherits or worsens whatever problem the synchronous version has. Second, the "depragmatised" version, where nobody actually bets and the appeal is instead to something like accuracy or dominance among credence-assignments; ask whether that rescues the argument or just relocates the extra premise. And say what the whole thing shows, if anything, about an agent who simply doesn't care about money or won't take bets.
6. Finally, say which parts of their dispute you think are merely verbal, and why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, then the cruxes. Don't bother with references or who-said-what — just the positions themselves, since nobody in this can look anything up.

Casual voice is fine, this is for a reading group not a journal. And at the end, be straight with me: tell me where this document is most likely to fall apart, which of the agreed claims is really doing all the work, and what argument or consideration would make you redraw the line between the genuine disagreement and the merely verbal one.
