I work as an organizer with a disability advocacy group, and for a few years now I have been trying to put my finger on a pattern I keep seeing and cannot find a satisfying name for. Here is the shape of it. One of our members, who uses a wheelchair, raises a fairly general point in a workplace meeting: that the way accommodation requests are processed is itself the barrier, because it forces people to disclose and justify needs they would rather keep private, and because it turns a right into a favor. The room is friendly, people nod, and someone thanks her for sharing her perspective. Nothing she said is disputed. But it is filed as her experience, and nothing changes. A year later a consultant, hired to advise on accessibility, presents a framework whose central claim is the same one, dressed in managerial language, and it is treated as an analysis, debated on the merits, and adopted. Now take a mirror-image case. The same member, in a different setting, tries to make a general theoretical claim — that most public discussion of accommodation is really about legal compliance rather than access — and this time people push back hard: they want evidence, they tell her she is generalizing from her own case, they call it her "view." So her autobiographical report is treated as if it carried no general weight, while her general claim is treated as if it were merely autobiographical; in both cases the remark is routed into whichever category makes it safe to absorb without acting on it. What strikes me is that nobody calls her a liar or questions her sincerity, and it is not that we lack the words to describe what she is getting at; the content is often granted, at least nominally. The wrong seems to lie in the routing itself — in which shelf the remark is placed on, and in who is permitted to restate it as knowledge.

I have read a little of the philosophy of testimony, and I know two ideas that are obviously in the neighborhood. One is the thought that a speaker can be given less credence than she deserves because of who she is. The other is the thought that a whole society can lack the shared vocabulary needed to make a wrong intelligible, so that the wrong cannot even be articulated. Neither quite fits. In the cases I have in mind, she is not disbelieved, and the vocabulary exists — she is using it. What seems to be happening is a kind of misfiling of the speech act: a general claim is heard as a personal one, a personal one is heard as a general one, and the misfiling is what does the damage. Is there an established name for this? I am less interested in a label than in the carving, so please tell me what exactly the phenomenon is, how it differs from the two ideas I just mentioned and from any other close relatives you think are relevant, and where its edges lie — for instance, whether it can occur with no identity prejudice at all, or whether it always requires an asymmetry of power between speaker and audience. If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it, and give me a boundary case or two that the definition should classify one way rather than the other.

Finally, I would like you to be explicit about how confident you are in each of your claims, and to flag the single point in your answer about which you are least sure.
