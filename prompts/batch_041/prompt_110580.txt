Draft (working title: The Decipherer's Gap)

For a workshop next month I have drafted the essay below. Its target is Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and my aim is to get past the rabbit. I have come to think the rabbit is the least useful illustration of the thesis, because it invites the reader to fix on a single exotic noun and to treat the puzzle as one about evidence: how much of it the linguist has, how patient he is, whether he can point at rabbit-stages. I want to argue that the thesis is really a claim about the structure of interpretation, and that it can be shown more sharply on a case where the evidence is abundant and the answer is not in doubt: the decipherment of dead scripts.

The claim I want to defend is this. Decipherment succeeds, when it succeeds, at what I will call thin decipherment: the recovery of a phonetic or logographic inventory, a set of proper names, numerals and calendrical terms, and a skeleton of syntax, secured by bilingual keys, name-guesses that check out, and the internal regularities of a large corpus. Thin decipherment is a constraint-satisfaction achievement, and it is genuinely constrained — a wrong assignment fails on the corpus, sometimes spectacularly. What decipherment has almost never delivered is thick decipherment: the fine-grained distinctions a native speaker would draw, between two words that both come out as the same verb in our language, between a blessing and a curse, between a technical term and its near-synonym, between a euphemism and a literal description. Where we think we have thick decipherment, we have usually inherited it from a living descendant language, a bilingual text, or an interpretive tradition, and the inheritance does the work the corpus could not.

My further claim is that Quine's indeterminacy lives exactly in the gap between the two. Two manuals can agree completely on thin decipherment — same phonology, same names, same numbers, same syntax — and differ on thick decipherment, and no further tablet will separate them. That is a stronger claim than the familiar one that evidence is finite. The familiar claim is about how much evidence we have; mine is about what any amount of it would do. Even a linguist with a complete corpus, and with the dispositional facts of every speaker, would face the same gap, because the gap is not in the data but in the mapping from dispositions to meanings: the same dispositional profile can be described by manuals that attribute different beliefs, different semantic fine structure, and different degrees of metaphorical stretch to the speakers, with no fact of the matter to adjudicate between them.

I am aware of a problem. If thick decipherment never happens, my thesis is safe but thin: it says only that we have not done what we cannot do. So I want to give it a falsifiable edge, and I am not sure I can. Here is the attempt. If a case were produced in which the only evidence available was monolingual corpus evidence, and a community of competent readers converged, for reasons internal to that corpus, on a thick reading — including fine semantic distinctions, and including the direction of the metaphors — and independent later evidence, a bilingual text discovered afterwards, say, confirmed that reading against its rivals, then the indeterminacy thesis would be in trouble. I cannot think of such a case, and I suspect the reason is not that such cases are rare but that the notion of reasons internal to a corpus is doing illicit work. I do not know how to argue that without begging the question.

There is a second thing I am uneasy about. My whole framing treats the native's meanings as the thing to be recovered, and the gap as a shortfall in recovery. But Quine's point, as I read him, is not that there is a hidden determinate meaning we cannot reach; it is that the notion of meaning which would make hidden determinate meaning intelligible has no work to do. If that is right, my gap metaphor is misleading, since a gap presupposes two sides. I have kept the metaphor because it makes the argument vivid, but I suspect a critic will say that it concedes the point I am denying.

What I want from you:

1. Accuracy. Is my reading of Quine defensible, especially the worry I raise at the end — that framing the thesis as a gap concedes the very point I am denying? If I have misdescribed his position, say what the correct description is and whether it costs me anything.

2. Is the thin/thick distinction doing real work, or would a Quinean say it collapses into the familiar point about analytical hypotheses — that my thick layer simply is the layer of analytical hypotheses, so that I have renamed rather than clarified?

3. Is the falsification condition in the fourth paragraph coherent? I suspect it presupposes a notion of reasons internal to a corpus that Quine would reject, in which case it is not a test at all. If you can repair it, do; if it cannot be repaired, say so and tell me what to do with that paragraph.

4. What should I cut for a twenty-minute slot, and in what order should the remaining pieces go?

Tell me how confident you are in each of these judgments, and single out the one point you are least sure of, and why.
