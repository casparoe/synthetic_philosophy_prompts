I'm a software engineer with no real philosophy training, and I've ended up in a small reading group with two people who did philosophy degrees. We've spent the last three sessions on the extended mind thing — Otto, the guy with Alzheimer's who writes everything in a notebook, vs. Inga who just remembers, and whether the mind stops at the skull. Nobody budged. My read is that this is basically a fight about a word: 'cognitive', 'mental', 'part of the mind'. Fix the definition and everyone should agree on the facts, and the whole thing evaporates. My friend says no, there's a real disagreement under there — one side thinks cognition is whatever plays the right functional role, the other thinks there's something special about what happens inside the head — and you can't dissolve a substantive disagreement by stipulating a definition. We've been going in circles about which of us is right about the disagreement itself.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a ruling on who wins. Is this verbal, is it substantive, is it two groups answering different questions under one label (one doing conceptual analysis of our folk notion of mind, one trying to explain behaviour), is there a hidden empirical question underneath, or is it bedrock intuitions about where a person ends that nobody can argue for? Someone in the group also keeps pressing that being causally hooked up to something isn't the same as it being part of you, and that if you buy the notebook you have to buy the phone, the wiki, the internet, the library — I genuinely can't tell if that's a serious argument or just an intuition pump dressed up as a reductio.

Here's the thing that made me want to ask. My team builds an AI coding assistant with a persistent memory of our codebase, and I've noticed I now 'know' things I cant actually recall — I only know them by asking the assistant. Under the notebook view, is that assistant part of my mind? Does it matter that it's shared with six teammates, that it sometimes makes stuff up, or that my access could be cut off tomorrow (I could get fired, the service could go down)? And if a chip in my skull storing the same facts obviously counts while the same chip sitting on my desk obviously doesn't, what's actually doing the work there — location, reliability, or ownership?

So: what kind of disagreement is this really, what is each side actually committed to, and what would count as progress? Would a shared criterion for what makes something cognitive end it, or would each side just reject the other's criterion? Is there a case or experiment that could settle it, or is the honest answer that it's just a hard question we shouldn't expect to close? And if it really is verbal, why has it been so hard to just say so and move on?
