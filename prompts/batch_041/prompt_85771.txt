Hey — I'm a grad student in philosophy (ethics) and there's something I've been chewing over for about a year now. I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on normative ethics and on the ethics of cooperation — what we're allowed to do, and what we owe each other, when we disagree about what matters. Please think this through carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming a handful of candidate principles before you commit to one. And please don't give me citations or a reading list — I don't have library access right now, and honestly I want your own reasoning, not a summary of the literature.

Here's what's bugging me. I think it's fine — good, even — for people who disagree morally to make deals with each other, where each of us does something the other cares about. But my verdicts won't line up. My gut calls:

(a) My flatmate thinks the most important cause in the world is climate; I think it's animal welfare. We each give $300 to the other's cause. Fine, and actually better than each of us giving to our own — more of what we each value gets done.

(b) My vote is worthless where I live; my cousin's is in a marginal seat. We agree to vote for each other's party. Fine by me.

(c) Paying a stranger $300 to vote for my party: definately not fine, no question.

(d) Paying a stranger $300 to eat vegetarian for a year — she couldn't care less about animals, she just wants the money. This is the one I flip-flop on constantly. Half the time it's obviously fine: fewer animals suffer, nobody's worse off. Half the time it feels like I've bought a piece of someone's life.

(e) A colleague is pro animal testing; I think it's seriously wrong. He'll give $500 to an animal charity (he thinks that's a waste of money) if I publicly endorse his lab. My endorsement would change nothing — it's a tiny lab — so by my own lights the deal is a net win. And I still think it's not fine.

(f) A friend is a committed pacifist. She'll spend her Saturday canvassing for my candidate if I spend mine collecting signatures for her disarmament petition. I think the petition is pointless; she thinks my candidate is unimportant. This one feels completely fine to me — I'd do it in a heartbeat.

My half-baked principle: a deal is OK if each of us ends up doing more of what we each care about, measured by our own lights. But that can't be right, because it makes (c) and (e) come out fine and I'm sure they aren't. So it needs repairing.

What I want: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, with each clause traced to the case that forces it; your verdicts on a seperate case or two I haven't listed that you think is the real test; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle can't save — I'd much rather give up a verdict than fudge the principle. And tell me what the thing says about (d).

If the honest answer is that no single principle gets even close, then say so — but tell me exactly which of my cases are doing the damage, and what the best available repair costs me.
