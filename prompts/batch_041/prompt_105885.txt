Last November my wife died of a glioblastoma, nine weeks after her first seizure. Since then I have been on the receiving end of what I can only describe as a folk theodicy. Everything happens for a reason. She was needed elsewhere. This is the best of all possible worlds — that last one I have now heard three times from people who I am fairly sure mean it as a compliment to Leibniz. I am not a philosopher; I read Candide in college and have read a little since, and I no longer trust my own reading, but I do not want to keep talking past people.

Since January I have been facilitating a small bereavement group at my church — eight or nine people, mostly believers, one convinced atheist, one who says she believes in something but not in God. What keeps happening is that someone offers a consolation, someone else says it makes things worse, and nobody can say what the disagreement actually is. I want to hand out a single-page instrument that sorts the consolations rather than the people: a decision tree that takes a specific thing said to a grieving person and routes it to a position on the question of whether this world, with this death in it, is the best world there could have been, or the best a good God could have arranged, or neither.

Build the tree out of the logical space, not out of a list of famous names. I do not need citations, editions, or a reading list; I have no library here and could not check them. Every branch node should be a question my group members could actually answer — phrased in ordinary language, with no philosophical vocabulary, and without naming the position it leads to. Every leaf should be a position, carrying a plain-language label and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Keep it to twelve leaves or fewer.

These are the distinctions I want the tree to turn on, because they are the ones that keep surfacing in the group and I can never hold them apart cleanly:

- whether the claim is that the whole is better than any alternative whole, or that each part is good for the person who suffers it (I gather these come apart, and that the second is not what the position actually requires);
- whether the evil is something real that a good God permits, or an absence — a good that ought to be there and is not;
- whether God's will is one thing or two: a will that everyone be well, plus a will that permits the harm because of what it makes possible;
- whether the good that is supposed to justify the death is one we could in principle see, one we could never see, or one that no finite mind could hold at once;
- whether the justification is retrospective (it turned out well) or structural (the world's order and variety are worth having, and this belongs to that order);
- whether there is any best world at all, or whether for any world there is a better one, so that best is not a target a creator could have hit;
- whether the whole project of justifying God is something one should refuse to undertake, and whether that refusal is a religious position or an irreligious one.

The tree also has to handle the consolations that are not theodicies at all — the ones that refuse to explain, that offer company rather than a reason — and tell me where those land. I think that is where half the group actually is, and I do not want the instrument to imply that they have no position. It should likewise have room for the people who reject the presupposition of the question, either because there is no creator to justify or because a world cannot be ranked against others at all; the two non-believers in the room should be able to find themselves on the page.

Three things I need on it:

1. The tree itself, in words, with every branch labelled by the question that splits it, and every leaf carrying a name and its awkward commitment.
2. The combinations nobody occupies. Where the space allows a position I cannot match to anyone I have heard, say so, and say whether you think it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered.
3. The pairs the tree cannot separate — places where two positions I would want to distinguish come out the same, and why.

Then run the tree on four things that have actually been said in the group: everything happens for a reason; God's purposes are past our understanding; at least the good that came afterwards could not have come any other way; and I do not believe there is a reason, I am just sitting here with you. Say where each lands, and whether the instrument's verdict matches what the speaker would say about themselves.

One more thing, and it matters to me more than the rest. I trained as a physicist and I have been reading around this, and I keep finding that what is settled and what is merely the majority view among people who know Leibniz are not the same, and that popular summaries flatten the difference. So mark your branches and leaves accordingly: separate the claims any competent reader would grant from the live disputes — whether his account of possibility and necessity leaves room for the freedom the position needs, whether the principle of sufficient reason really selects a unique best world, whether the objection that there is no best possible world is fatal or merely awkward, whether the free-will response and the best-world response can be held together at all. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and where you are unsure which side of that line something falls on, say so rather than smoothing it over. I would rather have a page with three honest gaps in it than a tidy one that is wrong about a dispute I then repeat to nine grieving people.
