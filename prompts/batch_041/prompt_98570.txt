I'm a backend engineer by trade but I've somehow ended up TA-ing a second-year "ethics for engineers" module (the real lecturer went on leave and I was the only one who'd read any philosophy at all, which is to say almost none), and I've been handed the job of writing one of the final exam questions. I definately want it to test whether students can actually think about moral dilemmas rather than just wheel out the trolley problem for the hundredth time. What keeps niggling at me is whether a genuine dilemma — where you're really obliged to do two things and you simply can't do both — is even possible, or whether it's always just a sign that you've mis-described the situation. My gut says obviously they're possible, everyone's been stuck in one; but someone insisted there's a proper argument that they're logically impossible, and that it turns on the principle that ought implies can, and I can't see where the trick is.

So here's what I need: one essay question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. The question has to make them (a) reconstruct the strongest version of the argument that genuine dilemmas can't exist, (b) pinpoint exactly which premise someone who believes in dilemmas has to give up, and (c) defend that move against the obvious comeback that it's just fudging the logic to save the conclusion you wanted. To stop them recycling the standard examples, build it round a specific case I've sketched: a woman promised months in advance to be at her mother's surgery, and separately turns out to be the only match for a stranger's child whose transplant is moved to that very same morning — so she can't keep both, and one of the two obligations is partly of her own making because she volunteered for it. I'm curious whether the fact that she helped create the clash changes anything, and whether that's even the right thing to zoom in on. In the marking scheme, seperate a first from a 2:1 by naming the specific moves each has to make — I gather one of them involves a principle that lets you add separate obligations together into a single one, and another involves whether the "can't" is about the moment of choice or about the whole history leading up to it — and say where students typically go wrong on each. Keep the whole thing in British English, and don't bother with reading lists or citations; they can't use anything anyway.
