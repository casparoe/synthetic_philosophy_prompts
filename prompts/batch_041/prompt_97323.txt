I run the country-risk desk at a small advisory firm; our clients are pension funds and insurers with long-horizon exposure to mid-sized democracies, and every few weeks a client asks some version of one question: is country X entering its late-Republican phase? Our answers today are impressionistic, and I want to replace them with a procedure that two analysts — one with a classics degree, one without — can run in a single working day and re-run every quarter, so that the desk's judgments are comparable across countries and across time. Build the procedure out of the specific mechanisms historians actually invoke to explain the breakdown of the Roman Republic between the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus and the establishment of the Principate, not out of the general idea that republics decay.

I want the procedure to force two things. First, the analyst must commit to which reading of the Roman case they are using, because the two generate different diagnostics: the structural reading (a city-state constitution mismatched to a Mediterranean empire; a dispossessed smallholder class; a slave-labor economy; an army recruited from the landless whose loyalty ran to the commander who would settle them on land) and the contingent reading (a cascade of norm-breaks — the killing of a tribune, the first march on Rome, the first proscription — each of which made the next one cheaper). Second, for every Roman feature the procedure leans on, the analyst must say whether the modern case has an analogue, and if it does not, whether that makes the analogy inapt or merely incomplete. The Roman case has features I suspect most modern cases lack: an unwritten constitution, no permanent civil service, an army that became an autonomous political actor, provincial commands as a route to domestic power, and emergency decrees that hardened into precedents.

Give me the questions in the order they should be asked; for each, what counts as a sufficient answer, what answer stops the procedure with a no, and what answer hands the decision to a person because it is a judgment about values or about acceptable risk rather than a fact. Give the procedure a small verdict vocabulary of its own — something like apt, decorative, inapt — and say what the desk should tell a client under each verdict. Include the points where the procedure will misfire: where it will find an analogue if the analyst looks hard enough, where it cannot distinguish a republic that survives its crisis from one that does not, and where hindsight makes the Roman outcome look inevitable when it was not.

Then run it on two anonymized client cases. Case A: a mid-sized democracy whose head of government has just won a third term after a referendum amended the constitution, has enlarged and packed the highest court, has deployed a militarized national guard for domestic policing, and is prosecuting the opposition leader on charges his supporters call political. Case B: a small parliamentary democracy with a strong constitutional court, proportional representation and coalition government, no standing mass army, no imperial periphery, and a demagogic leader who won one election and then lost the next. I expect the procedure to tell me Case B is decorative at best — I would rather it did not, since the client is paying for a Rome narrative. Where the procedure gives me an answer I dislike, say so plainly rather than softening the verdict.

I do not want a historiography, a reading list, or citations; I want the procedure, the two applications, and an honest account of what the procedure cannot do.
