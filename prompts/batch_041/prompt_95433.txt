I write about philosophy and the mind sciences for a general readership, and I am at work on a feature about why personal identity has suddenly become a live empirical question — cortical organoids, the partial restoration of cellular activity in brains after death, the first crude interfaces that let one nervous system drive another body. My difficulty is that the reporting keeps outrunning my own convictions. I have a set of gut verdicts about the classic brain-in-a-vat and duplication scenarios, and they do not obviously descend from a single principle. I would like you to help me find the principle, if there is one, or to tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up.

I would like you to take the role of a philosopher who works on personal identity, and to treat this as a piece of conceptual work rather than a survey of a literature. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that are in play here — the ones on which identity follows the brain or the organism, the ones on which it follows the pattern or the psychology, the ones on which it follows the causal history of a particular system, the ones on which the question is empty — and then commit to an answer rather than adjudicating between them and leaving it there. Please think carefully and explicitly, and show me the steps.

What I want at the end is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in each of my cases, together with an honest statement of the verdicts it cannot accommodate. Do not hedge, and do not soften a conclusion to protect an intuition of mine; if one of my verdicts is confused, say which and why. I do not need citations, reading lists, or bibliographies — I will handle that side of it, and I would rather you reason than gesture at authorities.

Here is the frame, which I take from Dennett's essay Where Am I?: a brain is removed from its body and kept alive and functioning in a vat in one city, while the body, radio-linked to it, walks around in another city. My immediate reaction, the first time I read it, was that the person goes where the brain goes, and the walking body is a marionette. Now my other verdicts.

- Slow replacement. Over twenty years every neuron is swapped out, one at a time, for a silicon device that does the same job, with the person awake throughout and nothing perceptibly interrupted. Verdict: still the same person. Nothing has been lost.
- Blueprint and build. The same silicon brain is built in an afternoon from a complete scan, while the biological brain sits intact but anaesthetised. The new one wakes first. Verdict: not the same person. He is a copy who is wrong about who he is, even though he knows everything I know and means everything I mean.
- Teleportation. Scan, destroy the original, build at the far end. Verdict: the one who steps out is a new person and the traveller died. I am fairly confident about this — which commits me to saying that the slow-replacement person survives while the teleported one does not, though in both the matter at the end is entirely different from the matter at the start.
- Anaesthetic gap. I am unconscious for a year and then wake. Verdict: same person. So continuity of experience is not what I am tracking.
- Stroke. A stroke destroys most of my memories and changes my temperament sharply; I live another decade. Verdict: still me, though a diminished and altered me. So my memories are not necessary either — and yet the copy's possession of my memories does not make him me.
- Division. My brain is split and each half, which we are told would on its own suffice, is placed in a new body. Both wake, both speak, both remember being me. Verdict: I want to say that both of them are me, or at least that neither is a mere copy. I cannot square that with the blueprint verdict, where I insist the duplicate is not me. If anything I am more confident about the division than about the blueprint, which worries me.
- Two bodies, one brain. My brain stays where it is, intact, but its activity is also transmitted to a second body that behaves and speaks as I do, and can hold up its end of a conversation with my wife. Verdict: one person, two bodies — and yet my brain-in-a-vat verdict says the person is where the brain is, which would make the second body a puppet. It plainly is not a puppet; it is a person-shaped extension of me that my wife can talk to.
- Commissionotomy. The two hemispheres are separated within the same skull, as in the surgical cases. Verdict: one person, still me. I notice that this feels utterly different from the division case, and I cannot say why, since in both the two halves are running their own shows.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like this: identity follows the causal continuity of the system that carries the psychology, not the psychology itself. That would explain why slow replacement and the stroke are fine and why blueprints and teleportation are not. But it goes silent exactly where I need it — when the causal stream forks, as in the division case — and it does not tell me whether the vat brain or the walking body is the continuation, since the stream appears to run through both.

So: state the principle or principles precisely; show me clause by clause how each fares on the cases above; then apply your preferred one to cases I have not raised, and choose at least two that are genuinely awkward for it — for instance, a copy made while the original lives on for an hour and is then destroyed, or a brain half replaced gradually and half replaced at once, or the transplant of my brain into someone else's body. Tell me what your principle says about those, whether you would patch the principle or accept the verdict, and which of my verdicts above you would have me abandon. Finally, tell me what the revised principle implies for the reporting: whether the empirical developments I am writing about could in principle settle the question of who survives a given procedure, or whether they leave it exactly where it has always been.
