I have no training in philosophy — I studied something else entirely — but I have been reading about population ethics on my own for a while now, and I have reached the point where I want the specification before the candidates. Every argument I read that some theory is unacceptable turns on a single case; every defence turns on a single intuition I am asked to weigh against it, and I have no way of telling whether the two things being weighed are even the same kind of thing. So please write me the desiderata: what any theory of this subject has to deliver in order to count as adequate. I understand the subject to be the comparative value of possible futures in which the number of people, how well their lives go, and who they are may all differ — the sort of ranking that would tell us whether it is better to have a smaller number of people living very well or a vastly larger number living lives that are only just worth living.

Set out the requirements with the case or consideration behind each, and mark which of them you take to be genuine conditions on any theory in the area and which are the commitments of one camp rather than of the subject itself. Some of what I list below may be of the second kind; if so, say so.

The cases I want the list to be answerable to, at a minimum:

- the conclusion Derek Parfit named the repugnant conclusion — that for any population of people living very well there is a much larger population whose lives are barely worth living whose existence would be better;
- the step-by-step argument that gets you there, and whether refusing to accept the transitivity of better-than is a legitimate way of stopping it or a refusal to answer the question;
- the harsher version in which the huge population also contains a large group whose lives are worse than not living at all, and the theory still calls that world better;
- the mirror-image trouble on the other side, where a theory avoids the repugnant conclusion only by implying that adding people whose lives go well makes the world worse;
- the case of the woman who could postpone a pregnancy so that a different, healthier child is born, where it seems wrong to justify the postponement by saying the child she would otherwise have had would have been better off not existing;
- a case where a theory implies it is better to create people whose lives contain real suffering than people whose lives are just worth living;
- a case where a theory prefers a world because it is more unequal;
- whether the theory must say anything at all about futures containing infinitely many people, or may be silent about them.

The distinctions I want it to respect, or to explain why it need not:

- that something can be worse without being worse for anyone, and whether a theory is allowed to say a world is worse when there is no one for whom it is worse;
- the value of a world as against the choiceworthiness of an action — whether a theory may accept the repugnant conclusion about value while still not recommending the action that leads there;
- equal value as against genuine incomparability, and whether saying two futures cannot be compared is an answer or a failure to deliver;
- whether the ranking must be complete and transitive, and if giving up either is an acceptable price, what exactly we are buying;
- whether the theory must rank all possible futures or only those we could affect;
- whether it must remain usable under uncertainty, where we do not know how many people there will be or who they will be.

Then, on top of the list:

- flag any subset of the requirements that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, with an account of which ones collide and why. I have a vague sense that there are impossibility results in this area, and what I want to know is which conditions are the ones doing the colliding;
- tell me which single requirement rules out the most otherwise attractive theories;
- tell me which requirement you would abandon first if something has to go, and what we lose by abandoning it;
- give a short account of how the main families of view do against the list — total views, average views, views that set a critical level below which adding a life counts against, views on which the value of adding a life depends on how many others there already are, views that treat high welfare as a different category altogether from low, and views that make the value of a world depend on what is good or bad for particular people — including the option of simply accepting the repugnant conclusion rather than avoiding it. Keep the emphasis on the requirements, though; I can look up scorecards later.

One requirement is my own, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuine condition or just sentiment: that a theory should not judge a world worse on the strength of a comparison with people who will never exist. Tell me where that belongs on the list, whether it does any independent work or is only the non-identity worry in another guise, and whether a theory that gives it up is disqualified by doing so.

The purpose I have in mind, in case adequacy should be relativised to one, is a theory I could actually use to think about long-run questions — how many people there should be, what we owe to those who come after us — rather than one that only ranks toy populations. If the list should look different given that purpose, say how and why.
