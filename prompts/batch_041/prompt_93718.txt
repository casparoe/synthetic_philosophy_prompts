I'm designing a session for a graduate seminar on the political economy of climate policy, and I want to spend the whole of it on a single episode: the gilets jaunes, the protest movement that erupted in France in November 2018 after the government announced a further scheduled increase in the carbon component of the tax on diesel and petrol, and that eventually forced the government to suspend the increase and open a national consultation. Here is the picture I take us to be working from, and I want you to flag it if any part of it looks wrong to you: the increase was sold as a climate instrument, with the revenue nominally recycled; its incidence fell hardest on rural and peri-urban households that depend on their cars and whose purchasing power had already been squeezed; the movement's own articulated grievances were overwhelmingly about fiscal and social justice rather than about climate; and the government's answer was framed in the vocabulary of a just transition. What I want from you is not a chronicle of the protests but a two-directional analysis of the episode through the lens of degrowth.

Act as a political philosopher and an ecological economist, and think carefully before you commit to anything. Start by brainstorming the several distinct things degrowth might be taken to claim here — as a diagnosis of growth-dependence, as a critique of carbon pricing inside a growth economy, as a distributive program, as a theory of who the constituency of an ecological politics is — because I suspect the episode bears differently on each, and I want the differences kept apart rather than blended into a single verdict. Then map the episode onto the theory: who the relevant actors were, what options and constraints each actually faced, what degrowth says the French state was confronting and what it ought to have done, and which features of what happened degrowth genuinely explains as against those it merely redescribes. Be disciplined about the mapping, and be willing to report where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak.

The part I care about most is the reverse direction, the case testing the theory. The obvious reading is that the episode is a vindication of the degrowth critique of carbon pricing — a carbon tax within a growth economy passes the cost onto exactly the people least able to bear it and detonates politically. But I want you to press on whether that reading survives contact with the facts: the movement's demands were for more redistribution and purchasing power, not for less throughput, and its base was not the affluent urban environmentalist constituency that degrowth is often accused of speaking for. So say whether the episode is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case degrowth never anticipated, and what it implies about whom an ecological politics can claim as its subject. Throughout, state how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the one point you are least sure of.
