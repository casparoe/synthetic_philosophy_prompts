I'm on the methodology advisory panel of our national statistics office — we're the ones who have to sign off on how the office measures things, and whatever we decide has to go to the minister as a written recommendation we can defend publically. Here's the situation I need help with.

The minister's office has fallen in love with the World Happiness Report. We've been drifting up the rankings for a few years, and now there's a draft "Happiness 2030" strategy floating around in which the headline target is literally our rank in that report — climb into the top X by 2035. They've asked our panel to either bless that target or propose something better, before the strategy goes to cabinet.

I'm torn, and so is the panel. One camp says: it's a life-evaluation survey, it's what people say about their own lives, who are we to argue, and it's the only internationally comparable number we've got. The other camp says we'd be staking a national strategy on a construct we can't defend, that the top of the table is a horse-race among countries seperated by margins well inside the noise, and that the moment we start *targeting* the thing we've turned a measurement into a game and it stops measuring anything. There's also a proposal from an outside consultant to publish our own national index alongside it — something like a "relative life evaluation" measure that adjusts each person's Cantril-ladder answer for what the people around them report, on the theory that raw life satisfaction is contaminated by expectations and comparison effects.

I want you to act like a philosopher of social science who also actually knows measurement theory, and to think carefully before you write anything. Start by brainstorming to yourself: what exactly would the ranking have to be measuring for the target to be legitimate; what's the difference between a measure being valid and a measure being a good thing to optimize; whether "it's the best we have" is a real answer or a shrug; and how much of the objection is about the construct and how much is just Goodhart. Then draft the memo.

Make it the written opinion our panel will circulate: a concrete recommendation (bless the rank target, reject it, or bless a modified version — and if modified, say exactly what the modified target is), the reasoning, and a serious treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, not a strawman. And please say plainly what we should tell the minister about the consultant's relative-evaluation idea — whether it's better, worse, or just a different thing altogether.
