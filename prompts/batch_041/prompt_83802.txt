I have spent the past three years helping to run a very small charity that pays for school buildings in two districts abroad, and I have had more arguments about corruption than about anything else — with our partners there, with our donors here, and above all with my own colleagues, who split fairly neatly between those who think there is one standard and that anyone who falls short of it is simply falling short, and those who think we are quietly exporting the norms of a particular kind of state to people who organise their obligations on different lines. The arguments go nowhere because nobody ever says what they take the word to mean, so for a discussion evening I am trying to build something that sorts people properly instead of letting them talk past each other. What I would like from you is a decision tree — or a taxonomy in which every split is labelled with the question that separates the two sides — beginning from the question of whether there is a single correct account of what corruption is or whether the answer is fixed by the community whose norms are in play, and ending in named positions, each leaf carrying one sentence about the commitment that is hardest for whoever holds it. Every branch should be phrased as a question a person can answer without knowing any of the labels, and the branches should turn on concrete cases rather than on definitions, because I have found that people who agree about definitions still split over cases.

Please make the tree run through these cases: a public-hospital surgeon on a fixed salary, who would have operated anyway, accepts an envelope of cash from a family the day after the operation, offered unprompted and understood by them as thanks rather than payment; an elected village representative with a budget for one road builds it to her own village, which happens also to be the poorest and the worst connected; a civil servant appoints his nephew to a vacancy, where the nephew really is the best-qualified applicant but the post was never advertised; a bride's family is expected to hand over cattle to the groom's family, a customary and reciprocal transfer that nobody involved treats as a purchase, though it falls hardest on the poorest households; a company gives a pair of match tickets to the official who will decide its bid, tickets worth less than the threshold at which the company's own compliance code would require them to be declared; relatives abroad pay a nurse so that an elderly parent is seen sooner, in a queue ordered by clinical severity, where the parent's case is serious but not the most serious; an official accepts a facilitation fee to move a permit along, where the applicant receives nothing he was not legally entitled to, only speed, and the official is also the person who could have made the delay longer; a district council pays for the annual festival out of its public budget, where the festival is the centre of local religious life and the whole district benefits economically; a police officer waives a fine in exchange for a favour he expects to be returned later, by someone else, at some unspecified point; and a president's brother-in-law's firm wins a contract and then does the work well, on time, and at market price. Beyond the cases, I want branches on whether the concept requires a distinction between an office and the person holding it, and between a public purse and a household's money — that is, whether it can apply at all where offices are treated more like property; on whether the wrongness attaches to particular acts or to the way a whole system allocates things; on whether it matters that a practice is a second-best substitute for a state that does not work; on whether the giver is as culpable as the taker; and on whether the standard should be the one the participants themselves avow, the one the law states, or one imposed from outside — including the awkward possibility of a case in which everyone involved knows something is wrong but it is never called corruption.

I want these positions to appear as leaves whether or not you think they are respectable: the flat universalist who applies the one-line definition of corruption as the abuse of entrusted power for private gain that you see everywhere, everywhere and without qualification; the relativist for whom the word records only a local judgement; the two-level view on which there is a thin universal concept filled in differently by different thick local standards; the view that standards of impartiality are internal to a practice, so that where patronage constitutes the practice there is nothing for it to violate; the view that patronage is a defensible second-best where formal institutions are absent; the view that the whole anti-corruption agenda is a political export and should be refused; the structural view on which corruption is a property of systems rather than of acts; and the deflationary view that the word should be retired in favour of a neutral description of who transfers what to whom. Keep it to something I can put on a large sheet of paper — a dozen branch questions at most and no more than twelve leaves. Mark any leaf that nobody seems to occupy and say in a line whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has defended it, and tell me plainly which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, in particular whether the two-level view can be told apart from the practice-dependent one once we get down to the cases. I do not need any reading to go with it; I need the instrument itself.
