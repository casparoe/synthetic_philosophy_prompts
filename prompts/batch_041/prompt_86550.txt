I'm in a seminar on Buddhist philosophy of mind and I need help predicting my professor. She's a Theravada person at heart but she has heterodox tastes — she's always saying the Pudgalavadins got a raw deal, that there really is a person and it's neither the same as nor different from the aggregates, and that calling that a hidden self is just bad manners. Anyway all term she's been handing down verdicts on personal identity cases and I've been writeing them down, because next week is the big one. Her record so far: (1) a man lives forty years, every cell in him replaced, memories faded but continuous — same person, "conventionally speaking," and she refuses to give any ultimate answer on top of that. (2) A stroke wipes out all his episodic memory and changes his whole temperament, but his mental life never stops for a second — still the same person, and she says memory and character just aren't what makes it the same. (3) A machine scans a sleeping man and builds a perfect copy in the next room, memories, scars, the lot, and both wake up — two different people, and if the original did a crime we punish the original and not the copy. (4) Rebirth: someone dies, a child is conceived with no memory of the previous life but with a karmic causal link — not the same person and not a different one, the old image of one lamp lighting another, and she insists that isn't a cop-out. (5) Dreamless sleep and general anesthesia — same person, and she denies the stream was ever really interrupted, there's a subliminal continuum running underneath, which sounds like a fudge to me but fine.

Next week's case, which she's promised us: a man's brain is destroyed, but a scan of it was taken beforehand and kept; two hundred years later a machine rebuilds a brain from the scan, and the being who wakes up has all his memories and his character. The catch is that the society he wakes into has a legal convention treating these rebuildings as revivals — inheritance, marriage, debts, pensions all carry over, everyone calls them the same person, and he does too. So what does she say? I don't just want the verdict. Tell me the rule or rules she's actually been running, which ones fit all five of her earlier calls (including the ones that only look like they fit and don't), which of her earlier cases is the closest precedent for next week and why, what each surviving rule predicts here, which one she's most likely running, and what case I should put to her afterwards to flush the difference out. If her five verdicts genuinly underdetermine it, say what she'd be committing herself to with each choice. And if she comes back with "neither the same nor a different one" again, tell me whether that's a real third option or just a way of not answering.

Last line, exactly this form, nothing after it: Verdict: <same person / different person / neither the same nor a different person>
