For several years I have taught a seminar on privacy, and I have come to suspect that my verdicts on the cases I use there cannot all be right. I set them out as flatly as I can, with the verdicts I actually hold.

1. A houseguest opens the drawer in which I keep a journal and reads it. A serious violation.
2. I leave the same journal on a park bench; a passerby reads a page and puts it back. A violation, though a mild one: I feel wronged, and would be embarrassed to complain.
3. A retailer, working only from my purchase record, infers that I am pregnant and sends me coupons for baby goods. No one read anything I wrote. A violation, and not a mild one.
4. My neighbour, through a thin wall, overhears me say at ordinary volume that I am pregnant. Not a violation.
5. The same neighbour presses a glass to the wall, listens, and records me. A violation, and worse than 4, though he learns no more there.
6. I send my DNA to a genealogy service and accept terms permitting the company to share data with partners. The police later identify me through it as a relative of a suspect. A violation, notwithstanding the terms.
7. The police recover my DNA from a cup I threw into a public bin. A violation, though the police photographing me as I walk down the street is not one.
8. A company photographs that same street, matches every face to a name, and assembles a file of my movements. A violation, though it adds nothing to what was already publicly visible.
9. My clinic passes my record among the clinicians treating me. Not a violation. The same clinic sells the record to a data broker. A violation.
10. A friend asks to read something personal of mine, and I hand it over. Not a violation; my consent settles it.

Two principles keep presenting themselves, and I cannot get either to sit still.

The first is the concealment principle: a violation occurs when information about me reaches someone only by his overcoming a barrier I put up to keep it from him. This gets 1, 4, 10, and the innocent halves of 7 and 9. It calls 2, 3, 5, 6, the DNA half of 7, 8, and the sale in 9 no violations at all, unless one counts the wall in 5 as a barrier I put up, which I resist: I did not build the wall, and I did not lower my voice. Everything it discards is a case in which nothing was hidden.

The second is the consent principle: a violation occurs when information about me reaches someone in a way I would not have agreed to, had I been asked in advance, cheaply and honestly. This gets every verdict but 4, and perhaps the second half of 7 as well, since I would not have agreed to be photographed in the street either, though I am unsure whether in that case I am reporting what I would prefer or what I could reasonably refuse. It also threatens to be empty, since almost anything I would rather not have happen is something I would not have agreed to; and it says nothing about degree, though I hold 2 to be mild and 3 serious, and 5 worse than 4.

What I want is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that best accounts for these ten verdicts: stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want a formulation I can state, defend, and apply. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and why I should give that one up rather than add a clause. If something must go, I would rather keep 3, 5, 6, and 8 and sacrifice 2. Then tell me what your principle predicts for three cases I have not put to you: a journalist publishes a true and newsworthy fact about me that a source obtained by reading my private correspondence; a hospital sells de-identified records of my treatment to a researcher; and my insurer offers me a discount for wearing a wristband that reports my sleep, which I accept.
