I run a phil of mind reading group — grad students plus a few strong seniors, 12-16 people, 90 minutes, one session. We just did the intentional stance and everyone can parrot that beliefs and desires are posits we adopt to predict behavior. But none of them have felt the difference between "this prediction works because the thing really has beliefs" and "this prediction works, period." I want them to feel it in their hands.

Heres my sketch, but its half-baked and I need you to make it actually runnable — fill in the machinery, write the rules and materials, and tell me honestly where it'll blow up.

The centerpiece is a system called ORACLE. ORACLE answers questions about an invented little world (I'll make the world up; you tell me what facts it should have). In reality ORACLE is just a lookup table I consult — or a confederate consulting it — no inner anything.

I split the room into three stance teams: Physical (predict from mechanism — I hand them a scrap of info like "it's deterministic, and the answer depends only on the last word of the question"), Design (predict from what ORACLE is for), and Intentional (predict what a rational agent with a fixed set of beliefs and one standing desire would answer). Each round I put a question to ORACLE, teams lock in a prediction, correct predictions score. I want the intentional team to win round one even though I know ORACLE is a table.

Then the reveals and twists I want built in:

1. After round one, reveal the table. Intentional team still wins. Point: predictive success does not require inner states.
2. Swap in a human confederate running the same table, same questions, same answers. Identical predictions. Does anyone's belief attribution change? Keep this quick, one question.
3. Now the table has a hidden inconsistency — ORACLE answers as if it holds two facts that can't both be true, in one specific spot. The intentional team gets burned, because the stance assumes rationality. Physical and Design pick up the point. This is the one I most want to land.
4. Final round: I hand out transcripts of an LLM answering questions (I'll write the transcripts myself beforehand), and each team fills out a "belief ledger" — what does the thing believe, how confident, what would surprise it — then we audit the ledger for entries that are useful but weird.

Mechanics I want from you: a stance-switch penalty so teams feel the cost of committing (once you declare a stance you pay to change it), a scoring scheme, a timeline that fits 90 minutes with a real debrief, and materials I can print. No lecture up front — they should hit the problem before it gets named.

The debrief should map each round to a piece of the view: the three stances, the claim that to be a believer just is to be reliably interpretable that way, the rationality assumption baked into the intentional stance, and why attributing beliefs to a chatbot is or isn't the same move. Be specific about which moment in the game is which.

Also: what predictably goes wrong the first time — I'm guessing someone solves the table early and spoils the reveal, and someone insists the human confederate obviously has real beliefs so the symmetry doesn't land, but you'll know others. And be frank about the one thing the game can't reproduce: I suspect it can't reproduce actually not knowing whether the thing has an inner life, because everyone knows it's a game with a table. Tell me what I have to supply myself, and how.
