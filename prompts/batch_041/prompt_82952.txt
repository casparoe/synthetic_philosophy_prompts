I am a philosopher of language, and I have spent the better part of two years on a paper about Wittgenstein's private language argument that I now have to defend in public. My reading of the relevant stretch of the Investigations — the remarks about the diarist who tries to record the recurrence of a sensation, and the later figure of the beetle in its box — is that it is neither an argument about reference nor an argument about rule-following, but an argument about uptake. What defeats the diarist, on my account, is not that his sign lacks an object and not that he cannot follow a rule in private, but that nothing in his procedure leaves room for another speaker to correct him, resist him, or take his word up into a shared practice; the beetle passage, on my reading, does not change the subject but states the same point in a different register. I take this to cut against the two readings most of my colleagues favor — the one that treats the argument as a reductio of the idea that meanings could be fixed by inner ostension, and the one that treats it as a special case of the general problem of rule-following and therefore as having nothing essentially to do with privacy.

I will present the paper next month to a deliberately mixed audience, and I would like you to prepare me for the question period. In the room will be: three Wittgenstein specialists who know the text and the Nachlass extremely well and who will object first and hardest to any reading they think smooths over the movement of the remarks or ignores what Wittgenstein does with the first-person present; two philosophers of mind who are openly unsympathetic and think the whole argument is a piece of behaviorist confusion; a colleague in the metaphysics of language who reads the argument through the rule-following paradox and will hear my reading as a rival to that one; and a developmental psychologist who works on how children actually acquire sensation vocabulary and who will want to know what empirical difference my reading is supposed to make. The chair has given me twenty minutes for questions.

Give me a preparation sheet. Order the objections I am actually going to get by how likely each is and how much damage it would do if I fumble it, and phrase each one the way the person who raises it would phrase it — the specialists will put things differently from the philosopher of mind, and I want to hear the difference. For each, give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and tell me honestly where that reply is weakest and what I should concede rather than defend. I would also like the one objection I am privately hoping nobody raises, together with a note on whether it can be answered at all or whether the honest move is to narrow the paper's claim.
