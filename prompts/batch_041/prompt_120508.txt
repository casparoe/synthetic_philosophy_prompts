I am writing a final paper for a seminar on self-reference and the mind, and I have hit a problem I cannot resolve on my own. Our last unit was on Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and a classmate reconstructed the following argument for us, which I find at once compelling and unacceptable. I have set it out in the steps as we wrote them on the board.

(1) Let S be any consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic. By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem there is a sentence G(S) such that S proves neither G(S) nor its negation; and if S is consistent, G(S) is true, since it asserts that S does not prove it.

(2) Anyone who understands S, in the sense of being able to survey its axioms and rules and to see that they are sound, can also see that S will never prove G(S), and therefore can see that G(S) is true.

(3) So a person who understands S knows a truth that S cannot prove. If a mind were nothing over and above the formal system S, that would be impossible. Hence the mind is not S; and since S was arbitrary, no formal system is the mind.

(4) It is no use replying that the mind is a larger system S-prime. Collect every formal system the mind currently employs, take their union, and add whatever sound reflection principles it can certify; call the result S-star. The reasoning of (2) and (3) applies to S-star as well, so the mind again finds a truth outside it. The loop is strange precisely because each attempt to close it opens a new level.

(5) But nothing in (2) through (4) is specific to minds. Any entity that represents — a person, a research group, a corporation, a program that models itself — will outrun any single representation it has of itself. The argument therefore proves that self-transcendence belongs to representers as such, and not to minds as such; and if self-transcendence is what we were calling self-awareness, then either every representer is a locus of self-awareness, or the Gödelian analogy tells us something about incompleteness and nothing about the self.

(6) Our instructor remarked that this is the wrong moral to draw from the book. On her reading, the strange loop is meant to show how a self can be a machine, not how it escapes being one, and she thinks the argument above treats the Gödel sentence as a ladder out of the system, whereas the book treats self-reference as the mechanism by which a self is constituted. She declined to say where the mistake lies.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of the literature. Which step should I give up, why did it look so compelling in the first place, and what does giving it up cost? I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (5) — (5) looks to me like the argument consuming itself — but I want to know what I am paying if I cut there, and if you think a second step is also a live candidate, tell me in a sentence or two what makes it the costlier cut.

Two constraints on your answer. Open with a single sentence naming the step you reject and, briefly, why. And keep the whole reply under 750 words: I would rather have one diagnosis worked through carefully than several offered in passing.
