I'm trying to get clear on the Meno problem and I keep getting stuck on the swamping version of it. My grip on it is this: if the only thing about a belief that's valuable for its own sake is being true, and everything else -- justification, reliability, being based on evidence -- is valuable only as a means to truth, then once you've got the true belief there's nothing left for the extra bits to add. You've already got the coffee; a fancier machine doesn't improve it. So knowledge can't be worth more than true belief, which is absurd. Can you lay the puzzle out properly and then actually answer it rather than survey it? Two things I want kept apart, because I keep conflating them: whether the knower is better off, and whether the state of knowing is more valuable. And I want a verdict on the credit line -- knowledge is a true belief whose truth is creditable to you, an achievement rather than a lucky hit -- does that escape swamping, or does it just smuggle in a second value (credit, authorship) that has nothing to do with truth? Here's the case that trips me up: two people both truly believe the bridge is safe, one checked it properly, one just has a hunch that happens to be right; for the next five years both cross it and nothing happens. Over that stretch is the first person's state better in any way other than the fact that it would have gone worse in nearby worlds -- and if that's all it is, is counterfactual robustness really a kind of value, or just a disposition to be right later? Testimony makes it worse for me: a reliable friend tells you something true, you believe them, you know it, but the credit looks like it's theirs. Does that sink the credit answer, or is there a decent sense in which believing the right person at the right time is your own achievement?
