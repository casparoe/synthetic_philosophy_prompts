For most of my working life I was a biochemist, and since retiring I have been teaching a small upper-level seminar on conceptual problems in the life sciences; I set and mark the final myself. I have one essay question left to write. The topic is the definition of life and its borderline cases, and I want a question that cannot be passed by reciting the standard battery of criteria — metabolic, reproductive, thermodynamic, evolutionary — and then ticking off viruses and prions as borderline. My students have read the usual material. What they have not done is ask why the borderline cases are borderline.

The crux I want the question to force is this. I have come to suspect that the question whether something is alive is really two questions run together: whether a system now sustains itself and holds itself away from equilibrium, and whether it belongs to a lineage with a selective history. Call them the self-maintenance question and the lineage question. A virus answers the second about as well as a bacterium does and the first not at all; a mule answers the first and not the second; a corpse answers neither; a mitochondrion answers the first only inside someone else's cell. I may be reinventing a wheel that already has a name, and I do not want the question to presuppose that my distinction is the right one — the students should have to decide whether it is.

Please write me three things.

First, the essay question itself, for a ninety-minute in-class final. It should supply the case list rather than let students reach for the familiar one, and two of the cases should be ones they cannot have met. The first is drawn from my own laboratory work: a self-sustaining autocatalytic chemical network in a continuous-flow reactor that holds a far-from-equilibrium steady state, builds its own membrane-like boundary out of the feedstock, and transmits heritable variation in the composition of that boundary, but contains no nucleic acid and no coded information. The second is a population of self-replicating strings inside a computer that mutate and are winnowed by selection, but are not chemical at all. The remaining cases can be familiar ones — the virus, the mule, the prion, the mitochondrion — but the question should make the students say why each is on the list. It must require a verdict on one of the two unfamiliar cases, and it must ask whether life is a single natural kind, a disjunction of criteria that happen to co-occur, or a term whose content shifts with the project at hand — biochemistry, evolutionary biology, the search for life elsewhere, biosafety regulation.

Second, a model answer of about seven hundred words. I want it committed: it should say which criterion does the real work and why the rival verdicts are wrong, rather than survey the options and leave the reader to choose. If you think my self-maintenance/lineage distinction is the wrong way to cut the problem, say so plainly in the model answer and cut it your own way — but take a line.

Third, a marking scheme: five must-hit points, twenty marks in total, and for each point a one-line note on the mistake that most often costs students the marks. Close with a sentence on what separates a first-class answer from a solid second.

Two constraints. The students will have no library or web access during the exam, so nothing in the question or the model answer may turn on a citation, a named author, or a date; the arguments must stand on their own. And please do not let the model answer hedge. Where the cases are genuinely underdetermined, say which way you would decide and why, rather than reporting that both answers are defensible.
