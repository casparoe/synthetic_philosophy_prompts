I am preparing a seminar on the philosophy of fiction and want a sustained critical reading of Woody Allen's The Purple Rose of Cairo (1985) through the paradox of fiction. The paradox, as I understand it, is a triad: we appear to have genuine emotions toward characters and events we know to be invented; we do not believe those characters and events exist; and yet emotions seem to require some belief in the existence of their objects. The film literalizes the puzzle rather than merely illustrating it. Cecilia, a Depression-era waitress, is noticed from the screen by Tom Baxter, an archaeologist side character, who steps out of the film into the real world and falls in love with her, while the other characters in his film, who are also sentient, are stranded and refuse to continue without him. I want a reading that takes the paradox seriously and uses the film as a test of it.

Please work through at least the following, and add whatever else the film demands:

First, the film supplies an emotion with a fictional object that then becomes, apparently, real. Does Tom's crossing the screen change the kind of emotion Cecilia has, or only its occasion? Does the paradox survive the literalization, or does the film quietly dissolve it by making the object real after all?

Second, Tom and the actor who plays him, Gil Shepherd, are performed by the same man. When Cecilia loves Tom and then turns toward Gil, what individuates the object of her emotion? Is this a dramatization of the genuine problem of what our emotions are about when their objects are fictional, or a trick that trades on a confusion between actor and character?

Third, the film's other characters have interests: they refuse to perform, they are stranded, and the studio plans to destroy the prints once Tom returns, which reads like a threat of annihilation. Does the film attribute interests, or a kind of moral status, to fictional beings, and does the paradox of fiction need to say anything about whether fictional characters can be wronged? This is the part I most want you to press on, since it seems to me the film raises it deliberately.

Fourth, the ending: Cecilia, abandoned by Gil, sits watching Top Hat and begins to smile at the dancing. Read this against the question of whether emotions toward fiction are escapist consolations or genuine and valuable engagements, and against the worry that the film's own ending is an instance of exactly what it depicts.

Where the film resists the reading, say so plainly. I do not want the film reduced to an illustration of a thesis, and I do not want the philosophical puzzle bent to fit the plot; if the honest reading is that the film and the paradox part company at some point, tell me where and why. If you invoke standard positions in the debate, such as the pretense or make-believe view, the thought-theory view, or the charge that the emotions are irrational, do so by their content rather than by citation, since I want the reasoning and not references. Close by saying what the film adds to the debate about the paradox of fiction that argument alone does not, and whether it leaves the paradox standing, resolved, or exposed as a confusion. Write in American English.
