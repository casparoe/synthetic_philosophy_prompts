I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind seminar, and we have just finished Dennett's essay "Quining Qualia." A popular book on consciousness that I have been reading alongside the course summarizes the essay as follows: "Dennett's target is the philosopher's notion of qualia: intrinsic, private, ineffable felt qualities that each of us supposedly knows directly and cannot be mistaken about. His central illustration is the case of the two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who both stop liking Maxwell House. One reports that the coffee tastes just as it always did and that he is the one who has changed; the other reports that the coffee now tastes different and that he has not changed. Their preferences and behavior are the same, and each has privileged access to his own experience, so there ought to be a fact about whose qualia changed. But no possible test could settle it, which shows that qualia are unobservable posits on a par with phlogiston. Dennett concludes that experience has no subjective character at all — that the felt quality of tasting coffee is an illusion — and that a completed neuroscience would leave nothing about experience undescribed. The book adds that this makes Dennett an eliminativist about consciousness, which is why most philosophers of mind find the position too radical to accept."

I am inclined to think Dennett is simply denying something obvious — I know what coffee tastes like to me — but my professor insists that is a misreading, and I cannot yet articulate where the book goes wrong. Then say whether the book's concluding claim — that Dennett is an eliminativist about consciousness — follows from anything Dennett actually argues, and give a more accurate one-sentence statement of what Dennett is and is not denying.
