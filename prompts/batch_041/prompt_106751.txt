I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind (my thesis is on something else entirely, prediction and introspection, which is why I'm annoyed about this), and I've somehow been volunteered to run next month's session of our cross-department 'Big Concepts' reading group. 75 minutes, about a dozen people: psych and linguistics grad students, two CS master's students, one very keen second-year undergrad who reads too much, and nobody with any background in philosophy of biology or computability theory. The topic I got handed is the competence-without-comprehension thing, Darwin's inversion and Turing's inversion — the idea that you can get real competence (design, calculation, discrimination, problem-solving) out of a process that comprehends nothing, and that this reverses the order we normally assume, where understanding has to come first and the doing-well follows. I've been arguing with my flatmate about whether a chess engine 'knows' the rules, which is probably a sign of how confused I am.

Don't explain the idea to me — I need a plan for explaining it. Start with the order: what has to be in place before what, and why. My hunch is that you can't even state the Darwinian inversion until they've felt the pull of the thing being inverted (something like the intuition that a watch implies a watchmaker), and that the Turing side has to come second or else they'll hear the whole session as being about machines, but I dont trust that hunch and I want the dependency structure spelled out, including anything that has to come in earlier than I'd think. Then the carrying example: the one case that should hold the whole 75 minutes together, and what it has to have and lack. My current candidate is the mid-nineties experiment where someone evolved a circuit on a reconfigurable chip to tell two audio tones apart — it used a few dozen gates, worked reliably, used no clock signal at all, and apparently depended on a cluster of gates that weren't even wired to the rest of the circuit. I love it because the competence is undeniable and certified by behaviour, while there's nowhere in the thing you could point to and say 'here is where the reason lives'. But tell me if it's the wrong spine for this audience — maybe because it's too much about evolution and doesn't set up the second inversion, or because the spooky physical-dependence detail invites a mysticism I don't want. If it's wrong, what would be right, and what must the replacement have and lack?

The misconception map is the part I actually care about. What I expect: they'll smuggle comprehension back in ('sure, the chip doesn't understand, but whoever set up the selection did', which is fine as far as the chip goes but then gets used to defuse Darwin), they'll hear 'no comprehension' as a claim about consciousness or as eliminativism ('so you're saying understanding is an illusion / we're just machines'), they'll convert the inversions into a claim about how we should treat each other or about whether AI 'really' understands, and at least one of them will read the whole thing as behaviourism. Where in the sequence does each of those need to be headed off, and what's the one-sentence move I make at that point? Also what am I missing — I'd rather hear about the misconceptions I'm not predicting then the ones I am. Finally, give me a single question to put to the room at the end that will tell me whether the structure landed rather than whether they can parrot the phrases back, ideally something that makes them apply the inversion to a case I never mentioned; and a short list of what I should openly refuse to go into at this level, with a sentence for each on how to decline it without looking like I'm dodging. My instinct for the refusals: the mechanics of the 1930s computation work, anything about consciousness, and the 'is selection a mechanism or a principle' rabbit hole.
