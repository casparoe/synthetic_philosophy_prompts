I teach a small upper-level seminar on models and idealization in science and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on Friedman's F-twist — the idea that a theory's assumptions don't have to be realistic, only its predictions have to be good. Problem is, every year about half the class just writes that back to me in different fonts, so I need a question they can't answer by reciting.

Here's the thing I want to make them use. In week 6 I put a distinction on the board that I half made up and now I'm definately stuck with it, so the question should lean on it. Two ways a false assumption can behave when you try to relax it:

- Scaffold assumption: false but dispensable. Swap it out for the messy truth and the headline result either survives or degrades gracefully. Its falseness is a matter of convenience.
- Keystone assumption: false and indispensable. Swap it out and the result falls apart — not just the algebra, the actual explanatory claim. It's falseness is load-bearing.

And then the trap I want them to walk into and back out of: a keystone assumption is not automatically a fatal one. A result can be keystone-dependent in this model and still be robust across models, because some other model of the same phenomenon gets the same result without leaning on the false assumption. So "keystone here" and "keystone full stop" come apart, and the question should force them to say which one they mean.

The case I want them to chew on: a simple model of a market in which every firm is assumed to have perfect information and to maximize profit, and which predicts that prices track profit opportunities pretty well. Is the perfect-information assumption a scaffold or a keystone for that prediction? And if it turns out to be a keystone, does the F-twist still let you call the model a good one? If you've got a sharper case they can reason about cold without having read anything, swap it in, but keep the shape: one assumption, one prediction, and a real question about whether the assumption is load-bearing.

What I need from you: the essay question itself (something a good student can do in about 90 minutes), a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme give me the must-hit moves, what each is worth out of 20, and for each move a one-liner on how students typically get it wrong. What I care about most is the A/B split — name the specific moves that have to be there for an A, and the ones a B answer will be missing. Level is upper-level undergrad heading into a masters, so they know what a model is and they've read some of the idealization stuff, but don't assume they know any particular paper. And please don't have the model answer cite anything — it's closed book and I don't want them memorizing a reading list.
