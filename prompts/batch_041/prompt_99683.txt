I run a small reading group that's been going through the whole "Moloch" / multipolar-trap thing — you know, the idea that competition between agents who all see the problem coming and all regret the outcome still pushes everyone toward it anyway, races to the bottom, arms races, tragedy of the commons with extra dread. Half the group thinks it's basically just a prisoner's dilemma and we should go read some mechanism design, the other half thinks that's missing something and it's more like a curse on the whole setup. I want to settle this with a taxonomy insted of vibes, because I suspect people are actually disagreeing about three or four different things at once and using the same word for all of them.

So: please build me a decision tree / taxonomy of the positions on *why* multipolar traps happen and *what it would take to escape one*. Build it from the logical space first — each branch node should be a question a member could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and the leaves should be the named positions. I want the splitting questions to include at least these: (a) is the trap a fact about the payoff structure itself, or a fact about what the players know and believe — i.e. would perfect common knowledge of everyone's rationality dissolve it or entrench it? (b) if it's structural, is the bad outcome a genuine stable equilibrium, or a coordination failure where a better equilibrium sits right next to it? (c) does escaping require changing the game (rules, payoffs, an external enforcer) or changing the players (their preferences, their sense of who "we" is)? and (d) is the relevant pressure selection acting *between* agents — the ones who defect survive and reproduce the setup — or is it just a one-shot strategic bind with no such dynamic? I want those four to be the spine, but add whatever further splits you think earn their place.

Leaves I definitely want to see: the Hobbesian "there's no exit without a sovereign / external enforcer" view; the "it's a coordination problem and common knowledge plus a focal point fixes it" view; the "it's a selection dynamic, the defectors out-compete and the trap is an attractor rather than merely an equilibrium" view; and the "team reasoning / superrationality — if we each ask what we'd all do if we all did the same thing, the trap dissolves" view. If you can think of a position that lives in the space but that nobody actually holds, leave it as an empty leaf and say in one line whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two leaves your tree genuinely can't seperate.

Format it like this: start with a section called "The splitting questions" (a short numbered list, one or two sentences each, saying exactly what answers each question is sorting between); then "The tree" (the actual nested structure, with every leaf named and given a one-line summary); then "Empty and unreachable branches" (a short list); then "Pairs the tree can't separate" (a short list, or say none). Keep the whole thing under about 1200 words. For the leaf names, don't just use philosopher's names as labels — name each position by its claim, like "no-exit structuralism" or "attractor selectionism" or whatever fits, and I'd rather you invent a clear label than force a canonical one.
