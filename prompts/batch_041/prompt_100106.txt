I belong to a small reading group that has drifted from the technical decision-theory literature into the history of ideas, and next month I am to open a session not with a summary but with a prediction. The question I have been handed is this: what would John Maynard Keynes have thought about the Allais paradox? Allais's experiments, and the attack on the independence axiom that they launched, postdate Keynes's death, so there is no settled answer to be retrieved; I have to argue for one from what he was already committed to. The commitments I take to be fixed are, first, his view in A Treatise on Probability (1921) that probabilities are not always numerically measurable, and not always even comparable with one another, and second, his doctrine of the weight of argument — the thought that two judgements may carry the same probability while differing in how much relevant evidence stands behind them, so that an accession of evidence can leave a probability untouched and yet make the judgement more substantial. My difficulty is that these two commitments seem to pull in opposite directions when I try to say whether the Allais chooser is being irrational, and I keep switching sides.

One colleague in the group insists that Keynes would have been on Allais's side: the chooser who takes the certain million over the gamble, and then takes the gamble in the second pair, is not making an arithmetic error but expressing a rational preference for the option with more weight behind it, and a theory that brands this a violation has simply assumed a precision and a comparability of probabilities that Keynes denied. Another colleague insists the opposite — that Keynes would have sided with Savage, that the sure-thing principle is exactly the sort of consistency condition his logical theory of probability was meant to secure, and that the weight doctrine is about evidence, not about the psychological comfort of a certain outcome. I would like you to adjudicate between these two reconstructions and to tell me what Keynes's actual verdict would have been, with the reasoning that gets you there, rather than a survey of what later commentators have said. I am especially interested in how you handle the fact that in the Allais problem every probability is stipulated and known, so that it is not obvious that the weight of the two judgements differs at all — if that observation is correct, say so and draw the consequence for my colleague's reconstruction, and if it is wrong, show me where. To push the prediction past the point where it can be answered by recalling the standard positions, I have invented a variant I want you to run past him: take the two Allais pairs and present them twice, once with the stated probabilities generated by a well-understood chance device, so that each number is exact and rests on a great deal of evidence, and once with the same nominal probabilities described as resting on a single thin sample from a source whose composition is barely known, so that the numbers are the same but the evidence behind them is flimsy. Would Keynes predict that the pattern of choices changes between the two conditions, in which direction, and would he regard any change as a lapse or as a rational adjustment? Finally, if he had lived to see the repairs, would he have been most drawn to weakening independence while keeping sharp probabilities, to keeping independence while allowing probabilities to be interval-valued and imprecise, or to giving up the idea that rational choice under uncertainty is the maximisation of anything at all?

Please write in British English, and do not give me citations, a reading list or a bibliography — I want the argument, not the references. Structure the answer as follows: a section titled 'The verdict', roughly a hundred words, stating your prediction flatly and with a confidence level; a section titled 'Keynes's fixed points', roughly two hundred and fifty words, setting out the commitments that constrain the prediction and how firmly each is held; a section titled 'The Allais choices', roughly three hundred and fifty words, giving his verdict on the two pairs and on the charge of irrationality; a section titled 'The two reconstructions', roughly three hundred words, judging my colleagues' readings and adding any third reading you think is truer; a section titled 'The thin-evidence variant', roughly two hundred words; and a section titled 'Repairs and residual doubt', roughly two hundred and fifty words, covering the three repairs in the order of his likely preference and ending with the point at which your prediction is most likely to be wrong.
