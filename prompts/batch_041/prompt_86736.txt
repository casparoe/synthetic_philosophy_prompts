I've been following the whole give-the-looted-art-back thing — the Parthenon marbles, the Benin bronzes, everything Greece and Nigeria and others keep asking for — and everyone argues it in the same two registers, legal ownership or "it belongs to the culture it came from." Both feel exhausted to me. What nobody does is run any of it through G. E. Moore, which seems like its a missed opportunity, so that's what I want from you.

Take Moore's actual value theory and map it onto the repatriation fight. The pieces I care about: first, his claim that the two best things we can know of are the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of personal affection and friendship; second, his organic-unity thesis, that the value of a whole isn't just the sum of the value of its parts, so a whole can be worth more or less than its pieces added up; third, his isolation test — to find out whether something is good in itself, imagine it existing all alone and ask whether the world is still better for having it.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not vibes. For Moore, is the beauty of the marbles an intrinsic good? If so, does it survive the trip to a different museum, or does the organic-unity point mean that wrenching them out of the temple's frieze destroys a value no gallery, however good, can put back — because the value lived in the whole and not the parts? Where does the collective or heritage side of the argument sit — is a people's relationship to its own art an instance of the friendship-and-affection good he ranks so high, or am I stretching him past breaking? And be honest about where Moore is just silent, instead of ventriloquizing him.

Then flip it: what does this episode show about whether his theory can handle cultural property at all — does it confirm the theory, embarrass it, or reveal that he never had the resources for cases like this?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question.
