I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade and writing code for about fifteen years, but I never took a philosophy course. I keep running into the problem of universals — the old dispute over whether properties like redness or triangularity are real things that many particulars genuinely share, or just names, or just concepts in our heads — and I have a nagging suspicion that my day job already contains a working version of it. I want you to test that suspicion rather than flatter it. The blogs I read keep insisting that categories are tools for prediction rather than joints of nature, and I can't tell whether that's a solution to this problem or just a restatement of one of the answers.

Here's the task. Take the main positions — transcendent realism, on which universals exist independently of their instances; immanent or Aristotelian realism, on which they exist only in their instances; conceptualism, on which they're mental or linguistic rather than worldly; resemblance nominalism, on which only particulars exist and get grouped by resembling each other; class nominalism, on which a property just is the set of its instances; and trope theory, on which each particular has its own individual property-instance and "redness" is a resemblance class of tropes — and restate each one in the vocabulary I actually use: types and instances, classes and objects, interfaces, nominal versus structural typing, duck typing, database schemas and rows, JSON documents, prototypes, abstract types that are never instantiated, serialization and deserialization.

For each position, give me the same three-part report: what maps cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary.

Then the part I actually care about — the residue report on the debate as a whole. I suspect the interesting bits are these, and I want you to tell me whether they survive translation:

1. Uninstantiated universals. I have types with no inhabitants, and empty tables, and schemas nobody has ever written a row against. Does my vocabulary make the philosophers' puzzle look easier than it is, or is their puzzle just a fact about empty schemas dressed up?
2. The regress you get when you ask what makes a universal itself the thing it is. In my world that looks like the question of what type a type belongs to, and I know that road has been walked. Tell me whether the philosophical regress and the type-theoretic one are the same regress or two different ones that happen to rhyme.
3. Whether a universal is "in" its instances. In most languages an object holds a pointer to its class, which looks backwards from immanent realism; but in a prototype-based language the prototype is itself an ordinary object, which looks suspiciously like what the immanent realist wanted. Is that a genuine fit or am I being seduced by a pun?

Finish by telling me which single position translates most cleanly into my vocabulary, which one is a hopeless stretch, and what the residue amounts to: does the philosophy of universals have something my vocabulary cannot express — in which case say what, as concretely as you can — or is the whole dispute largely an artifact of taking nouns too seriously, in which case explain why the programmers' version of the question still feels like it has a right answer?

Two constraints. Don't name-drop texts or tell me what to read; I want the argument, not a syllabus. And don't let the translation do the philosophy's work for it — if a mapping makes realism look obviously true or obviously false, that's a sign an assumption got smuggled in, and I want that flagged rather than hidden.
