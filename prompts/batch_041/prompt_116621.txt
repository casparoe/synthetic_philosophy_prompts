Ok so I'm a fourth-year in a phil of science program, physics side mostly, and my office mate and I keep rehashing the Wigner thing and I want to actually nail down what I believe instead of vibing about it. Here's the deal: I'll give you a pile of cases where some piece of math turned out to fit the world, plus my gut on which are genuinely striking/unreasonable and which are just... normal, and I want you to tell me what principle (if any) is doing the work. Some of these I flip on, which is the whole reason I'm asking.

- Complex numbers: invented basically to solve cubics, and then it turns out QM needs them at the ground floor — amplitudes are complex and that's not a bookkeeping trick you can strip out. Gut: deeply striking.
- Calculus applied to planetary motion: Newton built the calculus to do this. Gut: not striking at all, that's literally what it was for.
- Non-Euclidean geometry: worked out in the 1800s as a pure consistency question, then GR comes along and spacetime is curved in exactly that way. Gut: striking, maybe the most striking one on the list.
- Group theory: started with permutations and whether polynomials are solvable by radicals, ends up classifying particles. Gut: striking.
- Knot theory: pure stuff, then it shows up in DNA topology and in QFT. Gut: striking-ish, less than I'd predict, and I can't say why.
- Fibonacci numbers in sunflower spirals: gut says this is NOT the same kind of thing as the ones above, feels more like a spandrel, but I can't articulate the difference to save my life.
- Fourier machinery and the heat equation: he derived it for that, so not striking.
- Fourier machinery showing up in optics, acoustics, signal processing, and again in QM: this is the one I flip on. Half the time I say "well it's just the general tool for anything with linear structure, of course it's everywhere," half the time I say "the fact that anything in nature has that linear structure is itself the miracle."
- Simple arithmetic in accounting: not striking, obviously.
- Matrices and rotations in graphics: not striking, built for it.
- e in compound interest: not striking, that's where it came from. e in the normal distribution and in the count of derangements: mild, kinda shrug.

My tentative principle is roughly: it's unreasonable when (a) the math was developed with zero thought of the domain, and (b) the fit is exact and load-bearing rather than approximate or decorative. That covers complex numbers, non-Euclidean geometry, group theory. But it maybe over- or under-generates on the Fibonacci case and the Fourier case, and I suspect knot theory breaks it too (knots were pure, but is the fit load-bearing? it kind of is now). And I have a nagging feeling the whole principle is doing something cheap, like "striking = I didn't see the connection coming," which is a fact about me and not about the world.

What I want from you: the principle, or two or three seperate candidate principles, stated precisely enough that they make different predictions. For each clause, name the case that forces it. Then give me your verdicts on cases I haven't listed — pick ones that actually discriminate between the candidates (the same ODE governing springs and circuits? probability theory invented for gambling and now underwriting half of science? take your pick). And be honest about which of my gut calls the best principle has to overturn, because I'd rather drop one verdict than keep a principle that's just gerrymandered to my intuitions. Last thing: if the "unreasonable" framing is itself a confusion I should just drop, say so and say what to replace it with — I keep going back and fourth on that too.
