I'm putting together an application for a small internal fellowship — roughly a term's worth of work, 8-10k words — and I need a two-page research proposal out of a half-formed idea. The idea came out of an arguement I had with a friend who does econ: I said Malthus was just wrong, he said Malthus was right but early, and we got nowhere because neither of us could say what would settle it. What I actually want to write about is that 'right but early' seems to cover at least three pretty different situations, and I want to know whether there's a principled way to tell them apart instead of just picking whichever one saves the theory. (a) The scope retreat: the theory was always about a restricted class of societies and the failure sits outside it. (b) The date deferral: the theory was a claim about a tendency rather than a date, so a postponed crisis isn't a failed prediction at all — except then I worry nothing could ever count against it. (c) The mechanism swap: the outcome the theory predicted (some check on population growth) does happen, but through a different mechanism than the one the theory named — falling fertility instead of famine — and here I genuinely can't decide whether to say the theory was right about the outcome and wrong about the mechanism, or just wrong twice, or whether 'the outcome was predicted' is even a coherent thing to say once the mechanism is gone.

So the proposal should be on something like: when a long-run prediction fails, what distinguishes a legitimate revision of the theory from an illegitimate rescue, and can that distinction be drawn without hindsight? State the question so it has a determinate answer, because mine is mush right now. Give me a concrete method — what I'd actually do week by week for a term, whether that's reconstructing the argument, building test cases, or comparing against other long-run predictions that failed or succeeded (I keep thinking the same structure shows up in resource-depletion arguments, or in the bet about whether compound growth can go on forever, so it'd be good if the method told me how to use those without the whole thing becoming a survey). Say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing — I have a bad habit of writing proposals where nothing could falsify my own thesis — and be honest about whether this is a term paper or secretly a dissertation, because I can't tell.
