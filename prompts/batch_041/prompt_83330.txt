For the past year I have been responsible for a customer-facing assistant that my company deployed, and I have spent much of that year watching it assert, fluently and with every appearance of confidence, things that are not so: a refund policy we do not have, a deadline that does not exist, a document that nobody ever wrote. What I did not anticipate was how completely my vocabulary would fail me. In an internal report I wrote that the system had lied, and then struck the sentence out. A colleague objected that lying requires an intention to produce a false belief in someone, and that what we are seeing is closer to the phenomenon Frankfurt describes as bullshit, speech offered with indifference to how things are. A third colleague said that both of us were being sentimental: deception, on his account, is a functional relation between a signal and a receiver's error, the way a harmless butterfly's wing pattern gets a predator to misread it as a toxic one, and asking after a system's intentions is a category mistake.

I would like the two concepts compared properly, and on this case rather than in the abstract. Set out precisely what each demands. The intent-based concept requires of the speaker a belief about how things stand, an aim that someone else come to believe otherwise, and, in the bullshit variant, some stance toward truth at all that one could be indifferent with; the functional concept requires only that the signal reliably produce a false belief in a receiver. Then tell me which of the two carves up what we are seeing better, and where each is silent rather than merely wrong. I am most interested in the pressure points. Can bullshit coherently be attributed to something that may have no stance toward truth to be indifferent with? Is the functional concept so permissive that a miscalibrated forecast or a mistranslation counts as deception, and if it needs a clause about benefit to the signaller in order not to be, can any sense be made of benefit for a language model? And who is the speaker when a model emits a sentence, the model, its developer, or the user who prompted it, since on that answer hangs whether lying is possible here at all. Finally, say what the surviving concept implies for the phrase honest AI: is honesty a disposition of the system, a property of what users are entitled to expect from it, or a property of the process that built it? And tell me which concept I should be using when I write the incident report, knowing that regulators will read it.
