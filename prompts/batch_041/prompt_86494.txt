I am curating a small gallery on the theme of myth and history, with three rooms — Troy, Arthur, Exodus — and I have to write the labels, which visitors will read as verdicts on whether any of it happened. A colleague has proposed the test we should apply, and I want to see what I would be committing myself to before I agree to it. Her test runs as follows: a traditional narrative should be presented as history, rather than as legend, exactly when positing that its central events really occurred forms part of the best explanation of why the narrative exists and persisted in the shape it did; where invention, borrowing or later political need explains the tradition at least as well, it stays legend. She adds two qualifications. The test concerns only the smallest historical core needed to explain the tradition, not the events as narrated; and it is to be applied to the tradition as a whole, rather than piecemeal to its parts.

Assume that the evidence for the three rooms is as follows. Troy: a real Late Bronze Age settlement, destroyed more than once, with no contemporary text naming the war or its heroes. Arthur: the earliest mentions come centuries after the supposed events and say almost nothing, while the familiar story is largely the work of writers many centuries later. Exodus: a foundational narrative of a people, with no contemporary attestation and no archaeological corroboration of a mass departure.

Set out the five most important implications of adopting her test, both for how these three narratives should be presented and for the business of drawing a line between legend and history at all. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, in British English.
