I've been arguing with a colleague in my lab about how we could ever be entitled to claims about the ontology of a system whose internal states are, in principle, sealed off from us — a black box we can interrogate only through the statistics of what it emits. He keeps reaching for an analogy from cryptography: identifying such a system's ontology, he says, is like identifying a document from its cryptographic hash. The hash is a fixed function of the document; barring a collision it is unique to that document; so if you can match a candidate document's hash to the one in hand, you have identified the document beyond reasonable doubt, even though the hash tells you nothing about what the document says and the document itself can never be reconstructed from it. On his view the same holds for the black box: if the observable fingerprint of some candidate ontology matches the fingerprint we actually measure, then we have identified the ontology, and the inaccessibility of the interior is epistemically irrelevant. He leans on this to wave away the worry that ontology-talk about such systems is unverifiable — the fingerprint, he says, is all the verification anyone could ask for.

I'm not convinced the analogy carries the weight he puts on it, but I want the audit done properly rather than by trading intuitions. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the document, the hash function, the hash value, the candidate database, the collision — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in what the analogy quietly assumes: that there is a well-defined function from ontology to observable behavior at all; that it is injective, so that behavioral equivalence implies ontological identity; that we can compute it in the relevant direction; and that we possess an independent catalogue of candidate ontologies to compare against. Which of these are features of the cryptographic case that have no obvious counterpart in the ontology case, and which survive the translation?

Finally, separate the disanalogies that actually threaten his conclusion — that behavioral identification licenses ontological identification — from the ones that are merely colorful, and tell me whether the conclusion survives once the mapping is corrected, or whether it was being carried by the analogy all along. If there is a better analogy for what is really going on when we try to pin down the ontology of a system we cannot open, I would like to see it, with the same treatment: what maps onto what, and where it breaks.
