I'm a novelist — four books, mid-list, you wouldnt have heard of me — and through what I can only describe as an administrative accident I've ended up co-teaching an upper-level seminar this term, cross-listed between philosophy and political theory, called 'Binding Your Future Self.' The premise is that we spend eight weeks on precommitment in the personal case — the Odyssey bit, the Elster stuff, rehab contracts, people who hand their credit cards to a friend — and then the last three weeks jump to constitutions, where the same structure is supposed to do a lot of heavy lifting. Final exam is in a fortnight and the essay question is mine to write this weekend. Last year's instructor set something like 'explain Elster on precommitment and apply it to constitutional entrenchment,' and I read the scripts: forty students, forty versions of the same essay, all of them fine and none of them thinking. I want a question where that move simply isn't available. The exam is 90 minutes, they choose one of two questions, so this one has to be answerable in roughly 75 minutes of writing.

So here's what I've built, and I'd like you to build the question around it. I invented a country, because I dont want them reaching for real constitutional law they half-remember — the Republic of Marisia. Sixty years ago it came out of a dictatorship and adopted a constitution with three relevant bits: Article 1 says all state power derives from the people; Article 90 is an eternity clause — it says no amendment may abolish the democratic order, or Article 90 itself, or the Constitutional Court's jurisdiction to review amendments for compliance with Article 90, and that any amendment violating Article 90 is void from the outset. Amendments need two-thirds of both houses plus a referendum. Now a movement, the National Renewal Front, wins the two-thirds and wins the referendum, and passes Amendment 27, which does three things: it repeals Article 90 and replaces it with an unamendable clause saying only that the people are sovereign; it extends the legislature's term from four years to nine; and it strips the Court of jurisdiction over amendments. And Amendment 27's preamble says it is enacted by the people in the exercise of their constituent power, not by the organs of the state in the exercise of constituted power, and that the referendum was the authentic, unmediated expression of the sovereign will. The Court — the same Court whose jurisdiction the amendment purports to remove — now has to decide whether to hear the challenge, and what to do.

What I want the question to force is a distinction I've been hammering all term, which I got from a footnote I can no longer find and have probably mangled beyond recognition: binding yourself to a rule versus submitting yourself to a ruler. Ulysses has a rope, but he also has a crew, and the crew has to exercise judgement — they have to decide whether the noises he makes later are the noises of a man who means it. And I've been telling them the personal case has three separable parts, the physical constraint, the delegation to the crew, and the instruction to ignore his later self's preferences, and that the interesting question is which one is actually doing the binding, because in the constitutional case you can only have the third if you have the second. So: you're a political philosopher who also knows constitutional theory cold, and I want you to think carefully before you write, and to actually start by brainstorming two or three candidate cruxes for this question and telling me which one you'd pick and why, because I'd rather see your reasoning than have you guess what I want. Then give me three things. First, the exam question itself, written the way I'd hand it to students — self-contained, so that a student who's read the seminar material but remembers no real cases can answer it entirely from the Marisia facts, and closed-book, so nothing in it can lean on a citation, a statute, or a name they'd have to look up. Second, a model answer of about 600-800 words. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks, with the marks per move and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong — I want to be able to tell an A from a B by ticking off which moves are present. And please dont let the model answer turn into a tour of real-world doctrine; if it needs a named case or a scholar to work, it's the wrong model answer.
