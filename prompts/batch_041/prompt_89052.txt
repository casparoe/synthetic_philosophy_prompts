I'm writing a final paper for a seminar on Gödel and the philosophy of mind, and I want to focus on a tension I haven't seen addressed well. In his 1951 Gibbs lecture, Gödel framed a disjunction: either the human mind infinitely surpasses the power of every finite machine, or there exist absolutely unsolvable Diophantine problems. I want to argue that this disjunction is more interesting than the Lucas–Penrose reading suggests, because it does not clearly support anti-mechanism, and because the second horn has been transformed by later work showing that Diophantine equations can encode any recursively enumerable set. If Gödel could see today's large language models and the contemporary computational theory of mind, which horn would he take, and how would he distinguish the claim that the mind is a finite machine from the claim that it is a Turing machine? Please also say whether he would regard the modern mechanist thesis as refuted, vindicated, or simply orthogonal to his disjunction.
