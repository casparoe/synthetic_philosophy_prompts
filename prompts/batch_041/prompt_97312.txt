Last night a friend and I argued at length about pain asymbolia—the condition in which a person reports that a stimulus hurts but the hurt does not bother them, and they show little or no withdrawal or distress—and about whether such cases undermine the idea that pain is bad in itself. I want your judgment on a narrower question. Below are six candidate accounts of what makes pain bad. Sort them into tiers by how well each can accommodate asymbolia while still explaining why ordinary pain is bad, and rank them within each tier. (1) Pain is bad because of its unpleasant sensory quality. (2) Pain is bad because it frustrates a standing desire to avoid bodily damage. (3) Pain is bad because it commandeers attention and motivates avoidance. (4) Pain is bad because it disrupts the subject's evaluative perspective, so a pain that never enters that perspective is not bad. (5) Pain is bad because the subject would pay or work to stop it, making its badness relational. (6) Pain is bad because the subject owns or identifies with it, so alienated or disowned pain loses its badness. For each, say in a sentence what it must claim about asymbolia and what would make its placement shift. End by identifying which account is most exposed to empirical uncertainty about what asymbolia patients actually experience, and which is least exposed.
