I'm building an anthology of short fiction that dramatizes hard philosophical puzzles, and I need one story for it. Here's the puzzle I want it built on, so we're on the same page: assume every truth could in principle be known by someone at some point. Take a truth that nobody in fact knows — call it p. Then "p is true and nobody knows p" is itself a truth, so by the principle someone could know it. But a knower of that conjuction would have to know p and also know that nobody knows p, and the first half of what they know destroys the second. So there turn out to be no unknown truths, and the anti-realist who wanted truth tied to knowability is left asserting that every truth is already known. (The proof needs almost nothing: that knowledge implies truth, and that knowing a conjunction gives you both halves.)

What I want the story to earn is the messy part. Dropping classical logic only half-helps: go intuitionist and you can block the final move to "every truth is known," but you're still left asserting that there are no unknown truths at all — you keep the shadow of the absurdity without the absurdity, and that may be the worse thing to have to live with. And the other ususal rescue is to narrow the principle so it only covers basic, observational, or otherwise tame statements, which costs the institution the ability to say the interesting thing it was founded to say about the cases that matter.

So give me an institution that has staked everything on the knowability principle — a guild of examiners, a records bureau, a licensing board, a research programme, whatever fits — and make the squeeze happen to a person inside it. Five scenes. In one of them the machinery has to actually run on the page: someone tries to make a particular unknown truth knowable, and you watch the two halves collide rather than a narrator announcing that they collide. In another, one character proposes the narrowing fix and another the intuitionist fix, and each gets to see what their fix costs them. Keep it honest — nobody wins by willpower or by a loophole the real argument doesn't have, and the institution has to end up doing or giving up something real. No philosopher cameos, no footnotes, no citations, no lecture in the narration; the reader should get there from what happens. And please no ending where someone learns that some truths are meant to stay unknown and finds peace.

Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five scenes, no introduction and no closing paragraph, present tense, each scene a short paragraph at most, whole thing under 800 words.
