My town is unveiling a new marker for the old Quarry Road schoolhouse next month and I've been asked to give the dedication speech, and I'm stuck. The story everyone grew up on is that Miss Ada Prentiss taught white and Black kids together in that one room back in the 1840s, way before anyone else around here did, and that when the county told her to shut it down she kept the door open anyway. That story is what saved the building from the bulldozer; it's on the old plaque, it's in the third grade curriculum. But the archivist who wrote up the nomination says the enrollment book only starts in 1871, the mixed years are thin, and the famous letter everybody quotes only survives as a 1903 transcription by a great-niece who was raising money for a monument. He's putting the corrected version on the marker itself. The Prentiss family association is paying for half the marker and all of the reception, and they've asked me very clearly not to contradict the story from the podium, since half the room will be their cousins.

So write the dedication speech. It has to be honest about the record and still be a dedication and not a debunking. I'm not going to stand there and tell a hundred people their grandmothers story is a lie, and I'm not going to repeat the 1840s date either. What I want it to actually do is take a position on what it means when a town's memory and the documents come apart, and which one ought to bend when they do, without turning the ceremony into a seminar. The audience is mixed: the family, a few real historians from the county society, alot of people who just came for the cake. Five minutes, American English, and dont quote the letter or the enrollment book at me, I dont have the exact wording and neither will they.
