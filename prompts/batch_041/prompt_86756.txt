Bit of a niche request. I run a very small philosophy podcast — like fifty downloads an episode, so this is a labor of love — and my guest in two weeks is a philosopher who's just written a book defending Quine's indeterminacy of translation. The thing is, he defends a version of it that I don't think I've seen anyone put quite this way, and I want to be ready for him.

The version I learned in grad school goes: the field linguist out in the jungle only ever has behavior to go on, and the behavior can't decide between 'gavagai' meaning rabbit and it meaning undetached rabbit part, so translation is indeterminate. His twist is that this puts the weight in the wrong place. He says even if you handed the linguist a magic scanner that read off every disposition and every internal state of the speaker, the choice would still be open, because the two hypotheses are connected by a systematic reshuffling of what every term refers to — you can reassign references across the board in a way that leaves the truth value of every sentence exactly where it was — and there's no further fact that could break the tie. So for him the behaviorism is a red herring, and the thesis isn't about our ignorance of meanings, it's about there being no fact of the matter about reference in the first place. He also thinks it applies to two native English speakers talking to each other, not just to exotic cases.

I need eight to ten interview questions, and I really don't want a list of 'so, you're saying meaning is indeterminate?' variations. I want them pressing on the actual machinery. At minimum one each on:

- what the reshuffling move assumes, and specifically what limits which reshufflings are allowed — if any permutation of reference is as good as any other, what stops the argument from proving too much?
- the difference between translation being indeterminate and reference being inscrutable: does he hold both, does either imply the other, and does his reshuffling get him the second one for free?
- whether the argument really needs holism about confirmation, or whether the indeterminacy is just a special case of the general underdetermination of theory by evidence — and if it's the latter, why is meaning supposed to be any worse off than physics?
- the self-application problem: if his own sentences don't have determinate meanings either, is that a reductio or is he happy to bite it?
- the objection that the argument shows the notion of meaning is empty rather than that meaning is indeterminate — i.e. a deflationary result being sold as a discovery
- whether the thesis is empirical at all: what would count as evidence against it, and what would the world have to be like for translation to be fully determinate?
- what the practical upshot is for people who actually translate — lexicographers, translators, the field linguists themselves — who seem to muddle along fine
- whether it generalizes to rule-following in general (the worry that any rule you follow can be reinterpreted so a different rule fits everything you've done so far and only diverges later) or whether language is a special case

For each question give me a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, since he's a smooth talker and I don't want to lose him at the first deflection. And I'd love at least one question that forces him to commit to something he can't walk back — e.g. whether he'd accept that his own book has no determinate content.

Don't restate any of this back to me, and dont open with a preamble or a summary of what I asked for — just start with the first question.
