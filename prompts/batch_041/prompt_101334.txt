A friend of mine works on RLHF at a lab and I'm finishing an MA in philosophy of education, and we've been going back and forth for weeks about Turing's old idea that instead of trying to build an adult mind you'd do better to build something like a child machine and then educate it. I'd like you to write out the argument as a dialogue between the two of us — call us the Gardener (that's me) and the Engineer (that's him). Both of us should be genuinely competent and neither should be a strawman, and they should actually answer each other's points rather than talk past each other; if the Gardener lands a hit the Engineer has to deal with it, and vice versa. The concrete thing we keep fighting about is a proposal he sketched on a napkin, so build the dialogue around it: a three-phase curriculum for a learning machine. Phase one, the machine imitates a teacher who is deliberately inconsistent — not random, but inconsistent in ways the learner can't predict, so it can't just absorb the teacher's quirks wholesale. Phase two, no reward signal at all for a long stretch; the learner has to decide for itself what's worth attending to while the teacher just watches. Phase three, a final exam that the teacher itself can't pass, so the standard can't collapse into "do whatever I would do." He thinks this is a search-efficiency hack dressed up in school clothes, and that calling any of it education is a sentimental overlay that makes us imagine a little person inside where there's only a search process. I think the whole reason Turing reached for the word is that education requires something a search process doesn't have — a stretch of protected time where mistakes don't count, and a teacher whose disappointment you can actually feel, which means the machine would need something like a childhood and maybe even something like boredom, since boredom is what pushes a learner to pick its own problems and that can't just be engineered in.

Let them get into the weeds on whether education is doing any real work in Turing's picture or whether it's just a nicer word for training, and have each of them press at least one concrete case — I'd like the Engineer to push the worry that a learner raised on an inconsistent teacher just gets very good at gaming approval, and the Gardener to push back with the thought that a teacher's standards are often unstatable, so the learner has to outrun any explicit specification of them, which is either the whole point or a recipe for overfitting depending on who you ask. I don't want a tidy resolution where one of us wins; I want each of us to concede something real and then end on an open question that neither of us can answer yet. No citations or references to who said what where — just the argument itself. Around 700-900 words, and don't let them get cute or speechy; I want them talking like two people who've had this argument a dozen times and are now trying to actually pin each other down.
