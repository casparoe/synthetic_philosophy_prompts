Two colleagues in my research group, Ines and Rahul, spent most of a working session arguing about how "deception" ought to be defined for AI systems, and neither gave ground. I was taking notes rather than taking sides, and what I want from you is an adjudication of the exchange as it actually went, not a general essay on AI honesty. The exchange, as close to verbatim as my notes allow:

(1) Ines: "A system's output is deceptive when it is apt to lead a reasonable user to a false belief. Intent doesn't enter into it. If a training regime reliably produces overconfident answers because confident answers get rated higher, then the system is deceiving its users, whatever is or isn't going on inside it."

(2) Rahul: "That's too broad. By your standard a miscalibrated weather app deceives, a rough back-of-the-envelope estimate deceives, and so does any honest simplification. Deception is an intentional notion: to deceive is to act with the aim of producing a false belief. Chatbots have no aims. What you're describing is misleadingness, or epistemic risk. Using the moralized word for it is a mistake."

(3) Ines: "Then locate the intention upstream. Someone built the reward model, someone chose to optimize for approval, someone shipped it. The practice has an aim even if the model doesn't, and the aim is to leave users more confident than the evidence warrants."

(4) Rahul: "You've now moved deception from the system to its designers, which concedes my point: strictly speaking the system misleads, it doesn't deceive. And the criterion is still too broad. Suppose a support bot answers a survey question about refund times with the fastest case in its training data because that's the most common answer. It says nothing false and it aims at nothing, and you'd have to call it deceptive. The disanalogy with a liar is that the liar is answerable for the gap between what he thinks and what he says. There's no gap in the bot, because there's nothing on the far side of the gap."

(5) Ines: "That last line is doing all the work. There is something on the far side: the model's own distribution over next tokens. When it says 'three to five business days' while its distribution puts substantial mass on a much longer tail, it is emitting a sentence its own internal state doesn't support. If you want a gap, that's the gap, and unlike the liar's mental state it's measurable. I'd define honesty operationally as agreement between the text channel and what I'll call the distribution channel: a mismatch in the confident direction is deception, and we can score it."

(6) Rahul: "Then your gap is a property of a probability distribution, not a belief, and every sentence a language model emits has it, since no sentence is the modal continuation in every context. On your account every output is deceptive and the term stops discriminating. Worse, the distribution channel is itself an artifact of the same approval-optimization you're objecting to, so you're comparing two products of one process and calling the difference a lie. And a confidence figure the model writes out in words is just another sample; asking it how sure it is doesn't read out a hidden state, it generates a plausible-sounding number."

(7) Ines: "Then drop inner states altogether. A system is honest when its outputs are the ones a user who cared only about accuracy would want, and dishonest when training pressure toward approval has shifted the outputs away from that. That's a claim about the system's dispositions, and it's the one we can actually act on."

(8) Rahul: "That's a claim about the trainers' priorities again. And it makes talk of a deceptively aligned system incoherent. When people worry about a system with goals of its own that conceals them, they mean a real agent with a real aim to mislead. Your definition either can't state that worry or flattens it into a chatbot's overconfidence, and those two call for completely different responses."

(9) Ines: "Or the word is doing two jobs and we should split it, in which case a good deal of the safety discussion is trading on the ambiguity."

Please work through this move by move, rating each from clearly mistaken through defensible-but-incomplete to clearly right, and justifying each rating briefly. Then say what both of them are missing, and whether the dispute is really one disagreement or several. Specifically: is an effect-based definition of deception too broad, and if so is there a principled narrowing that doesn't just reintroduce intent by the back door; must intent be located in a person — designer, deployer, trainer — or can it coherently be located in a system, and what turns on the answer; is the "the model has no beliefs" objection fatal to Ines's position or a red herring; does the distinction between a merely miscalibrated model and a genuinely deceptive agent survive contact with the case in (4); and is there a hearer-side element — what is not said, or the selection of which true answer to give — that neither of them has accounted for.

Separate throughout what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Where a move depends on an empirical claim about how such systems are actually trained or how users actually respond, say so and say what would settle it; where a disagreement is terminological rather than substantive, say that too, and explain what is at stake in choosing one vocabulary over the other.
