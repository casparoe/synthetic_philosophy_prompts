I'm in a philosophy of perception seminar and I keep getting into it with my roommate, who has much stronger opinions than I do. She's a disjunctivist — she thinks that when you genuinely see something, your experience is a fundamentally different kind of thing from the experience you'd have if you were merely hallucinating or being fooled, even if the two are indistingushable from the inside. I'm more of a representationalist: I think the two experiences have the same content and differ only in whether the world happens to cooperate, which to me is not a difference in kind at all. Please write a dialogue between the two of us, each trying to talk the other round. Make us both as sharp and as fair as possible — no strawmen, and no talking past each other; every reply should engage the actual point just made.

Keep it concrete. Have us fix on a case I can't stop thinking about: a woman with a neural implant wired into her visual cortex, so that whenever it's on she has a vivid visual experience of a bowl of fruit sitting on the table in front of her. Sometimes the implant is just relaying a camera aimed at a real bowl, and sometimes it's running a stored image with no bowl there at all, and from the inside she can't tell the difference. My roommate wants to say that only the first case is her seeing the fruit at all, and that the second is a different kind of mental event wearing the same disguise; I want to say they're the same event and she owes me an account of what's supposedly different about them beyond our willingness to hand out the word 'seeing' to the lucky ones. Have her press me on why introspective indistinguishability should be allowed to settle the metaphysics, and have me press her on whether her view can avoid making 'seeing' a mere honorific. Let it end genuinely unresolved, but with both of us having shifted a bit, and don't worry about naming names or citing papers — just have us argue.
