I have spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about the Allais paradox, and I want you to help me see my own position clearly. Please take the role of an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of practical rationality — someone who knows the formal results but also takes the interpretive questions seriously. Before you answer, brainstorm for yourself: list the distinct ways the independence axiom has been defended and the distinct ways it has been attacked, then sort them, and only then give me your considered answer.

Here is where I have landed. I hold that the independence axiom is a genuine requirement of practical rationality and not merely a convention of the expected-utility representation. More specifically, I think the sure-thing principle is close to a theorem about what it is to have a consistent ranking over lotteries — if two lotteries agree on some event, your preference between them should not turn on what happens on that event — and that anyone who violates it is not ranking lotteries at all but something else. On this view the Allais preferences are a mistake, however psychologically natural, and the fact that they are robust across cultures and stakes is evidence about our psychology, not about what rationality requires.

My friend pressed two things I could not fully answer. First, he says independence quietly assumes that only the marginal distribution over final prizes matters — that the way the uncertainty resolves is irrelevant — and he calls his own view resolution-sensitivity: he claims he may rationally prefer a lottery that resolves early, with a chance to change course, over one with the same marginal distribution that resolves all at once, and that this is not an error. Second, he says the money-pump argument against Allais preferences proves too much: any sufficiently contrived sequence of choices can be pumped, and the pump against the Allais agent requires him to reverse his preferences across time, which is a separate irrationality he is free to reject.

What I want from you: tell me what would count against my position. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward — which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results should move me, and which I should dismiss. Which of them would a defender of independence concede as genuine costs, and which would they wave away? I am especially worried about one thing: I suspect I can always save independence by enriching the outcomes — if someone cares about the resolution, I can just say the resolution is part of the outcome — and I want to know whether that move is a legitimate refinement or whether it makes my position unfalsifiable, and whether that matters. Also tell me what my position predicts that its rivals do not, so I know where a genuine difference should show up. I would be most moved by a case in which an agent with Allais-type preferences can be shown to be unable to represent her own choices as maximizing anything, even a non-expected-utility functional; if no such case exists, say so. And tell me plainly whether, after all the qualifications, my position still risks anything at all.
