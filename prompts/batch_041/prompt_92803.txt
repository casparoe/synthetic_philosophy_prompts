I'm putting together a teaching unit for a graduate seminar on the norms of credence, and I want a satire my students can use as a case study in how a real epistemic constraint can be bureaucratized into absurdity. Please write an appendix to the employee handbook of an invented forecasting firm — numbered sections, deadpan administrative prose, no winking, no narrator's commentary — setting out the firm's Credence Compliance Policy. The firm has adopted the reflection principle as internal policy: every employee's current credence in any proposition must equal her expected future credence in that proposition, and the handbook treats departures from this as personnel matters rather than philosophical ones.

Every joke must be licensed by an actual feature of the principle rather than by generic corporate silliness. The handbook should, without ever explaining itself: treat an anticipated change of mind as misconduct in itself, so that an analyst who expects tomorrow's briefing to leave her more optimistic about a forecast is required, today, to be exactly that optimistic; permit conditionalization on new evidence as the sole legitimate ground for revision, so that unexplained opinion drift becomes a disciplinary category; provide a carve-out clause and a form for employees who know their future selves will be corrupted by something they do not endorse — a pill, a very persuasive seminar, an anaesthetic, a cult — and specify what documentation the carve-out requires; instruct employees to treat their own future selves as epistemic superiors whose credences they are to adopt on pain of noncompliance; include a worked example of a two-stage betting audit in which a noncompliant employee can be shown to lose money whatever happens, with the second stage settled by her own later credence; and include a cheerful clause conceding that compliance guarantees nothing whatever about accuracy, since the policy governs the internal coherence of forecasts and not their truth.

The document must never state or imply that any of this is unreasonable, and it should be impossible to tell from the prose whether the firm is right. Roughly 700–800 words. No citations, no names of philosophers, no literature review — it should read as something pulled from a filing cabinet. A reader who knows the formal epistemology should recognize that the author does, and a reader who does not should nonetheless be able to reconstruct both the principle and the standard objections to it from the handbook alone.
