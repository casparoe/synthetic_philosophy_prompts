I want to know what principle, if any, unifies my verdicts about when pain is bad for the person feeling it. In each case I mean the badness of the pain itself, not the badness of its cause or its downstream effects, except where those seem to make the pain itself better or worse. I am willing to use the distinction between the sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational dimensions of pain, but I do not want to assume in advance that badness tracks either one.

1. A migraine that is intense, unpleasant, and hard to ignore is bad, and bad roughly in proportion to how much it hurts and how much it monopolizes attention.

2. In pain asymbolia, a person can report a distinct pain sensation, sharp and burning and located in a limb, while denying that it hurts in the way that makes them want it to stop. I think the pain is still bad for them, though less bad than the migraine. This is the verdict I find hardest to square with the others.

3. Post-surgical soreness that the person welcomes as evidence of healing is less bad, perhaps not bad at all, even though the sensory quality is similar to pain they would otherwise dread.

4. A mild ache that persists for years is worse than a sharp pain that lasts ten seconds, even if the sharp pain is more intense at its peak.

5. Pain that a doctor dismisses as imaginary, but that the person feels just as vividly, is just as bad as pain with an obvious physical cause.

6. The burning of a hard workout, which the athlete endorses and would not remove if offered, is not bad for them in the moment, or at least not bad in the way that a headache is.

7. A pain that is intensely disliked only because the person falsely believes it signals a fatal illness is less bad once the belief is corrected, even if the sensation is unchanged.

8. A pain that is felt in a dream and forgotten on waking is bad while it lasts, but I am unsure whether it is bad for the dreamer’s life at all.

My tentative principle is that a pain is bad for someone just to the extent that they dislike it, and that sensory intensity matters only through the dislike it produces. That principle seems to get cases 1, 3, 4, 6, and 7. But it makes case 2 come out not bad, which I resist, and it makes case 8 bad only while the dislike lasts, which may be right. It also seems to make case 5 depend on whether the person dislikes being dismissed, which is not what I meant.

Please state the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures these verdicts. For each candidate, identify the case that motivates it, its verdicts on cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the best fit requires overturning one of my verdicts, say which one and why. Then tell me what your preferred principle implies about a further case: a pain that the person sincerely says they do not dislike, but that still makes them flinch, withdraw, and avoid the stimulus, and that they later remember as having been awful.
