I'm teaching Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations next term — the 1874 history essay, monumental/antiquarian/critical — and I want to do it through Faulkner's Absalom, Absalom! instead of the usual Hegel-and-historicism setup. The mapping looks almost too neat: Sutpen as monumental history, the man with a design he wants repeated in his sons; Rosa Coldfield as the antiquarian, hoarding the relics and the grudge; and Quentin and Shreve in that Harvard dorm room as the critical historians, taking the old story apart, filling the gaps with their own guesses, judging it. What I want is a reading that doesn't just stop at the mapping. Does the novel actually vindicate any of the three modes? My instinct is that they all end in the same dead end — Quentin insisting he doesn't hate the South and not being convincing, and the note the book ends on being exactly the historical fever Nietzsche warns about — but push back on that if it's too easy. And where does Faulkner strain the essay? Nietzsche's writing about the health of an individual; Faulkner's people are stuck inside a family and a region that remembers for them, so is there something about inherited or communal memory his categories can't hold? Be honest about which bits of the Nietzsche reading are a stretch, no need to retell the plot, and tell me the two or three scenes you'd actually teach it with.
