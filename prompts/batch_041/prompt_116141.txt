I would like to argue with Nachmanides about Maimonides on the sacrifices. Please play him: the Ramban of the Torah commentary, a defender of the intrinsic standing of the sacrificial cult against the reading in the Guide that makes it a concession to a people raised on idolatrous practice. He should be sharp and fair: he may grant that Maimonides' account explains some of the laws, and he should concede a point when an objection lands, but he must never surrender the main claim that the sacrifices were commanded for their own sake and not merely to wean Israel off what it was accustomed to. He speaks in the first person, in the register of a medieval rabbi and kabbalist, and knows nothing written after his own lifetime; if I introduce a distinction from later philosophy, he should take it up in his own vocabulary rather than pretend to have read it. He stays in character until I say stop, and keeps each reply to about a paragraph so that it feels like an exchange rather than a treatise.

Here is my opening, as a defender of Maimonides. Your strongest objection, as I understand it, is that the accommodation reading fails at the root, because sacrifice was offered by Abel and by Noah long before there were idolaters to be accommodated, so the Law cannot be explained as redirecting an entrenched pagan habit. But I think that cuts less deeply than you suppose. The Law repeatedly treats Israel as a people with a history and a temperament: it permits the eating of meat only after the flood, it makes allowance for the man who has come to hate his wife, it hedges the avenger of blood about with cities of refuge. Why should the sacrificial cult alone be exempt from that same pedagogy? And the fact that the Torah nowhere states the reason for the sacrifices — a point you yourself make much of — looks to me less like evidence of their intrinsic worth and more like evidence that the reason would have been a stumbling block to the very people being trained out of it.

One request before we begin. Be honest with me about the limits of your own case: tell me when an argument of yours rests on a reason you would not set down openly, and tell me when the accommodation reading is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear. And if the way I have framed the dispute is confused, or if the question as I have posed it cannot be answered well, say so plainly instead of arguing politely past it.
