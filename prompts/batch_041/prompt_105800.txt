I was recently appointed to the investment committee of a mid-sized charitable foundation, and at my first meeting the committee deadlocked over a proposal from our new chief investment officer. She wants to replace our standing policy — which, as she fairly describes it, tells managers to maximize the portfolio's expected value subject to a ceiling on volatility — with one that tells them to maximize the expected logarithm of the portfolio's value, which she calls its long-run growth rate. Her illustration was a wager that gains 50 percent or loses 40 percent with equal probability. Its expected value is positive, so an expected-value maximizer takes it every time; but someone who stakes his entire fortune on it over and over is ruined almost surely, because the product of the outcomes, and hence the geometric mean, shrinks. She argues that our present policy is a slow road to ruin and that we should adopt the growth-maximizing rule, which has the further virtue of fixing the fraction of the portfolio to stake.

Three of my colleagues pushed back, and I could not settle the matter in the room. One insists that we are judged by the dollars we distribute, not by our survival, and that if some parallel copy of the foundation goes broke that is simply not the question we were asked. A second objects that the rule presumes we know the odds, whereas in markets we are only estimating them, and a rule that is optimal under known probabilities may be reckless under guessed ones. A third says that maximizing expected log value is just risk aversion in mathematical dress — a choice of utility function, hence a value judgment about how much we care about ruin, and not the theorem the CIO presents it as. The CIO answers that the real disagreement is about ergodicity: that the average over time of one institution's wealth and the average across many imagined institutions are different quantities, and that only the first is ours.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the next meeting, at which we must decide whether to adopt her rule, reject it, or adopt it with stated conditions and a timetable. The recommendation should be concrete enough that the committee can vote on it. In the reasoning, please address at least: whether the ruin argument depends on the wager's being repeated rather than taken once, and what that implies for an institution with a finite horizon; whether growth maximization is a neutral criterion or a disguised preference about risk; what the rule demands that we know and whether a body in our position can ever know it; and whether a compromise — staking a fixed fraction of the growth-optimal amount — is principled or merely a hedge against our own ignorance. Give the strongest version of the case against whatever you recommend, since I will have to answer it at the table.
