I was at a friend's place over the weekend with two people from a little reading group I go to -- both of them have been hanging around the rationalist blogs and forums for years, so they've each got Opinions about the old Achilles-and-the-tortoise dialogue, and somehow it came up. I'd raised it because it genuinely bugs me: the tortoise accepts A and B, and accepts that if A and B then Z, and still won't accept Z; Achilles writes the conditional down as one more premise, and the tortoise just does it again with the new premise, forever. My friend Dana took the tortoise's side and said this is a real result and not a trick -- there's no finite pile of premises that forces the conclusion, so modus ponens can't be derived from anything, we just find ourselves doing it, and any attempt to justify deduction either goes in a circle or never terminates. My friend Marcus said that's a con, not a proof: the tortoise only keeps refusing because it's treating the rule of inference as if it were one more thing to believe, and once you've accepted the conditional you've already got everything, because accepting if A then B just is being disposed to move from A to B -- there's no extra step where you believe the conditional and then need a further shove to actually detach. Dana shot back that this is just stipulating the answer, since the tortoise can perfectly consistently say it accepts the conditional and simply declines to detach, and if the only way to rule that creature out is by definition rather than by argument, then Marcus has conceded the point.

Marcus came back that a thing that accepts if A then B and accepts A and still won't accept B isn't holding a position, it's just a creature that refuses to reason, and asking for a justification that would move it is like asking for an argument that convinces someone who's decided in advance not to be moved by arguments -- that's not a hole in logic, it's a misfire in the question. He added something I hadn't heard before, that the regress only even gets off the ground if you model believing a conditional as a static sentence sitting in a belief box, and if you instead model it as a real dispositional state -- a tendency to actually make the move -- then there's no gap left for the tortoise to wedge itself into. Dana said that's swapping out one picture of the mind for another and calling it an argument, and that you can't refute a skeptic about inference by adopting a theory on which the skeptic doesn't exist. At that point they both turned to me and I had nothing. Can you referee this move by move -- who's right about what at each step, what each of them is missing, and who has the better of it overall? I especially want to know whether Dana's regress is a genuine discovery about the limits of justification or whether Marcus is right that the question is confused, and if it's confused, exactly which assumption the tortoise is smuggling in that does the work -- and please don't just tell me "it depends on your theory of rules," I want the actual crux, definately ranked from "this settles it" down to "this is just a restatement of the disagreement."
