For about a year a reading group I sit in on has been circling one question — whether self-deception is even possible — and I have come to suspect that we are not in fact disagreeing about that at all. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on the question.

The shape of it, as it presents itself to us: one camp holds that deception is an essentially intentional affair, that to deceive someone is to bring it about by design that they believe what you want them to believe, and concludes that the self-deceiver must therefore be both deceiver and deceived — which lands us in the familiar paradox of a subject who believes p and believes not-p at once. This camp treats the paradox as the central problem, and treats the available escapes as costly: a partition of the mind into a believing part and a believing-otherwise part, a temporal lag so that the contradictory states never coexist, or a demotion of one of the two attitudes to a mere wish or hope rather than a belief.

The other camp denies that intention is required at all. On this view self-deception is simply motivated irrationality — a biased selection and weighing of evidence that issues in a false belief — and the paradox is an artifact of importing the interpersonal model of deception into a case where there is no deceiver. The two camps agree about most of the psychology: the wishful reading of evidence, the avoidance of disconfirming data, the persistence of the belief under pressure. The stock cases are likewise shared — the spouse who cannot see the evidence of infidelity, the patient who credits the treatment with a placebo's effect, the person who sincerely denies a jealousy that visibly governs her conduct — and yet nobody moves, because each side treats the other's central cases as marginal or as cases of something else.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion: the real dispute is not about self-deception but about the metaphysics of belief. The intentionalist camp tacitly assumes that a belief is a determinate, occurrently available, all-or-nothing commitment of a single subject, and given that assumption self-deception really is paradoxical. The other camp tacitly assumes that belief is a graded, frequently unconscious functional state, distributable across sub-systems, and given that assumption the paradox cannot even be stated. If that is right, the parties may not be contradicting one another so much as answering different questions under one heading, and the possibility question is malformed until the theory of belief is settled.

What I want from you is this. First, is the hypothesis correct, and if not, where is the actual fault line — a verbal dispute over the word 'deception', a disagreement about which cases are canonical, a hidden empirical question, or bedrock intuitions about the unity of the mind that neither side can argue for? Second, does the diachronic formulation — on which the belief is acquired at some point and thereafter merely maintained by motivated processing, so that the contradictory states need never coexist — dissolve the paradox or only relocate it? Third, should the growing availability of measures that appear to dissociate what people sincerely avow from what they implicitly expect be treated as a wedge that settles anything, and if so, what? Fourth, and most importantly, what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal model that tolerates inconsistent or indexed credences, an experiment, or the frank admission that the term should be retired in favor of a cluster of more specific notions? If the honest answer is that no progress should be expected, say so and explain why.
