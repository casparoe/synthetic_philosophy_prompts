Last night I spent two hours arguing with a friend about Wittgenstein's On Certainty and came away convinced that her reading of the book is unfair to Wittgenstein in ways I could not articulate. She is not a careless reader — she has a graduate degree in philosophy and has worked through the text — so I want a careful audit rather than a debunking. Here is her summary, as close to verbatim as I can manage:

'Wittgenstein's last notebooks are his answer to the sceptic, and the answer is that some of our beliefs are exempt from doubt. He calls them hinges. They are the things Moore claimed to know — that here is a hand, that the earth has existed for a long time — except that Wittgenstein sees that we can neither doubt them nor justify them. They are the bedrock, the ground on which all our other beliefs rest, the things we simply have to take for granted. So he ends up a foundationalist after all, if an unusual one: where Descartes found truths that cannot be doubted and therefore serve as foundations, Wittgenstein finds certainties that cannot be doubted precisely because they cannot be justified — foundations that are themselves ungrounded. And since the hinges cannot be justified, the choice between one set of hinges and another is ultimately arational; he says that someone who denies our hinges is not making a mistake but playing a different game, living in a different world, and he concedes that the river-bed of our certainties can itself shift over time. That is why he is read as a relativist, and why On Certainty is often treated as anticipating Kuhn — the thought that what counts as rational depends on the framework one happens to occupy. In the end he gives up on refuting the sceptic and settles for saying that the sceptic's doubt is meaningless, since meaning itself depends on sharing hinges.'

My instinct is that the relativist conclusion is too quick, though I cannot tell whether that is a genuine objection or merely my reluctance to follow the argument where it leads; and I suspect the foundationalism charge is the shakiest part of her summary without being able to say why. Please go through the summary claim by claim. For each claim, tell me whether a careful reader of On Certainty would accept it as stated, whether it is a defensible simplification that needs a caveat, whether it is a live interpretive dispute rather than a plain error, or whether it is a genuine misrepresentation — and say where a defender of Wittgenstein would push back hardest. Then tell me whether the relativism conclusion survives once the inaccurate parts are corrected, and whether anything in the accurate parts actually supports it. Finally, tell me what the summary gets right that I should not throw out along with the errors. I do not need a bibliography; where a reading is associated with a particular interpreter, describe the reading rather than trying to place it.
