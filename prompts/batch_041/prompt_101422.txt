I teach a second-year ethics seminar — 18 students, 75 minutes, one long table we can rearrange, and a corridor I can send people into for a couple of minutes if a design needs it. I've taught Glaucon's ring four times now and it always goes the same way: I describe the shepherd finding the ring, somebody says "yeah I'd rob a bank," everyone laughs, and then we drift into a discussion of whether you'd get caught, which is the one thing the ring rules out. I don't think they ever feel the actual shape of the challenge, which as far as I can tell isn't really "would you be good if no one could see you" but something more uncomfortable: that the honours and rewards go to the person who *seems* just, that the person who is actually just but has a bad name ends up miserable and gets read as a hypocrite, and that when you look at it that way you can't tell the just life from the unjust one by its fruits. My flatmate does behavioural econ and says this is just a public goods game with extra steps, which I think misses it, so I'd rather not end up with an activity that's really a cooperation experiment.

What I want from you is a runnable activity for that session that gets them to that uncomfortable place themselves, before anyone says the word "Glaucon" or "ring" out loud — so no framing lecture up front, and the name only comes out in the debrief. Give me the rules, the materials (cheap — I'll definately be printing things the night before), the roles if there are any, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff or reward scheme that doesn't need money. I'm happy to use the seminar's own currency: who gets first pick of the biscuits at the break, or points that decide who chooses the topic for the last week, that sort of thing. The bit I can't solve on my own is how to get the *reputation* half of it into the room. I need them to end up allocating something valuable on the basis of what they think of each other, so that being genuinely decent but distrusted costs you and being charming and self-serving pays, and I want that to come out of the mechanics rather than out of me standing at the front saying "notice how unfair this is." Also assume they're nice people who mostly cooperate, because they are, and it still has to bite.

Then the debrief. Map the moments in the activity onto the parts of the challenge — the hidden action, the reputation, the comparison between the two lives — and tell me which moment is meant to do which job, so I can point at it afterwards. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and what to do when it does; my guess is that the stakes will be too small for anyone to care, and that at least one person will work out the mechanic early and start playing it, but you'll know better. And be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. I already suspect it can't touch the part where the just person is supposed to be happy *because* of the justice in their soul regardless of what happens to them, and it obviously can't run the whole-life comparison — but tell me what else it flattens, and how I should flag that honestly instead of papering over it.
