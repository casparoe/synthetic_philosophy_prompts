I have just spent most of a weekend arguing with a close friend about politics, and we ended it not angry but stuck: she is convinced that moral psychology has already settled the question between us, and I no longer know what to say to her. I would like you to take the role of a philosopher who works on both moral psychology and metaethics, and to audit the argument as carefully as you can. Take your time with it; I would far rather have a slow, careful answer than a broad one. Think on the page before you deliver a verdict, so that I can follow how the pieces are meant to fit together rather than only where you come out.

Her case, as fairly as I can put it, runs like this. Moral psychology — she names Jonathan Haidt's moral foundations theory and the social intuitionist model behind it — has shown that moral judgment is the output of a small number of innate foundations that culture then tunes. She recites them: care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and whatever has been added since, noting that the list itself keeps changing and taking that as a sign of health. Liberals, she says, weight the first two heavily and the rest lightly, conservatives weight all of them, and neither side can see the other's moral world. She also insists the research shows moral reasoning to be mostly post hoc: the intuition arrives first and the reasons are manufactured afterwards. From all of this she draws three conclusions. First, that our convictions are caused by our foundations rather than held for the reasons we give, so our stated reasons are decoration. Second, that since the foundations are innate and were fixed by evolution and upbringing, neither of us chose our moral outlook, and so neither of us is more rational than the other. Third — and this is the one I cannot get past — that there is therefore no fact of the matter about who is right between us, that arguing about politics is like arguing about whether cilantro tastes soapy, and that the only sensible questions left are strategic: how to live together, and whether a person can deliberately cultivate the foundations they supposedly lack.

What I want is an audit of the inference, not a summary of the psychology. Here is the thought that has been nagging at me. If a psychological theory explained the origins of our moral judgments completely, would that really show that no side is right? And if the foundations are innate, is it even coherent to speak of cultivating the ones I supposedly lack, or is that like deciding to have different eyesight? More generally, what would a genuine empirical discovery about the origins of moral disagreement have to look like in order to bear at all on the question of who is right? I would also like to know whether her closing recommendation escapes her own argument: that we should stop arguing and simply live together sounds to me like a moral judgment, and I cannot see which foundation it comes from.

Please begin by setting the argument out in premise-and-conclusion form, so that we can both see exactly where the inference is supposed to happen. Then tell me which of the premises I have reported in a way that misstates what the theory actually claims, and mark clearly which parts of the psychology are settled, which are contested among the researchers themselves, and which are her extrapolation. Then address the confusion you think is doing the most work here.

I would rather be corrected than reassured. If I have conceded something I should not have, or if my question rests on a mistake about what a theory of that kind could ever show, say so plainly instead of splitting the difference politely; and if the honest answer is that part of this cannot be settled from the armchair, say that too. Assume I can follow an argument but have no training in philosophy: avoid jargon where you can, and define any term of art in a sentence.
