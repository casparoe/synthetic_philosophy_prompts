I teach the second half of a nineteenth-century philosophy survey, and the session I have come to dread is the one on the master–slave dialectic in Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit. My students arrive having already heard the story — one party dominates, the other labors, the positions invert — and they nod along, and nothing in the argument touches them, because they have only ever met it as a plot they can retell. I want an activity in which they run into the pressure that produces the inversion before the vocabulary of the dialectic is anywhere on the board; I do not want to explain the point first and then illustrate it. I have been sketching a mechanism and would like you to develop it into something actually runnable. The plan is a two-person game played across a low screen or an upright folder, so that partners cannot see each other's holdings. Each pair gets a bowl of chips, a small deck of recognition cards, and a score sheet. Each round, a player chooses exactly one of three moves: Take (remove two chips from the partner's bowl and add them to your own), Build (add two chips to your own bowl from the bank), or Speak (hand the partner a recognition card, at no cost in chips). A recognition card is worth four points to whoever receives it if the giver held six or more chips at the moment of giving, and one point otherwise; a player's final score is chips in hand plus the points on cards received; and if in any round both partners choose Speak, each takes a bonus of three points. I want the pairings to start asymmetrically — one player with eight chips, the other with four — and I want the pairs to play eight rounds without being told that the asymmetry is the point. The dynamic I am aiming at is that the player who only Takes finishes holding the most chips and having received almost nothing worth having, while the player who Builds crosses the threshold at which their acknowledgment begins to count and becomes the one whose recognition confers value; and that the mutual-Speak bonus is visible from the first round but unreachable while one partner is still seizing chips. What I need from you is the full design: materials and setup, exact rules and a payoff table whose numbers you have actually checked so that this dynamic emerges rather than being something I have merely wished for, how the two roles are introduced and whether the initial asymmetry is disclosed, a minute-by-minute plan for a seventy-five-minute seminar of eighteen students, and a debrief that maps each phase of play onto a moment in the dialectic — the initial inequality, the master's dependence on a recognition that coerced compliance cannot supply, labor as the slave's transforming relation to the object, and reciprocal recognition as an end state that cannot be reached from inside the master's strategy.

Two things I want handled honestly rather than papered over. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect that some pairs will collapse into mutual Building and then mutual Speaking and feel they have solved it, that some players will be perfectly content to win on chips and see no problem at all, and that at least one will refuse the asymmetry as rigged and stop playing — and give me a way to turn each of those into material for the debrief rather than something I have to rescue the session from. Second, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. I already suspect that fixing a payoff table in advance means the game can exhibit the instability of the master's position but not the self-transformation of the concept that Hegel is actually after, and that a two-person coordination problem is a poor stand-in for an account of how self-consciousness is constituted through the encounter with another. Tell me where the mechanism distorts the view, which of my own design choices do the distorting, and what I will have to supply in the debrief because no arrangement of chips and cards can stage it.
