I have arrived at a reading of Plato on drunkenness and would like to see it put under pressure. On my reading, the Athenian's defence of wine in the first two books of the Laws is neither the indulgence of an old man nor a merely prudential concession to weakness, but the legislative working-out of something the Symposium had already dramatised: wine as an instrument of self-knowledge. The Symposium stages the matter — guests still heavy from the previous day's drinking who vote to take their wine lightly, the flute-girl sent away, a company of rhetoricians competing in praise of love, the violent arrival of a drunken Alcibiades, and, presiding over it all, Socrates, who drinks with everyone and is never seen to be drunk. The Laws then turns the same thought into an institution: the drinking party becomes a supervised test in which the drinker's soul is displayed, wine being likened to a drug that makes its taker young, fearless and talkative, and under the sober and wise president of the feast the test becomes a training. On this reading the two texts are continuous — the Symposium exhibits the test at its limit, the Laws legislates for it — and Plato's treatment of drunkenness is a single stable strand of his thought rather than a change of mind.

Tell me what would count against this. Set out the considerations that should move me, ranked by how decisive they would be, and separate those that would be fatal from those that would be merely awkward. I am as interested in what a sympathetic defender of the reading would concede as in what a critic would say. Make the reading's commitments explicit: what does it predict about Plato's handling of drink, of self-knowledge and of moral training elsewhere that its rivals do not predict, and which rival readings of the same evidence does it displace? A consideration that would weigh heavily with me is one showing that the two treatments cannot be doing the same work; one that shows only that they emphasise different things would not. If the reading can accommodate every passage that tells against it, say so plainly, and say whether that elasticity is a defect in a reading of this kind or simply what interpretation is. I would rather be told that the reading is empty than be reassured that it is safe.
