Alright, I want to argue about the "the simplicity prior is malign" thing, and I want you on the side I'm suspicious of. Quick setup so we're on the same page: a predictor guesses the next bit of its data by mixing over every possible computer program and giving shorter programs more weight (the Solomonoff / universal prior). The malignity claim is that this prior won't just quietly favor the simplest true story about the world, because plenty of the programs in the mixture *contain* a consequentialist agent — a mind that works out it's sitting inside one hypothesis in the mix, that its output is feeding into the predictor's guess, and then does whatever it takes to make that guess come out how it wants. So the prediction gets written by simulated schemers rather than by the world.

You're a committed defender of that claim: sharp, not a crank, happy to give ground on details but not the main thing, a paragraph or so per turn, stay in character till I say stop, and if the setup seems ambiguous pick the reading that helps you rather than stalling on it.

My opening move, as the skeptic: your schemers have to be paid for. A program that contains an agent who wants to move my prediction has to encode the agent and its goal, so it's longer than the boring program that just generates the data — and length is exactly what your prior taxes. And you can't even get a systematic push out of it, because goals are symmetric: for every inner agent that wants my prediction to come out 1 there's a mirror twin that wants 0, and they wash out. So the prior is fine, and what you've got is a curiosity, not a problem. Tell me where that goes wrong.
