I received a common psychiatric diagnosis a few months ago, and it has had the unexpected effect of forcing me to re-examine a set of views I had previously held only in the abstract. Before I commit any of this to paper for a seminar, I would like an audit of whether the package hangs together. Here, in the order in which I would defend them, are the commitments.

1. Realism. What I have is a genuine disorder: a malfunction of some psychological or neural system, not merely a label that my culture and my clinic happened to have available.

2. Validation. A diagnostic category earns its place only if it can be checked against something independent of how the people placed in it describe themselves — some fact about what the relevant system is for and how it is failing.

3. Looping. Classifications of people interact with the people classified: they learn the description, come to fit it, and the description is then revised in light of how they behave, so that a psychiatric category is a moving target rather than a fixed object awaiting discovery. The distinction between kinds that are indifferent to how we classify them and kinds that are not strikes me as one of the more important observations anyone has made about the human sciences.

4. My own case. I believe I am an instance of the loop. My symptoms and my self-descriptions changed once I had the diagnosis and had read other people's accounts, and I do not think that shows I was talked into anything. I have come to think of my condition as a self-sustaining process in which symptoms sustain one another rather than as the surface expression of a single broken mechanism; and I take that process, loop included, to be a real causal process. Nor does the fact that the category is sustained partly by people like me make my understanding of myself through it any less a piece of knowledge.

5. Boundaries. The edges of the category should be fixed by causal structure — by what responds to what — rather than by the surface similarity of symptoms or by clinical convenience.

6. Measurement. I prefer dimensional and transdiagnostic measures to categorical checklists; the comorbidity and heterogeneity that the checklists generate seem to me decisive against the categorical approach.

7. A verdict. I think the most recent loosening of the criteria was a mistake, even though I was diagnosed under the loosened criteria and would not have met the earlier ones.

Which of these are actually in tension, ordered by severity rather than presented as equally bad? Which are only apparently in tension, and what makes the appearance misleading? For each genuine tension, which commitment has to give, and what is the cheapest repair and what would it cost? Finally, which single revision would resolve the largest number of them, and what would I be giving up by making it? I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I am asking what this set of commitments commits me to.
