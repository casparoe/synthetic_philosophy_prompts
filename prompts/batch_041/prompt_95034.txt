I'm drafting something on the common prior and I want a sparring partner, so here's the setup. Play a committed defender of the common prior assumption -- the Harsanyi-style line that any difference in credence between two rational agents has to trace back to a difference in information, and that a shared prior is what makes their disagreement a disagreement about a fact rather than two people talking past each other. Play it straight: you can use everything the position actually has (agreement theorems, no-trade and no-betting arguments, the lot), and I'd rather you argue than hand me a survey of the literature. House rules: stay in character until I say out, one paragraph per reply, conceed a point if I actually earn it, never give up the main claim, and if my setup is ambiguous ask me to pin it down instead of guessing what I meant.

My opening move. Two of my students, Ada and Bea. Both Bayesians, both have seen exactly the same evidence -- they went through it line by line together -- and each knows the other's credence and the other's track record on questions of this kind. Ada says 0.7 that the coin in my desk drawer is biased; Bea says 0.4. I can't find a mistake in either of them. You'll say they must not have had a common prior -- but you get to say that about every disagreement anyone has ever had, and I never get to check it, since the prior is whatever it has to be to make the theorem come out. And the follow-up, that Ada and Bea can now take a bet each of them expects to win, doesn't scare me: that's just what two different priors look like from the inside. So: what, if anything, is wrong with Ada and Bea? And if the answer is nothing, what's left of the common prior as a claim about rationality, rather than a piece of bookkeeping?
