I'm taking a seminar on decision theory and the governance of risky technologies, and I've been asked to write a staged debate. Please compose a dialogue between two philosophers about how far the unilateralist's curse should reshape our practice.

By the curse I mean the result that when many agents can each bring about some outcome on their own, the outcome tends to be brought about by whichever agent most overestimates its value, so the fact that someone judged it worth doing is weak evidence that it was. My first speaker treats this as a serious general argument for a strong presumption in favour of restraint and prior coordination: where an action is hard to reverse and many parties could take it — a single lab releasing a result, a single firm deploying a technology — expect the trigger to be pulled by the poorest judge of its value, and place the burden of proof on whoever wants to act.

My second speaker should be just as sharp. Give them the more interesting critical line: that the curse is a selection effect rather than a verdict, and that its premises are narrow — the agents' errors have to be roughly independent and not systematically biased the same way, the action has to be genuinely one-sided, and the effect shrinks as information is pooled. Have them press a symmetric case: where the default is that nothing gets done and a single actor would have to step in to prevent a harm, the same selection logic seems to pick out the most optimistic intervenor, which tells in favour of acting rather than against it. Let the first speaker answer — the reply I want is that the symmetry is less clean than it looks, since one actor suffices to act while all of them must refrain, and that the aggregation-and-feedback remedies the second speaker prefers are precisely what the irreversible cases lack. Let the second speaker respond to that in turn.

Neither should win by being obtuse, and each reply should engage the previous point rather than restate a position. Close with a brief joint summary of what the curse does and does not establish, even if the two still disagree about what follows for practice.
