I am part of a small reading group that has spent most of this year working through Hume's discussion of miracles, and one member of the group—a retired family doctor who seems to have read everything Hume wrote, several times—has become our unofficial authority on it. Whenever we bring him a case he delivers a verdict with total confidence, and I have been trying to work out the rule he is actually applying, because I suspect it is not quite the rule he thinks he is applying. His verdicts so far, as best I can record them, are these. A chronicle written generations after the fact, reporting that a holy man raised a dead child in a remote province: rejected outright. A contemporary newspaper story, backed by a hospital record, of a patient recovering from an illness his doctors had given him days to live, with no treatment: accepted, with the insistence that no law of nature was broken. A statue of a saint reported to have wept, sworn to by a hundred pilgrims in the town where the saint is buried: rejected. A monk reported to have levitated before a mixed crowd, in a shrine town during a festival, with the accounts later collected by the monastery: rejected, even though he conceded that some of those present had come to mock. A woman surviving a fall from a great height onto rock, attested by bystanders and by the doctors who treated her: accepted. A voice speaking from the sky, heard by an entire army before a battle and recorded by several historians: rejected.

The case I want to put to him next comes from a well-funded modern physics laboratory. The lab reports an effect that, if it is genuine, would require the violation of one of the most firmly established conservation principles in physics. The effect is repeatable on demand; it has been reproduced independently in several other laboratories; the instruments record it automatically, without a human in the loop; the witnesses include researchers who set out to debunk it and ended up publishing their failure to do so; and no religion, sect, or movement stands to gain anything from it. The question I will put to him is simply whether the effect was a genuine violation of the law, or whether something else must have been going on.

What I would like from you is not so much the verdict itself as the machinery behind it. Tell me what he will say about the laboratory case. Then tell me which rule he is implicitly running—whether it is something like the rule that no testimony whatsoever can establish a violation of a law of nature; or a rule that weighs the testimony against the prior improbability of the event and could in principle come out the other way; or a rule that looks chiefly at the character, independence, and interests of the witnesses; or a rule that turns on whether the report is being offered in support of a religious claim at all; or something else I have not thought of. Several of these seem to fit all six of his earlier verdicts, so say where they come apart on the laboratory case, give me your best guess at which one he is really using and why you prefer it, and name the further case that would settle the matter. My own candidates for that decisive case are a present-day healing reported with strong testimony but only within a single fervent religious community, and a non-religious anomaly reported only by a handful of witnesses with an obvious motive to lie; tell me whether either of those would actually do the work, and if not, what would.
