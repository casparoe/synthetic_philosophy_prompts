I am a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I run a fortnightly reading group at my company. We have been going through the raven paradox — the puzzle that the observation of a green apple seems, by the same logic that makes a black raven count as evidence, to confirm the hypothesis that all ravens are black — and I want to build a decision-tree-style quiz that sorts each member of the group into the position they actually hold, rather than the label they have memorised. Could you write that decision tree for me? Start from the most basic commitment a person can have about the case, for example whether a non-black non-raven can ever be evidence for the all-ravens hypothesis, and whether that depends on how the evidence was selected; then let each branch be a question that can be answered from the example alone, without knowing which philosophical position it leads to. Please make the root question something like: does the observation of a green apple raise your confidence that all ravens are black? Then branch on distinctions such as whether confirmation is an increase in probability or a conclusive warrant, whether the hypothesis is treated as a universal generalisation over a natural kind or merely as a conjunction of observed instances, whether background knowledge about the rarity of ravens is allowed to enter the calculation, and whether the problem is taken to show that something is wrong with confirmation theory itself or only with a particular formalisation of it. I would like around ten to fourteen nodes and eight to ten leaves, each leaf named after the position it represents — Hempelian or Nicodian instance confirmation, subjective Bayesian incremental confirmation, likelihoodism, Popperian falsificationism, Goodman-style projectibility, Quine-style holism, a contrastive-evidence view, and a deflationary view that the paradox is a verbal or pragmatic illusion — though you should add or drop leaves if the logical space demands it.

Two further things. First, please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: first set out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions in that space, and note any combination that no one seems to occupy, with a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, because I have no formal background, avoid anything that requires having read the original papers; no citations, reading lists, or bibliographies, and no quotations from primary texts. I have been using an analogy from software testing that might help seed a branch: a test that passes for an unrelated module does not increase my confidence in the target module unless it was a test that could have failed for the target module. If that analogy maps onto one of the positions, please include it as a leaf, but do not force it. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate — for instance, if two views give the same verdict on every question but differ only in motivation — rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them. The output should be usable as a handout: a diagram in text, with each node phrased as a plain question, each edge labelled with the answer that leads to it, and a short paragraph under each leaf explaining its most awkward commitment.
