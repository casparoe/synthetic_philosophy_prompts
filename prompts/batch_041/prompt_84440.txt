I teach ethics and decision theory, and I sit as a trustee on the board of a small private foundation whose mission is to bring a treatment to patients with a rare degenerative disease within twenty years. The board votes on October 9 between two five-year research strategies, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before the vote. I would like you to draft that opinion.

The two strategies are these. The first, which staff call the portfolio, spreads the next five years of grantmaking across roughly forty established laboratories working on incremental improvements: better supportive care, a repurposed compound, better diagnostics. It is close to certain to produce modest but real gains for patients within the decade, and close to certain not to produce a cure. The second, which staff call the platform bet, commits most of the same money to one laboratory and one unproven delivery technology. Staff put the chance that it yields a therapy that halts the disease at roughly one in eight, and the chance that it yields nothing clinically usable at better than even, in which case the five years are largely spent. Staff scored both strategies on the foundation's own impact metric and put the platform bet ahead, but not by much, and the gap depends heavily on the assumed probability that the delivery technology works at all; small changes in that assumption reverse the ranking.

Two trustees will not accept the platform bet. Their objection, as far as I can extract it, is not that the probability is too low but that the foundation is not entitled to stake its only shot on a long shot while patients are waiting. A third trustee, who has some background in decision theory, proposes that the board stop scoring options by expected impact and adopt what he calls a risk-weighted impact score. His rule runs as follows. List an option's possible outcomes with their probabilities, and give each outcome a number on the foundation's impact scale; order the outcomes from worst to best. Replace each probability with a weight: for any given outcome, the weight is the difference between the c-th power of the probability of doing at least that well and the c-th power of the probability of doing better than that. Multiply each outcome's impact number by its weight and add. The exponent c is a single number no less than 1, set by the board. At c equal to 1 the weights are simply the probabilities and the rule is ordinary expected impact; as c rises, the rule behaves as though the worst outcomes were likelier than they are and the best outcomes less likely. He argues that this is the only honest way to write down what the objecting trustees are doing when they refuse the platform bet, and that it captures a real difference between a foundation that can absorb a loss and patients who cannot.

I have not made up my mind, and I would rather the opinion change it than flatter it. What I need from you is the following.

First, a recommendation stated in the opening paragraph: which strategy the board should adopt on October 9, and whether it should adopt the risk-weighted rule at all, and if so at what value of c, set by whom, with what date for reconsideration. Give me a resolution the board can actually vote on, not a framework for deciding later.

Second, whether the risk-weighted rule is a defensible account of what the board should do or a formalization of a mistake. Does it ever rank one option above another that is worse in every state of the world? Does it violate a principle the board would insist on in any other context, for instance that the ranking of two options cannot depend on what they have in common, so that replacing the outcome they share in some state with the same outcome for both cannot flip the preference between them? Exhibit a concrete pair of options, built from the numbers above or from plausible variations on them, in which the rule reverses the ranking that expected impact gives, and say whether the reversal is defensible.

Third, whether the objecting trustees' caution can be redescribed as ordinary expected impact applied to a concave impact scale, that is, as diminishing returns to impact rather than as anything about probabilities. If it can, say so, and the rule is unnecessary. If it cannot, identify the kind of case that separates the two, say which better matches what the trustees are actually objecting to, and say what the rule is supposed to do when the probabilities are contested estimates rather than known chances.

Fourth, at what level the rule is to be applied. Because the weighting depends on an option's whole outcome distribution, scoring the two five-year strategies is not the same as scoring the individual grants within them and adding. Tell the board what it must decide about aggregation, and what turns on that decision.

Fifth, who bears the risk and who bears the regret. The trustees bear embarrassment; patients bear the consequences of five wasted years. Say whether the trustees' reluctance is a reason for the board to act as they wish or a bias the board should discount, and make the recommendation consistent with your answer.

Include a short statement of the best case for the losing side, in terms its advocates would accept. Address the document to the board, keep it to about twelve hundred words, and write it so that trustees with no background in decision theory can follow the argument without its being flattened. Do not include citations, a reading list, or a survey of what has been written on the subject; argue from the structure of the options themselves. And do not tell the board that this is a disagreement between expected value and risk aversion. They have heard that already, and it does not help them vote.
