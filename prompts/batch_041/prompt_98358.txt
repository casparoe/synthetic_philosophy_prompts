Two philosophers are working through a puzzle in the metaphysics of self-location. Mira accepts the self-sampling assumption (SSA): very roughly, you should reason as though you were a randomly selected observer drawn from among the observers in your own world. Theo accepts the self-indication assumption (SIA): very roughly, you should reason as though you were a randomly selected observer drawn from among all the observers that exist across all the possibilities you are entertaining, so that a hypothesis positing more observers is, other things equal, more likely to be the one you inhabit. Neither is a strawman. Each holds a view with genuine resources and genuine costs, each has heard the other's best objections many times, and each will concede a sub-point when it is right while holding the main line. Write their dialogue in three named parts, in this order.

1. "The case, stated." (100–150 words.) A neutral, third-person statement of the puzzle below, including the numbers, so that the reader has the facts before the argument begins. No argument here — just the setup and the two questions.

The case: A device runs one of two programs. It first tosses a fair coin. If the coin lands heads, it runs ONE: it creates a single room with a single occupant, and that occupant is you. If the coin lands tails, it runs MANY: it creates ten thousand rooms, each with a single occupant, and exactly one of those occupants is you, fixed in advance by a hidden lottery; all ten thousand occupants are, from the inside, indistinguishable — same memories, same silent room, no way to reach one another. You wake in your room, can observe nothing but the room, and know everything just described. First question: what should you think about whether ONE or MANY ran? Second question: before you look at anything else, you learn that a lamp in each room was set as follows — under ONE the lamp is always blue, while under MANY a fair coin made all ten thousand lamps blue or all of them red. You look: the lamp is blue. What should you now think?

2. "The exchange." (800–1100 words.) Mira and Theo work through the case together. Requirements: both characters are equally sharp and argue in good faith; each reply engages the actual last thing the other said rather than pivoting to a prepared speech; they put the relevant arithmetic on the table and either may challenge the other's arithmetic or the principle behind it; the exchange includes at least one real concession and at least one moment where a character says plainly that the other has located the genuine difficulty; and it reaches the point where the disagreement bottoms out — the premise on which everything turns — instead of trailing off. Do not resolve it by fiat, and do not let either side win by being handed weaker arguments. Theo may at some point press the worry that SIA licenses inferring enormous numbers of unobservable beings from the bare fact that you exist; Mira may at some point press the worry that SSA cannot specify which observers belong to your reference class, and that this makes it unusable.

3. "Where they part." (150–200 words.) In your own voice: name the single premise on which the whole disagreement turns, say what each side would have to surrender in order to concede, and say which of the two questions above is the sharper test of the two assumptions. Do not declare a winner.

Constraints: no citations, no titles of books or articles, and no appeals to what some well-known philosopher said — if a character wants to lean on authority, they must give the argument instead.
