Ok so I keep getting into fights about the problem of old evidence and I want to actually stake out a position instead of just vibing. Quick setup in case its fuzzy: E is "old evidence" when you already know it and assign it credence 1. Now suppose you cook up a hypothesis H that entails E. Bayesian confirmation seems to stall — P(E | H) = P(E) = 1, so discovering that H entails E can't move P(H) at all, even though intuitively explaining or predicting a known fact should support a theory. The usual responses are either to ditch Bayesianism or to find some trick to make E "new" again (rewind your credence in E to before you learned it, re-run the update, that kind of thing).

Here's the line I want you to argue for, and argue it hard: this is a non-problem once you notice that what you actually learn in these cases isn't E (you already had that) but the relation between H and E — i.e. that H entails, or predicts, or explains E. So the thing you should conditionalize on is that discovery, not E itself. Argue that (a) this dissolves the paradox with no counterfactual-credence gymnastics, (b) the standard fixes are basically smuggling this in under a different name, and (c) it earns its keep by explaining why old evidence confirms in some cases and not others — e.g. why it matters whether the entailment was a genuine surprise versus something you deliberately built H to deliver from the start.

Then flip it and tell me where this view is weakest. The worry I can't shake: if "learning that H entails E" is itself something you could have known all along, haven't we just relaunched the old-evidence problem one level up? If that's a real threat, say so — don't paper over it just to make the argument look clean.
