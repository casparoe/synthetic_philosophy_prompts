I'm in a decision theory reading group and we keep circling back to Death in Damascus, and I'm starting to think the debate has stalled in a way nobody wants to admit. Heres the setup as we discuss it: you're in Damascus, Death is coming for you, and you know that Death will be in whatever city you end up in — so if you stay, Death is in Damascus; if you flee to Aleppo, Death is in Aleppo. You die either way. The causal decision theorists in the group say the rational thing is to pick either option, since your choice doesn't causally affect where Death is; the evidential decision theorists say the same, or sometimes that you should flee, or that the whole case is incoherent because it asks you to deliberate about something that's already settled. And every time we discuss it, someone says "but if you stay, Death is in Damascus, so you should flee," someone else says "but if you flee, Death is in Aleppo, so you should stay," and we end up agreeing that you're doomed and that's that. Nobody's view has changed in months.

What I want is a diagnosis of why this has stalled. Is it a substantive disagreement about what rationality requires, or is it a verbal dispute about what "expected utility" and "causal" and "evidential" are supposed to mean? Are the two sides actually answering different questions — one about what your choice causes, one about what your choice is evidence for — under a single heading? Or is the real issue that the case is under-described, and once you specify the causal structure precisely (who knows what, when, and how Death's prediction works), the disagreement evaporates? My working theory is that its the last one: the case is a Rorschach test for whatever notion of "decision" you brought in, and the stall is a sign that the question is ill-posed rather than deep. But I want you to tell me if that's right, and to engage what each side actually says rather than just surveying the literature. And tell me what would count as progress here: a formal result, a distinction, a better thought experiment, or an admission that the question can't be resolved because it's not really one question.
