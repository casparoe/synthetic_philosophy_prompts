I'm a graduate student in economics who has started sitting in on a political theory seminar, and last week the instructor gave us his compressed account of Marx's critique of capitalism, which we're meant to evaluate in a short response paper. His summary went roughly like this: Marx's economic theory comes down to two ideas. Exploitation is the fact that capitalists pay workers less than the value their labor adds to the product, and the surplus value they pocket is essentially theft — Marx's complaint being that the worker has a right to the full product of her labor and is cheated out of it. Alienation is the psychological counterpart of the same phenomenon: the malaise workers feel when their jobs are repetitive, meaningless, and out of their control. On Marx's view both problems have a single remedy, namely higher wages together with democratic control of the workplace — give workers a genuine stake and a genuine say and the exploitation and the alienation both dry up. The whole picture, he added, stands or falls with the labor theory of value, which economists abandoned long ago.

I found this crisp and more or less convincing, but a friend in the seminar, who has read a great deal more of this than I have, insists it's a caricature, and I can't tell whether he's being precious or whether the instructor really has cut corners. What I want is an audit I can actually put to use: go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful reader of Marx would accept as accurate, which are defensible simplifications a specialist would merely wince at, and which are things Marx himself explicitly denied or argued against. I am especially unsure about three junctures — first, the idea that exploitation is a kind of theft or a violation of a right to the full product of one's labor; second, the idea that alienation is a subjective feeling that tracks exploitation; and third, the idea that the critique stands or falls with the labor theory of value. I would also like to know whether his parting conclusion — that the theory is defunct because the economics is — follows from anything in the accurate parts of the summary, or whether it requires a further premise he never supplied.

Please answer in American English, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, and in your own words rather than a string of quotations. Where you attribute a specific position to Marx, make clear whether you take it to belong to the early philosophical writings or to the later economic work, since I gather these are not obviously saying the same thing, and flag any point on which serious interpreters divide rather than presenting one reading as settled.
