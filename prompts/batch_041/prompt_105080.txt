I have no philosophical training, but I have spent an unreasonable amount of time trying to say when a collection of things adds up to a single thing, and my own answers keep contradicting each other. Here is where my gut lands on a set of cases. What I want is the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that produces these verdicts; what each candidate says about cases I have not listed; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to abandon.

A hammer's head and handle: one thing, plainly, because the two were made for each other and work together. A handful of sand dumped on a table: not a thing, just grains. Press the same sand into a brick, though, and it becomes a thing. Yet a cairn of rocks stacked in the woods also strikes me as one thing, and that is just rocks resting on one another in the same loose relation as the sand.

A swarm of bees: one thing, since it has a shape, moves and turns as a unit, and can be said to do things. The bees do not touch and are not fastened together at all. A crowd of strangers waiting at a bus stop, by contrast, is not a thing, only people who happen to be near each other. But when that crowd becomes a mob all pushing the same way, it starts to feel like a single thing.

A forest: I want to say it is not a thing, merely trees with a name attached. Yet a river is obviously a thing, and a river never contains the same water twice and its banks wander. My body is one thing although its cells are replaced. A company whose entire staff has turned over is still the same company. The statue and the lump of clay it is made of seem to me like one thing rather than two, until I recall that if I squash the statue the clay remains and the statue does not, which makes me suspect there were two all along. A clock lying in pieces in a box and the same pieces assembled and ticking share every part, but only one of them is a clock. A hole in a wall, a shadow, a smile: I call them things, but there is nothing there to be held together.

The principle I keep reaching for is this: parts make one thing when they are joined in doing something together, when a single job or life is served. That gets me the hammer and the swarm, and perhaps the mob. It makes a mess of the cairn and the sand, which share a relation but get opposite verdicts, and it says nothing about the river or the company, where the parts are always leaving.

I suspect I am running two questions together, one about when several things make one thing at a moment and one about when a thing stays the same thing while its parts change, and I would like to be told whether separating them repairs the account. Whatever principle you settle on, please state what it does with the cairn and the sand pile, with the crowd and the mob, and with the clock in pieces versus assembled. Then say what it implies for two cases I have not mentioned: a queue that forms at a ticket window and dissolves when the window opens, which no one intends and which nonetheless has a front, a back, and a motion of its own; and a marriage, which holds two people together by a promise rather than by contact. If no principle keeps all of my verdicts, I would rather drop a verdict than keep a rule with an exception bolted on for every case, so tell me plainly which verdict has to go.
