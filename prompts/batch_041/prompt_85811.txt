I am a doctoral student in metaphysics, and before I commit to a position on the identity of indiscernibles I want the specification written down: the desiderata that any adequate account of the matter has to satisfy. I am not asking you to defend a principle. I am asking for the requirements list — what the account must get right, which cases it must handle, which distinctions it must respect, which results it must not contradict — with the consideration behind each requirement, and with a verdict on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp rather than genuine constraints. The part I care about most is the possibility that the list is inconsistent with itself: if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, identify the subset and show the conflict, because that would be the argument of my paper.

Fix the target as an account that (i) retains the standard logic of identity — reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and the indiscernibility of identicals — unless it argues explicitly for revising it; (ii) issues a determinate verdict on each of the cases below; and (iii) does not settle the question by appealing to what we could know or could describe, since the issue is metaphysical rather than epistemic. The account must also state, as part of the specification, whether the principle it defends is a necessary truth, a contingent regularity, or a constraint on how descriptions describe worlds; and it must not be trivially true, which rules out counting properties such as being this very object or being identical with a among the indiscernibility-relevant ones.

The cases the account has to handle:

1. A world containing exactly two iron spheres and nothing else, arranged symmetrically: same size, same mass, same intrinsic properties, and — since there is nothing else in the world — the same relational profile as well, each being a mile from a sphere of that size and mass. Does the description describe one thing, two things, or an incoherent world? Whatever the answer, it must not be reached by stipulation.
2. The same two spheres embedded in an asymmetric world, so that each bears different relations to its surroundings. A relational version of the principle is satisfied here; but relations such as being a mile from the other sphere presuppose that there are two things to relate, and the account must say whether grounding distinctness in such relations is circular.
3. A pair of things with identical intrinsic properties in a symmetric arrangement, but each bearing an irreflexive relation to the other — each is, say, opposite in spin to the other, where that relation cannot hold between a thing and itself (what some call weak discernibility). Is an irreflexive relation enough to make them two, and if so, does the principle survive in a graded form or collapse into something trivial?
4. The permutation question. The description in (1) is invariant under exchanging the two spheres: does a complete qualitative description of a world leave it open which sphere is which, and if so, is the world obtained by swapping them a different possible world or the same one?
5. A world like (1) with a law that destroys exactly one of the two spheres at a given time. The outcome is symmetric, so the account must say whether there is a fact about which one was destroyed, and whether it owes an explanation of why there is or is not.
6. Two electrons in an atom's ground state. The formalism treats the labels as unphysical — exchanging them returns the same state up to a global sign — yet we count two. The account must say whether it treats them as individuals and, if so, what individuates them; if not, what licenses the counting. It should also compare this with, or explain the disanalogy from, the widely held view that two models of general relativity differing only by a smooth relabelling of spacetime points represent the same physical situation.
7. The mathematical case: objects that are pure sets, individuated by their membership structure rather than by any qualities. The account should say what it implies here, where extensionality appears to do the individuating.

Beyond the case-by-case demands, I want the general requirements: no brute cardinality unless it is argued for; no vacuity in empty and one-object worlds; consistency with the trivial converse, that identical things are indiscernible; a clear separation between what grounds numerical distinctness and what determines how many things a description describes; an explanation of why the intuition behind the principle has any force at all if the principle is rejected; and a statement of whether the account makes the dispute verbal.

Give me the desiderata as a numbered list, each with its motivating case or consideration, then a short section on the subsets that cannot be jointly satisfied, and then — briefly, since I will do the full scorecard myself — a note on which family of positions the list damages most. No citations or reading list; I want the criteria, not the literature.
