I write about science and ideas for a living, and I keep getting dragged back into the is-the-world-getting-less-violent fight. Every few months someone publishes a chart, someone else says the chart is a artifact of how the numbers were put together, both sides go home, nothing is settled, and I file 900 words that say nothing. I want to stop doing that. Before I write another piece on it I want the actual spec in hand, and I'd rather have the spec than another round of the argument.

So: what does any adequate measure of violence have to deliver? Not a definition of violence, and not a verdict on who's right — I want the requirements list. The cases any such measure has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts about the world and about record-keeping it has to stay consistent with. For each one, tell me the specific case or consideration that forces it, becuase a requirement without its motivating case is just a preference, and I need to be able to tell those apart.

Some candidates I've picked up from reporting, tell me which are real requirements and which are just talking points of one camp:

- the denominator question. Per what, exactly — per person, per person-year lived, per interaction between people? These don't move together, and I suspect the choice does a lot of quiet work.
- definition drift. Things that weren't counted as violence fifty years ago are counted now (marital rape, corporal punishment, hitting kids), so part of the apparent improvement is the class of things being counted getting bigger at the near end. Does that make the long series meaningless, or can a measure be built that survives it?
- recording bias. Better hospitals, better police records, better surveys, more literate populations — all of that makes measured violence go up over time. How much of the trend is that?
- the medicine point, which I think is the one that actually breaks things. A stabbing that would have killed someone in 1600 is a non-fatal assault now. So if you measure violence by deaths, medical progress alone produces a falling trend with no change in how violent people are.
- structural stuff: harm that comes out of how institutions and economies are arranged rather than out of anyone's blow. Count it and the trend may reverse; leave it out and you've defined the question so it can only have one answer.
- absolute numbers vs rates, and whether anyone should care about the former if the latter is falling.

Here's what I most want, and it's the reason I'm asking a philosopher and not a statistician: I have a hunch some of these can't be had together. If fixing the measure to get one requirement right guarantees it fails another — a pair or a set that is jointly unsatisfiable — then that's the piece. Say so flat out, don't hedge it.

One more thing to run through whatever you come up with. Invented case, use it as a test: a society where trauma care is so good that no assault ever kills anyone. Homicides go to zero, assaults stay exactly where they were. Did violence decline? What does your measure say, and is that the right answer, or is the measure now telling me about medicine and not about violence?

And one paragraph, not a dissertation, on the question underneath all of it: is there a fact of the matter here at all, or is the world is less violent just a claim about which measure you picked?

Format: I want to paste this into my notes, so give me the requirements as a table with exactly these columns — requirement | the case or fact that forces it | what it rules out | negotiable or dealbreaker | which other requirement it collides with (write none if it doesn't). Then, after the table, a short JSON object with three fields: most_demanding_requirement (the one that knocks out the most otherwise plausible measures, one sentence), unsatisfiable_pairs (a list), and one_line_verdict (a sentence I could actually put in print). Two or three sentences of prose before the table is plenty — the table is the deliverable. And skip the scorecard of who said what, I know who said what. I want the criteria.
