ok so I'm a 3rd year PhD student in an interpretability group and I keep running into something that I'm now fairly sure is a real conceptual problem and not just me being sloppy with definitions.

The setup: we find some structure in a small model that does a specific job (say, a head that copies whatever token followed the previous occurrence of the current token). Then we find something in a bigger model, or in the same model after finetuning, that seems to do that same job. Or we find two structures in the same model that both do it, one firing on natural text and one on code. My labmate keeps saying these are "the same mechanism", I keep saying they're not, and I've realised we're not disagreeing about a fact — we just have different criteria and haven't noticed.

Two criteria got floated in our meeting and I'd like you to actually take them seriously rather than wave at them. (1) Swap-invariance: two mechanisms are the same iff you can interchange them — swap the weights, or drop one subgraph in where the other was — without degrading performance on the task. (2) Necessity-profile identity: the same iff the set of inputs on which each is causally necessary is the same. I can already see these come apart, but I want the general picture, not just a counterexample.

So: what is the conceptual issue here? What are the plausible criteria for mechanism individuation, and do any of them deliver sameness *without* relativising to a choice of perturbation class or level of description? If they all relativise, does that make the notion useless for mechanistic anomaly detection — where the whole point is to flag a mechanism as anomalous relative to some reference model or distribution — or does it just mean the reference class is a modelling choice we should be explicit about instead of pretending it's given? And I'd especially like the case where two mechanisms are indistinguishable on-distribution but come apart off it treated carefully, because I think that's where it definately bites but I can't articulate why.
