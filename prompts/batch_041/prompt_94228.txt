I'm a novelist who teaches a graduate seminar on choice and narrative, and I'm setting the final. I want a single question built on the absent-minded driver problem — the case in which a driver passes several identical intersections, cannot tell which one they are at, and must decide whether to continue or turn off. The textbook two-exit version is too easy to answer from memory, so build the question on a variant of my own: three indistinguishable intersections; turning off at the first pays 1, at the second pays 3, at the third pays 4, and continuing past all three pays nothing. Frame it as a case a student has to analyse; make them work out which strategy maximises the driver's expected payoff, showing the setup and the optimum; and force them to say what the driver's inability to tell the intersections apart implies for what a strategy can even be, and to explain the tension between the strategy that is best ex ante and what the driver would want to do at the final intersection — and what that tension reveals about rational action under imperfect recall. Students have ninety minutes; the question is worth 20 marks and must be answerable without consulting any sources.

Give me the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a 20-mark marking scheme that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make — the correct expected-payoff setup, the correct optimum, the correct diagnosis of the ex ante / sequential-rationality conflict, and its resolution — and, for each move, the characteristic error that should cost marks (assuming the driver can distinguish the intersections, treating a missed exit as a sunk cost that settles the matter, and so on).
