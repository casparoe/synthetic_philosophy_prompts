I've worked my way into a position I'd like you to try to break: that the criteria of authenticity used in historical-Jesus research — embarrassment, dissimilarity from both the Jewish setting and the early church, multiple attestation, coherence — can, applied jointly, establish what Jesus actually said and did. My version of the position is deliberately quantitative, and I want that version tested rather than a caricature of it. Treat each criterion as a diagnostic test with a sensitivity and a specificity for the hypothesis that a given saying or deed goes back to Jesus; treat the prior as the base rate at which sayings in the relevant tradition were invented rather than remembered; and treat positive verdicts on several criteria as multiplying the odds.

What would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I do not need to be told that the sources are late or that the gospels are theological documents; I take those as given. Press instead on the framework's own joints. Are the criteria independent enough for the multiplication to mean anything, or are they correlated in ways that make a four-criteria verdict nearly worthless? Do any of them test historicity rather than earliness — I suspect several are quietly tests of the latter, and that a saying can be very early and still be an invention? Is the prior estimable at all, and what becomes of the apparatus if it is not? And does the habitual practice of publishing the authenticated sayings while letting the failures drop make the surviving picture an artifact of the method rather than a portrait of anyone?

Then tell me two things: which of these a defender of the criteria would themselves have to concede as counting against the position, and whether the position as I have stated it risks anything at all — or has been qualified into a shape that no possible evidence could touch. If the latter, say whether that is fatal for a position of this kind or simply the price of doing history with the evidence there is.
