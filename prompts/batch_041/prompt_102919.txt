For an upper-level seminar on legal interpretation, I am setting a 90-minute essay worth 20 marks. The problem rests on an invented statute, so students cannot answer it by reciting an existing case. Here it is.

The Remote Credential Protection Act provides: "No person shall use a synthetic voice to obtain a credential from another person." Section 2 defines "synthetic voice" as "an audible output generated by a machine that is capable of being mistaken for the voice of a natural person by an ordinary listener." Section 3 directs that the Act "shall be construed to advance its purpose, which is to protect persons from being induced to surrender credentials by outputs they reasonably believe to originate from a natural person." Section 4 states that the purpose in section 3 "is the Act's only purpose and shall not be supplemented by extratextual sources."

The facts: D operates a voice assistant whose output is in fact capable of being mistaken for a human voice. But every call opens with a clear disclosure that the speaker is automated, and the listener, who understood this, nonetheless read out a one-time code in response to the assistant's plausible-sounding request. D is charged under section 1.

Write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks. The question must require students to (i) resolve the charge under a textualist method and under a purposivist method and identify precisely where the two diverge, and (ii) say whether, on this statute, the textualist/purposivist distinction survives, or whether each method is forced to borrow the other's central move. Have the model answer take a definite position on how the case should come out and defend it, rather than laying the options out evenhandedly. In the marking scheme, name the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks, allocating marks to each. No marks should be available for citing real cases or commentators; the exercise is confined to the text supplied.
