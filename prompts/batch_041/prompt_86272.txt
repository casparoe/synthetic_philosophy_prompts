I'm in a decision-theory reading group that has now spent three weeks circling the common prior assumption, and I'd rather settle it with a flowchart than another hour of arguing. The annoying part is that we all agree about the result itself — if two people start from the same prior, both update by Bayes' rule, and each knows what the other believes (and knows the other knows what they believe), then they can't still be disagreeing once they've compared notes — and then disagree completely about what that result is a result *about*. One guy in the group thinks the common prior is a claim about how heads actually work, so any real disagreement has to trace back to different evidence. Another thinks its a requirement of rationality: to be rational just is to believe what you'd believe if all you had was the information everybody shares. A third thinks it's a modelling device for writing down asymmetric information and has no business telling anyone what they're allowed to believe. We also can't agree on a prior question — whether a prior is a psychological fact about your degrees of belief before the evidence showed up, or just bookkeeping for how much the evidence leaves open — or on whether agents who don't even share the same list of possible states can be said to have a common prior at all, or on whether the whole agreement story is really a theorem about common knowledge rather than about rationality.

So what I want is a decision tree, a which-position-do-I-actually-hold chart, that starts with someone who accepts that opening result and sorts them onto a position. Every branch node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and wherever you can, make the node a concrete case rather than an abstract label: the two traders who each know the other's beliefs and are still happy to bet against each other; the two doctors who disagree about a diagnosis and each know exactly how the other reasons; the two economists whose priors differ because one of them grew up somewhere with hyperinflation; the pair who can't agree even on which outcomes are possible in the first place. Each leaf should carry the name of a position where there is one and otherwise just be described and marked unnamed, plus one sentence on the commitment that position has the hardest time defending. Build the tree out of the logical space first — which questions genuinely split the positions and which combinations of answers they allow — and only then hang the names on it; note any combination of answers that nobody occupies and say whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate.

Keep it to something we can argue through in one session — maybe seven or eight branch nodes and no more than a dozen leaves — and dont bother with a reading list or citations, I know the literature is out there and what I want is the shape of the disagreement. Also, for me: tell me which branch you think is doing the real work, and what your tree predicts for a case I didn't list, say two agents who share a prior and know it, but where one of them thinks the other is only probably rational.
