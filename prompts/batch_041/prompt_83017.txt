You are an expert in game theory and the philosophy of the social sciences, and I need your help writing one essay question for the final exam of my graduate seminar on rational choice and the evolution of cooperation, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The students have already had the standard treatment of the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, indefinite repetition, discounting, and the folk theorem, so the question must be one they cannot answer by reciting any of that. Think it through carefully before you draft, and work out the equilibrium conditions yourself rather than assuming the familiar result carries over.

The exam runs 90 minutes and is worth 60 marks in total; this question is worth 25. Please build it in three parts around the following variant of the repeated prisoner's dilemma, which I have constructed for the occasion. In the stage game, mutual cooperation pays 3 to each player, unilateral defection pays 5 to the defector and 0 to the other, and mutual defection pays 1 to each. Players maximise expected total undiscounted payoff. After each round the game continues with probability p if both players cooperated in the round just played, and with probability q if at least one of them defected, where 1 > p > q > 0. Actions are observed and all of this is common knowledge.

Part (i) should ask for the condition under which cooperating every round, with permanent reversion to defection after any deviation, is a subgame-perfect equilibrium, and should ask whether letting the continuation probability depend on the round's outcome makes cooperation easier or harder to sustain than in the fixed-continuation game the students already know, with the algebra to back the verdict.

Part (ii) should press on the credibility of the punishment: whether the permanent-reversion threat is one the players would actually want to carry out once the moment arrives, what that does to the incentive to cooperate in the first place, and what the most defensible verdict is once the question is put that way.

Part (iii) should be conceptual rather than algebraic: what the variant shows about the claim that cooperation needs a sufficiently long shadow of the future, and what the analysis has to assume about where the continuation probability comes from.

Then give me three things: (1) the question as the students will see it, with the marks for each part shown; (2) a model answer of about 700 words, self-contained, with the key derivation displayed in a line or two; and (3) a marking scheme naming five must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of 25, the most common way candidates get each one wrong, and a sentence on what separates a distinction-level answer from a merely competent one. No citations or references, please.
