I teach a final-year seminar on determinism, chaos, and prediction, and I want the final exam to turn on a distinction I have been drawing in lectures rather than on anything students can look up. I call a model closed when its state and its law leave nothing unmodelled — no external input the dynamics fail to account for — and tight when small errors in the initial state stay small under the dynamics; my running claim is that chaotic systems are typically closed but not tight, and that arguments for determinism usually establish only closure while the familiar worries about prediction are worries about tightness. Write me one 60-minute essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard material on sensitive dependence: it should require students to work through two mismatched cases — one closed but not tight, and one tight but not closed, such as a model whose predictions stay accurate only because an unmodelled external mechanism keeps correcting it — and to say what each case does, and does not, show about the inference from predictive success or failure to determinism or indeterminism. The marking scheme should name five must-hit moves with their marks, and for each the characteristic error that costs the mark, for instance treating a large prediction error as evidence of indeterminism, or accurate prediction as evidence of closure.
