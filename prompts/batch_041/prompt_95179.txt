After a long career at the bench in molecular biology, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of biology, and I keep meeting the claim that DNA is a code, or that genes carry information, in contexts where I can no longer tell what is being asserted. Before I commit to any account, I want the specification: what must an adequate theory of genetic information — or of the coding claim in particular — be able to do? Set out the desiderata, each with the case or consideration behind it, and separate the requirements that are genuine from those that are merely commitments of one camp. Three considerations from my own work must appear on the list, and I want to see how each constrains the others: that the same codon can be reassigned in different lineages, so that what a triplet means is settled by the translation apparatus rather than by the sequence alone; that a single stretch of DNA can be read in overlapping frames and yield more than one product; and that information in the Shannon sense is defined without any notion of content, so the term cannot carry the explanatory weight by itself. Tell me whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect that demanding a meaning intrinsic to the sequence collides with the context-dependence above — and which single requirement does the most to rule out the candidate accounts. The purpose I have in mind is a practical one: an account that will let me say, in a way I would defend at a seminar, when it is literally true that a piece of DNA carries information about something, and when the coding talk is only a metaphor. You need not score the existing accounts against the list; I will do that myself.
