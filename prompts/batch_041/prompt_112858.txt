I was in a seminar on moral change last term, and a friend offered an analogy that has been nagging at me. She said moral revolutions are like scientific revolutions: a received moral framework accumulates anomalies -- practices that its defenders can no longer square with its own stated principles -- until the anomalies produce a crisis, the old framework loses its hold on the community, and a new framework replaces it. On this picture, abolition and the end of dueling are episodes in which arguments and evidence finally won: the anomaly of slavery's contradiction with natural-rights talk, or of dueling's contradiction with Christian ethics, became impossible to ignore, and the better moral theory displaced the worse one. She used the analogy to support a larger conclusion, namely that moral progress is fundamentally argument-driven, and that where interests or honor seem to explain a change, they are really just the transmission belt for arguments that had already done the justificatory work. I am not sure the analogy can carry that weight. I want an audit rather than a verdict.

Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the paradigm, normal science, anomaly, crisis, conversion, incommensurability, and the scientific community, and what plays the corresponding roles in a moral revolution. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Some breaks may not matter for her conclusion; separate those from the ones that do. I am especially interested in what the source domain quietly imports -- truth-tracking methods, a community of experts, empirical anomaly, rational conversion, cumulative progress -- and which of those the target domain lacks. Bring in the competing accounts as pressure tests: the Williams-style thesis that abolition is better explained by economic and political interests, and Appiah-style accounts that dueling and footbinding ended because honor codes shifted, not because arguments refuted them. I want the analysis to keep separate four things that are often run together: what made the old norm vulnerable, what triggered its collapse, what sustained the new norm, and what would have happened under a nearby counterfactual. I also want you to say whether the analogy forces a choice between arguments, interests, and honor, or whether it can accommodate overdetermination and mixed mechanisms.

Finally, tell me whether her conclusion -- that moral revolutions are argument-driven -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, what a better analogy would be: moral revolutions as regime changes, as status cascades, as market corrections, as epidemics, or something else. I do not need bibliographies, dates, or citations; I want the conceptual audit. Please write the answer in American English.
