I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough to remember when the memetics fights there were still hot, and I would like a piece of criticism that treats that argument as a live controversy rather than as settled background. Take the American film The Ring, and the Japanese film it remakes, and read them through the question of whether culture is Darwinian. On its face the film is a memeticist's thought experiment: a videotape that kills whoever watches it within seven days unless the viewer makes a copy and shows it to someone else, so that the tape's persistence is paid for by its hosts and its incubation period is just long enough for transmission to occur before the host is spent. The characters never understand the tape; they reverse-engineer its rules, and the winning move is propagation rather than comprehension. (Keep meme in its original sense throughout — the tape is a replicator, not a reaction image.)

That is about as strong a case for the meme's-eye view as fiction could build, which is why I distrust it. The tape is a physical artifact copied at near-perfect fidelity, and that is precisely the feature that skeptics of memetics, from the cognitive-anthropology camp onward, say cultural items do not have. If transmission is reconstructive rather than reproductive — if what travels is an attractor in a space of psychological dispositions, not a copy — then the film has quietly given its meme a gene's fidelity and called the result culture. So I want the reading to cut both ways. Where does the meme's-eye view genuinely illuminate the film: the seven-day deadline as a solution to the tradeoff between virulence and transmission, the copy-and-pass rule as a fitness constraint, the protagonists' slide from puzzle-solvers into vectors? Where does the film instead put pressure on the theory: the personified ghost as an illegitimate stand-in for impersonal selection (the same move we make when we personify selection as a demon), the gap between a cultural item's fitness and its host's interests, the awkward fact that the tape's meaning is irrelevant to its spread but not to our experience of it? And where does the film simply resist the reading, so that an honest critic has to admit the analogy breaks? Please end by saying what the fiction adds to the debate that argument alone cannot — whether a story about a mind-virus can make a case that the epidemiology of representations can only assert, and what it costs to make that case by turning a statistic into a ghost.
