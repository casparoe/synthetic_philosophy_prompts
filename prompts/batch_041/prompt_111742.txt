Ok, I need help settling an argument. My friend and I have been going back and forth about the two-envelope thing for two weeks and I want to know what rule my gut is actually running, and whether that rule is defensible or just a pile of habits. Here are my honest verdicts, gut level, no trying to be clever:

1. Vanilla. Two envelopes, one has twice what the other has, I know nothing else about how they were filled, I take one, and before opening I'm offered the swap. I don't take it. The argument that mine is X and theirs is either 2X or X/2, so the average is 1.25X, feels like a magic trick, but if you asked me which step is the trick I'd mumble.

2. Same setup, except I peek. Mine has $10, and the person who filled them tells me — and I believe her — that she flipped a fair coin to decide between filling them with (5, 10) and filling them with (10, 20), before I picked. Now I swap. It's fifty-fifty between 5 and 20, so 12.50 beats 10.

3. Same as 2 but nobody tells me anything. I peek, I see $10, that's it. I don't swap. The coin in case 2 is what made it fifty-fifty; here the fifty-fifty would be something I invented. And I notice that if I let myself invent it, I'd be swapping no matter what number I saw, which can't be right.

4. Ceiling version. She tells me she picked the smaller amount at random from 1, 2, 4, 8, ... up to 1048576 — twenty one options, all equally likely — and then doubled it for the other envelope. I open and see 1048576. No swap: I've obviously got the big one, since the pair is either (524288, 1048576) or (1048576, 2097152), and the second isn't on her list. But if I'd opened anything else on that list I'd swap, even 524288.

5. The one that started the fight. She says: I put some amount in one envelope, then I flip a fair coin; heads the other envelope gets double that amount, tails it gets half; then I shuffle them and hand you one. I open, see $10, and I'd swap. My friend says I've fallen for the exact same trap as in case 1 and that case 5 is case 1 with extra decoration. I don't think that's right, but I honestly can't say what's different about it.

6. This is the one that keeps me up. No ceiling, no coin, no list: the smaller amount is 2^n for some whole number n, she picked n somehow, I know nothing about how, and I'm asked before opening whether I'll take the swap. My gut says sure, probably swap. But that's just case 1 again — same setup, same ignorance — and in case 1 I said no. So one of those has to go, and I want you to tell me which.

What I want: the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that gets my six verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't seen. If it takes three clauses and a caveat, fine, but tell me what each clause is doing and which case motivates it. Then: (a) what it says about two cases I haven't listed — she tells me the pair is (10, 20) or (20, 40) by a fair coin and I open and see 20; and she tells me she rolled a die and the smaller amount is 1 through 6 dollars, equally likely, doubled for the other, and I open and see $6; (b) whether I should pay a $2 fee to swap in case 2, and in case 1; (c) which of my six verdicts your principle forces me to give up, and if it's none, say that plainly and tell me what case would force a choice.

Please commit. Don't hand me the menu of positions with an "it depends who you ask" at the end — I want your account, your reasons for it over the obvious rivals, and a straight answer on case 6. And don't send me off to read anybody, I want to know what you think the right answer is.
