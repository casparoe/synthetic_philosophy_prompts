I'm a novelist — three books in, the last two about people whose jobs put them at an odd angle to new technology — and I'm trying to start something new. The premise I keep comeing back to: a clinic somewhere on the Yorkshire coast that treats an inherited degenerative brain disease by swapping out the patient's neurons for silicon equivalents, a few thousand a day over about fourteen months, until nothing biological is left. Everyone calls it uploading; the clinic hates the word. It's routine, licensed, on the NHS, and nobody involved thinks of themselves as doing anything dramatic. My patient is Ruth, sixty-eight, a retired piano tuner; her husband Alan keeps a diary, and the clinic's technician Dev keeps the statutory log, and I want the story to alternate between those two documents. What interests me isn't the sci-fi arrival, it's the boredom — the fact that there's no moment where anyone can point at Ruth and say that's where she stopped. Dev does his job, Alan watches, Ruth says she feels absolutely fine, which is precisely what she would say if the lights had already gone out and something else were doing the talking. That, I think, is the spine: asking a person from the inside whether they are still there is the one question that cannot work.

I don't want the Ship of Theseus in a lab coat, so here are the knots I'd like the story to actually pull on. First, the path-dependence thing — reach the same end state by a sudden destructive scan-and-build and everyone calls it a death, but do it a neuron at a time and nobody will; is that a real difference or just squeamishness about the interval? Second, and this is the one I'm most keen on, the fission wrinkle: around the sixty per cent mark the team discover the remaining biological tissue and the new hardware can each be run independently, so for a fortnight there are two of her, plus a solicitor, plus a pension, plus Alan having to work out which one he's married to. Third, the logic problem underneath it all — if each individual swap preserves her, and identity is transitive, then the whole thing preserves her, so anyone who wants to deny she survived has to give up either the single step or the transitivity, and I'd like the story to make the reader feel the cost of both. And fourth, the small human thing: several patients report a seam, a fortnight of doubled will, like hearing a note and its echo; the clinic files it as a side effect, some of the staff are sure it's confabulation. I'd like Ruth to have it and Alan to be the only one who takes it seriously.

Give me roughly 2,500 words of story, then a seperate afterword of 400 to 600 words, written to me rather than to a journal. In that afterword I want you to separate what is actually settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and not to sell me a contested reading as the consensus — so if the path-dependence intuition, or the claim that a perfect copy would report exactly what Ruth reports, or the transitivity argument is a live disagreement, say so plainly and say which way the weight currently leans. Also flag where the story takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a reading a specialist would push back on. No reading lists, no citations, no bibliographies — I don't have library access this month and I'd only use them to fake depth anyway. Invent the disease, the clinic and the law; I don't want a real case with real names. British English throughout, please, and if you can keep Dev quietly religious and Alan quietly furious without ever letting either of them say so, that's the register I'm after.
