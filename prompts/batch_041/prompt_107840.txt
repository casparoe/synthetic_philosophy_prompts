Bit of an odd ask. I trade options for a living — martingales, risk-neutral measures, incomplete markets, superhedging, bid-ask spreads, the fundamental theorem of asset pricing, that's my native language — and I have basically zero philosophy training. A friend who did a philosophy degree keeps telling me the only reason my degrees of belief have to obey the probability axioms is that otherwise some clever bookie could Dutch book me, and that the same move shows I have to update by conditionalising. I'd like you to restate that whole programme in the vocabulary I actually use — prices, arbitrage, replication, spreads — instead of credences and rationality. Include the part where a rational credence doesn't have to be a single number but can be an interval or a set of them; that's the bit that sounds closest to home, since our desk quotes a bid and an ask and the width of the spread is basically our model uncertainty, and we don't believe any one of the risk-neutral measures that fit our surface.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. What survives the translation cleanly, what survives only with fudging, and what has no counterpart at all in how I think about prices. A few things I already suspect will be trouble, and I want you to be straight with me about them: arbitrage is a relation between prices, not between a person and the world; nobody says a market maker who declines to quote is being irrational; the risk-neutral measure isn't anybody's belief; and I've heard there's a result where conditioning on new evidence makes your interval of probabilities wider rather than narrower, which I can only make sense of as the spread widening on news.

And then the real question. Where my vocabulary can't express what the philosophers are saying, is that becuase it's missing something, or because the philosophy was doing less work than it sounds like it is?
