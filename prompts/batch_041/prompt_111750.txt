Write a sestina about the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the worry that the states we call pleasures (the warm bath, the solved proof, the itch finally scratched, the instant a headache stops, the laugh at a stranger's stumble) may share nothing except the name, even though a hedonist about well-being needs them to share whatever makes them good. Use the six line-ending words of the sestina as six distinct pleasure-terms, no two near-synonyms, and draw at least one of them from a case that resists the idea of a single pleasant feeling: relief from pain, pleasure taken in another's misfortune, enjoyment of something bitter or painful, an acquired taste. Let the six six-line stanzas render the pleasures from the inside, so that the reader feels how little they resemble one another, and let the form's own habit of returning the same six words in a new order do part of the argument. The three-line envoi must gather all six end-words and must either name a genuinely common element or visibly fail to; a reader who knows the debate should be able to tell which it does, and the poem should not buy a false unity with a phrase like 'all are pleasant'. Keep the poem free of metadiscussion: no naming of theories, theorists, or the problem itself.
