In the Nyāya school of classical Indian philosophy, the analysis of argument was not an appendix to the theory of knowledge but a part of it. The foundational text lists, among the sixteen things a knower must understand, both the sources of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison and reliable testimony — and the anatomy of disputation: the honest debate, the wrangling match, the bare cavil, the fallacious reason, the quibble, the futile rejoinder, and the catalogue of moves that count as defeat.

Three features of that arrangement are worth pausing over. First, dispute is nowhere counted as a source of knowledge; whatever is known at the end of an argument is known through perception, inference, comparison or testimony, so the argument itself contributes nothing epistemic. Second, the catalogue of defeats is not a list of logical errors but of dialectical ones: a disputant loses who abandons the thesis they undertook to prove, who quietly substitutes a different one, who takes the opponent's words in a forced or non-standard sense, who answers a claim with a mere similarity or a mere example, who argues in a circle, or who returns a rejoinder that is unintelligible, incoherent or beside the point. Losing here is not being wrong; it is being ruled out. Third, and most oddly, the wrangling match — the dispute conducted for victory rather than for truth — is defined by its use of precisely these devices: the quibble, the futile rejoinder and the clincher are the wrangler's weapons, the very moves that would defeat them if turned against them.

What theory of argument does this practice presuppose? What must be true of meaning, of the participants' aims, and of the standing of whoever rules on a defeat, for a rule such as 'taking the opponent's words in a forced sense is a loss' to be applicable at all? Which accounts of argument — rhetorical, procedural, agonistic, consensus-based — does the practice rule out, and which does it merely happen to fit? Is the double role of the quibble a genuine incoherence, or does it disclose a single underlying view of what arguing is? And suppose a teacher today, working from this catalogue, proposed to add a rule that a disputant who cannot restate the opponent's position in a form the opponent accepts has lost. Does that follow from the commitments already present, or does it require a further one — and if the view that there is a fact of the matter about what the opponent's words mean were abandoned, what would remain of the practice?
