Here is the practice I keep tripping over, and I want to know what it presupposes. An AI lab's honesty benchmark scores each model response on a three-point scale: a confident, correct answer gets full marks; an explicit 'I don't know' or 'I can't be sure' gets partial marks; a confident, incorrect answer gets zero; and a tentative, hedged answer that happens to be wrong is scored exactly the same as the confident wrong one. The lab averages these into a single 'honesty' or 'truthfulness' number. The same lab then trains its models on human preference data in which raters reward fluent, decisive answers over hedged ones, and its public principles say the model must never deceive its users.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of honesty and deception here, and be precise about which parts the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits well with. Does it presuppose an output-based account, on which a statement is honest just in case it is true, or a belief-based account, on which honesty is a relation between what the model says and what it 'thinks' -- and if the latter, what could the benchmark possibly be measuring, given that no one has access to the model's beliefs? Does penalizing 'I don't know' presuppose that withholding an answer is itself a form of dishonesty, or that honesty is a virtue of answers rather than of answerers? Does the whole setup presuppose that deception requires intent, and if so, whether a model can bear the relevant intent at all? And is the preference-training half committed to a different and incompatible theory from the benchmark half -- for instance, that what matters is the user's confidence in the model rather than the model's fidelity to the truth?

Then tell me what would have to change -- in the benchmark, in the training signal, or in the public principle -- if the lab adopted instead a purely output-based view of honesty, and what would change under a purely intent-based view. Finish by naming the one question about this practice that the practice itself cannot answer.
