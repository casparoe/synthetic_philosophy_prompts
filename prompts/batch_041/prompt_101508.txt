I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm stuck in the middle third of a book where four characters — a hospice nurse, a nineteen year old who just found out she's pregnant, her uncle who's a defense attorney, and a woman in her sixties who runs a crisis pregnancy center — end up in an argument about abortion at a dinner party. Every draft turns into placards. One side says life, the other says choice, and it reads like a cable news chyron instead of people talking.

What I need is a decision tree I can tape inside my laptop, a sorting tool for the positions. Not for the characters — for me. So that when I write a line of dialogue I know which fork my character has actually taken and which forks they've skipped without noticing.

Rules I want you to follow:

Build it as a tree where each branching node is a question. Phrase every node as something a person at a table could actually say, or a concrete case they could react to, in plain english, no jargon. If a node reads like a textbook heading I can't put it in anyone's mouth and it's useless to me.

Make it a real decision tree, not a list of isms. First the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then put the actual positions into the space that's left. Every leaf gets a name (even if the name is just 'the one where...') plus one sentence on the most awkward thing that position has to swallow.

If two leaves come out the same on every case in the tree, say so instead of pretending they're different. And if some combination of answers is empty — nobody stands there — tell me, and tell me whether it's because the view is incoherent, just ugly, or simply nobody's bothered to defend it.

The distinctions I care about, because my characters keep tripping on them:

1. Whether the fetus/embryo counts as a full person, a partial one, or not a person at all.
2. If it is a full person with a right to life, whether that right includes a right to use the woman's body against her will.
3. Whether saying yes to sex is saying yes to pregnancy (my attorney character will absolutley push on this one).
4. Whether the whole thing is about the act of ending the pregnancy or about the character of the person who does it — my nurse keeps turning it into a question about what kind of person you are, and I can't tell if that's a dodge or a real position.

Cases you have to use at least once, as a node or a leaf: the famous one where you wake up wired to a violinist (I know it, I just don't want the whole tree hinging on it), the one where the wrongness is supposedly about depriving someone of a valuable future, and two I'm inventing for the book — (a) a woman carrying a baby for her sister who changes her mind at twenty weeks, and (b) a pregnancy that happened after two forms of contraception both worked exactly as advertised, no mistake, no accident, just bad luck.

Roughly seven to ten nodes, eight to twelve leaves. No citations, no reading list, I don't care who said what first — I care whether a person could say it out loud without sounding like a seminar.
