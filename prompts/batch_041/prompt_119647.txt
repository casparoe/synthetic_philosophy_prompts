I'm a science journalist working up a long feature on how people talk about moral progress, and I keep bumping into the same move from my sources. The version I got last week from a philosopher I interviewed was the strongest one: morality is a circle, history is the story of the circle getting wider — family, tribe, city, nation, all of humanity, then animals, maybe one day machines — and she insisted the image isn't just a description but an argument. On her telling, the next ring is already on its way, so the only real questions are how long it takes and how much noise we make about it. I'm suspicious of that, but I want to be fair, so before I write a word I'd like you to audit the picture itself and not the politics of it.

Start with the mapping. In the circle image, what's the center, what's the radius, what is the space inside the circle, and what exactly is being added when the circle gets wider? Then tell me where the image breaks — and here I'd like you to sort my own candidates into real disanalogies versus mere quibbles. (a) A circle has to have a center, and if the center is "us" then the picture has already assumed the thing the argument is supposed to establish, namely that we're the reference point against which everything else gets measured and ranked. (b) A circle's radius grows smoothly and monotonically by definition, so the image can't represent regression, reversal, or the way that admitting new members usually costs old ones something; there's no room in a circle for a trade-off. (c) The image makes moral standing a matter of distance from the center, i.e. similarity to us, which is precisely the criterion most moral theorists dispute — on most views a creature doesn't inch closer to counting by becoming more like us, it either is or isn't the kind of thing that counts. (d) An expanding circle has one boundary, but moral concern comes in uneven layers: someone who extends concern to animals doesn't thereby extend it uniformly to everything at the same distance. Which of these is actually load-bearing — is (c) the one doing the work, or does (a) do more than people notice? And a related thing I can't get straight: is the circle genuinely growing, or are we just drawing the same boundary with a finer pen? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does my source's conclusion — that the next ring is inevitable — survive in any form, or was the picture carrying the whole argument?

I'd also like to know what you'd put in the circle's place. A colleague suggested that moral concern is less like a growing circle and more like a set of overlapping jurisdictions with contested borders and endless litigation over who's in, which I like but havent thought through. If you have a better image, say what it buys us that the circle can't. And be straight with me about the confidence: tell me which one of these objections you'd have to be wrong about for your verdict to flip, and where in your own reasoning you'd put the least weight.
