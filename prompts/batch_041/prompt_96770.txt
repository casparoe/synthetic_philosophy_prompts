I am an undergraduate in my third year of a history degree, currently taking a seminar on historiography and the philosophy of history, and our final assignment invites us to write a piece of sustained satire rather than an essay. I would like to commission that piece from you.

Please take the role of a historian who is also a skilled satirist — someone who knows Ranke's aspiration to let the sources speak and the historian vanish, Carr's argument that the historian's selection of facts is already interpretation, and White's Metahistory (1973) well enough to be funny about them without misrepresenting them. Before drafting, think the problem through: what does each of these positions actually claim, what would happen if a journal took the strongest version of one of them as a binding editorial policy, and where does the comic pressure come from? A short bulleted plan is welcome; then write the piece.

The piece is a parody of a journal's "Notes for Contributors." Invent a journal — say, the Journal of Non-Emplotative History — whose editorial board has concluded, on the strength of White's claim that historians necessarily emplot their material (as Romance, Tragedy, Comedy, or Satire) and work through one of four tropes (Metaphor, Metonymy, Synecdoche, Irony), with ideological consequences the board files under Anarchist, Radical, Conservative, or Liberal, that the only way to avoid distorting the past is to publish no plots at all. The journal therefore accepts only pure chronicle. Its masthead commits it to showing the past as it actually was, and its first rule requires the contributor to disappear from the page.

The comic engine should be the board's repeated discovery that its own rules cannot be obeyed, and its response of adding a rule, a form, or an exemption:

- Danto's point about the ideal chronicle — a perfectly exhaustive, perfectly contemporaneous record — is that it cannot contain narrative sentences, since a sentence reporting that the Thirty Years' War began in 1618 is available only to someone who already knows how things turned out. The board should run straight into this. One device I had in mind: a certification scheme under which a submission qualifies as a Grade A chronicle only if no sentence in it requires knowledge unavailable at the time of the events described, Grade B if it requires knowledge available within a year, and so on, with the effect that almost nothing can be certified.
- Carr's point that selection is itself interpretation should defeat the rules demanding total inclusion and total self-effacement. The more rigorously the board pursues the historian's disappearance, the more editorial apparatus — declarations, compliance forms, waivers — accumulates in his place.
- The trope declaration should generate at least one regress: contributors who declare Irony must also declare that the declaration is ironic.

Include a preamble, a definitions section, a numbered set of rules (including at least one that forbids causal connectives and one that forbids periodization, on the ground that a period is a plot), at least two official forms, and a worked example in which the board attempts to rewrite an ordinary historical sentence into compliant form and watches it dissolve. End with a short appendix in which the board reports the history of its own policy and is forced to concede that the report is a narrative.

Constraints: about 800 words; house-style headings and numbered sections; deadpan throughout — the board must never wink, and the absurdity must come from the rules themselves rather than from editorial commentary. A reader who has studied this debate should see that each rule is the logical extension of a real claim rather than an invented absurdity, and a reader who has not should still be able to follow. Do not cite or quote Ranke, Carr, or White; paraphrase their positions if you need them, and do not put invented sentences in their mouths. The only voice that may be quoted verbatim is the fictional journal's own.
