Alaska did something in the 70s that I keep coming back to. When the oil started flowing from the North Slope, instead of just spending the money, they amended the state constitution so that at least a quarter of the oil revenue had to go into a dedicated fund — the principal is constitutionally locked up and can't be touched, only the earnings can be spent. Then in the early eighties they started paying out a yearly dividend to every resident, equal per person, kids included, no means test. The first formula gave more to people who'd lived there longer, but the Supreme Court struck that down in Zobel v. Williams (1982) as a burden on the right to travel, so now it's just a flat per-head payment. The fund's worth tens of billions, and the dividend has bounced around from a few hundred to over three thousand dollars a year. Hammond was the governor who pushed it through, and people usually describe it as the closest thing to a real basic income that actually exists.

What I want is a Rawlsian reading of this, and then a reading of Rawls through it. Take the veil of ignorance seriously: the founders are choosing constitutional rules without knowing whether they'll be born into a generation with lots of oil or one after it's gone, whether they'll be a long-term resident or someone who moves in at forty, rich or poor, urban or rural, an oil worker or a fisherman. What would parties in something like the original position actually pick here? Does the 25% constitutional lock-in look like the just savings principle — an intergenerational savings rule — or is it something Rawls's framework doesn't really have a slot for, since his savings principle is about accumulating capital to keep just institutions going, not about a depleting natural resource?

Then the harder part: the difference principle. A flat per-capita dividend is not targeted at the worst-off at all. It gives the same dollar amount to a millionaire and to someone on disability. So is the dividend Rawlsian? Or does the difference principle actually prefer a targeted program that would leave the worst-off better off than the dividend does? And if the answer is 'it depends on the empirical comparison,' say so, and say what would have to be true for the dividend to be justified under the difference principle. Also think about who counts as the least advantaged here — residents only, or the future people who inherit an empty fund, or the people priced out of Alaska by the dividend-funded cost of living.

The four-stage sequence is interesting here too, I think: the 25% lock-in is a constitutional-stage choice, the dividend amount is a legislative-stage choice, and Zobel is the judicial stage putting limits on what the constitutional stage can even do. Does that layering help or does it just show that Rawls's stages assume a closed, settled society in a way Alaska isn't?

Finally, what does the Alaska case do to Rawls? He assumes a closed society, and Alaska is about as open as it gets; Zobel is basically a court telling the state it can't treat the veil-of-ignorance parties as if they were all born there. And Rawls treats the distribution of natural talents as morally arbitrary — oil in the ground seems like the same kind of thing at the collective level, which might push toward a resource-egalitarian view (each person owns a share of the world's resources) rather than a difference-principle view. Is the Permanent Fund a confirmation of Rawls, an anomaly, or a case he never anticipated?

Be disciplined about the mapping. Tell me which features of the case Rawls's apparatus actually explains, which it misdescribes, and where it just goes silent — dont force it to speak. No need for citations, I know the texts. I'm mostly interested in the case analysis, not a summary of Rawls.
