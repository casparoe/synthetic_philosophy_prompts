I'm assembling the questions for a long-form interview with a game theorist in the epistemic tradition — the program that reads a solution concept as a claim about what players know and believe rather than as a claim about what they choose. Her position is that equilibrium play follows from common knowledge of rationality together with common knowledge of the players' conjectures about one another, and that common knowledge itself is a limiting idealization that can be approached but never attained. I want questions that force her to do work rather than restate the program, so please press where the technical literature presses, and build in the follow-up to the reply she is most likely to give.

Write eight to ten questions, each followed by one follow-up. The set must include at least one question on each of the following:

- why common knowledge of rationality is supposed to be insufficient on its own for equilibrium play, and whether the extra assumption she adds — that the players' conjectures about one another, and not merely their rationality, are common knowledge — does substantive work or merely restates equilibrium in epistemic vocabulary;
- whether a finite agent can literally hold the infinite hierarchy of beliefs that common knowledge of an event requires, and whether redescribing those beliefs as finitely specifiable rules that generate the hierarchy answers the objection or relabels it;
- two agents who are both rational, mutually informed, and who know one another's posterior probabilities, yet go on disagreeing; and then the variant in which they share a prior but the common knowledge stops short of their posteriors. Ask which assumption she would rather give up;
- the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma in which each player assigns a small probability to the other being the sort who cooperates until defected upon. Ask whether the cooperation that results vindicates the epistemic program or shows that common knowledge of the payoffs, rather than of rationality, was carrying the result.

Two further requirements: one question must press on whether the program is descriptive or normative — what it says about real players whose reasoning visibly stops after two or three steps — and the final question must ask what result or observation would lead her to abandon the program.

Format the response as: 'Framing', no more than three sentences, stating the guest's position as I will introduce it; 'Questions', numbered, each with its follow-up directly beneath it; and 'Where to press hardest', a short closing paragraph naming the two questions most likely to force a concession and saying what that concession would be.
