I am a high school senior preparing the closing remarks for a seminar on free will and divine foreknowledge, and I would like to end with a poem rather than a summary. We have spent the term on Molinism, the view that God possesses middle knowledge: before deciding which world to create, God knows what every possible free creature would freely choose in every possible set of circumstances. These truths are usually called counterfactuals of freedom. They are supposed to be known prior to any divine decree and yet to leave the creature’s choice genuinely free. The part of the view I find most arresting is that some of these counterfactuals concern people who are never created at all: there is a fact about what they would freely have done, even though there is no them.

Please write a villanelle in the voice of one such possible person. The poem should be nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains that rhyme and repeat exactly. Let the refrains be:

Had I been born, I would have turned away.
I was not born, and yet I chose that way.

The refrains should alternate in the usual villanelle pattern and meet in the final two lines. The poem should make the speaker’s non-existence and the reality of the counterfactual both felt at once, without collapsing one into the other. It should distinguish middle knowledge from mere foreknowledge: the point is not that God looks ahead and sees what the speaker will do, but that the speaker’s free choice is already true for every possible circumstance in which they might act. It should also let the grounding objection surface: if the speaker never exists, what makes it true that they would have chosen as they did? I do not want the poem to settle that objection or turn into a sermon. I want the form itself to carry the tension, with the returning refrains doing the work that a repeated counterfactual does in the theory, and the final quatrain holding both lines together without reconciling them. The speaker should sound like a person, not a theological position, and someone who knows Molinism should be able to see the view accurately in the poem, while someone who does not should still find it a good poem.
