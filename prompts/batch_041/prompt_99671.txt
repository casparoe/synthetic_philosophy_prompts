I chair the research committee of a small independent foundation, and on Thursday we must vote on a bequest that has put us in an awkward position. The donor left us a substantial sum to endow a program of empirical research into mind and its possible survival of bodily death — telepathy, apparitions, mediumship, the class of phenomena the Society for Psychical Research has studied since 1882 — and attached a preamble explaining why she wanted the work done under our name rather than at a parapsychology institute. Her preamble leans heavily on Henry Sidgwick, who co-founded the Society for Psychical Research and served as its first president. Her argument runs roughly as follows. Sidgwick's moral theory ends in a standoff he could not resolve by argument — the dualism of practical reason, in which rational self-interest and universal benevolence are each self-evident but cannot be shown always to agree — and the one thing that would reconcile them is a moral government of the world in which virtue and happiness ultimately coincide. Since that is a factual claim about how the universe is arranged, she contends, it is not absurd to look for empirical evidence bearing on it; Sidgwick's own interest in psychical research should be read as continuous with his ethics rather than as a separate eccentricity, the same man who could not argue theism into place hoping the phenomena might supply what argument could not. She therefore wants a program conducted in the spirit the Society announced at its founding — no prejudice or prepossession, exact and unimpassioned inquiry — and she explicitly warns us against running it as a debunking operation in which a negative result is the only respectable outcome.

The committee is split three ways. Some hold that we should decline: the endowment would lend our name to a field that has produced little but fraud and wishful thinking, and a serious funder should not dignify a question for which no methodology could settle the matter. Others hold that we must accept on the donor's terms: if the question is genuinely empirical, refusing to look is the very prejudice the Society was founded to avoid, and we should not decide in advance what the evidence may show. A third group wants to accept but reframe — take the money, keep the rigor, and quietly drop the Sidgwickian rationale as window dressing for what would really be a study of anomalous experience, belief, and testimony.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — accept, decline, or accept with named conditions and safeguards — and give the reasoning that a trustee who disagrees could actually engage. It should take seriously the strongest case for whichever option it rejects. And it should address squarely whether the donor's central move is sound: whether an empirical finding could bear on a problem that is, on Sidgwick's own account, a conflict within practical reason, or whether that is a category mistake no amount of evidence could repair — and, either way, what a modern program could honestly promise.

Please say how confident you are in your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
