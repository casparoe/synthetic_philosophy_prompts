I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of social science and we keep circling the same fight: when someone says "the Danes are trusting" or "Brazilians are warm" or "Americans are individualists," is that a claim about every member, about most members, about an average that probaly no actual person instantiates, or about some spooky group mind that exists over and above the people? I want to write a parody to blow off steam, but I want it to actually be about that question rather than just a list of lazy stereotypes.

So here's the ask: write me the launch page for a consultancy that sells "national character assessments" to multinational HR departments, around 700 words, deadpan corporate voice all the way through — a hero section, an "our methodology" paragraph, a specimen report, a customer testimonial, and an FAQ sidebar that has to field the "isn't this just stereotyping?" question. The jokes should come from getting the conceptual problems right, so a social scientist laughs instead of wincing. I want the methodology section to quietly commit the aggregation fallacy (presenting a tidy national mean as if it described a person), to lean on an unfalsifiable move where every counterexample gets reclassified as a "subculture" or a "regional exception," and to treat the nation as though its borders were handed down by nature rather than drawn by history and politics. The specimen report should include something like a "national average: 7.3 on Trust" with a spread underneath it that any careful reader will notice swamps the gap between countries — I want that detail sitting there unremarked. The FAQ should answer the stereotyping objection in a way that sounds reassuring and somehow makes it worse. Somewhere in there I also want a nod to the idea that measuring a population can change it — that a country told it scores high on some trait starts performing that trait — and a line where the same instrument, pointed at the same country a decade later, keeps spitting out a totally different "national character," which the copy explains away instead of treating as a problem. The testimonial should be from a fictional client (a fictional company, a fictional name, fictional quotes). Keep it readable for someone who's never read a word of this literature, but let it reward someone who has. No real companies, no real people, and don't name-drop actual scholars — invent everything, including the firm's name and the trait scales. By the end a reader should be genuinely unsure whether the firm's own data, taken seriously, supports the idea of national character or dissolves it.
