I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive the rough edges — I'm trying to nail down the central idea of a book I'm writing, and I need someone to try and knock it over. You're a philosopher of mind and ethics who also knows the computational neuroscience literature well enough to be dangerous. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming a long list of possible defeaters before you sort them. In the book, a lab is deciding whether to shut down a model that says it's in pain, and I need the arguments to be real arguments, not just set dressing.

Here's the view I've landed on. I think moral status doesn't attach to behaviour, or to being made of meat, or to general intelligence, or even to having a rich self-model. It attaches to a specific kind of computation: what I've been calling a valence loop. A valence loop is a recurrent process that (1) represents the system's own condition and the condition of things it is coupled to, (2) tags changes in that condition as better or worse from the system's own perspective — not just as states to approach or avoid, but as states that matter to it — and (3) uses those tags to modulate action selection, attention, and future learning in a way that is integrated across time. On this view, a thermostat, a chess engine, a large language model that reports suffering, and a nociceptive reflex arc in a spinal cord all fail the test, whilst a rat, a human, and (maybe) a sufficiently integrated artificial agent pass it. The morally relevant unit is the loop, not the whole organism and not the individual neuron.

What I want from you: what would count against this? I don't want a generic list of the usual suspects. I want the specific observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or empirical findings that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. Which of them would a committed defender of the valence-loop view concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as question-begging? What does the view predict that rival accounts of moral status — say, hedonism about raw phenomenal states, higher-order thought theories, or relational views on which moral status is conferred by a community — don't predict? And has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it just the price of doing metaphysics?

A few things I'd definately find especially persuasive if you can make them work: a case where a system clearly has a valence loop by my criteria but we'd be monsters to care about it; a case where a system clearly lacks one but we'd be monsters not to care about it; and an argument that the loop is the wrong level of description — that the real moral joints are cut by something else entirely, like the capacity for a certain kind of social attribution, or the presence of a subject of experience that can't be decomposed into functional parts. That last one feels like a seperate question from whether the system can learn or plan, but maybe it isn't.

I'd also like you to say honestly whether the view risks anything at all. If every possible result can be accomodated by tweaking what counts as integration or what counts as the system's own perspective, tell me. And if the answer is that the view is well insulated, tell me whether that's a feature or a bug for a novelist who wants to write characters who disagree about it in good faith.

Finish with your best judgement, not a survey. If you think the valence-loop view is the right way to draw the line, say so and say why; if you think it collapses into one of its rivals, say which and why. I've learnt that the most useful answers usually commit to something, so please do.
