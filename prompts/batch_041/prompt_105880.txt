I chair the editorial standards board of a nonprofit regional daily. In three weeks the board votes on a staff proposal to close our anonymous comment threads and to retire the automated most-discussed module that promotes whatever is drawing the most replies; the proposal would keep the Community Voices column but require signed contributions. The trigger was a two-month pile-on against a city council member that started in the threads and, our own newsroom believes, was amplified by that module. I am inclined to support the proposal, but the engagement side of the house argues that the threads are the only place our readers who are not letter-writers get to speak, and that anonymity is precisely what protects the people with the most to lose — renters, undocumented neighbors, public employees — when they criticize local power.

I want the memo to reason with Kierkegaard's critique of the press rather than merely name-drop it: his claim that the press conjures a public that is an abstraction no one actually belongs to, so that no one is answerable for what it says; his account of leveling, in which persons are made interchangeable and the individual's responsibility dissolves into the crowd's; his worry that endless anonymous talk substitutes reflection for action and turns everyone into a spectator of a spectacle. Reason from the ideas themselves; do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. Then engage the strongest case for the other side honestly — I would rather the memo concede something real than win cheaply, and I want it to face the possibility that Kierkegaard's contempt for the crowd is exactly what a democratic paper should refuse. Also weigh the compromise the staff floated but declined to endorse, verified pseudonymity, under which readers are known to us but not to one another, and a separate member's proposal to publish a short weekly ledger naming which threads shaped which stories, so that the paper rather than an anonymous crowd owns the consequences.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Make the first point the recommendation itself, stating the concrete action and the date by which we should take it; let the remaining four carry the reasoning, the concession to the losing side, and what we should watch for if we turn out to be wrong.
