Last month my brother made me sit through Oliver Stone's JFK — the 1991 one, Kevin Costner as Jim Garrison, the New Orleans DA who put a local businessman on trial for the assassination and lost. I went in rolling my eyes and came out shaken. It's basically three hours of a man noticing that the official story doesn't add up, and by the end I honestly couldn't tell whether I'd been convinced by evidence or by editing. Then I read around a bit on how people think about conspiracy theories and came out more confused, not less.

Here's my puzzle. There seem to be two camps. One says a conspiracy theory is just a theory about a conspiracy, and that the label is a way of waving something away without engaging it — real conspiracies have happened plenty of times, so the word can't be doing the work all by itself. The other says there's something genuinely distinctive and bad about the reasoning: the theory swallows every leftover oddity, absorbs each disconfirmation, and quietly counts the missing evidence as more evidence.

What I want is a reading of the film that takes both camps seriously. Where does Garrison's method on screen look like plain good detective work — the unexplained detail, the timeline that won't sit still, the witness nobody wants to hear — and where does the film show you rather than tell you the slide into something else? Is JFK an example of the bad reasoning the second camp is pointing at, or a counterexample to it, since the film keeps cutting to real newsreel footage and real documents and still leaves you feeling the truth got buried?

Two extra things, because they're what actually naggs at me. First, a test I made up, call it the inversion test: could you make a film just as gripping, out of the same footage and the same pile of anomalies, that argues the opposite conclusion? If yes, what does that tell us about the anomalies themselves — are they evidence, or just material that any story can be built from? Second, Garrison isn't some crank in a basement; he's the state, with subpoenas and a courtroom. Does that break the usual picture of the conspiracist as a powerless outsider? And what does the film lose by making its case where reasonable doubt is the standard, instead of out in the open where truth is supposed to be the standard?

Finish by saying what a film like this can show about all this that an argument can't, and where I should stop trusting it.
