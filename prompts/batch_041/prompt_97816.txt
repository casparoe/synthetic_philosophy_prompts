A standard objection to epiphenomenalism — the view that experiences have no causal powers — is that it makes a miracle of the fact that our reports and judgments about experience ("this looks red to me") are so reliably correlated with the experiences themselves. The familiar reply is that the report and the experience share a common physical cause, so the reliability needs no causal contribution from the experience. Construct a thought experiment that isolates the feature on which this reply depends. The scenario should be realistic — an ordinary perceptual or clinical case, with no zombies, no demons, and no perfect predictors — and it should contain a single feature that can be toggled: with the feature present, the common-cause reply accounts for the reliability of phenomenal judgment; with the feature absent, the reply collapses. For each setting of the feature, walk through what the epiphenomenalist says and what an interactionist, for whom the experience itself causes the report, says; indicate which view is worse off and why; name the feature that is doing the work; and say how an epiphenomenalist would try to rescue the reply by weakening its commitments. The exercise is meant to settle whether the common-cause reply genuinely explains the reliability of phenomenal judgment or merely relocates the unexplained correlation.
