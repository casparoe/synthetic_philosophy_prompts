I am a novelist. Three weeks from now I am giving the closing keynote at a small interdisciplinary conference on narrative and machine intelligence, and I am using it to defend a thesis I arrived at while writing my current novel: that coherent extrapolated volition should be understood not as a procedure for discovering a hidden, idealized preference set but as a literary genre — the counterfactual autobiography — and that its authority, such as it is, comes from the same place a novel's authority comes from, the willingness of a reader to be changed by a voice that is recognizably theirs and yet not under their control. My argument, compressed, is this: the standard picture treats extrapolation as either the completion of a preference ordering or a move to reflective equilibrium under idealized information; both inherit the problem that the idealized agent may be a more articulate version of the same adaptive preferences rather than a better judge. I want to defend a third reading, extrapolation as narrative self-authorship, in which what is extrapolated is not a set of preferences but a capacity to be moved by reasons, and coherence is the kind of coherence a character has when their contradictions become legible as development rather than inconsistency. That is why I say the view is a genre and why I think its authority is literary rather than algorithmic. The room will hold about sixty people: a dozen alignment researchers who treat coherent extrapolated volition as an aggregation-and-learning problem and will want to know what my reading buys them technically; a dozen moral philosophers who work on idealization, consent, and adaptive preferences; and the rest literary critics and novelists who are suspicious of any master algorithm for desire but equally suspicious of my aestheticizing a political question. Prepare my Q&A sheet, not a defense. Give me the eight objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get from an online rationalist forum — ordered by how much damage they do to the thesis, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person likely to raise it. For each, give me a two-minute reply in my voice, a note on where that reply is soft, and a one-line instruction on what to concede up front. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Be committed rather than balanced: tell me which two objections are fatal if I cannot answer them, which one I should defuse by conceding its premise, and which single reply you would stake the whole talk on. If my notion of coherence is ambiguous, choose the disambiguation you would defend and say why. Do not give me reading lists, citations, or bibliography; I need the shape of the room, not the literature.
