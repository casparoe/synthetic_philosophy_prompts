I'm in a third-year seminar on death, the self, and what can be said about either, and the final assignment has a weird practical component: I actually have to deliver the thing. My great-aunt Miriam died in March at 88 and she was a card-carrying logical positivist — did her PhD on Ayer, never let anyone forget it. Her memorial is in six weeks, humanist ceremony, maybe sixty people, and I'm the one giving the remembrance. I want to write it myself but I need help getting the philosophical spine right.

Here's the setup. Miriam's position, as she'd state it after a glass of wine: the question of whether we survive death is either a confused question or a thinly disguised empirical one, and either way it doesn't do the work people want it to. If surviving means some soul-thing carrying on, she'd say nobody has ever supplied a criterion of identity for that thing — what makes the after-death person *the same person*? Memory? Character? A soul-substance? — and until you can say what would count as the same person on the other side, the claim isn't false so much as unfinished. If it just means my memories and character continue somehow, then fine, that's an empirical claim and the evidence is thin. That's roughly Ayer in *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) and after, though she'd insist it's just the verification principle doing its ordinary job rather than a special anti-religious move.

But there are two complications, and this is why I'm asking.

First: in 1988 Ayer himself had a near-death experience — his heart stopped for four minutes when he choked on a piece of smoked salmon — and he wrote it up in the Sunday Telegraph. A painful red light, some kind of grim authority figures or guardians of space and time, a river he took to be the Styx, his thoughts turning into people. Afterwards he said it had slightly weakened but not destroyed his conviction that death was the end; he stayed an atheist and treated the whole thing as most likely a dying brain doing something strange. Miriam loved that story precisely because he didn't cash it in. She used to say the interesting part wasn't the experience, it was that a man who'd spent fifty years arguing that survival-talk is meaningless had one and still refused to call it evidence. That's the bit of her I most want in the speech.

Second: the room. Half of it is Miriam's relatives — my mum's side — who are quietly religious and who will absolutely say things at the reception like she's in a better place, or she's watching over us, kindly, and I don't want to fight them. The other half are her old department colleagues, three of whom will notice instantly if I say anything she'd have called unverifiable, and one of whom — her former student, now a professor — I strongly suspect is listening for whether I'm turning her into a soft spiritualist because it's a funeral and that's what funerals do.

And then the hook, which is the real reason I'm stuck. She left a note with her will. It says she wants her memorial run like a verification test: anyone who wants to say she has survived should first state what would count as evidence against it, and if they can't, they get a minute of silence instead. It's her note, not a published text, so feel free to quote it directly or riff on it. I don't want to be cute or turn a funeral into a stunt, but I also don't want to ignore it, because it's so exactly her, and honestly it's the only frame I can think of that lets the religious relatives and the logicians sit through the same ten minutes without either side feeling handled.

So: write me the remembrance speech, about 800 words, roughly six minutes spoken. Warm, a little wry, not a lecture — she hated being lectured at and she hated being sentimentalised even more. It has to do real structural work with the Ayer material: the criterion-of-identity problem for survival, why we survive isn't straightforwardly false but unfinished or unverifiable, and an honest handling of the 1988 near-death episode (don't oversell it as a deathbed conversion, don't sneer at it either). And it has to land for the relatives who've never heard of the verification principle — I want them to come away feeling given something, not corrected. If you can make her verification-test instruction the thing that shapes the whole speech rather than a gimmick tacked onto the end, even better. No citations and no quotations from real texts, please — if you need Ayer in there at all, paraphrase him in my voice.
