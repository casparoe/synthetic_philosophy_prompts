So I'm in this reading group on divine foreknowledge and we keep going in circles on Molinism. Someone proposes a version of middle knowledge, someone else says "but that doesn't actually solve the grounding problem," someone else says "your fix just makes the counterfactuals true because God decreed them, which is Calvinism with extra steps," and we never even agree on what the theory was supposed to deliver in the first place. Before I read another defense I want the spec written down. So: give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of middle knowledge has to meet. I mean the phenomena it's got to explain (how God could know what a free creature *would* freely do in circumstances that never actually happen; that this knowledge is somehow prior to any divine decision to create, so it isn't just God reading off his own decree; that it's distinct from merely knowing what's possible), the distinctions it can't collapse (knowing what someone *could* do vs what they *would* do; counterfactuals of freedom vs ordinary counterfactuals that hold because of dispositions or laws; middle knowledge vs foreknowledge of what in fact occurs), and the commitments it can't contradict (creaturely libertarian freedom above all -- if the counterfactual is already true and God leans on it to build the world, in what sense was the creature free?). With each requirement, say what's driving it -- the case or argument that makes it a requirement rather than a prejudice.

Two things our group keeps tripping on that I want on the list if they belong there. First, counterfactuals about people who are never created: is there a fact of the matter about what a merely possible person would freely do, and if so, what makes it true? Second, whether the whole package can be stated without quietly giving God a faculty for surveying worlds that haven't been chosen -- because that's the move that always feels like cheating to me but I can't say exactly why. Then mark which of the requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just preferences of a particular camp (I suspect pre-volitionality is treated as essential by some and optional by others, but tell me if I've got that backwards). Most of all I want the part where you flag any pair or triple of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied -- if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, that's the actual result, and I want to know which single requirement does the most to rule out the most popular versions. A table is fine for that bit. Keep the scorecard of who-said-what light, I'll do that part in class.

And be upfront about your own footing here: say which of your requirements you'd drop or demote if a certain argument went through, and which consideration would most likely change your mind about which requirement is the lethal one. I'd rather have your confidence calibrated to what you can actually defend than a tidy list that pretends the hard cases aren't hard.
