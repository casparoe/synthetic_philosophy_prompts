I sit on the risk committee of a mid-size pension fund (about $8B). For the past year we've been sizing our capital buffer off a vendor model that takes one bad scenario — a coordinated cyberattack that knocks out a big chunk of the payments system for a week or two — cuts it into about a dozen sub-events, assigns each one a probability, and multiplies them together into a single annual figure. The figure comes out small, so the buffer comes out small, and the committee is set to lock that buffer in at next month's meeting. I think the number is doing work it can't support and I want to argue that in writing.

My problem isn't only the usual one that the sub-events aren't independent (they aren't — they're barely separable at all, since the whole point of that kind of attack is that the pieces move together). It's the Keynes point from the Treatise on Probability that I keep coming back to: a lot of these propositions don't have a probability we can assign at all, only a rough ranking, and even where we can put a number on something, the number isn't the thing to act on — what matters is the weight of the evidence behind it. Two propositions can carry the same probability on paper and deserve opposite treatment, because one sits on years of data and the other on a hunch. Chaining a dozen of those together and multiplying launders the ignorance into something that looks like it came out of the data.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. Write for trustees with finance backgrounds, plus our CIO and the consulting actuary who built the model and will defend it — not for a philosophy seminar, and dont lean on Keynes's name like it settles anything.

Sections and rough lengths: Recommendation (one short paragraph, bottom line first — say what we should actually do with the buffer); Why the number can't bear the weight (2-3 paragraphs, plain language, and make the two-propositions-same-probability point concrete with an example); What to use instead (2 paragraphs — I'm open to a range rule, a stress test, a floor, or something else, but be specific about how it would change the buffer); The strongest case for keeping the model (1 paragraph, the way the actuary would put it); What I'm asking the committee to decide (short, with the date). Whole thing under about 900 words.
