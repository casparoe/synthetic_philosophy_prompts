I teach an upper-level seminar called Death and the Self (14 students, 75 min, we've already done the usual Parfit stuff) and I want to run an activity that makes Ayer's line on survival actually land. Not the bumper-sticker version where logical positivists say spooky talk is meaningless -- I mean the specific thing he does in his early-60s work on personal identity, where the claim that you survive death turns out to be not false but intelligble-challenged, because there's no criterion of identity that could be applied to a disembodied survivor. I want them to feel it as a practical failure, not a slogan: they should try to adjudicate cases and discover that in some of them the question has no determinate answer, while in others it does.

Here's the setup I have in mind; tell me if it'll work and fix what won't.

The conceit is a Bureau of Continuity. Students are case officers. Their job: for each claimant who shows up after a death, decide whether the claimant is the same person as the deceased, and issue exactly one identity card to whoever is the continuation -- or, if they can defend it, two cards (they should notice how much that costs them).

Rules:
- Before seeing any cases, they have 12 minutes to write a checklist: an operational test, in the form if X and Y, then same person. It has to be something a clerk could actually apply. No we'd just know.
- Then I hand out cases one at a time. After each case they must either issue a card, refuse to issue one (with a reason), or invoke a tie-breaker they wrote into the checklist. No new checklist items mid-case unless they get a two-thirds vote to amend, and amendments cost them the case they were on.
- Each case has a 5-minute clock and they must record a verdict plus one sentence of reasoning.

Cases (I have six, tweak these):
1. Cardiac arrest, full recovery, same body, same memories, same personality. Easy.
2. Same body, total amnesia, personality somewhat changed. Does the body carry it? (Ayer's answer, I think, is yes.)
3. A new body, all the deceased's memories and mannerisms, no physical continuity.
4. A disembodied voice that reports the deceased's memories, answers questions correctly, claims to be them. No body, no location, no way to check up on it.
5. Two such claimants, identical memories, produced from the same source -- fission. Now what?
6. A being with all the memories but a genuinely different character (kind where the deceased was cruel). Is it them? If you say yes, case 4's criterion is doing no work; if no, you're letting character trump memory and you have to say what grounds it.

The bit I most want: in case 4, most groups will keep issuing a card, then discover their checklist has nothing to apply -- there's no bodily continuity, memory and character can be faked or duplicated, and there's no fact of the matter about whether the voice is really the person. That's the moment Ayer's point should hit. I'd like the debrief to make them see that the failure isn't ignorance (we could find out) but a missing criterion (there's nothing to find out).

Then the debrief. I want to map: (a) the cases where they confidently issued cards to the role of bodily continuity; (b) the cases where they wanted to say it's really them but couldn't produce a test, to Ayer's claim that such claims are not false but without sense; (c) case 5, where they either bit the bullet on two cards or smuggled in something arbitrary, to the no-branching problem and to why survival as a determinate notion is hard to state; (d) case 6 to the tension between memory and character criteria. And a final beat where I bring in the short piece Ayer wrote near the end of his life, after his heart stopped, where he describes the experience and says it left him less sure that death was the end but still not a believer. Ask them: does a vivid experience of that kind count as evidence for survival, or does it just show that a dying brain can produce experiences? And does it touch the conceptual point at all, or is it orthogonal to it? I think the interesting answer is that it's orthogonal: even if the experience is real, it doesn't give us a criterion for the identity of the survivor, so Ayer's argument (if it works) survives the experience.

Two things I need from you:
1. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how do I handle it? My guesses: some groups will refuse to take case 4 seriously and say it's incoherent from the start (which is fine, but I want them to earn it); some will write a checklist so vague it can't fail; and one or two students will keep insisting on a soul as the hidden criterion and then deny they're doing it. Tell me what I'm missing.
2. What can this activity not reproduce, that I'll have to supply in the debrief? I assume the verificationist machinery proper (the meaningfulness argument, not just the identity criterion) and the fact that Ayer himself was shaken -- I can't make them feel that, I can only tell them. Anything else?

Also: I'd rather they not hear the name Ayer until the debrief. Can the rules be arranged so the cases themselves push them there? If not, say so.
