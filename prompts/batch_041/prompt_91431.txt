I'm a novelist about two-thirds of the way through a book set in London in 1960, and the hinge of the plot is the Lady Chatterley trial — Penguin in the dock at the Old Bailey, the prosecutor asking the jury whether this is a book they'd want there wife or servants to read, the parade of literary witnesses, the acquittal. I've got the atmosphere down, but I keep worring I've got the ideas wrong. So here's what I want from you.

Take on the role of someone who knows both mid-century British legal philosophy and the social history of the period — a philosopher of law who has read the Hart–Devlin debate closely — and think carefully before you write. Start by brainstorming the possible mappings, then commit to one.

The episode: R v Penguin Books, 1960, prosecuted under the Obscene Publications Act 1959, which gave a publisher the defence that a work was for the public good on grounds of literary merit. The theory: the Hart–Devlin debate, which was triggered by the Wolfenden report's proposal to decriminalise homosexual acts between consenting adults in private. Devlin's line was that a society has a prima facie right to enforce its shared morality through the criminal law, because the loosening of that morality threatens social cohesion; Hart's was the Millian line that the mere immorality of conduct is not a sufficient reason to criminalise it, and that the law may only reach conduct that harms others.

What I want is an analysis that runs both ways. First, through the theory: who in that courtroom is playing the legal moralist and who the harm-principle liberal? Does the deprave-and-corrupt standard the prosecution had to meet count as a harm standard or as a moralism in disguise? And would Devlin's social-disintegration argument even apply to a novel — is this a different kind of case from the homosexual-offences question that started the debate? Second, back the other way: what does the trial do to the debate? Does the acquittal vindicate Hart, or does it show that the real legal question was neither pure moralism nor harm to identifiable persons but something else — literary merit as an aggregative public good — that neither Hart nor Devlin had a place for? And if that's right, what does it tell us about the harm principle's ability to handle cases where the alleged harm is the corruption of a diffuse public rather than injury to a particular person?

Reach a verdict; don't just lay out the options. And be honest about where the mapping breaks — I'd rather have the friction than a tidy fit.
