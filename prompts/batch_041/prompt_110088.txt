The question, worth 25 points, was as follows.

Two agents, A and B, share a prior probability distribution P over a finite set Ω of possible states of the world. Each agent's information is represented by a partition of Ω: if the actual state is ω, A knows only which cell of her partition contains ω, and B knows only which cell of his contains ω. Each updates by conditionalizing P on her or his information. Aumann's agreement theorem states that if it is common knowledge at ω that A's posterior probability for an event E is p and that B's posterior for E is q, then p = q. (A fact is common knowledge when both agents know it, both know that both know it, and so on without end.)

(a) Explain how the proof works, and say what each of the theorem's assumptions contributes.
(b) The theorem is often paraphrased by saying that rational agents cannot agree to disagree. Is the paraphrase accurate? State precisely what the theorem rules out and what it leaves open.
(c) A colleague of yours claims that the theorem shows that when careful, well-informed people continue to disagree, at least one of them must be reasoning badly. Assess this claim.
(d) Does the theorem have any bearing on disagreements that are at least partly evaluative — for example, about whether a particular policy is just?

The student's answer, reproduced without alteration, was this.

The theorem is best read as a constraint on what can be publicly acknowledged rather than on what can be believed. Two agents who begin from a common prior and update by conditionalizing on their private information will routinely end up with different posteriors for E; that is simply what it is for them to have seen different things. What cannot happen is that the difference itself becomes common knowledge between them.

The engine of the proof is a fact about conditional expectations. The event "A's posterior for E is p" is an event that A can identify from her own information, and conditional on it the common prior assigns probability p to E. So if that event is common knowledge — that is, if both agents know it, know that they know it, and so on — then the common prior's conditional probability of E given it is p; the same argument run for B gives q; and because the two are conditioning on the same event, p = q. The martingale property of posteriors is doing the real work.

Of the assumptions, the common prior is the substantive one. The theorem has nothing to say to agents who begin from different priors, since it is the shared prior that makes both agents' posteriors martingales with respect to one and the same expectation. The agents must also be conditionalizers, and they must have the same information: the argument goes through only when A and B are working from the same partition of the state space, which is why the result is really about agents who are epistemic peers in the strongest sense.

On the paraphrase in (b): "cannot agree to disagree" is accurate if it is read carefully. The theorem does not forbid disagreement, and it does not say that disagreement is transient or that it will dissolve once the parties talk long enough. What it forbids is one particular configuration, namely both agents knowing that their posteriors differ. Since in most real disputes each party has only a rough idea of what the other thinks, the theorem leaves the ordinary case untouched.

On (c): the colleague is broadly right, provided we read the theorem as an idealization. Agents who share a prior and conditionalize correctly cannot both know that their opinions differ. So when careful and well-informed people do continue to disagree, and when each of them is aware of the other's position, something in the idealization must be failing — and the natural diagnosis is that someone is not reasoning well. The theorem thus gives us reason to look for a defect in inference, rather than to treat persistent disagreement as a legitimate resting place.

On (d): the theorem has little to say here, and for a fairly deep reason. Aumann's framework assigns probabilities to events, and evaluative judgments are not the kind of thing that admits of a prior distribution: there is no fact of the matter about the probability that a policy is just, so there is no common prior for the two agents to share. The disagreement between a retributivist and a restorative-justice advocate is therefore not the kind of disagreement the theorem is about; it neither licenses nor forbids treating such disagreement as reasonable.

The theorem is, in the end, a conditional: given a common prior, conditionalization, and common knowledge of the posteriors, agreement follows. It is not a claim about how much disagreement there is in the world, and it does not by itself tell us what to do about it.
