ok so im helping a small foundation figure out where to put roughly three years of funding for conceptual work on the value of originals in art -- why anyone should care about the real thing versus a perfect copy, and the whole cluster of questions around that. they can fund maybe four or five people and they want philosophers, not art historians and not empirical aesthetics people running studies.

the reason this feels live right now is that generative models can already produce a painting that's visually indistinguishable from a given original, and can plausibly fabricate a provenance chain to go with it -- documents, receipts, the whole paper trail. so the old version of the puzzle, two canvases you can't tell apart, does it matter which one the master actually touched, has gotten stranger, because now the history itself can be faked, not just the surface.

here's the specific thing i want you to take seriously, because i think it reorders the field. there seem to be at least three different places the value of the original could be living: (1) in the causal history of the object, i.e. this paint was actually touched by her, in that year, in that room; (2) in some property of the object itself that we could in principle perceive, or would perceive under better conditions -- brushwork, facture, whatever it is; or (3) in a social/institutional practice, the fact that we've collectively decided to treat this one as the real one, with the museums, markets and rituals built around it. most of the literature picks one of these and argues the other two are confused. i want you to at least consider that the honest answer might be all three, for different purposes, and then ask which open questions a funder should actually be paying for.
