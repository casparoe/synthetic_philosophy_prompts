I've been re-reading GEB and I keep thinking about Borges' 'The Circular Ruins' — the one where a man in a burnt-out temple spends years dreaming another man into existence, teaches him, sends him downstream, then walks into a forest fire and finds the flames don't burn him, because he's somebody else's dream too. It's the obvious literary poster-child for what Hofstadter calls a strange loop, but I want a reading that takes Hofstadters Gödelian claim seriously rather than just going 'ooh, levels'.

The claim, roughly, is that the same self-referential trick that lets a formal system build a sentence asserting its own unprovability is what lets a mind's symbols come to symbolise the whole mind — the self as a loop that closes back on itself. So: is Borges' loop a genuine fixed point, or just an infinite regress of dreamers dressed up as a circle? In Gödel the loop is earned by arithmetisation, and the self-reference is only legible from outside the system, to us. Borges' loop is causal and genetic (A dreams B, someone dreams A), and his revelation also only lands from outside, when the fire fails to hurt. Where does the analogy hold, where does it trade on a pun, and does the ending vindicate Hofstadter or quietly refute him — the dreamer's one scrap of self-knowledge is precisely what dissolves the self. Say so if the story resists the reading, and finish with what Borges gives you that the GEB argument can't.
