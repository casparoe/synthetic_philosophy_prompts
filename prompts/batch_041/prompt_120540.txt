So I just got roped onto my housing co-op's "governance modernization" committee (long story, someone dropped out and I was the only one who didn't have a conflict that month), and now I'm somehow supposed to have an opinion on whether we should run our big budget decisions through prediction markets instead of just voting on them at the monthly meeting like normal people. I mentioned this to two friends who are both smarter than me and they immediately started going at each other, and I honestly can't tell who's making sense. I'd like you to act as a referee here — someone who actually knows mechanism design, social choice theory, prediction markets, and a bit of philosophy of measurement — and think it through carefully before committing to anything. Maybe first sketch out for yourself what the strongest version of each side is, so you're not just scoring the version they happened to say.

Here's the scheme Ravi is pushing: we all vote on a single number that stands in for what we care about — say a composite index of rent affordability, maintenance backlog, and member satisfaction — and then instead of voting on policies, we let people bet in conditional markets. A market on "the index in two years if we adopt Policy A" and a market on "the index in two years if we don't." Whichever branch the market prices as giving the higher expected index, we adopt. He says this splits the question the right way: we vote on what we want, the market tells us how to get it.

Maya thinks that's a mess. Here's roughly how it went.

R1 — Maya: the values-vs-facts split is fake. Choosing that index IS the value decision, and it's the only decision that matters, because whoever writes the index controls the outcome. Ravi: but the alternative isn't index-free, it's just that the index is implicit and set by whoever controls the agenda. At least this makes it explicit and revisable.

R2 — Maya: the conditional markets don't even resolve properly. The "if we don't adopt A" branch is a world that never happens, so what exactly are traders being paid on? And it's circular — the market's prediction is what determines whether we adopt A, which determines which branch pays out. Ravi: you fix the decision rule and the resolution date in advance; the market prices a counterfactual but you only ever settle one branch, and the circularity is fine because everyone knows the rule when they trade.

R3 — Maya: even if the plumbing works, you're paying people to be good at forecasting your index, not to make the co-op better. Thin markets are easy to push around, and they'll be thinnest exactly on the questions where being wrong is most expensive. Ravi: manipulation is expensive and self-defeating — if you bet to move the price you have to hold the position. And we can require minimum liquidity or subsidize the market.

R4 — Maya: the real killer is that the index is a proxy. Once you start optimizing it, it stops tracking what we actually cared about. Traders will correctly forecast that the index goes up while the co-op gets worse. It's Goodhart's law with a trading fee. Ravi: then the problem is the index, not the mechanism — and voting has the same failure mode when council members chase whatever number gets them reelected.

Then give me your bottom line on the mechanism itself — not just whether it's theoretically elegant, but whether a co-op with like forty members could actually use it for anything.
