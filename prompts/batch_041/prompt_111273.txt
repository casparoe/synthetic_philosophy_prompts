I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about "good enough" by going on my gut about cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is behind them.

A friend of mine house-hunts in a city she knows well. She decides in advance that she'll take the first place with a real kitchen, decent light, and rent under a number she picks, and then she does exactly that. I think that's fine, even though a slightly better place came on the market a month later.

Same friend, first week in a city she's just moved to, having seen two apartments total, takes one because it's "good enough." I think that's definately a mistake — not a sin, but she's kidding herself if she calls it a rule. Though I admit I can't say what's different, since she could have said "first decent place" both times.

A hiring manager I worked for interviewed against a bar she wrote down before the search opened, found someone who cleared it, and stopped. Fine. The same manager, after two weeks of mediocre candidates, rewrites the bar downward so that the next person counts as clearing it, and stops. That one I think is bad, and bad in a specific way — it's the rewrite I object to, not the stopping.

Someone using the 37 percent rule for hiring or dating — look at the first 37 percent, then take the next option better than all of those, commit. That gives me the willies even though I'm told it's optimal. I'd rather be the person with the bar. But then I can't explain why the second apartment bothers me and this doesn't, since the 37 percent rule is also a rule that can leave you stuck with something bad.

A woman I know stopped looking for a partner at 34 because she'd had enough and this one was enough. I think that's fine. If she'd said "whoever's around when I turn 34" that would be awful. The difference can't be the deadline, because it's the same deadline. In the first case the "enough" was about him; in the second it was about her.

A doctor stops a workup once the picture fits a common illness that responds to treatment. Fine. A researcher stops collecting data the moment p drops under .05. Not fine — and I don't think it's fine even when the effect is real, which I can't really defend.

A committee I sat on settled on a candidate nobody loved because everyone was sick of meeting. I don't think that's satisficing at all, and I'd say the same if they'd all agreed it was good enough without believing it.

The one I keep going back and forth on: a painter friend stops mixing colors once the wall looks right to her, having tried maybe nine of the forty swatches she bought. Some days that's exactly what "good enough" is for and she's the sane one in the story. Other days she's just tired of looking and calling it taste.

My hunch is that the rule is something like: stopping is OK when you could have written the stopping rule down before you started and it would still have gotten you a decent option in most of the worlds you might have landed in. I've started calling the first kind a brought bar and the second a found bar. But the hunch seems to condemn the partner case, which I don't want, and to excuse the researcher, which I do want but for what feels like the wrong reason.

So tell me what principle actually fits these — or the smallest set of candidates, if one won't do — stating each clause precisely and telling me which case motivates it. Then say which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and what it does with two cases I haven't mentioned: someone who quits a job search to take a worse-paying job they actually like, and an agency that adopts the first policy clearing its cost-benefit threshold because it has to decide by Friday.
