For years I have followed the running argument about whether omissions can be causes — the threads on causal graphs and counterfactual dependence, the recurring fight over whether failing to donate makes you a murderer, the weary reply that the lawyers settled this long ago and the philosophers are only now catching up. Before I read another account of it, I want the specification rather than the theory: the desiderata that any theory of causation by omission has to meet, each with the case or consideration that puts it on the list.

Fix the purpose this way. I want a theory good enough to do two jobs at once: to adjudicate the attributions that actually get made in medicine, law, and ordinary blame — the nurse who does not hang the drip, the parent who does not feed the child, the engineer who does not inspect the bridge, the bystander who does not throw the rope — and to tell me whether the rationalist commonplace that you are not responsible for everything you merely fail to prevent follows from the metaphysics of causation or is a separate moral doctrine quietly doing its own work. It also matters for machines: I want to know whether a system that could have intervened and did not is, in the strict sense, a cause of what followed.

So: what must such a theory deliver? At a minimum it has to (1) count at least some omissions as genuine causes, not mere failures of a necessary condition; (2) avoid profligacy, since on a naive difference-making reading every one of us is a cause of every famine we did not fund; (3) either respect the act/omission asymmetry or explain why the asymmetry is not causal at all; (4) handle causation by absence generally, not just human inaction, since we say the missing guardrail caused the crash and the absent rain caused the crop failure; (5) get the hard cases right — two lifeguards who both fail to swim out, an omission preempted or trumped by another, an omission sitting inside a longer causal process such as the builder who leaves out the footing, the difference between switching off a respirator and never switching it on, and probabilistic omissions that only raise the chance of harm; (6) say whether it is a theory of the same causal relation we apply to actions or of a second, distinct relation; and (7) state what it is a theory of, given that omissions are not events and yet turn up inside causal explanations. I may be missing requirements — add any you think belong, with the case that motivates each.

For every item, give the consideration behind it and mark whether you take it to be a genuine requirement or the prejudice of one camp. Then flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied. I expect the interesting tension to lie among the demand that omission causation be real, the demand that it not be profligate, and the demand that causal facts not depend on norms, conventions, or what we happen to single out; if that trio has to give somewhere, say which member you would sacrifice and why. Finally, name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate theories, and give a brief scorecard against the list — difference-making and counterfactual accounts, interventionist graph-based accounts, accounts that let norms or conventions do the filtering, and pluralist accounts that split the question into several. No citations and no reading list; I want the spec, not the syllabus. Prose for the criteria, a table for the scorecard.
