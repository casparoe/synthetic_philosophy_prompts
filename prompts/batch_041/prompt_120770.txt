I'm part of a small group putting together a funding bid for a philosophy-of-medicine research unit — three postdocs, three years — and I have to write the "research agenda" page this week. The umbrella topic is the old naturalism vs normativism scrap over what health and disease actually mean: whether they're descriptive biological categories, or whether value judgements are baked in from the start. I dont want us to spend three years re-litigating whether Boorse was basically right or whether every diagnosis is a social construction. I want genuinely open questions where a handful of people with the right background could make real progress in three years.

A few directions I've been kicking around — tell me if there any good, and how you'd order them:

- "Pre-disease" states: the growing pile of conditions defined by a risk number rather than any current dysfunction (pre-diabetes, "at risk" categories built off a biomarker, etc.). Does calling a presently-fine person pre-diseased commit you to something weird, and does either side of the debate handle it better?

- Stretching the concept off organisms. Do ecosystems, institutions, economies, or for that matter software and AI systems get to be healthy or diseased? Is there any principled reason the concept stops at biology, and does anything in the naturalism/normativism fight survive the stretch?

- The state vs trait distinction. A drunk person, a pregnant person (historically pathologised), someone in a cast, someone asleep, someone on dialysis, someone with a pacemaker. Which of these are unhealthy, and is that question about their biology or about what we're willing to treat as needing intervention?

- Populations vs individuals. Can a population be healthy or diseased in a sense that doesn't just reduce to the health of its members? And does the whole debate look different in veterinary or plant medicine, where the patient doesn't have values of its own to be harmed?

- The overdiagnosis/underdiagnosis symmetry. The same normativist machinery seems to license both "you're medicalising normal variation" and "you're ignoring a real problem". Is that a defect of normativism, or just what concepts like this are like?

What I need from you: rank the ones you'd actually build the unit around, and defend the ranking. Say which are upstream of which — settling X would make Y much easier — which are tractable with conceptual work rather than needing new data, and which sound juicy but wouldn't change anything downstream. Also tell me what you'd expect to see over the next couple of years that should make me move one up or down the list. If there's an obvious question in this area I've missed that beats everything above, say so. Assume we've got one clinician and one lawyer on the team and we'd like the work to matter to someone outside philosophy. No reading lists, I know the literature.
