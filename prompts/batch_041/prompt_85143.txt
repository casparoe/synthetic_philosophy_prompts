Ive been trying to actually understand Aumann's agreement theorem instead of just nodding along when people cite it, and there's a wall I keep hitting. I know the headline: two agents with the same prior, and if it's common knowledge between them what credence each assigns to some proposition, then their credences have to be equal — you can't agree to disagree. Three things I want from you.

First, the mechanism, in plain terms. Not the hand-wave that common knowledge is strong. I want to see what common knowledge buys you here that mere mutual knowledge of each other's number doesn't, and exactly where the common prior is doing work. Do it on a small case if that makes it concrete — two or three states is plenty.

Second, and this is the one that bugs me alot. Coin under a cup. I peek, you don't, and it's common knowledge that I peeked and you didn't. You're sitting at 1/2, I'm at 1 (or 0). My instict is that our disagreement is common knowledge here: we both know we disagree, we both know the other knows, and so on up. In fact it seems trivially common knowledge, since we disagree whichever way the coin landed. So either my instinct is off or the theorem is narrower than the slogan, and I'm pretty sure it's the latter. Tell me exactly which piece of common knowledge the theorem needs that this case is missing, and then tell me whether common knowledge of the bare fact of disagreement — not of the numbers — is possible at all when the priors really are shared. If it is, give me the general shape of when that happens. And if you think the cup case only works because I know the coin and you don't, say so, and tell me whether you can pull the same trick when neither of us knows.

Third, the payoff. People use the theorem to say that in any real disagreement at least one of us must be irrational, or that the sensible move is to split the difference. What's wrong with that inference? What is the theorem actually a constraint on, and what would have to be true of a live dispute between two actual people before it even applies? I'd rather come out of this knowing what the theorem does and doesn't license than knowing the proof.
