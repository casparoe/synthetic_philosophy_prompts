In my distributive justice seminar I teach the entitlement theory as the most serious challenge to patterned conceptions of justice: holdings are just when they arise from just acquisition and just transfer, and the Wilt Chamberlain case is supposed to show that any patterned principle must either be abandoned or held in place by constant interference with people's transactions. I want a candid map of the view's exposure rather than another exposition of it. What would count against the entitlement theory — which cases, intuitions, and formal or empirical findings — and which of those would its own defenders concede as counting against them? Rank them from decisive to merely awkward, and keep separate the objections that tell against the theory of justice itself and those that tell only against the further claim that it vindicates the minimal state. I am especially interested in fortunes whose value comes from what nobody transferred or earned individually — network effects, shared standards, publicly funded research — so that every transaction in the chain looks impeccable while the resulting holding is enormous. If the theory can absorb such cases only by retreating to a weaker claim that the Chamberlain example no longer supports, say so, and tell me whether that is a defect or simply what a well-behaved theory does under pressure. Reason it out rather than pointing me to the literature.
