I'm the youngest member of a stratigraphy committee — we're the group that has to recommend where to draw the base of a proposed new epoch, and after three years of this I honestly can't tell whether we have a method or just a mood. Here's what we've actually done. We rejected a proposal to put the base at the start of the industrial revolution: our own cores showed the distinctive residue layer turning up in Britain in the 1780s, in central Europe decades later, in east Asia later still, and barely at all in most of the southern hemisphere, so the line would be a different line in every place you looked. We rejected a proposal to put the base at the earliest domesticated cereals, for that same reason plus the fact that the pollen only shows up in a handful of lake basins. We approved — this is our current recommendation — a proposal that fixes the base on a chemical fallout signal that appears in ice and lake mud at every latitude within about two years of each other. We rejected a proposal that would have defined the base from a global dataset of population, GDP and land-use curves rather than a physical layer, and we told the proposers our remit is what's in the sediment, not what's in the statistics. We also rejected a proposal to define the base by the point at which the term Anthropocene entered general use, on the same grounds. And we have a standing procedural rule, which nobody has seriously challenged yet, that whatever marker we pick has to be pinned to one reference section somewhere that a future researcher can go and physically put their finger on.

Next month's item is a new one and I have no idea which way it'll go. A group wants the base fixed at the first appearance of a distinctive manufactured particle that spread worldwide within a few years of its invention and is unambiguous under a microscope. Two complications: it shows up in the record about a hundred and fifty years before anything anyone would call humans becoming a planetary force — in a stretch when, by our own earlier reasoning, our imprint on the carbon cycle and the landscape was still trivial — and it can only be detected by chemical analysis of bulk sediment, so there's no layer you can actually point at in an outcrop. Do you think we'll take it? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in which of our earlier decisions is really doing the work, what rule we're implicitly running, and whether there's a different rule that fits everything we've done just as well but decides this one the other way. My officemate says we already decided this in 2019 and I just don't remember, which I don't buy, but it's got me second-guessing myself. If our record genuinely underdetermines it, tell me which principle we'd be adopting with each choice, which of our precedents is the closest analog, and what case — some other candidate boundary — would force us to admit which of the two we've actually been using.
