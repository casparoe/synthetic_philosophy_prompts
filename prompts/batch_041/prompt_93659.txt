I keep bumping into a line that people drop like a mic-drop in arguments about blame: "you can't be responsible for who you are, because you'd have to have made yourself, and nobody made themselves." Most recently a friend used it to argue we shouldn't blame a guy who did something genuinely awful — his whole move was that since the guy didn't choose his genes or his childhood, the blame kind of evaporates. I later found out this is roughly a real argument, Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" (I think that's the name), and that people who study this have a lot more to say about it. But I want to understand the one-liner itself, not just his version, because I suspect it's smuggling in several different claims at once and my friend is grabbing whichever one he needs in the moment.

Can you seperate out the precise things "you'd have to have made yourself" could be asserting? Like: is it a claim about causation (you didn't cause your own starting materials), a claim about a regress (every reason you act on traces back to something you didn't choose, forever), a claim about what responsibility even *means* (that to be really responsible you'd have to be the ultimate source of yourself, the whole causa sui thing), or a claim that making yourself is incoherent because you'd have to exist before you existed? For each one, say whether it's trivially true, genuinely contentious, or flat-out false. And I keep hearing that this argument doesn't actually need determinism to go through — that it works even if your choices aren't caused by anything — which I don't get, so work that in too, because I think it matters for which reading is live.

Then the part I actually care about: which of these readings supports my friend's move, i.e. which one gets him all the way to "so he's not to blame at all"? My suspicion is that most of the readings only kill off some super-demanding notion of responsibility and leave ordinary blame totally intact, and that he's sliding between a strong reading and a weak one without noticing. Lay it out as a menu with a verdict on each, not one big explanation, and tell me if there's a reading where the slogan is true but basically harmless — which is what I'd bet on.
