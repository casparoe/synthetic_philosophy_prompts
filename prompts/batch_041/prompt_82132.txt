I'm in a seminar on Enlightenment ethics and we've just finished with Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness — she wrote it in the 1740s essentially for herself and it wasn't published until decades after her death, so it reads less like a treatise with a spine and more like a working notebook of advice, which I think is part of why I'm floundering. Our library only has it in French and my translation is shaky at best, so instead of pasting the passage I'll just give you my best rendering of the chunk I'm stuck on, and you can tell me where I've gone wrong. Early on she says she's writing for a fairly specific audience: people whose basic fortune is already settled. Her reasoning is that happiness is relative to your situation and to your particular constitution, age, and circumstances, so it's a mistake to exhaust yourself trying to change your station — you're better off being content in it. Then she runs through her list of what happiness needs: health, freedom from prejudice (religious prejudice especially), virtue, and tastes and passions that you actively cultivate and put to work as the engine of a continuing happiness. And then the bit that throws me: she also insists you have to be susceptible to illusions and should carefully preserve them, because illusions give us agreeable feelings and let us put some art into our lives. She goes on to say we should decide what we want to do and what we want to be, keep sad ideas — our own death, the deaths of people we love — at a distance and substitute happier ones, never repent of anything, and sow flowers along whatever road we choose. She also ranks her own three big passions quite frankly: gambling, love, and study. Love, she thinks, depends on another person and is therefore a bad bet; gambling is worth keeping in reserve for old age; study is the one that stays with you. And she has some pointed things to say about women's position being more precarious, though my French isn't good enough to be sure how far she pushes that.

Here's my first problem: I can't make the illusion requirement sit alongside the anti-prejudice requirement. Getting free of prejudice sounds like it's about seeing your situation clearly and refusing to be governed by other people's opinions, and then she turns around and tells me to cultivate illusions on purpose. Is she a rationalist making a concession to human weakness, or is the whole thing actually consistent and I'm just importing a modern assumption that clarity and comfort have to trade off? Second, I'd like help with the shape of the argument overall. She starts from something that sounds almost deterministic — your happiness is relative to your given station, your organization, your age — and then lands on something very voluntarist, where you're the master of your own happiness and should choose your path deliberately. Are those two compatible, or is there a seam she never closes, and if she does close it, what's doing the work? Third, the advice about making your passions serve your happiness doesn't obviously apply to the passions she names. Love seems like exactly the kind of thing that can't be instrumentalized and that wrecks your self-command precisely when it matters, so I want to know whether she has a story about that or whether she just changes registers when it suits her. What I'm after is a patient walkthrough: what the moves in the passage actually are, which apparent contradictions are real and which are just me misreading, how the pieces are meant to hang together as a single position, and what a sympathetic defender of her would say to each objection I've raised — plus, if you think the position has a genuine problem she doesn't have an answer to, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.
