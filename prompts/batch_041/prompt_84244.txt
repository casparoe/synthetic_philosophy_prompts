I teach an upper-level seminar in political philosophy — third and fourth years plus a couple of MA students — and I'm writing the final. It's a closed-book, 90-minute in-class essay, one question, 20 marks, on whether states can apologise for historical wrongs. The questions I've used before keep getting answered with a canned pros-and-cons list about collective responsibility, and then people either say "states can't feel remorse, so no" or "states are corporate agents, so yes," and that's it. I want something where they have to do the work instead of reciting the two positions.

So I've invented a case for them. Here it is:

THE CASE. In 2021 a national legislature passes, 214 votes to 199, a resolution reading in full: "This House records its deep regret at the suffering caused by the resettlement programme of 1957 to 1964, and acknowledges that many families lost their homes, land and livelihoods. The House affirms that this resolution creates no legal liability, obligation or cause of action against the state, and is not to be construed as an admission of unlawful conduct. The House honours the resilience of those who endured these events." The same week the state opens a memorial whose official description calls the programme "a difficult chapter in our national story." Six weeks later the state is in court defending a claim brought by the families of some of those removed, who say a land registration law dating from that period has never been repealed, that it is why their title is unregistered, and that they are still kept off the land. Some descendants of the removed want to accept the resolution; others call it an insult.

What I want back: (1) the essay question itself — one question, answerable in 90 minutes, naming maybe three or four things I want them to touch on so they don't wander off into generalities; (2) a model answer of about 600 words; (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of 20, a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong, and one sentence at the end on what seperates an A from a solid B.

Don't make them cite anyone, and don't let the marking scheme reward name-dropping — I don't want them reaching for a theorist instead of making an argument. What I actually want to test is whether they can see the thing I built the case around: that the state can't invoke continuity with the past in order to have the standing to apologise and simultaneously deny continuity in order to escape liability. If they see that, they'll also see that the ongoing land claim is what turns a partial apology into a substitution for one, and they'll have to say something about whether apology is even the right category here. Write it as if you're me setting the paper.
