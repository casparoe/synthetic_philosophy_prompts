I chair the research ethics board at a university whose psychology department has submitted a protocol on voice-hearing, built on a method its authors call heterophenomenology: interviewers record what participants say about the voices, presences, and entities they encounter, treat the transcripts strictly as data about what the participants are disposed to say, and take no position on whether any of it is real. My clinical liaison objects that many participants will be in early psychosis or acute distress, and that a script forbidding the interviewer ever to tell a participant that what she encountered was not real amounts to abandoning a duty of care in the name of method; another member adds that the method is not neutral all the way down, since it treats the participant's belief that she is being visited as a real fact about her while suspending judgment on the visitor, so the protocol misdescribes itself when it advertises neutrality. The lab replies that a debriefing declaring the voices unreal would contaminate the data, impose a contested metaphysical claim on people whose frameworks we have no standing to override, and repeat the dismissiveness that voice-hearers report as the most harmful thing clinicians do to them. The board meets Thursday and must approve, approve with conditions, or reject. Draft the decision memo I will circulate: give a concrete recommendation, with specific conditions and a time frame if you attach any; engage the strongest argument on the losing side rather than dismissing it; and be explicit about which demands the board is and is not entitled to make of researchers whose method brackets the question of what is real. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by the recommendation in a few words.
