I'm giving a talk at a law school workshop in a few weeks and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the question period — not a script for the talk itself. Heres where I'm at.

My thesis, roughly: the whole American-vs-European fight over hate speech law gets misdescribed as a fight about *how much* speech to protect. It's actually a fight about *who the guarantee is owed to*. The American model runs the free-speech guarantee to the speaker, against the state: the state stays neutral about content, and when bad speech shows up, the fix is more speech. The European model runs the guarantee to the citizenry as a whole, and part of what it guarantees is the background condition that everyone can take part as an equal — so the state isn't a neutral referee standing outside the game, it's a guarantor of the conditions of the game. That's basically the old Weimar idea of militant democracy — that a democracy is allowed to restrict the rights of people who'd use those rights to wreck the democracy — which is why European hate-speech law isn't a watered-down version of American law, it's a different animal.

Two things I want to push. First, once you see it this way, "more speech vs. less speech" is the wrong axis; the real axis is who bears the risk of error. The American says: bear the risk of letting some poison through, because the alternative is a state that gets to decide what counts as poison. The European says: bear the risk of some over-suppression, because the alternative is a permanently silenced minority. Second — and this is the part I expect to get killed on — the American ideal of "content neutrality" is itself a substantive conception of what speech is *for*, not a neutral default, so the US casting itself as the baseline and Europe as the exception is question-begging. I also want to flag the internal European critique: some people on the European side think hate-speech laws are bad for democracy precisely because they treat citizens as unable to handle bad arguments, and because they hand the state a tool that eventually gets aimed at the weak. So don't let me pretend Europe is one bloc.

The audience: it's a US law school faculty workshop. Mostly First Amendment people — doctrinalists who think in terms of content neutrality, viewpoint discrimination, overbreadth, chilling effects, the Brandenburg line, heckler's veto. A couple of comparative/constitutional folks who actually know the ECHR side, and who know the Court isn't monolithic (it's sided with speakers in some hate-speech cases too), so if I paint Europe as a single bloc they'll pounce. Maybe one legal historian. Not a theory crowd — they'll be allergic to me doing political philosophy at them, and they'll want doctrine, not vibes.
