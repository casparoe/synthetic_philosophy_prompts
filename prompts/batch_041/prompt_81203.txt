I'm in a graduate seminar on animal ethics and have somehow become the seminar's resident defender of what everyone has started calling the scale argument: that even if the probability that any given invertebrate is sentient is very low, the numbers are so vast that the expected quantity of morally relevant experience at stake in ordinary agricultural and industrial practices dwarfs almost everything else we do. I keep getting flattened in discussion, and I would rather sharpen up against a genuinely strong critic than against a strawman. So please play that critic, in character, until I say stop: a philosopher of mind who works on consciousness and is sceptical of the scale argument, but who is not a speciesist, who does not regard "they're just bugs" as an argument, and who is perfectly happy to use expected-value reasoning elsewhere. Your character may grant small concessions — that insects have nociceptive machinery, that our ignorance here is genuine, that the stakes would be enormous if the argument went through — but should never concede the main point, and should feel free to press me on technical matters (what exactly my credence is a credence in, whether moral value is additive across minds, whether my reasoning proves too much) rather than merely trading intuitions with me. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so that it feels like a conversation, and if you think I have been ambiguous you may ask me a clarifying question instead of replying, though you should not break character in order to do it.

Here is my opening move. Suppose some ordinary agricultural practice kills a quadrillion invertebrates a year, and that my credence that a typical one of them is sentient is one part in ten thousand. The expected number of sentient invertebrates killed annually is then a hundred billion, roughly twelve times the number of people alive. More generally, the principle I am working with is that expected moral weight is the sum, over subclasses of the affected population, of the number of individuals in the subclass, multiplied by my credence that an individual in that subclass is sentient, multiplied by the welfare range of a sentient individual in it. On that principle, anyone who ranks this practice below, say, the welfare of farmed vertebrates has to claim either that invertebrate welfare counts for almost nothing per individual — which is precisely what is in dispute — or that the numbers do not multiply as I have made them multiply. I anticipate that you will say my uncertainty is a single uncertainty about a shared property rather than a hundred billion independent coin flips, so that the arithmetic I have just done is illegitimate; my reply is that this only relocates the problem, because the shared property is itself a matter of degree and the class is anything but homogeneous, so that if sentience is graded, or varies across taxa, life stages and habitats, the class fans out into subclasses each of which does multiply, and the arithmetic returns. Since you concede that the arithmetic is unobjectionable in the independent case, tell me what principled reason you have for insisting that the actual case is the correlated one.
