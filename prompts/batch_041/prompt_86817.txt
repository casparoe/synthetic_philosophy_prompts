Ok, I'm writing something on precommitment -- Ulysses contracts, Elster's stuff, self-binding, that whole literature -- and I want to use the EU's Stability and Growth Pact as the central case. I know the basic facts and I don't need them explained to me: the 3% deficit limit, the 2003 episode where the Council just declined to move to sanctions against Germany and France even though everyone could see they'd breached, the no-bailout clause that was supposed to make the whole thing credible, and then 2010-2012 when the no-bailout clause got quietly walked back the moment an actual member state was on the edge. So the pact has this weird shape: it sort of held for a few years, then failed at the exact moment it was built for, and then a bunch of new binding rules got written to replace it.

What I want from you is a proper two-directional analysis. First direction: read the SGP as a Ulysses contract. Who's Odysseus here, what are the mast and the ropes actually, what are the sirens, and is this a plausible instance of the thing or just an analogy that sounds clever? Be specific about the mapping, don't be gestural about it.

Second direction is the one I care about more. Where does the analogy break, and what does the breaking tell us about the theory of precommitment rather than just about the EU? Heres my own hunch, so you can agree or push back: a Ulysses contract binds one agent across time, but the SGP binds a bunch of distinct agents to each other and then tries to bind their successors. So there's at least three things going on that the word "self-binding" smears together -- (a) one agent against its own future self, (b) many agents binding each other mutually, (c) an earlier demos binding a later demos that never agreed to any of it. And then a fourth: somebody has to actually enforce the binding, and in the EU the enforcers are the states themselves, so the sailors are tying the ropes and also voting on whether to untie them.

So: does the case confirm the precommitment framework, extend it, or show that political "precommitment" is really a different phenomenon wearing the same word? And a specific thing I keep getting stuck on -- if a binding fails precisely when the stakes are highest and defection is most tempting, does that mean it was never binding in the first place, or should we think of these devices as graded, binding-ish, rather than on/off? Is there any sense in which the SGP did work, by shaping expectations or by making defection expensive to explain, even when it didn't shape behavior? And what would a better-designed version of it look like if you took the theory seriously -- or is the honest answer that the EU case shows the theory has no good advice to give once the binder and the bound are different people?

Don't give me a literature review and don't cite anything, I just want the argument.
