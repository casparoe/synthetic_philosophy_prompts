I have been mulling over, in a strictly amateur way, a puzzle that I believe is called the money pump, and I should like you to help me make something of it. Let me set out my understanding, so that you can see what I have in mind; you may sharpen or correct any of it as you write.

The puzzle, as it reached me: a person whose preferences run in a circle — preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A — can be led, by a run of trades that she welcomes one at a time, to hand over a little money at each step and end up exactly where she began, only poorer by the total. No single trade looks like a loss to her; the loss lives only in the sequence. I am told the same trick can be worked on someone whose preferences are perfectly coherent at every instant but shift in a predictable direction over time, so that a person who is rational at each moment is still exploitable across the whole span of her life. What interests me is the gap this opens between being reasonable step by step and being reasonable all told, and the fact that someone can be made worse off without ever being made an offer she would, in the moment, refuse.

What I want from you is a piece written from inside the business of doing this to people. Please invent a firm — give it a plausible, boring name — whose trade is precisely this, and write its internal induction handbook, or its customer-service script, for new employees. The humour must come from the firm taking itself entirely seriously as a legitimate enterprise. Its staff should be able to insist, without telling a lie, that every trade it offers is voluntary, that it coerces no one, and that the customer's misfortune is the customer's own inconsistency; the comedy is in watching a respectable-sounding company reason its way to that conclusion in the blandest possible language.

I should like the handbook to include, at least: a taxonomy of clients, with the firm's own private names for the circular sort, the sort whose tastes merely drift, and the awkward 'sophisticated' customer who tries to see the whole sequence coming and refuses the first trade, together with the firm's counter-measures for that last type; a compliance section that addresses, as bloodlessly as possible, whether a run of trades each of which the customer welcomes can add up to a harm; and a short passage on the firm's duty of care, in which it explains why it does not consider itself answerable for a loss that is invisible at every single step. Toward the end, let the reasoning quietly expose the real question — whether being exploitable in this way shows a person to be irrational, or merely unlucky in the hands of someone patient enough — without ever stating that question outright.

Roughly 800 words, in numbered clauses grouped under short headings, and legible to a reader who has never heard of any of this.
