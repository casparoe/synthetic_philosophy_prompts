I write a column on ideas and public reason, and in the long argument over whether unelected judges ought to be able to strike down legislation, one analogy keeps surfacing: judging is like umpiring. Its most-quoted form is John Roberts's, from his 2005 confirmation hearing, where he likened judges to umpires who apply rules they did not write and observed that nobody goes to a ballgame to watch the umpire. In the hands of those who repeat it, the analogy has a specific job to do: it is meant to defuse the counter-majoritarian objection by showing that judicial review is not a quiet transfer of legislative power to judges but the mechanical application of a settled rulebook to a play that has already happened.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than another round of the underlying debate. Set out the correspondences explicitly, in a table if that is clearest: what plays the rulebook, the pitch, the strike zone, the umpire, the league that writes the rules, the appeal or review mechanism, the record of past calls. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do from those that are merely decorative. Pay particular attention to what the source quietly imports and the target lacks: a determinate rulebook whose application is a matter of perception rather than interpretation; an authority above the official that can amend the rules, overrule him and dismiss him; decisions that bind no future game. Say whether the conclusion the analogy is used to license survives once those are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along.

I would also like a verdict on a refinement of my own. In cricket, the ball-tracking review operates an umpire's-call convention: when the prediction is that the ball would only clip the stumps, the data are treated as inconclusive and the on-field decision stands. That is a system that institutionalises deference at the margin of measurement error, and it seems to model something the baseball version cannot — a standard of review, a threshold, a margin within which the first decision-maker's call is authoritative. Does importing that two-level structure rescue the analogy, or does it wreck it, given that the margin there is fixed by the rule-maker and arises from uncertainty about the facts rather than from contestation about the norm? And if neither version holds, say what a better analogy would be.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
