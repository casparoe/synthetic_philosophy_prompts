I run a reading group on probability and decision theory: sixteen participants, mostly upper-level undergraduates with a few graduate students, one ninety-minute session, a whiteboard, and a room where people can stand and move around. I want an activity that makes the group feel the pull of two competing ways of thinking about the question of what one should believe, given that one exists — and then feel how one of those ways runs away with you.

Here is the material I want them to feel, stated flatly so you can design against it. Reasoning about your own place in the world requires a rule for what you are a random sample of. One rule — call it the sampling rule — says: treat yourself as a randomly chosen observer drawn from the observers who actually exist. A second rule — call it the indication rule — says: treat yourself as a randomly chosen observer drawn from all possible observers, so that the bare fact that you exist is itself evidence for hypotheses that imply more observers; a hypothesis on which there are a thousand times as many observers is, on this rule, a thousand times better supported by your finding yourself here. (In the literature these two are usually called the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions.) The rules agree across most ordinary reasoning and come apart in questions about how big the world is.

The consequence I most want them to feel in their bones is the runaway one. Under the indication rule, if two theories of the cosmos fit everything you could ever observe, and one says there are vastly more observers than the other, the indication rule makes you overwhelmingly confident in the larger theory — confident enough to set aside strong empirical evidence for the smaller. The character who does this is usually called the presumptuous philosopher: he concedes that the physicists' data point to a small universe and insists he is nonetheless nearly certain it is enormous, since in a small universe he probably would not have existed. I want the group to feel why this move is seductive and why it is absurd, and to see that the entire dispute is about which rule for sampling observers one accepts.

Design the session around that. They should arrive at the presumptuous conclusion themselves, through the rules you set, before anyone names it; the labels can come in the debrief. The rules, incentives, or roles should be arranged so that the group makes the tell-tale move and then discovers what it costs.

Give me:

- the setup and the rules, in enough detail that a facilitator who has never thought about this could run it cold;
- the materials — assume nothing beyond index cards, tokens, a coin or two, and a whiteboard;
- the payoff scheme, in points or tokens rather than real money, with the stakes small but real enough that the choices carry weight;
- a timeline for the ninety minutes, marking where the reveal lands;
- the debrief, mapping particular moments in the activity onto the sampling rule, the indication rule, and the presumptuous philosopher;
- a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run, and how to handle it;
- and a frank statement of which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to supply myself from the front of the room.

Make definite choices rather than offering me a menu: commit to one setup, one payoff scheme, and one arc for the debrief, and say why you chose them. Take a position, too, on which of the two rules your activity ends up making feel more natural, and defend that judgment instead of presenting the two as equally live.
