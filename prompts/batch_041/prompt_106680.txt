I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am assembling a set of exercises on how apparently intractable disagreements can be made productive. Please draft the joint statement that a committed counterpart theorist and a committed transworld-identity theorist could both put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on de re modality — that is, on what it is for a particular individual to have a property possibly or necessarily, and on whether the very same individual can exist in more than one possible world.

Fix the two camps rather than inventing convenient versions of them. The counterpart theorist holds that individuals are world-bound, each existing in exactly one world, and that a claim such as "this thing could have been otherwise" is true when some counterpart of it — a distinct individual in another world, standing to it in a similarity relation — is otherwise; on this view the counterpart relation need be neither symmetric nor transitive nor unique, and it can shift with context. The transworld-identity theorist holds that the very same individual is present in many worlds, so that de re claims concern one individual's properties across worlds, and that at least some of an individual's properties, its origin being the standard example, are essential to it. Take both positions at their strongest; let neither win by caricature. (These are the positions usually associated with David Lewis and with Saul Kripke respectively, but do not rest anything on that attribution.)

I want the collaboration to grapple with two items I am injecting, because I do not want a reheated version of the familiar exchange.

First, a proposed third position that I suspect is either a genuine alternative or a disguised notational variant, and I want the document to settle which. Call it rigid counterpart theory: as in ordinary counterpart theory, individuals are world-bound, but the counterpart relation is stipulated to be global and context-independent and to be an equivalence relation — reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. Determine whether this is a substantive view distinct from transworld identity or merely transworld identity under a new label, and have both camps record their verdict with their reasoning.

Second, a case rather than a doctrine. Consider a particular object — a machine will do — that in the actual world was assembled from a specific set of parts in a specific order. In one other possible world there is a machine matching the actual one in material composition and causal origin but differing in what it does; in a further world there is a machine that does exactly what the actual one does but is built from different parts. The counterpart theorist must say which of these, if either, is a counterpart of the actual machine, and the answer will depend on which respects of similarity are salient; the transworld-identity theorist must say which of them, if either, is the actual machine itself. Have both camps state what de re claims hold in this case — whether the actual machine could have been built differently, and whether it could have done something else — and what is doing the work in each answer.

Deliver the result in this format: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign (about the semantics of de re claims, about what the disagreement is not about, and about which of the usual objections they jointly set aside), followed by a disagreements table. Each row of the table should give the sub-question at issue, the two positions stated in wording that each side would accept for itself, whether the row is a genuine crux or a merely verbal dispute, the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about how that case comes out. The cruxes I expect to see addressed, though you may add or drop rows if the argument warrants it, are: whether the context-sensitivity of the counterpart relation is a defect that changes the subject or a feature that tracks ordinary de re talk; whether rigid counterpart theory collapses into transworld identity; whether the necessity of identity is respected on each view; and whether the essentiality of origin is a datum to be explained or an artifact of how counterparts are selected.

Throughout, state how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of, with your reason.
