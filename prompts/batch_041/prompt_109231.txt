I teach a mixed upper-level seminar — eighteen students, roughly half of them physics graduate students and half philosophers, ninety minutes a week — on evidence and theory choice in the physical sciences, and the session I have to build next is on a topic that has become uncomfortably personal to me. I did a doctorate in theoretical cosmology before moving over to philosophy, and I left in large part because I could no longer give an honest account of why the programme I had been working in deserved my time: it was unified, it was mathematically fertile, it had no serious competitor, and it made no prediction that anyone expected to check within my working life. My students have begun asking me, politely, whether that was a decade wasted, and I have discovered that I have no criterion to hand them — only the suspicion that my confidence rested on considerations that were never empirical, and that those same considerations, applied with equal honesty, would license programmes I regard as nonsense. I would much rather they find this out than be told it. So please act as a designer of teaching games in the philosophy of science: someone who knows the live debate over non-empirical grounds for theory preference — the appeal to the absence of viable alternatives, the appeal to a theory's unexpectedly unifying a domain it was not built for, the appeal to explanatory coherence where prediction is unavailable — as well as the older literature on underdetermination and demarcation, and who also knows how to build something that actually runs in a classroom with no equipment. Before you write anything, think carefully about what would have to be true of the game's incentives for a group of students to end up defending a theory on non-empirical grounds without anyone telling them that this is the point, and give me a short sketch of that reasoning before the design itself.

The activity I want is a mock funding panel. Four teams, each acting as a review panel for a national agency whose remit I will phrase so that the safe answer is ruled out — something along the lines of maximising the probability of a transformative discovery within twenty years — with a fixed budget in tokens that they must allocate in full across the dossiers they are given. The programmes should be drawn from contemporary fundamental physics — quantum gravity, early-universe cosmology, particle theory — but described in plain language, and the students must not be able to identify the real programmes by name, so anonymise everything and give each dossier a neutral label. Make them differ in what they offer rather than in how famous they are: one that unifies domains currently lacking a common language, one whose central argument is that it has no competitor, one that is empirically respectable but theoretically dull, and — this is the part I care most about — at least one decoy whose dossier is written so that it scores well on every criterion the students will naturally reach for while being transparently worthless to anyone who asks what its central entity actually does. I also want two of the dossiers to be the same underlying programme written in two registers, one in the vocabulary of a mature and prestigious field and one in the vocabulary of a fringe one, so that we can see whether the panels rank them differently. Structure the session in rounds: an initial allocation; then the arrival of a null result that every dossier can accommodate, forcing a renewal decision; then a final round in which each panel must write down the criteria it actually used as a public rule that an oversight committee could hold it to. Give me the timeline, the materials, the exact wording of the remit, the allocation and scoring mechanics, the role assignments if you want roles, and what I should be doing as facilitator at each stage — including when to say nothing at all. The words string theory and multiverse should not be spoken until the debrief.

Then the debrief, which is the part I will lean on: a map from what happened in each round to the philosophical issue it stages, written in your own words and without a reading list — I am teaching from a handout and would rather not have citations, names, or suggestions for further reading, since anything of that sort I would have to check and the ideas stated plainly will serve me better. I want the debrief to press on why the null result failed to discriminate, on the difference between a theory that cannot be tested yet and one that cannot be tested in principle, and on the arguments that some of these programmes do make statistical or indirect predictions — the smallness of the cosmological constant being the usual example, and signatures in the cosmic microwave background or at colliders being the usual hope — so that the students do not leave with the lazy conclusion that the whole area is pseudoscience. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: which teams will game the exercise by importing their prior opinions about who the real programmes are, what I should do when a student tries to end a discussion by declaring something not to be science at all, and how to keep the philosophers from being talked over by the physicists. Finish with a frank paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce — the technical difficulty that makes the real arguments hard to assess, the decades of embedded work that give the non-empirical considerations their weight, and whatever else you think the game will flatten — and say what I will have to supply myself in the last ten minutes if the students are to get the point rather than merely enjoy the exercise.
