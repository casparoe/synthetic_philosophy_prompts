Ok so I'm a novelist, about 60k words into a book about a mid-sized factory town that automates over roughly a decade, and I'm trying to not sound like an idiot when my characters argue about it. I've been reading Capital for research (slowly — I'm not a philosopher, I just steal from one) and I keep bumping into a move I don't have a word for, and it's driving me up the wall.

Here's the pattern. Somebody says, the robots took our jobs. Somebody else says, no they didn't — look, the company that builds the robots hired a thousand people, and the laid-off guys ended up in the warehouse anyway. And that's supposed to settle it, like labor is a fluid that just flows to wherever there's a hole, so the total never drops and nobody can ever really be said to have lost their job to a machine. A friend of mine who's a Marxist says Marx already took this apart in the machinery stuff, but she gave me an argument, not a word.

What bugs me is that the second person's claim is exactly as strong and exactly as unfalsifiable as the first person's, and I don't think there's a name for their mistake. The first mistake has a name, I know — the idea that there's a fixed pile of work that machines nibble at. Fine. But what's the name for the mirror image, the assumption that the pile refills itself automatically, every time, everywhere? Is it just Say's law, or the compensation theory, or is there something narrower? Because it doesn't quite feel like those to me — it feels less like an economic doctrine and more like a conservation law, the assumption that a quantity is preserved across the whole system no matter where it moves. Like the way energy can't be destroyed, only relocated. I want to know if anyone has actually carved up that specific thing.

Second instance, same shape: the self-checkout thing at the grocery store. Store says, nobody was fired. Which may even be true — the cashiers got moved to the floor, or their hours got cut, or they quit. But notice the asymmetry: the vanished job is treated as a hard fact about the world, and the reappearing job is treated as a gift. Nobody ever has to name who specifically got reabsorbed. Is that the same fallacy wearing a hat, or is it a seperate one?

And a third thing that might just be me being thick — both arguers seem to agree the machine itself is neutral, and the only question is the arithmetic of jobs. My Marxist friend says no, the same machine does one thing under one set of owners and another under another set. Is there a word for the move of treating a technology's effects as independent of who owns it? Or is that just the first thing again?

Anyway — does the pattern have a name? And if it doesn't, please name it and tell me where its edges are. I need to know what it's *not*, so I don't put the wrong arguement in a character's mouth. Don't just hand me a label — I want the actual shape of the thing, the boundary cases, and which of the nearby concepts it's closest to.
