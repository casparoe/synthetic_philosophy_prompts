I am a control engineer by training — state-space models, setpoints and actuators, feedback and feedforward, open-loop and closed-loop operation, supervisory control, fail-safe and fail-dangerous defaults, dead man's switches — and I have recently been working on safety cases for autonomous systems, where the question keeps recurring whether the system ought to intervene or leave the plant to evolve on its own.

Philosophers attach considerable weight to a distinction between doing something and merely allowing it to happen, and in the applied literature between killing and letting die. I would like you to restate that debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Work through it term by term. What does 'doing' correspond to in a control system — an actuation, a change of setpoint, a supervisory command, a mode switch? What does 'allowing' correspond to — a null control signal, an unchanged setpoint, a plant left to its own dynamics, a deliberate failure to trip? Is there anything in my framework that marks a difference between an operator who holds an input at zero and one who never touches the system at all? Bear in mind that in my world a null command is still a command: the controller is running, the actuator is at zero, and the plant's trajectory is as much a consequence of my code as it would be of any intervention. Tell me whether that observation does any philosophical work or whether it is a triviality. Where a distinction does exist in my vocabulary — default states, fail-safe design, the requirement that an operator act in order to depart from the nominal state — say precisely what it is a distinction between, and whether it is a causal distinction or a conventional one.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' debate translate cleanly into control-theoretic terms, which survive only in a distorted form, and which cannot be said at all? I am chiefly interested in whether the residue tells against the doing/allowing distinction — that is, whether it shows that the asymmetry the philosophers want is not to be found in the causal structure of the situation, so that any asymmetry there is must come from elsewhere, or from nowhere at all — or whether it tells against my framework, because a control engineer's description of a system leaves out something that matters morally. If you judge that the distinction does survive translation, tell me what I would have to add to my vocabulary in order to express it.

Finally, say how confident you are in each of the main claims you make, and single out the one point at which you are least sure.
