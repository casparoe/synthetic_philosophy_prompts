Two people in my book club got into it after a session on rational belief, and I've been turning it over ever since. I'd like you to referee.

The case they were fighting about: your friend is a bit of a flake. He tells you the meeting got moved to Thursday. In your experience he's right about this kind of thing about 70% of the time. Going in, you were 50/50 between Wednesday and Thursday. How should you actually update?

Priya said: just conditionalize, like always. You conditionalize on the proposition that he told you it's Thursday. The 70% lives in your likelihoods — how likely he'd be to say "Thursday" if it really is Thursday, versus if it's Wednesday. No special machinery needed; ordinary Bayes handles uncertain sources fine.

Marcus said: that's dodging the question. When your evidence is genuinely uncertain you don't get to pretend you learned some proposition for certain. You shift your credence in the evidence itself and let it spread. That's the whole idea of Jeffrey conditionalization: your new credence in A is your old credence in A given E times your new credence in E, plus your old credence in A given not-E times your new credence in not-E. It updates straight on partial evidence instead of laundering it into a certainty.

Priya came back: there's always a certainty lying around — namely "Marcus's friend said it's Thursday." You know that for sure. Conditionalize on that. And besides, the Jeffrey rule is a mess. It's order-dependent: hand it two pieces of uncertain evidence and the order you feed them in changes where you end up. That can't be right — evidence is evidence, the sequence shouldn't matter. It can also move your credence in stuff that has nothing to do with the evidence.

Marcus: the order thing is overblown — feed everything in at once, or stick to one partition, and it disappears. And your move just relocates the hard part: to conditionalize on "he said it's Thursday" you need the likelihoods P(he says Thursday | it's Thursday) and P(he says Thursday | it's Wednesday), and getting those is exactly as hard as the original problem. That's why we don't want to route everything through a proposition about what he said.

Priya: fine, but the likelihoods aren't harder than anything else you're doing — you already said he's right 70% of the time, that IS a likelihood. Whereas your rule needs me to hand it a partition and new numbers for the cells, and it just takes those on faith, and then it still can't decide between the two orders.
