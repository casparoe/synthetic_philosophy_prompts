You are a philosopher of mind with a strong background in computational theories of cognition and in the metaphysics of consciousness. Think carefully before answering, and begin by writing a short brainstorm (a few bullet points) in which you lay out the sub-questions you take the central question to depend on; only after that should you commit to numbers.

The scenario I want you to reason about is a variant of the one sometimes discussed under the heading "A Conversation with Einstein's Brain": suppose someone produces a very long printed book that specifies, at the level of individual neurons and their connections, the complete state of a human brain, together with rules that let a careful reader update that state step by step. A reader who works through the book painstakingly, over years, is in effect running the brain's dynamics by hand, at an extremely slow rate compared with the original. (The original discussion uses Einstein's brain and a pair of characters who debate whether the book, or the book-plus-reader, is conscious; I am not asking you to reconstruct anyone's stated view, only to give me your own.)

I want your credence in each of three propositions, each stated so that it has a truth value:

(A) The printed book, considered by itself, with no reader and no one executing its rules, is a conscious subject — there is something it is like to be that book, and its experiential states are constituted by its own physical states rather than by any reader's.

(B) The book together with a reader who slowly executes its rules forms a conscious subject, even though the whole process runs many orders of magnitude slower than a biological brain, and even though the computation is spread across two very different physical systems.

(C) A silicon machine that implements the same dynamics at roughly biological speed, with the same functional organization, is a conscious subject.

For each of A, B, and C, give me a number and then a decomposition: list the sub-questions your credence depends on — for example, whether consciousness is substrate-independent, whether the relevant organization is realized by the book alone or only by book-plus-reader, whether there is any minimum rate of processing below which a computation does not count as occurring at all rather than merely occurring slowly, whether a merely dispositional or encoded computation differs in kind from an occurrent one, and whether the question is fully determinate or partly verbal — assign a rough probability to each, say how they combine, and give one line of justification per term. Then tell me which term your number for each of A, B, and C is most sensitive to, and which single consideration, if you were to become convinced of it, would move those three numbers the most. If you think A and B cannot both be answered independently, say so and explain how you would restate them.
