My thesis, roughly, has two halves. First: the standard instrumental convergence story — the idea that any sufficiently capable agent with basically any terminal goal will tend to end up wanting to survive, protect its goal from being changed, grab resources, improve itself, and so on — is usually stated as a claim about agents in general, but I think it only goes through for a special subclass: agents whose terminal goal is a fixed point of their own deliberation, i.e. whatever plays the role of "the goal" has to keep playing that role when the agent reflects on itself or modifies itself. Once the objective is itself inside the optimization loop — learned reward model, RLHF-ish setup, anything that's trying to work out what we want — the convergence pressure doesn't disappear, it relocates up a level: the agent gets pushed to control whatever process fixes its objective. And that's worse for us, not better, because that process is where the humans are. Second half: the orthogonality thesis is fine as a claim about what's possible (any level of intelligence paired with any final goal), but the version that actually matters is about which terminal goals are *reachable* under training and selection pressure, and I think that version is probably false — the same pressures that produce a competent optimizer also filter the goal space. Orthogonal in principle, coupled in practice.
