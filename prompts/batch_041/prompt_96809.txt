I'm a second-year psych student and I just started as an RA in a sleep lab, and something about our methods has been bugging me for weeks. Here's the setup. We bring people in overnight, wire them up for brain waves and eye movements, and when they hit REM we buzz them awake and ask "what was going through your mind right before I woke you up?" and they tell us a story and we code it against a manual that has separate boxes for "dream" and "just sleep mentation." If they don't come around fast enough, or the tech is slow getting to the room, we throw the report out or mark it degraded, on the theory that the memory is already falling apart and we're getting a worse copy. We also run a few lucid dreamers who've been trained to move their eyes in a prearranged pattern the moment they realize they're dreaming, and we watch the eye channel to confirm it, and then we have them do things inside the dream -- count to ten (one thousand and one, one thousand and two...), look at their hands, try to read a page -- and signal when they're done, so we can compare their dream clock to our wall clock. And there's an older line of work where you spray water on someone's face or play a tone while they're in REM and then check whether it turns up in the report, and we treat that as evidence about what was actually happening to them while they were out. Meanwhile "no report" gets counted as a dreamless period, even though everybody in the lab knows people forget stuff.

Here's the part I can't get past. Every single thing we do treats the report as a readout of something that really happened to the subject while they were asleep -- an experience, unfolding in real time, that the person was having and could sort of remember afterward, and that we can corrupt by waking them too slowly. But a guy in my seminar said a philosopher back in the 50s argued that dreams aren't experiences at all: nothing is going on while you sleep, you just wake up with a story and a habit of telling it in the past tense, so "I dreamed that I was flying" is closer to "I made up a story about flying" than to "I saw something." And then my roommate, who reads too much sci-fi, told me there's a version where the whole thing gets written into your memory in the second you wake up -- the dream is assembled at the moment of waking, like a file finishing its download, and it only feels like it lasted an hour. Neither of these people has ever set foot in a sleep lab.

So what I want is this: what theory of dreams does our lab's practice actually presuppose? Lay out the commitments for me -- whether dreams are experiences at all, whether they occupy real time, what a report is (a record of a past event, a reconstruction, a performance?), whether the dreamer is a subject who can notice things and make decisions while asleep, and whatever else the methods smuggle in. Then tell me which parts of the practice strictly need that theory and which parts would be fine if we gave it up -- could the cassette story, or the no-experience story, predict everything we observe, including the eye signals and the water on the face? Where is the practice underdetermined, in the sense that several rival theories would license the exact same methods? And what would actually have to change around here -- what we'd stop doing, what we'd have to redescribe -- if we took the skeptical view seriously? One more thing that looks like a contradiction to me: we treat the report as a corrupted record of a past experience (toss it if it's late, distrust it if it's vague), but the clinicians two floors down treat the very same report as a text to be interpreted, where what matters is the story and not whether it ever happened. Is that a real tension, or are those two things perfectly compatible? Write it in American English, and don't bother with citations or reading lists -- I can't look anything up anyway.
