(c) The deepest thing in that chapter isn't the river-and-dikes image but the claim that a man prospers when his fixed mode of proceeding happens to match the times and is ruined when the times turn and he can't turn with them — and that men mostly *can't* turn, because nature and long habit hold them in place. (d) Given all that, Machiavelli's own final verdict is that it's better to be impetuous than cautious, since fortune rewards the young and the bold. (e) Virtù has nothing to do with moral virtue at all — it's a functional excellence, closer to skill or effectiveness, and he's perfectly happy calling a man virtù-ous whose hands are filthy.
