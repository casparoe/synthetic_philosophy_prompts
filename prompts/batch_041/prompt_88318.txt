Ok, I've been reading too much about sortition — picking people for political jobs by lottery — and my gut verdicts don't fit together. I want the principle, or the closest thing to one, plus which of my verdicts has to go.

Here's where I actually land, case by case:

1. Jury duty, picked by lot off the register: fine. Not a regrettable compromise, the right way to do it.
2. A citizens' assembly — 500 people picked at random, paid, briefed by experts, deliberating for months on whether to change the voting system, recommendation going to a referendum: fine, and the recent Irish experiments make me think it's the best institutional idea in decades.
3. Same assembly, same procedure, but the question is wether a specific named man should be deported: not fine. Feels like a mob that's been taught manners.
4. Picking at random among the forty people who've passed a judicial competence exam and appointing one of them a judge: fine.
5. Picking one citizen at random and making them sole ruler for a day, briefing them as much as you like: not fine.
6. Coin flip between two parents, both fit, for sole custody: not fine.
7. Lottery among equally eligible patients for the single kidney: fine, better than the transplant board deciding.
8. Lottery for conscription when you only need a third of the cohort: fine, or at least I can't beat it, even though it's handing out a burden rather than a benefit and I still feel ok about it.
9. Coin flip between two sites for a new hospital when the cost-benefit analysis is a geniune dead heat: fine.
10. Coin flip between two equally good maths textbooks for the district: not fine, and I can't say why that one bugs me when the hospital one doesn't.
11. A randomly chosen panel of 500 citizens that can veto any statute, the legislature needing two thirds to override: I think this is a genuinely good idea.
12. Replacing the legislature entirely with a random 500, no elections at all: against it, which surprises me given 11 and 2.
13. Stratifying the assembly by age, sex and region so it's a miniature of the country: fine.
14. Stratifying the jury by ethnicity: uneasy, but I suspect that's only because I've heard it argued badly. Can't tell.
15. A lottery you can pay to opt out of, like a fine for skipping jury duty, applied to the assembly: not fine, and this one I'm sure about.
16. An assembly drawn only from people who volunteered for it: not fine, that's just a poll of activists.

My own rough guess at a principle is something like: the lot is legitimate when it's choosing a decider (impartiality) and when it's breaking a genuine tie (equal claims), and illegitimate when it's settling something that reasons could settle, or when what's being handed out isn't fungible. But that already looks shaky. The impartiality clause is meant to carry 1, 2, 4, 11 and maybe 13. The tie clause is meant to carry 7, 8 and 9. The "reasons could settle it" bit is meant to carry 3, 10 and 16. And "not fungible" is meant to carry 5, 6 and 12. Except 11 has the panel deciding contested questions where reasons obviously matter, and 7 has the lot settling a real person's fate, not just choosing who does the settling. So the two halves of my guess are eating each other.

What I want: the best principle, or small set of them, that keeps as many of 1–16 as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself, with a note of which clause is earning its keep on the strength of which verdict. Then tell me which verdicts I should surrender, since I'd rather lose one or two than keep something that's just a list of cases in a trenchcoat. And say what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: (a) a lottery for the order in which two equally urgent, equally survivable patients get the operating theatre tonight, and (b) a rule under which any citizen can be selected by lot to sit for three years on the central bank's rate-setting board, with the same vote as everyone else on it.
