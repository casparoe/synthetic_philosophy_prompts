I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and for the past month the reading group I co-organize has been working through contemporary illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view that the phenomenal properties we seem to be introspectively aware of (the redness of red, the sting of a headache, the felt difference between seeing and imagining) are not genuine features of our mental lives but the contents of a systematic introspective illusion, so that the real explanatory task is not to account for how such properties arise but to account for why creatures like us are built so as to seem to be aware of them. The group is deliberately mixed — two cognitive scientists, a historian of psychology, and a few of us in philosophy — and the historian has been pressing a question I have now promised to answer properly: which of the dead would have recognized this as a live option, and which would have thought it not even false? I have taken on William James, partly because his late denial that consciousness names an entity is the obvious candidate for an ancestor, and partly because his better-known insistence on the stream of thought as the primary datum, together with his diagnosis of the psychologist's fallacy, looks to me as though it might cut the other way.

What I want from you is a careful reconstruction rather than a survey of his reputation. First, how much of the illusionist package James could take on board — and here I would like the distinction kept firm between the claim that consciousness is not a special stuff or entity and the claim that the phenomenal seemings themselves are illusory. A colleague has been insisting that James was an illusionist avant la lettre, that his attack on the entity simply is the attack on the seeming; I suspect he is collapsing precisely the distinction James was most careful to draw, but I would rather be corrected than flattered. Second, which single premise of the illusionist's argument James would be most likely to reject, and what in his own commitments — his radical empiricism, his account of introspection, his pragmatism — gives him standing to reject it. Third, whether the pragmatist has any independent reason to regard the dispute as either resolvable in principle or idle in practice, since that bears directly on how I should frame the session's closing discussion. I would rather have one argued verdict with the pressure points marked than a balanced catalogue of possible readings.

Please close with a final line beginning 'Verdict:' in which, in no more than twenty-five words, you say whether James would accept or reject illusionism and name the one premise he would refuse.
