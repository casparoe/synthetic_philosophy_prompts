I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics of algorithmic decision-making. The class is 18 students, mostly philosophy and public-policy majors whose only statistics is a first course, and I want an activity that makes them feel the force of the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness from the inside — specifically the arithmetic by which, when two groups differ in base rate, a predictor cannot at once equalize positive predictive value and equalize false-positive and false-negative rates. The point I want them to reach on their own is that the argument they will instinctively want to have about "fixing the biased algorithm" has no technical fix, and that what remains is a choice about who bears the errors. They should hit the wall by trying to satisfy two auditors in succession, and they should not hear the word "impossibility," or any of the standard names for these criteria, until the debrief.

Set it in a county health department allocating a scarce preventive-treatment program. There are two catchment areas, North and South — use neutral invented labels, and have the debrief note that the labels stand in for any protected attribute. Each area's clinic has exactly 150 slots and must fill all 150, so students cannot escape by shifting volume between areas. An outside vendor's risk score ranks residents; a certificate in the materials attests that the score is calibrated within each area (in every score band, the observed rate of the outcome matches the band) and that it uses no group label and no obvious proxies. That closes off both "fix the model" and "just don't look at group membership." Give me the handout as a small binned table the students can work by hand: ten score bands per area, with counts and outcomes, and prevalence differing between areas. Check the arithmetic so that the two audits are genuinely jointly unsatisfiable for these numbers — I do not want a handout in which a clever student finds a threshold pair that satisfies both.

Run it in rounds. First the equity office requires that among those enrolled, the same share in each area actually go on to develop the condition. That round is satisfiable, and the route to satisfying it is to lower the cutoff in the lower-prevalence area. Then the civil-rights office requires that equally sick people have the same chance of being enrolled and equally healthy people the same chance of being wrongly enrolled. Now nothing works. Arrange the sequence so they exhaust the options themselves, including the tempting ones: flag nobody, flag everybody, re-engineer the vendor's score, split the difference.

Then the debrief: map what happened onto the result; make them see that the trade-off is arithmetic rather than a failure of cleverness or of the vendor's engineering; and walk through the escape routes the result does not close — changing the base rates upstream, which this activity deliberately denies them; randomizing so that a coin decides the borderline cases; and accepting unequal error burdens and arguing openly for that. Include a second, ten-minute variant in which the facilitator hands out a version with equal prevalence in both areas, so they can watch the impossibility evaporate.

Also give me a minute-by-minute timeline; a materials list with the exact tables and both auditors' questions written out verbatim for me to read aloud; an account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure; and a frank statement of what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, that a hand-computed sample table shows the impossibility as a fact about these numbers rather than as the general claim about all predictors, and that it cannot convey how much accuracy the randomized fix costs.
