I have a favor to ask of a different kind than usual. A colleague who works in decision theory but not on the foundations of probability recently gave me his summary of the Principal Principle, and I want a careful audit of it before I let it stand in a graduate seminar. His gloss runs roughly as follows: the Principal Principle is simply the claim that a rational agent's credence in a proposition ought to equal the objective chance of that proposition, so anyone who knows the chance of rain is seventy percent and still believes it at one half is just being irrational; objective chance is itself best understood as a special kind of credence, namely the credence a perfectly informed agent would hold; the principle is therefore analytic rather than substantive, a constraint on the meaning of chance rather than a discovery about rationality; the admissibility condition is a technicality added to handle cases where you also happen to know how the coin landed; and since Lewis himself conceded that the principle cannot be squared with a Humean account of chance, the whole approach collapses and the principle is best treated as a useful fiction that earns its keep without being true.

Please go claim by claim. For each, tell me what someone working on chance and credence would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification that a good popularizer might permit themselves, and what is a misrepresentation that the view's defenders would reject; and be explicit about where a claim that is fine on its own becomes false only in conjunction with another. I would also like your judgment on the concluding inference, since that is what my colleague takes to be the upshot: does the fate of the principle really turn on the Humean question in the way he suggests, or is the relationship between the two more delicate than a refutation? Close by telling me what would change your mind about the audit, and where in your own reasoning you would expect the weakest link to be.
