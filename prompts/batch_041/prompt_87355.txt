ok, bit of an odd ask. I'm a 4th-year phil PhD and I've been roped into setting the final for the upper-level decision-theory seminar I TA (prof's on sabbatical, don't ask). It's a 2-hour closed-book exam and I want exactly one essay question on Pascal's mugging, worth 30 marks, that a good student should be able to spend ~70 minutes on.

Every version I draft collapses into "state the mugging, list the standard replies — bound the utility function, use infinitesimal probabilities, use imprecise credences, or just give up on expected-value maximization — then say which one you like." My students will barf that back from memory and I'll learn nothing about whether they can actually reason. So I need the question to be in the weeds in a way that can't be recited.

Two things I want the question to force:

1. That the mugging is not just a weird counterintuitive consequence but a pincer — a two-sided version where the *same* reasoning that says "hand over your wallet for a tiny chance of astronomically large good" also says "hand over your wallet to avoid a tiny chance of astronomically large bad," and these two demands can't both be right. I want them to say why the pincer is a stronger objection than the one-sided case, and whether the usual fixes actually dissolve the pincer or only the easy version.

2. A variant I've been kicking around: the mugger comes back and the payoff isn't a huge finite number anymore, it's an actual infinity (infinitely many lives, or a literally infinite utility). I want to see them realize this breaks the "just cap the utility function" move and makes them confront what an infinitesimal probability times an infinite payoff is even supposed to mean — and whether the probability-based fixes survive here.

So please write me: (a) the question itself, with lettered parts if that helps, (b) a model answer of about 900 words, and (c) a marking scheme out of 30 that seperates a strong first from a solid 2:1 — i.e. name the specific moves a top answer has to make, the moves a merely-good answer makes, and the specific mistakes that should cost marks (including the tempting-but-wrong moves). Don't make it depend on any citations or named papers — it's closed book and they can't look anything up — and don't let it turn into a trivia quiz. I want them reasoning in front of me.

Level: mixed 3rd/4th-year undergrads plus a couple of masters students who've seen a bit of formal epistemology. Assume they know basic decision theory and expected value but nothing exotic. And please, if there's a part where the "obvious" fix definately fails, make sure that's in there so I can see who notices.
