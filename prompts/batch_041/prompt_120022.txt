I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of mind course in which we have spent the past few weeks on commissurotomy and the unity of consciousness, and I want to read Fight Club through that material. I want the reading to be honest about a disanalogy that most discussions gloss over: the Narrator's condition is dissociative identity disorder, not commissurotomy, and these are not the same phenomenon — they differ in etiology, in phenomenology, and in what they suggest about how many subjects might inhabit one body.

Here is the scaffolding I would like you to work with. First, separate at least three things that might be meant by the unity of consciousness: phenomenal unity (having a single experience), representational unity (integrating information into one model of the world), and agentive unity (there being one locus of decision and action). Second, treat the film's voiceover narration as evidence rather than mere exposition. The Narrator turns out to have been constructing a fluent, coherent, and false account of his own actions, which is structurally what the left hemisphere is said to do in split-brain patients: supply confident explanations of actions it did not initiate and whose causes it cannot access. I want to know whether that parallel is doing real work or is only a coincidence of vocabulary, and what it implies for how we should read the reveal.

With that in place, I have three questions. Which of the three unities does the film actually dramatize, and which does it merely presuppose? Does it present two subjects sharing one body, or one subject who is systematically mistaken about the boundaries of himself? And does the ending — in which the Narrator rids himself of Tyler and takes Marla's hand as the buildings come down — commit the film to an answer, and is that answer one the clinical and experimental record can support?

I care most about whether the film can do something an argument cannot. If unity of consciousness comes in degrees rather than being all or nothing, then a novel or a film might render what partial unity is like from the inside in a way that a lesion study cannot. So tell me whether Fight Club achieves that, or whether it cheats, trading on the audience's privileged hindsight to manufacture a unity of understanding that no genuinely divided mind could possess. And where the film resists the reading, say so rather than forcing the fit.
