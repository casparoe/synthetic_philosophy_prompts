Our epistemic game theory reading group keeps having the same fight, and I want a diagnosis rather than another round. The question is whether common knowledge of rationality, formalized in the standard possible-worlds way, really does force the backward-induction outcome in games like the finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma and the centipede. One side holds that the derivation is airtight once the epistemic assumptions are granted, so defecting on the last round is simply irrational; the other holds that the result proves too much, either because no realistic agents could meet the assumptions or because it goes through only if "rationality" means something more substantive than choosing what is best given your beliefs, so that the assumptions, not the players, are at fault. My own hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about the mathematics but about what the models are for, and that an unexamined question -- whether knowledge should be represented as a partition fixed in advance or as something built up over the course of reasoning -- is doing most of the work. Is that the right diagnosis, or is this a genuine disagreement about rationality that no distinction will dissolve? Give me a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually claims, say what would count as progress (a formal result, a reinterpretation of the assumptions, a change of question), and tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Write in American English.
