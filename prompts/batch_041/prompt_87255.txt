I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of mind, and we've spent the last two sessions on the old dispute about whether dreams are experiences — the exchange between Malcolm, who holds that dreaming isn't a state of consciousness at all and that the concept is tied to the waking telling of a tale, and Dennett, who holds that since the dream report is our only access to the dream we are not entitled to assume there was an experience, and who floats the possibility that the dream is assembled at the moment of waking. I've been asked to open our final session with a short piece staking out a position, and I'd rather be told where it's weak before I'm told in front of everyone. I've written a draft of about a page; I'd be grateful for comments on it. I don't need readings or citations — I'll add those myself — and I'd rather you engage with the argument as stated than with whatever the standard positions are called.

Here is the draft, close to as it stands:

Two things we call a dream. The question "are dreams experiences?" is normally treated as a single question about whether anything is undergone by the sleeper. I want to argue that it is two questions with different answers, and that the dispute looks intractable only because each party answers one of them while hearing the other. The first question is whether, during sleep, there is a subject of phenomenal states — something it is like to be the sleeper. The second is whether the thing we call the dream — the red door, the unstudied-for exam, the corridor that keeps going — exists as an object independent of the waking report. I answer yes to the first and no to the second, and I claim the asymmetry is not ad hoc. Consider what the report is answerable to. When I say I dreamt of a red door there is no procedure for checking whether the door was red; I cannot be corrected by the dream, only contradicted by myself at a later hour. Contrast my saying that a room I saw yesterday was red: the room is the standard of correctness for what I say. The dream report has no such standard, and not because our access is poor but because there is nothing on the far side of the report to be accessed. The dream-narrative, then, is constituted by the recounting rather than described by it; call this retrospective constitution. But retrospective constitution of the narrative is perfectly compatible with there having been an episode. The episode stands to the report roughly as an old injury stands to a limp: the limp does not describe the injury, and can be faked without one, and is nonetheless not for that reason unrelated to it. If that is right, the dispute dissolves in a lopsided way. Malcolm moves from the uncheckability of the report to the conclusion that there is no experience to check it against, when the premise only supports the conclusion that there is no narrative object to check it against. Dennett moves from the report being our only access to the conclusion that the experience is manufactured at the moment of waking, when the premise only supports treating the report as a trace rather than a description. Malcolm is right about the dream and wrong about dreaming; Dennett is right about dreaming and, if he means the waking assembly of the report to show that there was no episode, wrong about the dream. The interesting question is therefore not whether dreams are experiences but what a retrospective report can be evidence for, and how much of the phenomenal character we assign to an episode is contributed by the act of reporting it afterwards. The same structure recurs wherever the object is available only through later testimony — reports of experience under anaesthesia, the guesses of patients who can respond to a stimulus they deny seeing, the cries of infants, other minds generally — and in each case we are tempted either to say that there is nothing there or to say that the report is all there is, temptations that both come from confusing the object of the report with the object of the experience. Where I am least sure of myself: I have no criterion for distinguishing the cases in which a report constitutes its object from the cases in which it describes one, and I may be presupposing the very point at issue, since calling the report a trace of an episode already answers the first question in the affirmative.

What I'd like from you: first, whether I have Malcolm and Dennett roughly right, and if I've turned either into a caricature, say precisely how, stating the position in your own words rather than pointing me at a text. Second, whether retrospective constitution is a real distinction or a piece of verbal bookkeeping — if it is real, what would show that a given report constitutes rather than describes, and if there is no test, whether that is a fatal concession or merely the honest admission that the distinction is not one we apply by rule. Third, whether the anaesthesia and blindsight analogies earn their place or smuggle in a disanalogy, since in those cases the report can eventually be checked against something other than the reporter's say-so. Fourth, the strongest objection to the paper, stated as an opponent would state it, and a judgement about which of the three claims — that there is an episode, that the narrative is constituted rather than described, that the dispute is thereby dissolved — is the most vulnerable. Finally, if the last paragraph is as damaging as it reads, tell me whether it should be cut, softened, or made the opening of the piece instead of the ending.
