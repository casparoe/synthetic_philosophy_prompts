I keep getting told that big historical events have a whole pile of causes at once and you can't rank them, and I don't buy it. So build me the hardest version of that, concrete. Make one short, realistic historical episode — no fantasy, no sci-fi — where the outcome is overdetermined in the strong sense: two seperate things happen beforehand, each one would've been enough on its own to bring the outcome about, and neither one depends on the other. One of the two should be a deliberate human choice, and make it an omission — someone deciding not to act — and the other should be a slow background condition (economy, climate, demographics, take your pick). Then, on that one case, show me how three ways of deciding "what caused it" come apart: the but-for test (no cause, no outcome), the "it was bound to happen anyway" regularity view (a cause is whatever always turns up in the pattern leading there), and the hands-on view where a cause is whatever you could've reached in and fiddled with to change the outcome. I want you to actually pick which of the three gets this case right and defend the pick — don't hand me a menu and leave it to me. Then tell me which single feature of the case is really doing the work, what happens to the puzzle if I drop that feature, and how someone backing one of the other two views would argue the case doesn't count. Keep it to about a page.
