I'm a science journalist, and I'm preparing an application for a year-long residential fellowship at a university center for science and society. The fellowship buys a year of research, and the deliverable is a long reported essay, with a shorter public-facing piece to follow. The subject I want to propose is the old argument over whether disease is a natural fact or a value-laden judgment — the naturalism-versus-normativism dispute — but I don't want to propose yet another recap of it. My half-formed idea is this. The dispute stays stuck because both camps keep trying to answer a single question — is this condition really a disease? — while the word is doing at least two distinct jobs in clinical, legal, and insurance settings. The first job is to license intervention: to mark a condition as the kind of thing a physician may or should try to fix. The second is to license excuse and entitlement: to mark its bearer as not responsible for the associated incapacity, and as entitled to coverage, accommodation, or leave. I suspect the naturalist intuitions are tracking the first job and the normativist intuitions the second, and that the argument looks intractable mainly because one yes-or-no answer is being demanded of a word that answers to two different tests. I also half-notice something that may cut against my own framing: the two jobs seem to interact rather than sit side by side, since the more treatable a condition is, the stronger the case for intervening on it and the weaker its power to excuse. If that interaction is systematic, the two jobs cannot be cleanly assigned to two different kinds of facts, and my tidy diagnosis of the stalemate collapses.

Please turn this into a two-page proposal. I need: a statement of the question precise enough to have a determinate answer, together with a clear statement of what that answer looks like if my two-jobs hypothesis is right and what it looks like if the hypothesis is wrong; an account of why the standard naturalist and normativist positions leave the question open rather than settling it; the method — what I would actually do, month by month, across a year, and in particular how a reporter with no laboratory and no dataset makes progress on a conceptual question, including how the test cases would be assembled and what would count as one of them being decisive; a handful of test cases where the two jobs pull apart, including at least one that I have not mentioned above, chosen so that they remain recognizable to non-specialists; what would count as the project failing, since I would rather know the failure conditions in advance than discover them in month eleven; why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as a survey of how clinicians and insurers actually use the word, a normative argument about what the word ought to mean, or a synthesis of the existing literature; and an honest assessment of what fits into a year and what does not. Assume the readers of the proposal are not philosophers but are intelligent and skeptical, and that they will want to know why a journalist is the right person to do this and why it deserves a year of someone's money. Don't include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I'll add those myself.
