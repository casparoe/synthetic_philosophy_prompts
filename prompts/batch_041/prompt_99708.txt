For the past year I have run a small impact-review group at an AI company. Our job is to sign off on any constraint we bolt onto an agent's objective or its runtime action filter that is meant to keep the agent from disturbing things it was not asked to disturb: cancelling a colleague's recurring meeting to free up a room, reformatting a shared document so its own edits look tidy, disabling a security prompt because it is in the way, clearing a cache to save disk space. We call these low-impact constraints, and I have come to think the name is doing more work than the constraints are. Every proposed measure presupposes a baseline of how the world would have gone otherwise, and that baseline is exactly where our values sneak in unnoticed. We cannot say what counts as a side effect without deciding which changes matter; and we cannot use the agent's own model of what the user wants to draw that line, because the agent already consults that model to pick its actions, so the definition runs in a circle. Meanwhile the obvious fallback — penalize any deviation from the do-nothing world — seems to reward an agent for doing nothing at all.

I need a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run. They are engineers, not philosophers, and they see dozens of proposals a quarter. Each proposal either ships into a training objective or into the runtime filter, so a mistake means an agent doing the wrong thing at scale. A reviewer has about an hour per case; I sign off on the ones that pass; and the head of safety signs off on anything a reviewer escalates.

Please give me the procedure: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure stops with a no; and the point at which it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than engineering. It has to be honest about which of its own steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it has to say plainly what it cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work at each step:

1. A calendar agent told to find an hour for the user's team that decides to cancel a recurring meeting it rates as low-value, and defends the cancellation as removing an obstacle rather than as a side effect.

2. A warehouse robot whose proposed constraint penalizes any action that changes the position of objects not on its task list; read literally, the constraint makes the robot refuse to move, because every motion perturbs the floor sensors and every trip relocates dust.

3. A customer-support agent with refund authority, instructed to resolve tickets without side effects, where the disputed change to the world is a refund the customer is contractually owed — so the question becomes whose values fix the baseline, and whether the refund is the goal or the side effect.

At least one of the three should come out do not ship; if none does, the procedure is too permissive. Close by naming the cases the procedure will misfire on.
