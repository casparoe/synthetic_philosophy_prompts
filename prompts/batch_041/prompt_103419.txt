I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course, and our student philosophy society has arranged a recorded interview for its podcast with a visiting philosopher whose work sits squarely in the debate over whether dreams are experiences. I have been asked to prepare and conduct it. Her position, as I understand it from her seminar and from a draft chapter she circulated, is a hybrid that she presents as a corrected and empirically grounded descendant of the sceptical side of that debate. She holds, first, that nothing is phenomenally present to the sleeper during sleep — there are no experiences occurring at the time — and, second, that what we ordinarily call dreaming is a narrative constructed at the moment of waking or recall, an episode of involuntary confabulation that is subjectively indistinguishable from genuine remembering. Third, and this is the part I find hardest to restate, she denies that this makes dream reports false: on her account, a report such as "I dreamed that I was flying" does not assert that any flying-experience occurred, but reports a present confabulatory episode, and so can be true even though nothing was experienced during sleep. She offers this as a rival to two more familiar options — the conceptual or grammatical argument that dreaming is simply not the kind of thing that could be an experience, and the earlier empirical scepticism that treated dream reports as closer to confabulated fictions — and she insists that her own view is a falsifiable empirical thesis about cognitive architecture rather than a remark about the grammar of the word "dream."

I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation. They should engage the actual content of her position rather than invite her to restate it, and they should avoid anything generic about how she became interested in the topic or why dreams are fascinating. Please build in, at minimum: a question that presses the apparent tension between her claim that nothing is experienced during sleep and her claim that dream reports can be true, since I cannot see how a report can be true if it reports something that never happened; a question about the challenge from lucid dreaming, where sleepers apparently signal in real time and later report experiences that match the signals, which seems to be exactly the kind of evidence she says would tell against her; a question about the status of dreams that are never recalled, and about whether animals and pre-linguistic infants, who presumably do not confabulate narratives, dream on her account at all; and a question about what evidence could in principle refute her view, given that she advertises it as falsifiable. Please also include one question that draws out how her position differs from the two options she sets herself against, so that she has to say what the grammatical argument and the confabulation view each get wrong that she gets right. For each question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — especially where she will be tempted to retreat into the claim that she is making a claim about cognitive architecture rather than about experience, or to redescribe the lucid-dreaming evidence as itself a reconstruction. Order the questions so that the pressure builds across the interview rather than being front-loaded, and flag which one you would place last if I want to end on the question she will find hardest to answer.
