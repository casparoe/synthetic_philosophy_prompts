I am preparing a graduate seminar on value pluralism and want a diagnostic handout on whether two people with conflicting value systems can genuinely both gain by compromising. Please build a decision tree that begins from the premise that such compromise is sometimes possible and sometimes worthwhile and ends at named positions on what a moral compromise is and when it is justified. Each branching node should be a question that separates the positions without revealing where it leads — for instance whether the value of a compromise lies in the joint outcome it produces or in the relationship it expresses; whether the parties' values admit a common metric, so that trade and mutual gain are the right model, or whether they are incommensurable; whether agreement requires a party to act wrongly or merely to forgo something; whether any content is a floor below which compromise is impermissible; and whether integrity can survive the concession. Let the leaves be named views — mutual-respect compromise, bare modus vivendi, integrative or gains-from-trade bargaining, tragic compromise that leaves a remainder, dirty hands, integrity-based refusal, and the unconscionable-floor view — and use these cases as branch tests: a climate negotiator weakening a target to keep a pivotal state inside a treaty, a university taking a donor's money on condition that a controversial speaker be disinvited, and a legislator trading a vote she believes wrong for one she believes urgent. Finish by flagging any pair of positions the tree cannot separate and any combinations of answers it leaves unoccupied, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive or merely undefended.
