I'm building a problem set on what biological function actually is, and I want a case made to order rather than the swampman or the heart-pumps-blood stuff everyone's already seen a thousand times.

Invent one made-up but biologically plausable organism and give it a handful of traits, then set things up so the etiological account of function (a trait's function = what it was selected for) and the causal-role account (a trait's function = the contribution it currently makes to some capacity of the whole system) come apart in three different ways:

(1) a trait the causal-role account credits with a function but the etiological account says has none;
(2) a trait where it's the other way round — etiological yes, causal-role no;
(3) a trait both agree has a function, but they name different functions for it.

Rules: no intelligent designer, no swampman or spontaneous duplicates, no sci-fi, and nothing that just stipulates the answer — the trait histories should be ordinary evolutionary fare.

For each of the three, say what each account actually says and why, and which feature of the case is carrying the verdict. Then do the isolation move: give me two traits that make exactly the same current contribution to the organism — same effect, same magnitude, same reliability — with only their selection histories differing, so that the two accounts split on them. If that can't be done without cheating, tell me why not.

Then: how would a defender of whichever account comes off worst try to worm out of it? Take case (1) and give me the best line they've got, and tell me whether it works.

This is to show students that "function" isn't just a posh word for "effect," so the verdicts have to fall out of the two theories themselves, not be baked into the setup. Keep it tight — a page at most.
