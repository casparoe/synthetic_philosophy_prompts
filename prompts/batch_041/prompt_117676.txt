The standard division of epistemic injustice into credibility deficits and gaps in shared interpretive resources is assumed throughout. I want to pressure-test that division against a harder case: an institution — a research lab, a hospital, a newsroom; use whichever you find most instructive — that is well-intentioned by every available measure. Nobody lies, nobody consciously discounts anyone, and no formal rule treats any group differently, yet credibility still tracks social position. Below are six mechanisms I have sketched for how that could happen. The labels are mine; treat them as definitions to reason from rather than as established terms.

1. Credibility laundering. A discount that originates in something non-epistemic — status, accent, gender, in-group comfort — is publicly justified in epistemic terms: the claim was vague, uncited, "not really my field," delivered in the wrong register. The discount survives scrutiny because its stated reasons are legitimate ones.

2. Hermeneutical backlog. The vocabulary needed to describe a harm does not yet exist in the institution's shared language, and the people who experience the harm have the least standing to introduce new terms, so the gap persists even after individuals would be receptive to closing it.

3. Testimonial pre-emption. Speakers who anticipate a discount adjust what they report — hedging, omitting detail, adopting the listener's framing — so the discount never has to be applied and leaves no trace in any record. The injustice is invisible precisely because it works.

4. Second-order discount. A complaint that a discount occurred is itself discounted, as oversensitivity, self-interest, or a procedural violation. This removes the main feedback channel by which the institution could have corrected the first discount.

5. Distributed discount. No single step in a pipeline — a form, a deadline, a rubric, a meeting norm — is unreasonable, but their composition yields a large, systematic credibility deficit that no individual chose or would endorse.

6. Epistemic free-riding. The institution relies on members of a disadvantaged group to do the interpretive labor of naming and explaining the problem, then treats that labor as noise rather than contribution, so the group subsidizes the institution's self-understanding while losing standing for speaking at all.

Give each mechanism a rating from 0 to 10 for how much of the pattern it explains on its own, and a tier: 1 if it does most of the work, 2 if it is genuine but secondary, 3 if it is largely a restatement of something above it. Then rank all six from most to least explanatory. Assume the mechanisms may overlap, and hedge in a closing note if you must; the ordering itself should be definite rather than a declaration that the categories are mutually entangled.

Then answer three questions. First, which mechanism, if removed, would leave the largest explanatory gap, and which is closest to redundant given the rest — or, if none is redundant, what each contributes that the others cannot. Second, whether any of the six is not really a case of epistemic injustice at all but ordinary miscommunication or ordinary disagreement, and which one is the best candidate for that charge. Third, a single number between 0 and 1 for the share of the pattern you would attribute to cumulative structural processes rather than individual bad judgment, with a defense of that number.
