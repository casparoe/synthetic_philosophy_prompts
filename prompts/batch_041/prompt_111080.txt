Two views about rational belief under uncertainty have been in contact for roughly a century without ever settling into either agreement or refutation, and I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a survey or a verdict. The first view treats probability as degree of belief and rational choice as expected-utility maximization with respect to those degrees; on this account "how much evidence one has" is not a separate quantity at all, since evidence does its work by fixing the credences, and two agents with the same credences and the same utilities occupy exactly the same decision situation. The second view, which runs from Keynes's A Treatise on Probability through later work on partial entailment, treats probability as a logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, and holds that the weight of the argument is a further dimension alongside its probability: two agents may assign the same probability to the same hypothesis while one rests it on abundant, convergent evidence and the other on a single thin study, and the difference is supposed to bear on what each may rationally do. Keynes's own statement of the idea is that "New evidence will sometimes decrease the probability of an argument, but it will always increase its 'weight.'" The first view has won the field — it is in the textbooks, in decision theory, in economics — yet the second keeps returning, in work on ambiguity aversion, in decision rules that deliberately decline to maximize expected utility, in statistical practice when the model class itself is in doubt, and in the everyday complaint that a precise probability manufactured out of almost no information is not an estimate but a fabrication.

The case I find hardest to file is this. An agent must make a single, unrepeatable decision. She assigns probability 0.7 to the intervention's succeeding and would take the bet at those odds. She also knows that the 0.7 comes from one weak study and an analogy. A second agent, equally coherent, arrives at the same 0.7 from a large and convergent body of evidence and would take the same bet. The subjectivist says the two are in identical decision situations, and that any felt difference in how they should act is either an error or a matter of taste. The weight-of-evidence theorist says that treating them as identical just is the error — that the thinness of the first agent's evidence should license caution, and that no re-arrangement of credences and utilities can express this. Contemporary attempts to split the difference usually replace the single credence with a set of credences, or treat the agent's own estimate as itself an uncertain object, and then choose against that; I want to know whether such devices reconstruct the weight idea or quietly change the subject.

What I am asking for is a structural diagnosis, argued from what each side is actually committed to. Is this a substantive disagreement about what rationality requires? A verbal dispute about whether "probability" names one thing at all? Two groups answering different questions — one about the logic of partial belief relative to a body of evidence, the other about how an agent should act when the relevant quantities must be estimated rather than read off? Or a disagreement about what a theory of rational choice is for: a theory of what to do, or a theory of what one is entitled to do? My own hypothesis, held loosely, is the last of these — that the two sides are not really disputing probability but disputing whether the object of rational assessment is the act or the agent's grounds for it. Test that against what each side says and tell me whether it survives. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a representation theorem showing the weight dimension to be redundant given something else, a decision problem on which the two accounts diverge and one is plainly wrong, an experiment, a distinction, or the admission that they were never answering the same question.
