I am taking a final-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and our written assignment asks us to construct a case rather than survey the literature, so I would like you to build one to my specifications.

What I need is a single case that presses on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. As I understand it, the argument runs: you do what you do, in any situation, because of the way you are; to be ultimately responsible for what you do you would have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are, at least in certain crucial mental respects; but you cannot be ultimately responsible for the way you are; so you cannot be ultimately responsible for what you do, and the desert-entailing blame that requires ultimate responsibility is never warranted. Most cases in this area are built to make the sceptic's conclusion look plausible. I want the opposite: an agent who acts wrongly and whom we judge fully responsible in the strongest, desert-entailing sense, while the case itself concedes that she is not responsible for the aspect of herself from which the act flows. The whole interest lies in whether the second concession can be made without the first collapsing.

Please respect the following constraints.

The case must be realistic and contemporary, no longer than a paragraph, and free of science fiction: no neurotechnology, no counterfactual interveners, no hypothetical neuroscientists, no brainwashing, coercion, pathology, or severe deprivation. The trait in question should be a central, reason-giving part of her outlook, a commitment or a trained capacity for judgment, not a peripheral habit, and she must have acquired it through ordinary participation in something socially valued, such as a trade, a profession, a faith, a family, or a linguistic community. It should be an unremarkable formation, not a damaged one. The act must be genuinely wrong, its wrongness must be transparent to her at the time, and it must issue from that trait through an ordinary stretch of practical reasoning rather than through a lapse, a mistake of fact, or an overpowering urge. Nothing may turn on whether determinism is true. The case must establish her non-responsibility for the trait rather than assert it: show where the trait came from, show that she never chose or endorsed it, and show that there was no point at which she could have been responsible for having it. She should be recognisably ordinary rather than monstrous, her act serious enough that a real verdict is at stake, and the case must not be defused by a hidden fact you withhold and then reveal.

Once the case is written, please do the following. First, walk it through the argument step by step, saying which step the case makes plainly true, which step a compatibilist must deny, and what she must put in its place, and making clear whether the verdict of responsibility is generated by the case or merely stipulated by it. Second, name the one feature doing the work, and demonstrate that it is doing the work by describing the single smallest change to the case that reverses the verdict, holding fixed the wrongness of the act, her knowledge at the time, and her causal history up to the moment of action. One change only, with an explanation of why it is the difference-maker. Third, give Strawson's strongest reply, distinguishing in particular the reply that our verdict is an illusion manufactured by our ordinary ignorance of causes from the reply that we have secured only a thinner, non-desert notion of responsibility, and say which of the two the case actually threatens. Fourth, say honestly whether the case survives that reply. If it does not, name the exact step at which it breaks, and do not repair it by stipulating the verdict or by smuggling in a moment of self-creation; a diagnosed failure is worth more to me than a patched case.

I do not need citations or reading suggestions. Everything should be in the case and the reasoning.
