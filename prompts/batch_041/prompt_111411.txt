I spent thirty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and I have spent the four years since doing what I always promised myself I would: reading philosophy seriously. For the last two years I have hosted a small interview series for an audience of scientists and engineers who came to the subject the way I did — late, and with more appetite than training. My next guest is a philosopher who defends an anti-realist conception of truth, on which a statement's truth cannot outrun our capacity in principle to recognize it, and who therefore wants to keep the thesis that every truth is knowable. I have an hour with her and I would like to arrive with questions rather than with a recitation of her position.

The difficulty she has to navigate, as I understand it, is this. Take some truth that in fact nobody knows, and form the compound claim that it is true and that nobody knows it. That compound is true. If every truth is knowable, the compound must be knowable; but to know a conjunction is to know each of its parts, and since knowledge implies truth, knowing the second part would require that nobody knows the first, while knowing the first would require that somebody does. So the compound cannot be known. By the knowability thesis it therefore cannot be true either — and since the truth we started from was arbitrary, there are no unknown truths. I am told the derivation survives even in intuitionistic logic, the one classical step being the last, which turns the absence of unknown truths into the claim that every truth is known. The literature calls this the knowability paradox.

Her line is that the knowability thesis should be restricted so that it covers only those propositions which it is not self-defeating to suppose known, and not propositions that embed our own ignorance of them. That strikes me as precisely the sort of move a physicist makes when a no-go theorem threatens a favoured principle — either identify the guilty premise or restrict the principle's scope — and I want the conversation to establish which of those two she is doing.

Please draft eight to ten questions. I want at least one that forces her to state the restriction precisely and to say what independent motivation it has, so that it does not look like a patch applied to save the thesis; one on what the restriction costs her, in particular whether conceding that some truths are unknowable in that sense surrenders what anti-realism was supposed to secure; one that puts the rival escapes to her — denying that knowledge distributes over conjunction, and denying that the knowability thesis applies at all to propositions of this ignorance-embedding kind — and asks why she does not take them; one on whether the same argument template collapses every other candidate operator, so that whatever is true must be verifiable, provable, or foreseeable, and what that would mean for her view; and one that asks what would persuade her to abandon the knowability thesis outright rather than restrict it. For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

I do not need a reading list. What I do need is a note on how confident you are in each question — confident, that is, that it names a live point of pressure in this literature rather than something I have half-remembered from a blog post — and an identification of the single question you are least sure of, along with an indication of how she might answer it in a way that shows the question misfires.
