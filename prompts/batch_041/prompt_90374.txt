I am a third-year student in a philosophy of psychiatry seminar, and I am putting together a mock final for our study group. We have spent several weeks on Hacking's account of psychiatric classification and looping effects, including his contrast between kinds that are indifferent to how they are classified and kinds whose members can become aware of the classification and change in response to it. I need one essay question that a student could answer in sixty minutes and that cannot be passed by restating that contrast.

Build the question around the following hypothetical. A new diagnostic category is proposed for a condition defined by intolerance of unstructured social time. It is applied simultaneously to adults who seek out the diagnosis and read about it, and to young children who are diagnosed by parents and clinicians and who cannot understand the category. The adults report that the diagnosis reorganizes how they read their own past and alters what they do; the children do not. Yet clinicians observe that the diagnosis changes how parents treat the children, which changes the children's behaviour, which in turn feeds back into how clinicians apply the diagnosis to the next cohort.

The question should ask the student to decide whether this is a looping effect at all on Hacking's account, whether the route through parents and clinicians counts as a loop or as something else, and what the case shows about whether looping requires the classified person's own uptake. It should also ask what this does to the claim that looping is what makes human kinds distinctive. The question should force a position rather than invite a survey.

Write the essay question, a model answer of about six hundred words, and a marking scheme worth twenty marks. The marking scheme should name five must-hit points with the marks each is worth and, for each, a one-line note on the mistake that most often costs students the marks. The model answer should commit to a definite verdict, and the marking scheme should give full credit to a student who argues against that verdict provided the argument is sound.
