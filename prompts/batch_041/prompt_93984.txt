I am assembling an anthology of poems that think, and I need one on Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness. Write a sestina in her voice — not a biography in verse, but a poem that could have been written at the desk where she kept the private discourse on happiness she never published, addressed to a younger lover or friend who has asked her how to live.

Her view, as I understand it, runs roughly so. The Stoic demand that we extirpate the passions is a mistake, because the passions are the very stuff of a happy life; the task is not to kill them but to make them serve our happiness. But passions age differently. Love, gambling, the things that seize us, fade or turn ruinous; what lasts, and what we must cultivate to replace them, is taste — above all the taste for study, which can fill the last years when the others have gone. Illusion is not the enemy of happiness but one of its instruments: we should keep the illusions that give us agreeable feeling, put art into our lives, and refuse the philosopher's demand that we see through everything. We should banish the thought of death without pretending it is not true. And all of this is work we do within a fortune, a body, and a sex we did not choose; she writes for those whose circumstances are settled enough to allow the project, and she knows that many are not.

Let the sestina's six end-words be: health, prejudice, passion, taste, illusion, death. Choose the permutations so that the form does the argument's work: the goods should trade places across the stanzas the way they do across a life — passion giving way to taste, illusion holding its ground against death, health and freedom from prejudice functioning as preconditions rather than prizes. The envoi should gather all six and leave the tension open: happiness as something contrived and maintained, not bestowed.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone who has read her would recognize her rather than merely hear her name, and good enough to stand on its own for someone who has not.

Then, after the poem, add a brief note — a few sentences — saying where the poem takes liberties with her views or leans on a contested reading of them (for instance, whether her defense of illusion can be squared with her insistence on truth, or whether her happiness is egoistic or perfectionist), and which textual or philosophical consideration would most likely push a reader toward a different reading than the poem's.
