I'm a novelist, and I've gotten stuck on something that's maybe not even a writing problem. Last month two of my oldest friends, R and J, got into an argument at my kitchen table about something our neighborhood association did, and I've been chewing on it ever since becuase I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. I want to use a version of the fight in the book I'm working on, so I actually need to understand it.

Background, since it matters: there's a vacant lot on our street, and a developer wanted to put up a twelve-story building. The association negotiated, and the deal that went through lets them build but only six stories, and a third of the units have to stay rent-controlled for thirty years, and they have to replant the trees they're cutting down along the block. There's also some clause where after ten years they can pay a lump sum to buy their way out of the rent-control requirement. R voted for the deal. J voted against it and is still sore about it.

So at dinner J said something like: You sold the one thing that shouldn't have a price. The light, the quiet, the fact that kids can actually play out there — those aren't chips you push across a table in exchange for a few cheap apartments. Some things you don't put a number on, and the moment you do, you've already lost, no matter what you get back.

R: But look at what actually happened. Everybody got more of what they wanted. You care about the block; we kept it at six floors and got the trees back. I care about whether normal people can still afford to live here; we got the units. Nobody got everything, and both of us got more than we'd of gotten if we'd just dug in and the lot sat empty for five more years. That's just a win. Would you rather everyone lose, so long as nobody traded?

J: You're sneaking in a common ruler. More of what we wanted, measured how? If your apartments and my quiet are both just amounts on one scale, then sure, add them up and call it a gain. But they're not on one scale. My problem isn't that I got too little. It's that the whole shape of the deal — that these are quantities to be balanced — already assumed the answer.

R: Then how'd you decide to vote no? You must have been weighing something against something, you didn't flip a coin. You thought: a third rent-controlled is worth less to me than the extra height costs me. That's a trade. You just did it in your head instead of at the table. So don't tell me there's no scale, you use one every time you make a decision.

J: Using a scale to make a choice isn't the same as thinking the scale is real. I compare a poem to a sunset all the time and that doesn't mean I can price them. And even if I do weigh things, that's me weighing them. What the deal does is make the weighing official. It announces to the whole street that the quiet was worth exactly one third of the apartments, and the buyout clause even names a dollar figure for the rest of it. It turns a choice into a rate. That's the part I can't swallow.

R: Here's the thing though. Think about how it works between two people who care about different things. You don't have to care about what I care about for both of us to end up better off. You can't stand the noise; I don't much care about the noise, but I can't stand that the block's turning into a place only rich people can afford. So each of us gives up the thing we care about less and keeps the thing we care about more. That's not me betraying my values or you betraying yours. That's both of us getting more of our own.

J: That only works if we've each got a tidy separate list of what we care about, and if there's some exchange rate between your list and mine. I don't think either of those is true. And even if it were — the deal is what the developer wanted. They'd much rather pay a third of the units than have the project stalled for years. You didn't beat them, you found the number where they'd say yes. Finding a price doesn't mean you should be happy about the price.

Then J said something else about it and R laughed and we changed the subject, and I genuinely don't remember the rest.

Can you referee this properly? Go through it move by move and tell me where each of them is right and where they're talking past each other, and what both of them are missing — not just who won. I don't want a general essay on compromise, I want to know which specific claims actually hold up.
