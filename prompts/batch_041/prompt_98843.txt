I am a software engineer with no philosophical training, and I have been trying to work out what view of freedom is built into the way my company runs its shift-booking app for warehouse and delivery staff. On paper everyone is free to turn down any shift: the contract obliges no one to work, and the app says as much. In practice shifts are handed out by a scoring system that ranks people by how often they accept work at short notice; anyone who declines too often slides down the ranking until they are offered nothing at all, and the company may rewrite the scoring rules at any moment without telling anyone. The company's public line is that it gives people the freedom to choose when they work, and it treats a manager ringing someone on their day off as an intrusion on that freedom. What conception of liberty does this presuppose — that freedom is just the absence of someone forcing you, that it is being one's own master in a fuller sense, or that it is not being subject to anyone's arbitrary power? Which of these does the practice strictly require, which does it merely sit comfortably with, and where is it underdetermined? Why does the manager's phone call count as an intrusion while the ranking system's pressure does not? And if the third view were right — that you are unfree whenever you are exposed to another's arbitrary power, whether or not they use it — what would have to change before the staff could be called free?
