I run an upper-level seminar on rational choice and collective decision-making — about 20 students, one 75-minute session — and I want a game on the paradox of voting instead of a lecture on it. What I want them to feel is the weird asymmetry: one vote at a time looks like a waste of time, since the odds that you personally decide the outcome are negligible, but the outcome is nothing but those votes. And I want the scale to change inside the game — not me saying "now picture ten million voters" — so they watch their own verdict on voting flip when only the size of the electorate changes.

A rough sketch, dont feel bound by it: everyone gets a secret card saying how much they stand to gain if their side wins a binary decision, everyone starts with the same small pile of tokens (candy, poker chips, nothing real), and casting a vote costs a token while abstaining keeps it. Simultaneous vote. I'd like them to end up at least once in the spot where each person's individually sensible move — keep the token — produces a result basically nobody in the room wanted. Then rig the second round so the thing lands at one vote, so that in the debrief they can see that the tiny chance your vote is decisive beforehand and the fact that your vote was decisive once it comes down to 10-9 are two different facts that people mash together.

Also leave room in the debrief for the strongest way out, which is that even a microscopic chance of being pivotal might still be worth paying for when the number of people affected is huge — I don't want them walking out thinking "so don't vote" is the smart answer. No jargon before the debrief, and they shouldn't hear the name of the paradox until it's over.

I've never run anything like this. Tell me what will predictably go sideways the first time (my guess is that some of them just won't play the payoff and will vote out of duty no matter what — how do I handle that?), and be honest about which part of the puzzle the game can't reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud myself.
