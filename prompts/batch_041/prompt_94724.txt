Which is definately too easy, and I also think it skips the only interesting step.

Here's what I want them to have to do. Wolpert's NFL results for learning say, roughly: average uniformly over all possible target functions, and every learning algorithm has the same expected error on points outside its training set. So a learner that always predicts 0 and a learner that predicts whatever parity function fits the data come out identical under that averaging. Everyone reads this off as "so induction has no rational foundation". But the averaging is over a uniform measure on the space of all functions, and a uniform measure is a prior. So I want the question to force them to say what has actually been shown, and about whom: is the theorem a formalisation of Hume's problem, a refutation of it (because it shows the demand for a bias-free method was incoherent — anything that generalises at all must already have a bias), or a change of subject (it's about expected performance under a prior, whereas Hume is asking what justifies the prior)? And they have to commit to one and defend it, not list all three as if that were an answer.

The second thing I want is for them to say what would have to be different in the theorem's set-up for it to bear on an actual scientist with a finite dataset and one particular problem, rather than on the average learner across all possible worlds. That's where the wheels usually come off.

They've had Hume on induction, the standard Bayesian/subjectivist line about priors, and a bit of computational learning theory (PAC-style, generalisation bounds), so you can assume that background. Don't assume they know any measure theory beyond "uniform distribution over a finite set".
