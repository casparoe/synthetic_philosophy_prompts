I have drafted an essay on the internal/external reasons distinction and would like detailed comments before I revise it. I am not asking for a reading list, citations, or pointers into the literature; I want your assessment of the argument as it stands, in your own words.

The essay's target is the way the distinction is usually drawn: a reason for an agent is internal just in case it is grounded in something already in that agent's motivational set, and external otherwise. The essay grants the internalist the standard replies to the amoralist case and to the bootstrapping worry, and then presses a different point. The motivational set is not a fixed inventory that an observer can read off the agent, because it is partly constituted by the agent's own self-interpretation and is revisable in deliberation. If that is right, then whether a given consideration counts as internal can change with nothing changing in the agent except the agent's own act of taking stock. The binary therefore sorts the same consideration differently at different times, and it does so in a way that tracks the agent's self-conception rather than the consideration's normative status.

The essay's constructive proposal is to replace the binary with two scalar dimensions. The first is tether strength: how tightly a consideration is fastened to motivations the agent already has, cashed out as the size of the smallest change to the agent's motivational set that would be needed to make the consideration count as a reason for them. The second is deliberative distance: how far the agent could get toward that change by deliberating well, without any change in their situation or in the world, from where they now stand. On this picture there are no external reasons, only reasons at greater or lesser deliberative distance; but the framework is meant to preserve what externalism wanted, namely that some considerations obligate an agent whether or not the agent can currently be moved by them.

The essay then claims that the normative practices we actually care about -- blame, advice, criticism, the appropriateness of moral reproach -- track deliberative distance rather than the binary. It works through three cases: a person who would be moved if they thought about it for a minute; a person who would be moved only by a change in their circumstances, say having to care for someone; and a person who would be moved by nothing short of a different upbringing. It argues that the first is the case the internalist handles well, the third is the case the externalist handles well, and the second is the interesting case the binary mishandles, because the reason is neither present nor wholly absent but latent in the agent's situation. The essay closes by suggesting that the whole debate looks like a dispute about where to draw a line on a continuum that is really about the agent's relation to their own future deliberation.

I would like you to comment on this. I am especially interested in whether the two scalars are genuinely independent -- I worry that tether strength and deliberative distance collapse into one another -- whether the proposal really preserves the externalist's point or quietly concedes it, and whether the third case is genuine or is just a case of external reasons with extra steps. If the two-scalar framework turns out to be a relabeling of a position already on the table, say so plainly and say what, if anything, the relabeling earns.
