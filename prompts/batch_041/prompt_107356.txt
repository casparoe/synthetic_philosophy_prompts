The question:

At time 0 a lamp is off. It is switched on at t = 1/2, off at t = 3/4, on at t = 7/8, and so on, the k-th flick occurring at time 1 − 2^(−k). Infinitely many flicks therefore occur before t = 1, and no flick occurs at or after t = 1.

(a) Set out J. F. Thomson's argument that the lamp's condition at t = 1 is paradoxical, and say what he took the example to show about the possibility of completing infinitely many steps in a finite time.

(b) Paul Benacerraf's reply denies that the description determines any state for the lamp at t = 1, and denies that a contradiction follows, on the ground that the state at t = 1 need not be determined by the states that precede it. State which response you take to be correct, and explain what the example does and does not establish.

(c) In your answer, use both distinctions from lecture: between the description's determining a state and there being a fact of the matter about the state, and between completing a task by performing a final step and completing it by performing every step.

The student's answer:

"The lamp is flicked at times 1 − 2^(−k), so infinitely many flicks happen before t = 1. Since the lamp starts off and each flick reverses its condition, the sequence of states after successive flicks is off, on, off, on, ..., which does not converge. Thomson's argument is that the lamp can be neither on nor off at t = 1: not on, because every time it was switched on it was later switched off; not off, because every time it was switched off it was later switched on; yet it must be one or the other. He takes this to show that infinitely many steps cannot be completed in a finite time.

Benacerraf's reply is that the state at t = 1 need not be determined by the preceding states, so that no contradiction follows. I think this is right, and I think it is also what Thomson was driving at: his talk of paradox is a way of insisting that a description of a supertask need not settle what happens at the limit. Where the flicks have no last member, there is no last state for the lamp to be in, and so the sequence of states has no limit for the description to report.

It is also worth noting that the absence of a final step is not in itself a defect. A task may be completed in the sense that every one of its steps has been performed even though there is no last step; that is what happens in Zeno's race, and it is what happens here. And since Thomson's argument turns on the assumption that a description of the lamp must settle its condition at every instant — an assumption we have already seen to be unwarranted — the example does not show that supertasks are impossible. It shows, on the contrary, that they are possible.

Someone might object that the lamp must be either on or off, so that the description cannot simply be silent about t = 1. But the law of excluded middle tells us that the lamp is in one of the two states; it does not tell us which, and it does not tell us that our description settles which. The divergence of the state sequence is a feature of our description, not of the lamp."
