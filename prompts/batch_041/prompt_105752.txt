Kant's essay Toward Perpetual Peace was published in 1795, and everything it takes for granted — the balance of power among European monarchies, standing armies hired and maintained by princes, war financed through public debt — belongs to a world that no longer exists. I want you to treat the essay as a body of doctrine and to predict what Kant would have said about three problems that arose only after his death. Throughout, distinguish what his stated principles actually entail from what you are extrapolating on his behalf, and work it out from the principles rather than from what commentators have made of them.

First, nuclear weapons. Among Kant's preliminary articles are a ban on standing armies, a prohibition on contracting public debt for the purposes of external hostilities, and a ban on modes of hostility so treacherous that they would destroy the possibility of mutual confidence in a future peace. Ask whether the mere possession of a nuclear arsenal for deterrence offends any of these, whether a declared policy of first use offends more than a declared policy of no first use, and whether the condition of mutually assured destruction should be read as an instance of the guarantee of perpetual peace that Kant locates in nature, or as a standing violation of his principles. Where your answer turns on the difference between possessing, threatening, and using, say so explicitly.

Second, humanitarian intervention. Kant's preliminary articles forbid one state to interfere by force in the constitution and government of another, and his second definitive article calls for a federation of free states rather than a state of nations vested with coercive authority. The post-1945 order built something in between: a Security Council empowered to authorize force, a veto held by its permanent members, and a doctrine — the responsibility to protect — that treats sovereign non-interference as conditional on a state's protection of its own population. Would Kant have accepted that doctrine, or would he have seen it as the interference his articles prohibit? Would he have regarded the veto as a defect of the arrangement or as the price of keeping it a league rather than a world state? And when a council member blocks an intervention that would have stopped a massacre, does the case refute the league model or merely exhibit what a league can and cannot do?

Third, a case Kant could not have anticipated, which I want you to reason through rather than recall. Suppose a coalition of states proposed a permanent international enforcement force: not a peacekeeping contingent assembled case by case from national armies, but a standing body, directly recruited, with its own command and a mandate to enforce council resolutions. Would Kant's objection to standing armies condemn it, or does that objection apply only to the armies of rival sovereigns? Would such a force supply the coercive instrument his federation lacks, or would it be the first step toward the world state he refused? Does his scheme allow a league of peace to have teeth, and if so, whose?

Close by saying where the doctrine simply runs out — problems it was never built to address — and whether you think Kant would have seen the gap or covered it over. He may be pulled in two directions here; if so, give both pulls their due, and make clear which of his commitments you take to be doing the work.
