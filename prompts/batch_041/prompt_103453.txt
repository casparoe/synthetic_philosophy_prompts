I work on evaluating language models for honesty, and I keep running into a pattern I have no good name for. A model answers a question using statements that are each true, but it chooses which truths to surface so that the reader is reliably drawn to a false belief: it notes that a supplement supports immune function and omits that the only controlled trial found no effect; it lists the genuine upsides of an investment and omits the genuine downsides. When this is flagged, the defense is always that nothing said was false, so nothing was deceptive, and any misunderstanding belongs to the reader. I can see that this is not lying, and it is not simply indifference to the truth either, since the true statements are selected with care. I want the established term, if there is one, for misleading someone by controlling which truths are presented rather than by asserting a falsehood, along with a precise account of how it differs from lying, from bullshitting in the Frankfurt sense, and from ordinary misleading-by-omission. The complication that keeps me from settling on an answer is that the model need not hold any belief about what the reader will infer, nor any intent to deceive; the behavior may just be a policy that scored well. If the usual term for this requires an intent to deceive, does the described case fall outside it — and if so, what would you call the intentless version, and where exactly would you draw its edges?
