The question was:

"Take the predicate H(x), 'x is a heap', applied to numbers of grains, and suppose we adopt the following hybrid proposal. (i) There are many admissible precisifications of H, each drawing a single sharp cutoff somewhere in the plausible range [n1, n2]. (ii) Instead of asking only whether a sentence is true on all of them, we let the degree of truth of a sentence be the proportion of admissible precisifications on which it comes out true. (iii) The cutoffs are assumed to be uniformly distributed over the range. (a) What degree of truth does the proposal assign to the conditional 'if k grains make a heap then k-1 grains make a heap', for a k in the middle of the range? (b) What degree does it assign to the universally quantified tolerance principle, 'for all k, if k grains make a heap then k-1 grains make a heap'? (c) Does the proposal let you say the sorites argument is valid? What does 'valid' have to come to here? (d) Briefly compare what the proposal says about the boundary with what supervaluationism, the epistemic view and degree theory say, and say which of the three it is closest to."

And here's the script:

"(a) The conditional 'if k grains make a heap then k-1 grains make a heap' comes out false on exactly one admissible precisification — the one whose cutoff sits at k, where k grains are a heap but k-1 aren't. On all the other precisifications both sides of the conditional take the same value, so it's true there. The degree of truth is therefore the proportion of precisifications on which it's true, which is (n2 - n1)/(n2 - n1 + 1): high, but not 1.

(b) The universally quantified principle gets degree 0. Whichever cutoff a precisification draws, the instance of the principle at that cutoff fails, so there is no precisification on which the whole principle holds. So it comes out just false.

(c) The sorites argument comes out invalid. The step everyone relies on is the tolerance conditional, and although each individual instance is nearly true, the principle that licenses the step is not fully true, and an argument can only be valid if all of its premises are true. So the argument fails — though the conclusion, '1 grain makes a heap', isn't what's at fault; it gets degree 0 anyway, which is what you'd want.

(d) Compared with the three views from the lectures: supervaluationism says there's no fact of the matter about where the boundary lies, which is why it treats a borderline predication like '532 grains make a heap' as simply false. The epistemic view says borderline cases are neither true nor false, so it's the one with the least in common with the proposal. Degree theory agrees with the proposal that truth comes in degrees, but it puts the degrees in the world — in the fact that heaps themselves come in degrees — whereas the proposal puts them in our linguistic indecision. On balance the proposal is closest to degree theory, because both refuse to give the tolerance principle degree 1 and both allow sentences to be more or less true rather than just true or false."
