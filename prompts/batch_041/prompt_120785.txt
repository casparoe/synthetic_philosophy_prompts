"Leibniz is often summarized as holding that justice is the charity of the wise. Explain what that means, how it fits with his division of natural right into degrees, and why he thinks justice is not a matter of God's arbitrary will. Say something about the role of the love of God in the account."

The student wrote:

"Leibniz's formula that justice is the charity of the wise means that justice is benevolence guided by wisdom. Since wisdom is what tells us how to bring about the greatest good, justice for Leibniz is essentially the maximization of happiness across all persons; the wise person is the one who calculates best how to achieve that, and charity is the disposition to want it. He divides natural right into three degrees. The first and highest is strict right, which forbids harming others and is the most binding because it's enforceable; the second is equity, which goes beyond strict right by requiring us to give each person what is due to them and to take others' good into account; the third and lowest is piety, which concerns only our private relation to God and so isn't really a part of justice at all, just a matter of religion. Leibniz rejects the voluntarist view that justice is whatever God commands. He thinks God's will is bound by the principle of the best: God commands something because it is just, not the other way around. That's why the love of God matters for him. Love, on this view, is a matter of taking pleasure in someone else's happiness, and loving God, the most perfect being, is the source of the disinterested benevolence that justice requires."
