Here's the half-formed bit. Lewis's conventions are regularities in behavior where everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, and given that expectation each person has a reason to conform -- plus there's common knowledge of all this, i.e. I know you know I know, indefinitely. That last clause is doing a ton of work: it's what makes the convention a shared object rather than just a pile of overlapping expectations. But the evolutionary and learning stories for how norms actually emerge -- agents adjusting, copying successful neighbors, drift, occasional mistakes -- seem to get you stable regularities without ever handing anybody common knowledge. Nobody carries an infinite hierarchy of beliefs around; at best you get everyone knowing up to some finite depth plus a lot of confidence. So the question I want to sharpen is this: is Lewisian convention just this evolutionary picture with an idealization bolted on, so that common knowledge is a convenient fiction you can drop, or does it pick out a genuinely different kind of social fact?

The distinction I want to try out, and this is the part I'm least sure of: call a regularity a shallow convention when it's stable on first-order expectations alone -- each of us expects the others to do their bit and so does ours -- and a deep convention when it's stable and also common knowledge in Lewis's full sense. My hunch is these come apart in interesting places, that alot of what we casually call a norm is shallow, and that deep conventions need something extra to get off the ground (a public event, a salient precedent, a shared vocabulary for justifying yourself) that evolutionary dynamics don't supply on their own. But I could be wrong. Maybe any shallow convention that's robust enough is deep in effect, or maybe the distinction is just verbal once you spell it out.
