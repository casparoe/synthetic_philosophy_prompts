Doing a session on An Inspector Calls with my undergrad phil of responsibility reading group next week. Most of them did it at school, which is half the problem — they all reach for the same line, that Priestley shows we're all responsible for each other, and think thats the end of it.

What I want to dig at is this. The inefficacy objection — the thing that says my own emissions, or my one steak, or my one vote, can't make a difference, so why am I on the hook for the aggregate harm — is usually set up as an additive case: lots of tiny contributions, none of which does anything by itself, the harm only showing up in the sum. Priestley doesn't write that play. He writes a chain. Arthur sacks Eva, Sheila gets her sacked from Milwards, Gerald keeps her and drops her, Mrs Birling turns down her appeal, Eric steals the money and gets her pregnant. Each link looks like a but-for cause of what comes after. So on the face of it the play doesn't illustrate the inefficacy problem at all — it dodges it. And then the Inspector's closing bit, 'There are millions and millions of Eva Smiths and John Smiths still left with us... all intertwined with our lives... We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other', plus 'each of you helped to kill her' — that feels like it's doing rhetorical work the plot doesn't actually cash out. Notice Arthur half-anticipates the whole objection early on: 'If we are all responsible for everything that happened to everybody we'd had anything to do with, it would be very awkward, wouldn't it?' and the play never really answers him except by mood music.

So read the play through the inefficacy objection and tell me which it is. Does the chain structure evade the objection, or does it illustrate a different one I don't have a name for — call it conjunctive rather than additive if that helps, where the harm is overdetermined and each link is redundant because someone else would have pulled the lever anyway, or because the arrangement was going to grind her up whoever did what? Is there a distinction between making a difference and being part of what makes the difference, and does the play need it and lack it? And what do I do with the twist — the whole evening maybe being a hoax, no girl having died, and then the phone going and an inspector on his way after all? If the chain may not even be actual, is the collective-responsibility claim still supposed to land on the Birlings, or is the play quietly telling us that its real object is the pattern and not the deed?

Be honest about where the play cheats: if the 'we' of that final speech smuggles in a collective agent the metaphysics won't license, say so, and say which reading survives. And if there's something Priestley gets that the philosophy papers on collective harm don't — the felt unavoidability of being implicated in a structure you didn't pick and can't step out of — I want that too, with the scenes that carry it (the engagement dinner, Sheila handing back the ring, the last phone call). Dont summarise the plot and dont tell me what Priestley intended. I want the traffic going both ways.
