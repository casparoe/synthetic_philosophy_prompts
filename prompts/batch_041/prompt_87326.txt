I am a novelist, and I am writing a scene in which a character is told, by something she has good reason to trust, that she will die tomorrow in one particular city, and that the telling already takes account of what she will decide to do about it. The more carefully I let her reason, the less sense her reasoning makes, so I went looking for help in the decision-theory literature on the case usually called Death in Damascus, and I have run into a wall that I suspect is not my wall.

As I understand the case: a man learns that Death will come for him tomorrow and that Death already knows where he will be; he may stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo; whichever he chooses is the city where he dies. What puzzles me is that the case is not argued over as a puzzle with an unknown answer. It is argued over as a test case — offered as evidence that one of our two leading theories of rational choice is defective — and the standing reply is that the case is defective instead, an artifact of the way deliberation happens to run rather than a verdict about the theory. In particular, the causal theory seems to deliver a determinate answer only once you add a rule about how to stop deliberating, and those on the other side say the addendum is ad hoc and that an agent who reasons that way remains exploitable in ways that betray a failure to understand its own situation. Nobody moves. Same case, same two readings, and no exchange of arguments appears to help.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict, and I want it argued rather than surveyed. Please do not give me names, dates, titles, or a reading list; I cannot use them and they would only pad the answer. I would like your own reasoning.

Take the role of a philosopher of practical rationality who works on this dispute and, more importantly, on the methodology beneath it: what it is for a theory of rational choice to be adequate, and how one tells a counterexample from a rigged test. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming on the page — set out the candidate diagnoses before you commit to one — then tell me which one the case actually supports and why the others fail. Consider at least these: that the disagreement is verbal, about what "rational" or "ought" is doing; that it is a real formal disagreement, for instance about which conditional probabilities a deliberating agent may use; that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, one about what to do and one about how to deliberate; and that the case is inadmissible as evidence because it idealizes a predictor of the agent's own deliberation. If you think the case is a proxy for a broader disagreement and that the proxy conceals what is really at stake, say so and say what it conceals.

My own hypothesis, offered for you to attack: the two sides are grading against two different jobs we ask a decision theory to do — one being to certify, for a problem already specified, which act is rational, the other being to describe a deliberator who can reach a verdict and remain stable under knowledge of its own choice — and a theory can do the first while failing the second, or the reverse, which is why the argument does not resolve. A second guess, which may amount to the same one: the case is ill-posed in a way that matters, because what the man's choice is evidence for depends on the very deliberation the choice is meant to settle, so the probabilities he is asked to weigh may not exist until after he has answered. If that is right, then "no stable choice" is a fact about the case rather than a defect in any theory, and the literature is arguing about the rules of a game whose rules it has not agreed on.

Then tell me what progress would look like given your diagnosis: a formal result, a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a change of question, or an argument that none should be expected and why. And close by answering the question I actually need for the novel — whatever the correct theory of his situation turns out to be, what should my character do in the morning? If my description of the case is wrong in any way that bears on your diagnosis, correct it first.
