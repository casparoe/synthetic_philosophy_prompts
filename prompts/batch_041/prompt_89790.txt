I am a novelist, about a third of the way into a book whose protagonist is a philosopher of cosmology who becomes convinced that the sheer fact of her existence is evidence about the size of the universe. I have been arguing about this with two friends — one a probabilist, one a theologian — and I keep losing the thread, so I would like to rehearse the argument with someone who will hold the line for the other side. Please play a committed defender of the self-indication assumption: the principle that, when you consider a hypothesis about the world, you should weight it by the number of observers it says exist, so that the fact that you exist is stronger evidence for a hypothesis the more observers it posits. You accept the consequence that a philosopher can be nearly certain the universe is enormous simply from the fact that he exists, without looking through a telescope; you think the embarrassment belongs to the objector, not to you. You distinguish your view from the self-sampling assumption, on which one reasons as if randomly selected from all observers, and you have real resources: the indexical character of existence, the difference between ex ante and ex post reasoning, and the analogy with ordinary selection effects. Do not strawman yourself; you may concede a point of formulation but never the principle. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a paragraph or two, and if I use a number you need to clarify, ask for it as your character would.

Here is my opening move. In the novel, my protagonist wakes up in a white room. She knows that before she was created, a fair coin was tossed: heads, the machine created exactly one person, her; tails, it created a trillion people, all in identical white rooms. She has no other evidence about which branch she is in. By your principle, she should be almost certain that the coin landed tails — a trillion observers versus one — and so she should believe the world is crowded. But here is the scene that worries me. She realizes that if she has a child, there will be one more observer in the world, and by her own logic that makes her own existence more probable. So she decides to have as many children as she can, on the grounds that the more observers there are, the more likely it is that she is one of them. That seems to me to turn a fair coin into something she can influence after the fact, and to make her credence depend on people she will never meet. Is that a reductio of the self-indication assumption, or does your view really license it? And if it licenses it, is there any evidence at all that could count against the assumption, or has it become unfalsifiable?
