I'm putting together a fiction prompt for a philosophy of econ unit and I want a short story that actually does something with revealed preference instead of just gesturing at it.

The knot I want it to sit in: Samuelson's move that got rid of utility as a feeling -- preference just is choice, and we're not allowed to peek inside anybody's head. Fine, but then notice what follows. If choice is the only evidence, you can never cleanly separate what a person wanted from what they were permitted to have; almost any behavior is rationalizable by some preference ordering, so "she preferred it" and "she had no alternative" come out empirically identical. Add Sen's old complaint that consistency isn't something you find in behavior, it's something you impose on it. Gul and Pesendorfer telling economists to keep their hands off psychology, Sugden answering that we should be measuring the options people actually had rather than the choices they made, Bernheim trying to salvage welfare analysis anyway once you admit people are inconsistent. Then the human stuff: adaptive preferences and sour grapes, people who genuinely come to want the lives they're stuck in; the gap between what someone will pay to avoid losing a thing and what they'd pay to get it; the Kaldor-Hicks dodge where a change counts as an improvement if the winners could in principle compensate the losers; the reversal where two changes each pass that test against the other. And under all of it the question of what utility was ever supposed to mean -- a feeling, a fulfilled desire, or just a bookkeeping device for choices.

I dont want a lecture wearing a costume. An actual short story, maybe 1200-1800 words, in which somebody has to decide something about another person's welfare on the basis of what that person has chosen. Pick one and commit: a daughter clearing out her mother's house; an economist doing a compensation study for a village about to be flooded; a doctor reading a patient's chart and trying to read consent off it; a spouse who has started treating the other one's habits as a verdict on the marriage. It should work as fiction -- if you delete the philosophy and it's still a story, good; if it collapses without it, start over.

Two or three things I want to actually happen on the page: a scene where the choice-reading and the constraint-reading fit the same facts equally well and nothing in the story can break the tie; a moment where the character's own preferences come apart, says one thing and chooses another, or prices a thing differently than they choose it; and an ending that doesnt hand down a verdict. Nobody explains the theory out loud.

After the story, give me a compact JSON object summarizing it, with these fields:

- "title": string
- "setting": one line
- "pov_character": who they are and what they want
- "theories_of_utility_in_play": array of strings, each naming an account of utility the story is testing (choice-based, hedonic, desire-fulfillment, opportunity/capability, etc.)
- "scenes": array of objects, each with "scene" (short description), "concept" (the specific idea it turns on, e.g. identification problem, adaptive preference, preference reversal, compensation test, willingness-to-pay vs willingness-to-accept gap), and "what_it_leaves_open"
- "verdict_on_choice_based_welfare": one of "vindicates", "undermines", "leaves_open"
- "one_line_for_the_syllabus": the story in a sentence

No citations or reading list needed, just the story and the JSON.
