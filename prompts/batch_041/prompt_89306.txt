I am preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of history and the historiography of philosophy, and I need a satirical text that will do real conceptual work. Please draft a mock grant proposal—deadpan, in the voice of a successful application to a major research foundation—for a new center whose mission is to make explicit and operational the historiographical vice often called Whig history: the tendency to write the past as an inexorable approach to the present. The center’s name, the foundation, and the applicant are yours to invent. The proposal should include the usual sections (project summary, significance, research plan, timeline, broader impacts, evaluation), and it should introduce at least one invented metric—say, a Retrospective Vindication Index, a Precursor Yield score, or an Anticipatory Fidelity quotient—that ranks past thinkers by their proximity to currently accepted positions. Have the authors defend what they call the Principle of Anticipatory Merit: the view that a historical figure’s importance is proportional to the extent to which their work can be reconstructed as a step toward ours. Include a scoring rubric for the center’s flagship fellowship, with criteria such as precursor density, teleological fit, and resistance to contingency. The humor should come from getting the underlying historiography right: the teleology, the presentism, the treatment of error as immaturity, the awarding of proto- status, the assumption that the past is a single conversation converging on us, the use of the present as the tribunal before which the dead are judged, and the quiet conversion of dead ends and contingencies into anticipations or failures of nerve. Let the proposal contain at least one passage where the logic quietly undermines itself—for example, by treating the present consensus as both the judge and the product of the inquiry—but do not have the authors acknowledge it. Do not wink at the reader; the piece should stay entirely in the voice of its authors, as if they saw nothing absurd in it. About 750 words. You may invent names, institutions, and examples, but do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and do not let the satire depend on obscure historical details. The target is the practice, not any individual historian.
