I teach an upper-level undergraduate course called Determinism, Chaos, and Prediction, which mixes philosophy of science with the concepts of dynamical systems. My students have met the logistic map and the phase portrait of a dissipative system, they can follow an exponential-growth argument, and most of them are not mathematicians. I am writing the final exam and need one essay question worth all 20 points, answerable in 90 minutes. It has to be impossible to answer by reciting the butterfly-effect slogan — that small causes have large effects, so the future cannot be known. Last year about half the class wrote that sentence, padded it, and stopped.

What I want the question to force is a distinction I have been teaching all term, between three grades of unpredictability:

1. Measurement-limited: the dynamics amplify initial uncertainty, so the horizon depends on how precisely the state can be measured, and nothing about the dynamics is indeterminate.
2. Computation-limited: the initial condition is known exactly, but there is no shortcut to the future state — the only way to get the state at time T is to let the system run to T — so the limit is resources, not data.
3. Ontic: there is no fact of the matter about the future state, because the dynamics do not determine it.

The thesis I keep pressing is that grades 1 and 2 are entirely compatible with determinism, and that grade 3 does not follow from either.

I also want one quantitative spine, so that a candidate cannot pass by gesturing. My students can derive that if nearby trajectories separate as δ(t) = δ0 e^(λt) with λ > 0, then a forecast that starts with uncertainty δ0 and tolerates an error of at most Δ has a horizon T = (1/λ) ln(Δ/δ0). The question should make them say what that formula does and does not establish about the predictability of a chaotic system in principle.

Write the following, in this order, under these headings.

"The question." The exam question exactly as it will be printed, about 200 words, with lettered parts. Do not print point values on the paper; I allocate all 20 points in the scheme. It should be readable by a student who has the course material and nothing else.

"Model answer." About 750 words. Write it the way a very good student would write under exam conditions — committing to claims and defending them, not hedging or surveying. It should use my three grades explicitly.

"Marking scheme." Five must-hit points, 4 points each, 20 total. For each point, say in a sentence or two what move the candidate must make, note which part of the question it belongs to, and add one line on how candidates typically lose that point.

"What separates an A from a B." About 150 words, naming the specific additional moves — not better writing or more detail — that lift a script from the middle band to the top.

Constraints: no citations, no bibliography, no names of books, papers, or authors, and no quotation marks around any phrase that would have to be checked against a source. The question must be answerable from reasoning and course concepts alone, with nothing looked up.
