I want to defend a thesis about self-reference and hierarchical structure, and I want you to tell me what would count against it. The thesis: every genuine tangled hierarchy — every strange loop — presupposes what Hofstadter calls an inviolate level, a level that is not itself caught in the loop and from which the loop's closure can be described. Escher's two hands each draw the other, but neither draws the sheet of paper they rest on; the sheet is the inviolate level. In Hofstadter's dialogues with nested genies and lamps, each genie can act on the levels beneath it, but no genie can act on the frame that contains the story he is in; that frame is the inviolate level. And in Gödel's construction, the formal system can encode statements about its own provability, but the reasoning that establishes that the undecidable sentence is true rather than false is carried out on a level the system itself cannot reach.

Now the harder commitments. I take the inviolate level to be (a) necessary rather than merely convenient — a loop with no outside is not a strange loop but an incoherence; (b) explanatory, in that it accounts for why the loop closes rather than generating a vicious regress; and (c) not a restatement of the type-theoretic or Tarskian hierarchy, since the point is precisely that the levels are tangled, and the inviolate level is what makes the tangling well-formed rather than what forbids it. I also take the Escher case to be a fair structural analogue of the Gödel case, not merely a suggestive picture.

What would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, sort them by which commitment they threaten, and say which ones a defender of the thesis would concede as counting against it as opposed to which only a critic would press. In particular:

1. Cases of self-reference that seem to need no outside at all — paraconsistent and dialetheist treatments of the liar, grounded-truth approaches in the Kripke style, autopoietic and second-order-cybernetic accounts of systems that produce and describe themselves, reflexive programming constructs, biological self-organization. For each, say whether it is a genuine counterexample or whether the inviolate level has merely been relocated somewhere I did not think to look. And tell me what the thesis predicts here that its rivals do not: what observation, proof, or construction would distinguish a world in which loops require an outside from one in which they do not.

2. The migration problem inside the Gödel case itself. If the meta-level from which we see that the undecidable sentence is true is itself formalizable in a stronger system, and that system has its own undecidable sentence with its own meta-level, then the inviolate level seems to move rather than to stand still. Is it then a single level, a limit of a sequence, or an idealization that is never actually occupied? If the last, in what sense is it explanatory rather than a promissory note?

3. The analogy's disanalogy. In the Escher lithograph the artist is arguably inside the picture too, since the hands are drawn from his own, so it is not obvious that the paper is cleanly outside the loop rather than one more element represented within it. If the pictorial case does not survive scrutiny, does the thesis lose its most intuitive support, or is it independent of that case?

4. The insulation worry. Any apparent loop without an outside can be met with the claim that we simply have not yet located the outside. Does the thesis predict anything that could fail, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability?
