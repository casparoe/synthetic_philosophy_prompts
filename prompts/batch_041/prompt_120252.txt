Write a poem of between sixteen and twenty-four lines, in rhymed couplets, on the problem of universals, and make the rhyme scheme itself carry the argument. This is for the closing session of a metaphysics reading group, so it will be read aloud by people who already know the debate; the poem's job is not to summarise the positions but to make one of them fail in the reader's ear.

The constraint is this. Open with rhymes that are full and effortless — pairs that plainly share a sound the way two red things plainly share a colour. Then let the rhyming loosen across the poem: true rhyme first, then slant rhyme, then assonance, then pairs that hold together only because the poem has trained the reader to expect a rhyme in that position. By the last third the reader should be unable to say whether the paired words are bound by something common to both or merely resemble each other closely enough to be paired at all. That ambiguity is the philosophical point, and the poem should stage it rather than state it.

Let the early stanzas speak for the realist: what licenses a rhyme is one thing, present in both words, located in neither. Let the later stanzas answer as the resemblance nominalist: nothing is shared, only a chain of pairwise resemblance, each link explained by the next. Use the turn to make the reader feel the cost of that answer — if resemblance is what binds the pairs, then resemblance is one more thing that any two resembling resemblances must themselves share, and the poem's own loosening chain becomes a regress it cannot stop.

The final couplet must not resolve this. It should rhyme audibly, and the audible satisfaction should come from precisely the resource the poem has just shown it cannot afford. A reader who knows the debate should be able to say, line by line, which side is speaking and why; a reader who does not should still feel the ground give way. You may use the technical vocabulary if a line needs it, but no line should depend on it. Do not name any philosophers.
