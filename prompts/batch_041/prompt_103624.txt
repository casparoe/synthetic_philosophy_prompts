I'm a postdoc in philosophy, and next month I'm giving a colloquium talk at a department where I would very much like to end up, defending a view I've been developing for two years. It's a version of infinitism about epistemic justification — roughly, that a belief is justified only if its chain of supporting reasons is infinite and non-repeating — but the distinctive part is a claim about what it takes to "have" such a chain. I argue that the chain need not be traversed, completed, or even held occurrently; it is enough that the agent command a finitely stateable rule that generates the series, so that an infinite structure is available to a finite mind in the way an infinite set of theorems is available to someone who has grasped an axiom system and a rule of inference. I also deny what I take to be a hidden premise in the traditional regress argument — namely that a chain of reasons can justify only if it terminates in something not itself in need of a reason. My line is that the force of the regress argument comes from smuggling in a "completion" requirement that foundationalists and coherentists share and that neither of them argues for; I want to make them either defend it or drop it.

The audience is small and I know roughly who will be there. Two committed foundationalists: one an empiricist who thinks perceptual seemings supply non-inferential justification and will hear my "rule" talk as just another way of smuggling in a foundation; one a rationalist who accepts self-evident axioms and will be impatient with the very idea that self-evidence stands in need of defense. Two coherentists, one of them a holist who insists that the relata of the justification relation are doxastic through and through and will therefore suspect that a dispositional rule is a change of subject rather than an answer. There's a formal epistemologist who will want to know whether justification can really supervene on a finitely specifiable structure, and who will press me on what becomes of my proposal when the links are probabilistic rather than deductive. And there's a historian of philosophy who has published on Sextus and on Aristotle and will say that the regress argument I'm attacking is a twentieth-century reconstruction rather than anything either of them actually held. The format is a forty-five-minute talk followed by thirty minutes of questions.
