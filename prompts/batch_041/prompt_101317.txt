Philosophy of science reading group has been stuck on Lakatos for three weeks and on Tuesday someone brought in this argument, and none of us could break it. It runs like this:

(1) On Lakatos's picture you can't kill a research programme with an observation. Any anomaly, however embarrassing, can be absorbed by rewriting part of the protectice belt around the hard core, and that isn't a bug in the account, it's the account.
(2) So the only real question you can ask about a programme is a historical one: has this series of theories been progressive, i.e. did it keep anticipating facts it wasn't built to anticipate, or degenerating, i.e. did it only ever clean up after the fact? And that verdict is only available once the series has mostly played out.
(3) But every such verdict requires you to have already decided a pile of things nobody tells you how to decide: what sits in the hard core and what in the belt, when the programme begins and whether/when it ends, which rival counts as a rival rather than a cousin, and what counts as a novel fact -- novel to the world, or only novel in the weaker sense that the theory wasn't built to fit it.
(4) Those decisions aren't read off the historical record. Two equally careful, equally well-informed historians can cut the same stretch of history differently and land on opposite verdicts, and nothing in Lakatos says which cut is the right one.
(5) So the verdict is never forced by anything. Whenever you say a programme is degenerating, that's a fact about where you drew the lines, not about the programme.
(6) So the methodology gives you no guidance in the only situation where you actually need it: right now, deciding whether to keep working on the thing you're working on, or jump to a rival that currently looks stagnant but might just be young.
(7) A criterion that can't guide is not a criterion of rationality. So the whole project of showing that theory change is rational -- the thing that was suposed to be the answer to Kuhn -- fails.
(8) And by (4) and (5) you can give the Lakatosian programme itself either verdict depending on where you cut, so it can't even say why it beats Kuhn's account or Feyerabend's.

Someone tried to save it by saying yes, the verdict is revisable, and revisability is a feature not a bug, because we can always go back and re-appraise. But that felt to me like it just restates the problem. I don't want to accept (5) or (7) -- I still think there's a real difference between a programme that's out of ideas and one that's just having a bad decade, and I'm not handing the field to Feyerabend. But I also like (3), it seems obviously true, and if (3) is true I can't see how (4) doesn't follow. So which step goes, and what do I pay for giving it up? If the trouble is a slide rather than a false premise, I want the slide named and I want to know why it looked fine, and what a defender of Lakatos has to say instead about how you individuate a programme in the first place.
