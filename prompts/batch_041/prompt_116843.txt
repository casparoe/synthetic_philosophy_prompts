OK so I've fallen down a rabbit hole on this old puzzle called Molyneux's problem — the one where a man born blind learns to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, then gets his sight, and the question is whether he could tell which is which just by looking, before touching them. It got posed to Locke back in the day and he said no, and I guess people have been going back and forth ever since.

Here's whats bugging me. I've been arguing about this on a forum for like two months and nobody budges. One side says obviously he can't do it, you have to learn to connect what you see with what you feel, and they point at the real cases of people who got their sight back as adults and couldn't recognize shapes at first. The other side says there has to be something built in, some shared sense of shape, or else there'd be nothing to connect in the first place — the two senses would just be two seperate movies with no bridge between them. Both sides think the other is obviously wrong and it's driving me nuts.

My own hunch, and I might be totally off, is that they aren't even arguing about the same question anymore. The original thing seems to have been about whether we're born with ideas already in us (Locke's whole thing), but now everyone treats it as a question about how the brain puts sight and touch together, and those aren't the same question at all. Also "recognize" is doing alot of sneaky work — does it mean instantly, or after a second or two? Does it mean being able to say "cube," or just having a feeling that it matches what he felt? And is "the same shape" one single thing in the mind, or two separate things that got glued together?

So what I want is not a history lesson or a list of who said what. I want you to diagnose it: is this a real disagreement, a word game about "recognize," two different questions wearing one name, or a genuine empirical question that just hasn't been answered cleanly? And be honest about whether the real-life sight-restoration cases can actually settle it, or whether they're too messy to (the person's eye or brain might be damaged, "at first" isn't really instant, the surgery isn't a clean test, etc.). If you think the question is just badly posed, say so and tell me how you'd re-pose it so it could actually get somewhere.

One more thing — tell me what would actually change your mind. What argument or piece of evidence would flip your diagnosis, and where is your reasoning most likely to be wrong? I'd rather have your honest weak spot than a confident answer.
