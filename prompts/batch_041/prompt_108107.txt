Write a dialogue between two equally competent people who disagree about how much it matters that a scientific model's assumptions are false. Call them the Modeler and the Philosopher. The Modeler built the model; the Philosopher sits on the advisory committee that has been asked to endorse the policy recommendation the model supports. They have an hour before the vote, and they are spending it on this disagreement.

The Modeler holds the position associated with Milton Friedman's methodological essay on positive economics, sometimes nicknamed the F-twist: a theory is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions, not by the realism of its assumptions, because a theory's assumptions are neither descriptions of the world nor separately testable claims, so the charge that they are unrealistic is a category mistake. The Philosopher does not deny that predictive success counts for a great deal, but argues that the F-twist cannot be right in general: some assumptions do work in an inquiry that prediction cannot license, by fixing what the model can represent, what it can explain, and which counterfactuals it can answer.

The model in front of them is a model of whether farmers take up a new crop-management practice. Each farmer is assumed to decide independently, by comparing the expected yield of the new practice against the old. There is no imitation, no learning from neighbors, no effect of what other farmers are already doing or of status, and everyone is assumed to have the same attitude to risk. The model was fitted to five years of regional adoption data and reproduces the aggregate adoption curve well. The recommendation is a subsidy that lowers the up-front cost of switching.

Requirements for the dialogue:

Real exchange. Each speaker must respond to the other's actual last point rather than to a general position. At least once, one of them should concede part of the other's case and then explain why the concession does not rescue the position. At least once, one of them should notice that they are using the word assumption in two different senses and stop to separate them.

No strawmen. The Modeler should not be naive about the difference between a model and the world, and the Philosopher should not be indifferent to predictive success. Neither may win by being given the weaker arguments.

No appeals to authority and no citations. They argue from the model in front of them, not from what anyone has written.

The counterfactual objection. The Philosopher should press the point that the policy question is counterfactual and that the model's assumptions settle that counterfactual by stipulation, so a good fit to history cannot by itself discriminate between this model and its rivals. The Modeler needs a serious reply, not a dodge.

The distinction they should reach. Have them arrive at, and then fight over, a distinction between two kinds of false assumption: one that is false but close enough to the truth in the domain at issue to count as a good approximation, and one that is false in a way no approximation story covers, adopted only because it makes the model tractable. Let them disagree about whether this distinction is stable, whether there is any independent way to tell which kind a given assumption is — for instance, by varying the assumption and seeing whether the conclusion survives — and whether that test already presupposes the answer it is supposed to deliver.

A contrasting case. Introduce a second case, drawn in a sentence or two, in which the idealization looks harmless, and let them disagree about whether the reasoning that handles the first case handles this one too.

Ending. Do not let either side simply win. End with the disagreement narrowed to a single statable question.

Keep the scene-setting to a minimum; the interest is in the argument. Roughly 1,200 words, as alternating speech.
