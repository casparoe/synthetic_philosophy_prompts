I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training past a gen-ed ethics class, but I've fallen down a rabbit hole on this philosopher Ian Hacking and I want to write a short story out of it before I forget the idea. His thing, as I understand it: most scientific categories are what he calls "indifferent kinds" — calling a rock igneous doesn't change the rock. People are different. We're "interactive kinds": we can know we've been classified, and knowing it changes how we feel, act, and describe ourselves; then the researchers who built the category see the changed people and revise the category; the revision changes people again, and it just keeps going, with no clean ground truth anywhere in the loop. He called this making up people. In one of his books about a 19th century European outbreak of compulsive wandering he adds two more pieces: a new mental illness needs a niche — an actual social space (a clinic, a newspaper, a railway network) where it can take hold — and some of these illnesses are transient, meaning they flare up, spread, and quietly die out without ever being refuted.

I want a story, maybe 1400-1800 words, that shows the loop turn at least twice. Setting I had in mind, change whatever you need: a mid-sized city about ten years from now, and a made-up diagnosis — call it "attenuated volitional collapse," or rename it, whatever sounds like something a young-adult mood clinic would actually put in a manual — that first gets named by the regulars in a support forum/Discord, then gets picked up by a hospital clinic that starts screening for it, then earns its own entry in the next edition of the manual. Use two point-of-view characters so we can see both jaws of the loop: one person who reads the criteria online and starts fitting them (or deliberately refuses to), and a junior clinician on the committee that rewrites the criteria, where the rewrite is plainly downstream of the patients the previous version produced. The niche should matter too — the whole thing nearly dies when the forum migrates or gets shut down. And if it falls out naturally, let the loop reach down into the biology, so that even the objective-seeming part of the assessment (bloodwork, a sleep study, whatever) starts drifting and you can't tell whether the body changed because the category did or the other way round. The last scene should make the reader feel the loop rather than resolve it — definately no wise old doctor announcing whether it's real or invented, thats the version I don't want. Also no character should ever explain any of this out loud, it should be visible in the events, and obviously no citations or reading list, just the story.
