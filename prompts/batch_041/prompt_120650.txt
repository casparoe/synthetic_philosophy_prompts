A friend in my reading group has latched onto the wasp. You probably know the one — she drags a paralyzed cricket back to her burrow, ducks inside to check the place over, and if you slide the cricket a few inches away while she's in there, she comes back out, hauls it over again, and has to go check the burrow all over again, and you can do this to her all afternoon. He calls this being sphexish and he uses it on everything. His argument is that free will is a non-starter, because I'm doing exactly what the wasp is doing, just with more steps: the only difference between him deciding whether to have the soup or the salad and the wasp running her loop is the number of moving parts, and if you could see my wiring the way we can see hers you'd stop congratulating yourself. He thinks the whole thing is a nice clean picture of why deliberation is a feeling rather than a cause.

I did thirty-odd years of field work before I retired, a lot of it on insects, and something about the comparison bothers me, though i can't yet say what. So I want you to take it apart for me. What is supposed to line up with what — the wasp with me, the burrow inspection with deliberating, the cricket with whatever it is I'm weighing, the experimenter's hand with circumstance? Where does that mapping hold and where does it quietly stop holding? And I especially want to know what the picture smuggles in. Does it assume that a behavior that repeats is the same thing as a behavior that can't be interrupted or revised? Does it assume that if the routine is fixed at one level of description it's fixed at all of them? Does the conclusion about free will still stand once we've noticed where she and I come apart, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole way?

One more thing, and this is probably the scientist in me: I'd like to know whether she even does what the story says. My instinct from watching things in the field is that nothing that rigid would survive a season, and I have a suspicion the anecdote has been passed around a lot more often than it's been checked.

No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, please — just write it out as continuous prose, the way you'd explain it to me on a walk. And tell me straight, at the end, whether there's a better comparison for whatever he's actually getting at, or whether he should just drop the wasp.
