A friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about the socialist calculation debate, and I would like the argument audited rather than merely re-summarised. His position is that the whole dispute was, at bottom, a claim about information technology, and he has a tidy way of putting it: the market is a distributed computer. Prices are the shared memory, every buyer and seller is a processor, and the trial-and-error adjustment of prices — the tâtonnement that Lange built his market-socialist scheme around — is simply the program the machine runs. On his reading, Lange's Central Planning Board is the auctioneer and the auctioneer is an algorithm; Mises's impossibility claim, and Hayek's insistence on dispersed, local, often tacit knowledge, were really claims about the hardware of their day — no single processor could hold the data, no channel could carry it fast enough, and nobody could solve the system in time. He takes that as the reason Lange's answer looked plausible when it was made, and also as the reason the critics won the literature: they were right about the machines they had and wrong about the machines we would get. As a clincher he added that firms already do internally exactly what he wants the state to do — a large company allocates capital, sets transfer prices, and coordinates thousands of activities without relying on internal markets for everything — and nobody calls that impossible.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy, not a survey of the debate. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the processors, the shared memory, the bus, the program, the clock — and then say where the mapping breaks. I am not interested in the trivial breaks that any metaphor has; nobody thinks buyers are silicon. What I want are the breaks that bear on his conclusion, that is, the features the computer picture quietly imports that a market does not have, or has only because of something the picture leaves out. Three questions in particular. First, is the knowledge Hayek cared about the sort of thing that gets cheaper as bandwidth and compute grow, or has the analogy misdescribed that knowledge from the start? Second, is solving for the equilibrium prices even the right description of what a market does, given that on some accounts the point of the price system is discovery rather than allocation — and what becomes of the analogy when the thing supposedly being computed is not given in advance? Third, does the firm comparison help him or cut against him: what does a firm's internal planning actually depend on from outside itself?

Finally, tell me whether his conclusion — that the calculation problem was a hardware problem and the hardware has arrived — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, whether there is a better analogy for the point he is actually trying to make. Please separate the disanalogies that matter for his use from the ones that are merely decorative, and be explicit about which parts of his argument the analogy was doing the work for.
