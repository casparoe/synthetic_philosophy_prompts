I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital, and I have to draft the written opinion we will vote on at Thursday's meeting. This past weekend I had a long argument with a close friend about whether it is ever legitimate to act on a moral judgment one has taken entirely on someone else's say-so, and the argument left me less sure of my own view than I would like — which is inconvenient, because the case in front of us turns on exactly that question.

The case is this. An elderly patient with advanced dementia has lost the ability to swallow safely after a stroke. The clinical team recommends against a feeding tube, arguing that it would prolong a life the patient can no longer value and would cause discomfort with almost no prospect of meaningful benefit; they propose transitioning to comfort care. The patient's properly designated surrogate is his adult daughter. She is devoted, involved, and plainly acting without self-interest. But she has told us, with some anguish, that she will not weigh the moral arguments herself — they are too painful and she does not consider herself qualified — and that she has decided to follow the guidance of her long-time counselor and moral mentor, who has told her that continuing to provide nutrition is what morality requires. She is not pretending to have reasoned it out; she is deferring, and she is candid that she would not be able to defend the conclusion if pressed. I should add that our discomfort is not about religion or about the content of the mentor's view; it is about the structure of the reliance. Our written policy says the committee honors a surrogate's decision when it is made in good faith and is consistent with the patient's known values, and that the committee decides for itself only when there is no surrogate or when the surrogate's decision is not made in good faith.

My friend's position, roughly stated, is that there is nothing especially wrong with moral deference. We take others' word on medicine, on law, and on countless evaluative matters; our queasiness about "outsourcing" a moral verdict is either a romantic illusion or a way of insisting that laypeople do work we are already paid to do. My instinct is that something changes when what is deferred to is the moral verdict itself rather than the facts bearing on it, but I could not say clearly what changes.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. I want a concrete recommendation — whether to honor the daughter's decision, decline to honor it, or honor it under stated conditions — with a time frame the team can act on, and with the reasoning stated so that both the daughter and the clinical team could respond to it. Some things I would like it to work through rather than assume:

- Whether the relevant line is epistemic (can a moral judgment be transmitted by testimony at all, or only a moral conclusion without the understanding that makes it usable?) or practical (does deference undermine the surrogate's standing to decide?), and whether the policy's phrase "in good faith" is the right hook for either.
- Whether the useful distinction is instead between a conclusion borrowed wholesale and a conclusion the surrogate reached herself while leaning on others for facts — and if so, whether the case is really a hard one.
- Whether it matters that the surrogate could not answer challenges or apply the judgment if the facts changed; if that is the crux, say what work it does.
- The awkward follow-on: if we decline to honor her decision and decide ourselves, we must either make an independent moral judgment, which may exceed our mandate, or rely on someone's moral testimony ourselves. Say how the opinion should handle that, and whether the committee is held to a different standard than the daughter.
- An honest reckoning with my friend's challenge: whether our hesitation reflects a genuine distinction or a disguised paternalism or jurisdictional grab.

The opinion should reach a bottom line I can defend in the room, and it should treat the position it rules against as its strongest rather than its weakest form.
