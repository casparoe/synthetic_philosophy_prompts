I'm trying to get my head around the "cluelessness" objection to consequentialism — the worry that the causal ripples of anything you do run on and on, so that even when you know the near-term effects cold, you can't actually compute the good and bad, and that's supposed to be a problem for the theory as a decision procedure. I want a thought experiment built to order for this.

Here's the spec. Two options, everyday setting (nothing sci-fi, no time travel, no global-scale stuff), one paragraph. The near-term effects of both are crisp and known, and near-term expected value clearly favours one of them. But the long-run effects of the favoured option have to be the kind where a consequentialist can't even assign a well-defined expected value — not just "we're unsure of the odds" but "the sum doesn't converge." And that has to fall out of ordinary causal messiness in the case, not from me or you just stipulating "and then anything could happen."

Then do the work: what does the objective consequentialist say (right act = the one that actually maximizes the good), what does the expected-value/subjective one say, and what do a rule consequentialist and a satisficing one say? Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting, and what happens if I strip that feature out — does it collapse back into a boring decision under risk? Also, how would a defender of consequentialsm try to wriggle out of the case, what's the cheapest escape hatch, and is it any good.

Don't fence-sit on the last bit: say straight out whether you think cluelessness actually sinks consequentialism as a decision procedure or whether there's an escape that survives this case, and give your reasons. If you're leaning on a contested way of framing the problem, flag where you're taking a side.
