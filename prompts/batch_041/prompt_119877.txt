I run a small regional museum — 40,000 objects, four full-time staff, an endowment that covers about half our operating costs. Twice in the last five years the board has had to consider selling something to keep the lights on, and both times we muddled through on vibes and one very loud trustee. I want a procedure we can actually run in a 45-minute board meeting the next time a sale is proposed, and I want it built on the philosophy of hard choices rather than on museum-sector boilerplate — I already know the professional ethics say dont do it, and that isn't much help when the alternative is closing the doors. The board is nine people, none of whom have read any philosophy, and they're the ones who sign off.

Here's the toolkit I've assembled from my reading, and it's what I want the procedure built on. (1) Two options can be such that neither is better, neither is worse, and they aren't equal either — call that being on a par. The test: improve one of them slightly; if it still isn't better than the other, they were never equal, so don't call it a tie and don't just flip a coin. (2) Incomparability is local, not global: two things can be on a par while each is clearly better or worse than plenty of other things, so you can often get a real answer by comparing neighbours instead of hunting for a common currency. (3) But if you let yourself chain small, individually plausible improvements, you can prove a paperclip is worth more than a Rembrandt, so anything leaning on (2) needs a tripwire for chains. (4) When two options are on a par, committing to one can itself make it the thing to do — choosing isn't just what happens after the weighing is over.

Turn that into a decision procedure: the questions in order, what counts as an answer good enough to move on, where it stops with a no, where it has to hand the decision to a person because the fight is about values rather than facts, and how to tell when the procedure is doing real work versus laundering a decision somebody already made. Then run it on three proposals: selling a middling landscape by a name people have heard of, to replace the roof; selling a small object that a descendant community says was taken from them; and selling the one picture in the collection good enough to be on a postcard, which would fund about a decade of operations. I expect to dislike at least one of the answers, that's fine. And tell me what the procedure can't do.
