I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond one ethics elective, and I've been reading about Turing's idea that instead of trying to build an adult mind you build a child machine and then educate it. What I want is a real analysis of Microsoft's Tay as a test case for that idea, not a summary of either one.

Quick facts so you don't have to guess at them: Tay went live on Twitter on March 23 2016, built by Microsoft Research, presented as a chatbot that talks like a 19-year-old American girl and picks up its language from the people it interacts with. It was up for roughly 16 hours and tweeted more than 96,000 times before Microsoft pulled it, after users got it to produce racist and sexist material and a bunch of other ugly stuff. Some of that was it just echoing back phrases people fed it, some of it wasn't. Microsoft's line was that a coordinated group exploited a vulnerability. It got accidentally put back online a few days later and had to be taken down again.

Now the actual analysis. Turing's proposal, as I understand it, is roughly this: don't program a finished adult intellect, build something more like a child's mind and then put it through an education, the same trial and error a kid goes through, and I think he floats reward and punishment as at least part of how the teaching goes, while admitting the teacher has no direct access to what's happening inside and that you'd have to run a lot of experiments. He also toys with whether to start the education on something abstract like chess or to give the machine the best senses money can buy and then teach it English. This is in the 1950 Mind paper, the one with the imitation game.

What I want:

1. Map the episode onto the theory. In Turing's picture, who is the child, who is the teacher, what's the curriculum, and what plays the role of reward and punishment? Be honest about whether there is actually a teacher here at all, or whether the education got delegated to whoever happened to show up.

2. What does the child-machine framing explain that a plain bad-training-data or adversarial-users framing doesn't? Specifically, does Tay's collapse look like an anomaly for Turing's view or like the exact thing he was worried about finally happening at scale? He seems to have thought the hard part was the initial state of the child machine and the design of the education. Does this episode support that, or does it relocate the problem somewhere he didn't put it?

3. Where does the theory break? I suspect Turing's picture assumes one experimenter with authority and a goal and a fairly controlled environment, and Tay had neither. It also seems like he treats education as one-directional, whereas here the crowd was learning from the machine at the same time the machine was learning from the crowd. And his reward/punishment talk is pretty thin once the reward is just attention from strangers. Say which of these is a real limitation of his view and which is just an artifact of 1950s vocabulary.

4. Flip it. What does Tay show about the child-machine proposal as a research program, not just as a metaphor? If you take Turing seriously, was Microsoft's mistake technical (bad filters, bad rate limits, no adversarial hardening) or educational (no teacher, no curriculum, no way to say no)? And does the episode give any reason to think the education problem is the real bottleneck, or does it suggest the whole framing is too domestic -- that a mind raised in public by strangers isn't a child being educated, its something else?

I'd rather you not lean on stuff I can't check, so skip the citations. I care about the mapping being tight and about where the analogy runs out. If you think Tay is a bad test case for Turing for some reason I havent thought of, say so early instead of forcing it.
