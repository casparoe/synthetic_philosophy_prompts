I would like a careful conceptual explanation of the problem of self-amendment: what is genuinely at issue when a system of rules contains the authority to change itself. Please reason entirely from the two invented cases below, rather than reaching for any real constitution, statute, or game, and without presupposing that I already know the academic literature on the subject.

Case one. A club's rules contain a single amendment clause: "Any rule in this set may be amended by a simple majority of the members present." At a meeting, a member moves that this very clause be replaced by: "Any rule in this set may be amended only by a unanimous vote of all members." The motion passes by a simple majority, and nothing else is changed. Is the new clause now in force?

Case two. A society's rules contain both "Every rule in this set may be repealed by a two-thirds vote" and "Rule X may never be repealed." A two-thirds majority votes to repeal Rule X. Does the repeal succeed?

I want you to explain whether cases like these generate a real paradox or only the appearance of one, and what would have to be true of a rule system for such cases to have determinate answers. Along the way, keep two questions separate: the logical question of whether a rule can coherently refer to itself and to the conditions of its own adoption, and the institutional question of which rule is actually in force at a given moment. Be explicit about the regress that threatens when we try to authorize an amendment by a further rule, and about the difference between a rule's validity and its content. If you think the answer ultimately rests on something that is not itself a rule in the set — a settled practice of the relevant officials, say — say so plainly, and then consider whether that dissolves the difficulty or merely relocates it.

Structure your answer as follows:

- The cases, restated (about 100 words): fix the two scenarios precisely enough that the later discussion can refer back to them.
- The appearance of paradox (about 250 words): lay out, step by step, the reasoning that makes each case seem to oscillate between two answers.
- What the paradox really turns on (about 350 words): identify the underlying conceptual issue, distinguishing the logical question from the institutional one and separating validity from content.
- The best resolution (about 350 words): give the account you find most defensible, and say why the rival accounts fail.
- What the resolution leaves standing (about 200 words): the residue of genuine puzzlement that survives your preferred account.
- Bottom line (about 100 words): the single paragraph you would give someone who asked what the problem of self-amendment amounts to.
