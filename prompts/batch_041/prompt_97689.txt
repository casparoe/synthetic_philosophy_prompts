I am a secondary-school student who has been following the debate over the European Union's future — in my politics class, in newspaper commentary, and in the speeches my teacher plays for us — and I have noticed that one comparison keeps recurring, usually without ever being defended. The Union, it is said, is like a bicycle: it stays upright only so long as it keeps moving forward, and the moment it stops — the moment integration pauses, or a member state digs in its heels and refuses any further pooling of sovereignty — it topples over. On this picture, every new treaty, every extension of qualified majority voting, every fresh competence handed to Brussels is a turn of the pedals, and the ever-closer union promised in the founding texts is simply the direction the bicycle happens to be pointing. The moral drawn is almost always the same: those who want to halt integration are not proposing a stable alternative but are asking everyone to fall off.

I would like you to audit this comparison rather than simply endorse or dismiss it. Please set out, as explicitly as you can, what is supposed to map onto what — who or what is the rider, what plays the role of the pedals and the forward motion, what corresponds to balance, to the brakes, to the handlebars, and to the road or the destination — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in the features of an actual bicycle that the comparison quietly imports into the European project: a bicycle has a rider with a destination and the ability to dismount; its stability is a matter of physics and momentum rather than of ever-greater speed; a stopped bicycle can simply be leaned against a wall or wheeled along by hand; and a bicycle pushed forward too quickly can still be steered straight off a cliff. For each of these disanalogies, I would like to know whether it matters for the work the comparison is being asked to do — namely, discrediting those who wish to slow or reverse integration — or whether it is a harmless difference that can be set aside.

Finally, and most importantly, please tell me whether the conclusion the comparison is used to support actually survives once these differences are on the table, or whether the conclusion was being carried by the metaphor all along; and if the bicycle turns out to be a poor fit, say what a better comparison would be, and why.
