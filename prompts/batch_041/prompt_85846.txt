I coordinate a small volunteer rescue station on a remote stretch of coast. I am writing up an incident for our review board at the same time as the board drafts a standing rule for split call-outs, and I would like a considered answer rather than reassurance that we did the obvious thing.

On the night in question we received two distress calls at once, from two coves roughly twenty minutes apart in opposite directions. We have one vehicle and one set of equipment, and the tide was rising in both places. In the first cove a lone fisherman was pinned under a fallen beam; he would have drowned within the hour, and we could have freed him in time. In the second cove five teenagers had been cut off by the flooding; they would have drowned within the same hour, and we could have taken all five off in time. We could not reach both coves before the tide. We went to the five. All five survived; the fisherman drowned.

I do not regret the decision, but I can no longer tell whether the reason everyone gives for it — that five is simply more than one, so the choice is obvious — is a reason or a slogan that happens to point the right way here. A friend who studied philosophy says there is a serious position on the other side: that each of the six people had a life that mattered equally, that nothing in the world makes five of those lives outweigh one, and that the fair thing would have been to give each of the six an equal chance, which she took to mean flipping a coin between the two coves. When I pressed her, she conceded that flipping a coin between one person and a million people seems absurd, and she could not say why, or whether that admission cost her the argument.

Our station chief's position is the opposite and equally blunt: you always go where you can save the most people, and agonising afterwards is self-indulgence. A board member has now proposed a third rule that she says splits the difference: run a lottery over the six people and let the winner decide which group we save. On that rule the fisherman's chance of being saved is one in six and each of the five has a five-in-six chance, so the numbers are honoured without anyone being written off entirely. The chief calls this self-indulgence dressed up as fairness — five-in-six is still a gamble, and there is no excuse for gambling when you can be certain. My friend says the weighting corrupts the whole point, because a lottery that is supposed to express the equal worth of each person cannot then be tilted by how many people stand on each side. I cannot tell which of them is right, or whether the board member's proposal is a genuine principle or a fudge.

Please decide which of these positions is right — or show that all three are wrong — and say what our standing rule should be, reaching a verdict rather than surveying the options. Lay out the reasoning, and meet the strongest objections to your own conclusion rather than gliding past them. Please also be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who have thought hard about it and which are genuinely contested; I would rather have an answer that marks its own uncertainties than one that presents a disputed position as though everyone agreed.
