A classmate put an argument on the board last week that he says kills satisficing dead, and I can't stop chewing on it. It goes like this:

(1) What you should do in a choice is fixed entirely by the reasons you have for the options in front of you.
(2) The reason you have for an option just is its expected payoff — the higher the expected payoff, the more reason you have to take it.
(3) So if option A has a higher expected payoff than option B, and both are actually available to you, you should take A.
(4) Satisficing says it can be perfectly rational to take an option that's merely "good enough," even when a higher-payoff option is sitting right there.
(5) But by (3), whenever a higher-payoff option is available, that's the one you should take.
(6) So whenever you satisfice, you're doing something you shouldn't — you're just being irrational.
(7) Unless the "good enough" threshold is secretly part of what you're maximizing, in which case you weren't satisficing at all, you were just maximizing a payoff function with a threshold baked into it.
(8) Either way, satisficing isn't its own thing — it's either a mistake or a relabeled version of maximizing.

He thinks this sinks the whole Simon/Gigerenzer line.
