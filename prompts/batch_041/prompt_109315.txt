I have spent fifteen years writing a column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am now reporting a feature on a decision before the council of a mid-sized city. The city must replace an aging, human-staffed regime of traffic and parking enforcement, and it is weighing two designs. The first is a networked apparatus of cameras and sensors that issues citations automatically and uses an algorithm to direct enforcement toward the places where violations cluster. The second largely forgoes automated enforcement and instead rebuilds the streets themselves — bollards, raised crossings, narrowed lanes, daylighted corners — so that the conduct the city wants to discourage becomes physically difficult rather than merely punishable after the fact. The council's public framing is that both are simply tools, and that the choice turns on cost and measured effectiveness rather than on politics.

I want to examine, seriously and not as a gesture, the older and more unsettling claim that technical artifacts are not neutral instruments but carry political commitments in their design — the claim that crystallized in the question of whether artifacts have politics. Please set out the most important implications of that claim for this decision: the implications that would genuinely change how the council should decide and how I should frame the piece, ranked in order of importance, with the reasoning for the ranking. Distinguish the strong version of the claim — that some technologies are inherently political, in that they require or are strongly compatible with a particular social order — from the weaker version, that artifacts are shaped by the interests and assumptions of their makers but might have been built otherwise, and tell me which of the implications survive on the weak version alone. For each implication, say what it ultimately rests on: a conceptual argument, a contested empirical premise about how such systems actually behave once installed, or an assumption about who would hold power over the system after the contract is signed. I am not after a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want the two or three implications that would most change the decision, and the strongest reason a thoughtful skeptic would resist each.
