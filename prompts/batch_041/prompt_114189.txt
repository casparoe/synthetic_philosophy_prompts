I am a high school student on my school’s ethics bowl team, and our current unit is on democratic theory; I keep losing points because I can state a position but cannot hold it under a sharp challenge. I have been reading about the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos—and I find the all-subjected view tempting but fragile. I would like to practice against a serious opponent. Please role-play a committed democratic theorist who defends the all-subjected principle: the demos should consist of those who are subject to the state’s coercive laws, not everyone merely affected by its decisions. Make the character sharp and well-read rather than a strawman; let them concede peripheral points, but never the core claim; have them speak in a polished, seminar-style voice; and have them stay in character until I say “stop.” Before you write your first reply, think carefully about the strongest objections to the all-subjected principle and the best replies available to it, but do not show that brainstorming in the reply itself. Keep your in-character turns to roughly a paragraph so it feels like a conversation. At the very end of your first reply, after the in-character response, add a short bracketed out-of-character note that separates what is settled among democratic theorists from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.

Here is my opening move. Suppose a large industrial state adopts a carbon border adjustment: foreign producers must meet its emissions rules if they want to sell into its market, but they have no vote in its elections. Meanwhile, a low-lying island nation is sinking because of those same emissions; its people are not subject to the industrial state’s laws in any ordinary sense, yet the state’s decisions determine whether their country survives. Your all-subjected principle seems to exclude the islanders from the demos at exactly the moment their claim is strongest, while including foreign producers who are merely regulated at the border. If you widen “subjected” to cover anyone causally affected by the state’s choices, you collapse into the all-affected principle you reject; if you keep the narrow legal reading, you exclude the people with the most urgent claim. Which way do you go, and why is this not a fatal dilemma for your view?
