I have been working through the literature on diachronic rationality for a graduate seminar, and I keep finding that the money-pump examples are either toy cases or so abstract that they never touch how a real institution actually gets squeezed. I would like you to help me test the apparatus against a real episode: the near-collapse of Long-Term Capital Management in 1998. I am not asking for a narrative of the crisis or a verdict on the Fed-organized recapitalization. I want a disciplined mapping from the theory to the case and back again. In particular, distinguish two things that I think are often conflated. The first is the classic money pump: an agent with a synchronic cycle of intransitive preferences can be led through a sequence of trades that leaves it worse off and the counterparty better off, with the agent’s own choices at each step being locally rational. The second is what I have been calling a time pump: the agent is not exploited by a single external trader but by the succession of its own future selves, each of whom can rationally discount, renegotiate, or refuse to honor the commitments of earlier selves, so that the agent is drained by its own diachronic structure rather than by a clever counterparty.

Treat LTCM as the agent. The salient facts you may take as given: the fund held large, highly leveraged convergence positions; after the Russian default of August 1998, spreads moved against it; counterparties demanded more collateral; the fund had to deleverage into illiquid markets; and a consortium of banks, convened by the New York Fed, recapitalized it. What I want is an account of who or what is doing the pumping, what the preferences are at each stage, whether the fund’s predicament is better modeled as intransitivity, time inconsistency, or something else—perhaps a coordination failure among its creditors—and where the money-pump framework is simply silent. I also want you to say what diachronic rationality would have required of the fund’s principals at each stage, whether there was a coherent policy they could have adopted ex ante, and where that question becomes ill-formed because the fund’s agency is distributed across partners, traders, risk models, and margin calls. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theory: does it confirm that diachronic rationality is a substantive constraint on agents like funds, or does it reveal that the money-pump apparatus was built for idealized individual choosers and distorts the case of an institution whose self is scattered across a balance sheet and a creditor syndicate?
