I'm part of a small political-theory reading group that has just spent a semester working through Simone Weil's critique of political parties -- her contention that the party as an organizational form is structurally inclined to suppress truth, to reward loyalty over thought, to make the survival and cohesion of the organization an end in itself, and thus to poison the very political function it claims to serve. We've reached the point where further exegesis is diminishing returns, and I've been asked to draft an agenda-setting memo for the group: not a survey of what others have said about her, but a defended prioritization of the open questions her critique leaves behind. Assume the relevant resource is roughly three philosophers for one year, working conceptually rather than empirically, and that the group wants to know which questions are worth those years.

To keep the memo from collapsing into well-worn territory, I'd like it organized around a specific reframing, and I'd like you to take that reframing seriously rather than merely gesture at it. Weil's named target is the electoral party, but the force of her argument seems to rest on a more general property -- call it party-ness -- that could attach to any group that must hold a line in order to act together: advocacy coalitions, professional and academic guilds, scientific schools, religious orders, and the looser online factions that now do much of the work of political identity. If party-ness is a general pathology of coordinated collective action rather than something peculiar to parliaments and ballots, then several questions come into view that her own writing does not settle: what exactly is the mechanism that produces it (the need for discipline, the incentives of a shared public position, the psychology of belonging, something about representation itself), whether it is a matter of degree or of kind, whether any durable form of organized political action can avoid it, and what institutional alternatives -- sortition, deliberative assemblies, movement-like formations, federated or temporary structures, or something I haven't listed -- would count as genuinely non-party rather than a party wearing a different name. My own hunch, which I'd like you to test rather than adopt, is that the sharpest question is not whether parties can be reformed but whether the political functions Weil thinks are indispensable -- formulating and voicing collective needs -- can be discharged at all without some group somewhere acquiring party-ness.

What I want from you is a ranked and defended agenda: the main open questions this reframing generates, ordered by how much the others depend on their resolution, with your criteria stated explicitly (upstreamness, tractability for philosophers rather than empirical researchers, how neglected each is, and how much would actually change downstream depending on the answer). For each question near the top, say what a satisfying answer would even look like, what considerations would decide between the leading positions, and what -- a counterexample, a conceptual distinction, a case of an organization that manages coordination without the pathology -- would move it up or down the ranking. Please also name the questions you would deliberately set aside for now and say why, since a memo that recommends everything recommends nothing. I'll add the citations myself; I don't need a reading list, and I'd rather you argue about where the effort should go than catalogue who has written what.
