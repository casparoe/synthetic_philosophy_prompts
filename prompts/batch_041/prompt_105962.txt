I run the conservation lab at a mid-size museum and I keep having the same argument with our curators: after a treatment, is the thing on the plinth still the same artwork? I've been poking around the philosophy of material constitution (statue and clay, all that) and it clearly bears on this, but I can't hand my technicians a journal article. So give me a procedure we can actually run before we approve a treatment. It has to keep three things separate: the material the object is made of, the form it has, and its causal history, meaning who made it, when, and what's happened to it since. In order: what to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no because the treatment would amount to making a new object, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values question and not a factual one. Then run it on three cases: stripping later overpaint off a wooden figure to get back to the original surface; replacing a lost hand with new wood carved to match; and melting down a corroded bronze to recast it in the original mold from the same metal, or the even worse idea of 3D-scanning it and printing a resin copy and calling that the artwork. Take a definite stand, where the procedure yields an answer give the answer and defend it, even when it's the one the donor will hate. Plain prose only, no headings, no bullets, no tables, like an email to a colleague. And be honest about which steps are just judgment calls wearing a lab coat.
