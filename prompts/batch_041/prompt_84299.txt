I'm putting together a proposal for a small internal summer grant in my department — eight weeks, roughly six thousand words, one reader, no archive trips — and I'd like you to write the proposal for me. Heres the half-formed idea I've been chewing on.

Most of the fight over authorial intention is about whether the authors intention fixes what a text means: either it does, or the text (or the community, or the reader) does, and then everyone argues about which side wins. I think thats the wrong axis, because both sides are asking about intention in the sense of what the author meant to convey, and ignoring something adjacent that I want to call curatorial intention — what the author takes the text to be permitted to mean. These come apart in cases I keep running into. An author says: I didn't mean X, but X is a fine reading and I'm happy to let it stand. Or: I meant Y, but I now think readers who get Y out of it are misusing the text, so I disown it. Or a dead authors estate licenses a sequel, or an editor cuts a chapter, and we suddenly treat some readings as authorized and others as not. Notice that a permission can be granted for a reading the author never intended, and withheld for the one she did, so it doesn't look like a species of intention. But it also isn't just a social fact about what critics happen to accept, because the author seems to have some standing to grant or withhold it, and that standing survives her death in a weird way through whoever holds the estate.

So the question I want the proposal to nail down: is there a coherent notion of authorial authorization that is distinct from authorial intention, and if there is, does it have any authority over interpretation — over what the text means, or only over what we're allowed to do with it (quote it, extend it, canonize it, teach it)? I want it stated so that it has a determinate answer rather than just a vibe.

What I need from you: the question stated precisely; why the existing positions leave it open (the intentionalist ones and the anti-intentionalist ones, and also whatever the hypothetical-intention people say, since I suspect they smuggle the permission thing in without noticing); the method — what I'd actually do week by week for eight weeks, e.g. build a taxonomy of authorization cases, test whether each one can be reduced to intention or to social uptake, then see if anything survives the reduction; what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing or collapsing into a verbal dispute; and an honest scope check — is eight weeks enough, and what do I cut first if it isnt.

Dont give me a reading list or citations, I'll fill those in myself, just talk about the positions. And dont let it drift into a general essay on intention; it has to stay on the authorization thing.
