I have recently come round to hedonism about wellbeing, and before I put it in writing for a seminar I want an honest audit of whether the position I have assembled actually hangs together. Here is what I believe. (1) A life goes well exactly to the extent that it contains pleasure and not pain; nothing else is good or bad for a person in itself. (2) The taste of coffee, the relief of a sneeze, the satisfaction of finishing a proof and the gloating pleasure of a rival's failure have nothing felt in common, so there is no single phenomenal quality shared by everything we call a pleasure. (3) All the same, 'pleasure' names a genuine psychological kind rather than a ragbag, and the theory of wellbeing latches onto a real property when it speaks of it. (4) I am not a desire-satisfaction theorist: a person can be made better off without getting what they want, and worse off while getting it. (5) A masochist's pleasure in pain is a real pleasure and, as such, good for them.

Which of these pull against one another, which only appear to, and for each genuine tension which commitment has to give and what the loss would be? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, ordered from the most to the least severe tension. Write in British English.
