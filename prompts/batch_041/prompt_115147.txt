I chair the scientific standards committee of our national forensic science body — the committee that writes the binding guidance on how accredited laboratories may state conclusions in their reports and in testimony. In three weeks we must settle a question we have already deferred twice, and I need to circulate a draft opinion to the other members beforehand.

The question is whether an examiner may state, in any form, a probability that a particular person is the source of a trace. Three practices are on the table. First, the current default: the examiner reports a random match probability, or a likelihood ratio — the probability of the observed profile if the suspect is the source, divided by its probability if a random unrelated person is the source — and leaves the combination with the rest of the evidence to the factfinder. Second, a proposal from our statistical adviser: allow examiners to report a posterior probability of source, on the ground that a factfinder handed only a ratio will either invert it or ignore it. Third, a proposal from our quality manager, who wants to dissolve the dispute by defining the number operationally: our laboratory's probability of being right about a source attribution, measured as the long-run rate of correct attributions across our proficiency-testing trials, which currently stands at 99.2 percent. His argument is that a number we can audit is worth more than a number nobody can interpret.

What began as a technical dispute has become a conceptual one, and the members are dug in. Our defence-side statistician argues that the random match probability presupposes a reference population — random unrelated persons — that is not the class the factfinder actually faces, since the defendant is not a random person but one who was stopped in the victim's car with the victim's blood on his sleeve; that the posterior probability smuggles in a prior the examiner has no standing to supply; and that the quality manager's number confuses the reliability of a procedure with the evidential force of a particular result. Our laboratory director answers that the first option is the only one that keeps the examiner out of the factfinder's seat, that the second is simply what a rational factfinder wants, and that the third is at least honest, because the error rate is the only thing a laboratory genuinely knows about itself.

I have come to think we are not really arguing about statistics at all. We are arguing about which sense of probability our guidance is meant to invoke — the long-run behaviour of a sampling procedure, a rational degree of belief, or a disposition of the test itself — and none of the three proposals is coherent until we say which. A live prosecution has forced the issue: an examiner has already reported a posterior probability of 0.997 in a report now under challenge, and the court has asked us for our position.

Draft the opinion. It should recommend a specific rule, stated precisely enough that an examiner can follow it and a court can apply it; give the reasoning behind it; and take the strongest objection to the recommendation seriously enough that a member who holds it would recognise their own view in your statement of it. If the right rule is conditional — if single-source traces and mixtures should be treated differently, or if numerals should be barred altogether in favour of a graded verbal scale — say so and defend it. I do not need a review of the literature; I need a rule we can vote on, together with a short account of what would have to happen over the next year for us to revisit it.
