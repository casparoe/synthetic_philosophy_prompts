I'm putting together the next few episodes of a tiny interview podcast I do with friends from my old grad program -- basically ethics, no real audience, but we try to be careful. Next month's guest is someone I studied with who's been working up a manuscript on Bernard Williams and the integrity objection. Her line, as I understand it, is that the objection is best read as a claim about the agent's relation to her own deliberations rather than about the survival of her deepest projects and commitments, and that on this reading it survives the usual consequentialist comebacks -- the sophisticated-consequentialist move of separating the criterion of rightness from the decision procedure, the thought that a theory can be true while recommending that you not run the arithmetic in the moment, and so on. She also thinks the drowning-wife example, where Williams objects that the rescuing husband would be having a thought too many if he first had to register that saving her is morally permissible, is a seperate and weaker complaint than the integrity objection proper, and that she's willing to give it up.

I want eight to ten questions that don't just let her restate the view. At least one question each on: (a) whether the two strands really come apart, or whether dropping the rescuing-husband bit quietly costs her that case and maybe the whole grip of the objection; (b) whether integrity-as-a-relation-to-one's-own-deliberation is a distinct objection to consequentialism at all, or collapses into ordinary over-demandingness; and (c) how she handles the sophisticated-consequentialist reply, given that her objection is aimed at how a theory shapes the agent's dispositions rather than at its criterion. For each of those three, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Then close with one question about what could count against her reading -- what would have to be true of Williams's text, or of the idea of integrity, for her to abandon it.

One more thing: in your notes to me, keep the questions that rest on settled points (that Williams presses both an integrity worry and the rescue case, that the criterion/decision-procedure distinction is the standard consequentialist move) seperate from the ones that only work if you accept a contested reading (that he conflated the criterion and the procedure, or that the two objections are independent) -- and please don't write as if your take on the contested ones is what everyone agrees on, because half the people I've floated this to think the strands are basicaly inseparable.
