A group of us in my department have been chewing on an idea that one of my friends half-jokingly christened "the unilateralist's curse" — I think he lifted the name from somewhere, some rationalist-adjacent paper, I genuinely can't remember where — and I've started to suspect it's true in a much stronger form than we first meant it. The crude version goes like this: suppose a bunch of actors are each independantly deciding whether to do some thing — keep going up the ridge in the weather, run the experiment, release the preprint, start the company — and each one's judgment about whether the thing is worth doing is noisy, and each one acts just in case their own judgment says yes. Then even if the thing is on balance bad, it only takes one overconfident actor to make it happen, and whoever goes ahead is disproportionately likely to be the one who was most wrong. So decentralized unilateral action is a machine for surfacing your worst estimator. That part I'm fairly confident about; it feels like it falls out of the setup more or less by construction. What I've drifted into believing is the sharper claim, that this isn't really a story about coordination or about the tails of distributions but a story about selection — that any institution which leaves room for unilateral action will over time end up governed by the judgments of whoever is most reliably mistaken, because the mistaken are the ones who keep acting and everyone else's restraint just cedes them the field. I want to hold that, but I dont trust it yet.

So: what would count against it? I don't want the obvious stuff about correlated errors, or about everyone sharing a prior — I already see that those collapse the whole thing into a single bad draw, and I've conceded as much. I want the cases where the selection story should bite and doesn't. Which features of a real decision situation — repetition and learning from other people's mistakes, actors being able to watch each other before they move, reputations and blame attaching afterwards, the costs of a wrong yes landing on the actor while the costs of a wrong no land on everyone else, restraint itself being visible and praised — would sever the link between "keeps acting" and "keeps being wrong"? Which of those are genuine escapes and which are just ways of making the curse slower rather than weaker? And which of them are things I'm already implicitly committed to, such that if they fail the claim stops being about the world at all and becomes a theorem about an idealised case I built to get the answer I wanted? I'd especially like the asymmetries: where the noisy judgment is about whether something is permitted rather than whether it's worth it, or where the actor bears the downside themselves — does the curse survive, invert, or evaporate? Rank what you give me by how much it should actually move me, tell me which of the defeaters a defender of the strong version would cheerfully accept as not counting at all, and say plainly whether the strong selection claim risks anything.
