For the last meeting of a reading group that has spent the term on Gödel, Escher, Bach, I have been asked to write the closing poem, and I would rather you wrote it. The session is on the book's treatment of where meaning lives: the thought that a pattern out in the world becomes a message only when it reaches a receiver carrying the right decoding machinery, and the distinction between the frame, or outer, message — the part that announces that there is something here to be decoded and tells the receiver how — and the inner message, the content that actually comes out of the decoding. What has kept us arguing all spring is that the frame message is not a second, sneakier content hidden behind the first; it is more like the address on an envelope, or the primer that tells the machinery where to start, and the inner message turns out to sit neither in the ink nor in the grooves nor in the reader's head alone, but in the correspondence between the pattern out there and the machinery in here. I want a poem that makes that argument rather than illustrates it, and I want the form to carry the weight.

Here is what I have in mind. Sixteen lines, rhymed or not as you please, but it has to read cleanly out loud — we will be reading it in a kitchen with terrible acoustics. Write it in the voice of a carrier that has been found but cannot know whether its finder can read it: a record in a sleeve, a strand of DNA, a letter in a bottle, an inscription on a probe — whichever lets you say the most. The structural demand is two acrostics: the first letters of the sixteen lines, read downward, should spell a legible English sentence, and the last letters of the sixteen lines, read downward with spaces and punctuation ignored, should spell another, one letter per line, neither sentence longer than sixteen letters. One of the two should be an instruction for how to read the poem, the other a claim about where what the poem carries actually resides, and they should not agree with each other. If a reader notices neither acrostic, the surface text must still stand on its own as a coherent poem, so the same sixteen lines have to support three readings; and in that surface text I would like you to stay in the world of objects — salt, grooves, a needle, paper, enzymes, a shell, a sleeve — without ever using the words meaning, information, semantics, decode, or message, which should be enacted rather than named. (The acrostics themselves may use whatever vocabulary they need, since they are the ones doing the framing.) Do not let the last line settle the question: the book does not settle it, and the carrier's ignorance about its reader should still be intact when the poem stops. Two things in the book we have most enjoyed, if you want the spirit of either: the double acrostic in the Contracrostipunctus dialogue, where one acrostic spells out a sentence that describes the other, and the phonograph that destroys itself trying to play the record it cannot play.

After the poem, in four or five sentences of prose, tell me which acrostic is the frame message and which is the inner one, what a reader who knows nothing about decoding machinery would take away from the surface text alone, and whether the second acrostic is really a second message or only the first one's shadow. Please do not quote from the book, in the poem or in the note — I know what the book says and I want to hear what you make of it — and do not let the poem name-drop the ideas. A member of the group who has read the chapter should recognize the argument from the inside, and a friend who has not should still be able to read it aloud at the potluck without a glossary.
