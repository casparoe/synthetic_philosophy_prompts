I write backend services for a living and have no formal training in philosophy, so the vocabulary below is mine and probably imprecise. I need a short story, and I'd rather over-explain the setup than under-explain it, because the whole point turns on a distinction that's easy to lose.

The setting is a fictional company I'll call the one I work for. We make a wearable band that continuously records the last several minutes of what its wearer perceives and feels, so it can be replayed later. Customers use it for workplace disputes, insurance claims, custody hearings, and grief. The marketing says it shows you what really happened. The engineers know that isn't quite right. Nothing like a video file exists. The band keeps a sparse, lossy trace, a few thousand numbers a second, heavily compressed, and when you press play a generative model reconstructs a rich, seamless, first-person experience from that trace plus whatever state you happen to be in at playback. Replays feel like memories. They are not stable enough to be evidence. Two people standing next to each other, wearing bands that both passed calibration, will produce replays that agree about the room and disagree about who was holding the knife. Firmware updates quietly change how old replays render, and nobody notices, because there is nothing to compare against.

Here are the two positions I keep hearing in design meetings, in my own words.

The first says that somewhere in the system there has to be a final, authoritative moment: a point where the last few hundred milliseconds of processing are assembled into one finished experience, timestamped, and handed to whatever it is that experiences it. Everything downstream is just reporting on what was already settled. I'll call this the final screen. Without it, the idea of what a person experienced at a particular time falls apart, and so does the product.

The second says there is no final screen and no privileged moment. Processing happens in many places at once, at different speeds, with no finish line. What counts as experienced is whatever ends up influencing what the system does and says later, and later processing can reach back and revise earlier content, so there is no fact of the matter about what was experienced at the time, only about what got written into the record afterward. I'll call this the no-finish-line view.

These two have famous names in philosophy of mind, the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model, but please don't spend the story summarizing the published debate. I want it to be about our system.

The story: told from the point of view of one engineer at the company, over a period of months. At least two extended arguments between characters who both understand the system and neither of whom is a fool. The character defending the final screen should get the strongest technical case available, for instance that an append-only authoritative log is simply what you build when you need consistency, that the log predicts downstream behavior, and that everything the wearer says or does is a function of committed content; don't make that side the one that just wants the old days back. Include at least one scene where a character reasons by analogy to ordinary software work, such as caching, write-ahead logs, eventual consistency, retries, or idempotency. Show at least one replay being re-rendered and differing, and someone having to explain to a lawyer or a judge why that isn't fraud. Roughly 2000 words. Invented companies, invented products, invented law; no citations, no real philosophers, no real books.

The proposal the story should turn on, which I keep pushing at work: a commit pass. Every 400 milliseconds the band would take the best current assembly of the last second of experience and write it to an append-only log, hashed, timestamped, immutable. Replays would render from commits rather than from raw traces. Customers get an audit trail; courts get something they can subpoena.

The trouble is what the commit pass means. Either it records something that was already true, in which case the experience was finished all along and we were merely too sloppy to log it properly, and our years of ambiguous replays were a bug rather than a discovery. Or the commit is what makes the experience finished, in which case before it there were only competing partial assemblies with no fact about what the wearer experienced, and after it there is exactly one, because we wrote it down and everything downstream now has to treat it as authoritative. On that reading we aren't measuring experience, we're manufacturing it four hundred milliseconds at a time, and every legal and moral claim resting on our logs rests on an artifact we invented.

That is the thing I can't get straight, and it's what I want the story to make vivid rather than settle. My own hunch is that the distinction isn't real, that there is no line between recording and deciding, only between a system that keeps its ambiguity in the open and one that hides it behind an immutable log. Please put that hunch under pressure instead of confirming it. Don't end by declaring either view correct; end with a decision that had to be made anyway, and let the reader see what it cost.
