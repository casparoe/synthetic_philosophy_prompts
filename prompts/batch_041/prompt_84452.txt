I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on modal epistemology, and I would like a puzzle of mine made concrete. Two competent thinkers consider the same alleged possibility. One says she conceives it clearly, and treats that as evidence that it is possible; the other says she cannot conceive it at all, and treats that as evidence that it is impossible. Neither is confused about what the words mean, neither has made an obvious logical slip, and neither will be moved by the other's testimony. What strikes me is the awkward position this leaves the notion of conceivability in. A verdict of conceivability looks like a psychological report about the person who issues it — and reports of what one can and cannot imagine are difficult to challenge from the outside — yet it is used as evidence about the world, where the two thinkers cannot both be right.

Give me an example of a conceptual issue that this situation exposes, in the form of a single constructed case: state the disputed possibility, say what each thinker takes herself to be doing, and make it clear why no further information about either thinker's psychology would settle the disagreement. Avoid the well-worn territory — no consciousness-based scenarios, no identity statements of the water-and-its-chemical-composition kind — and choose or invent a dispute in which it is not obvious that one party is simply confused. Then say briefly what the example shows about what it would take for a conceivability verdict to be assessable as correct rather than merely sincere.
