I have been working through, on my own and without any academic training, Kant's 1784 essay on the philosophy of history — the one usually translated as "Idea for a Universal History from a Cosmopolitan Point of View" (I am reading the Lewis White Beck translation). I can follow the individual sentences but I cannot tell what kind of claim the essay is making, and I suspect the whole thing rests on a step I am not seeing. I would like help with three connected passages rather than a general summary of the essay's significance.

The first is the one that sets up the whole problem:

"Since men in their endeavors behave, on the whole, not just instinctively, like the brutes, nor yet like rational citizens of the world according to some agreed-on plan, no history of man conceived according to a plan seems to be possible, as it might be possible to have such a history of bees or beavers. One cannot suppress a certain indignation when one sees men’s actions on the great world-stage and finds, beside the wisdom that appears here and there among individuals, everything in the large woven together from folly, childish vanity, even from childish malice and destructiveness. In the end, one does not know what to think of the human race, so conceited in its gifts. Since the philosopher cannot presuppose any [conscious] individual purpose among men in their great drama, there is no other expedient for him except to try to see if he can discover a natural purpose in this idiotic course of things human. In keeping with this purpose, it might be possible to have a history with a definite natural plan for creatures who have no plan of their own."

The second is the principle I take to be doing the real work underneath it:

"All natural capacities of a creature are destined to evolve completely to their natural end. Observation of both the outward form and inward structure of all animals confirms this of them. An organ that is of no use, an arrangement that does not achieve its purpose, are contradictions in the teleological theory of nature. If we give up this fundamental principle, we no longer have a lawful but an aimless course of nature, and blind chance takes the place of the guiding thread of reason."

And the third, which is where my confusion about "laws" starts:

"Yet the annual tables of them in the major countries prove that they occur according to laws as stable as [those of] the unstable weather, which we likewise cannot determine in advance, but which, in the large, maintain the growth of plants the flow of rivers, and other natural events in an unbroken uniform course."

Here is what I want explained, sentence by sentence where it matters.

First, when Kant speaks of Nature as willing things, producing the men who will write the history, and setting purposes, is he asserting something about how the world actually is, or is he saying that this is the only way we can make sense of history at all? I want to know how to tell the difference from the text itself, and whether the essay would be damaged if the answer turned out to be the second.

Second, why does the bee and beaver comparison cut the way it does? It looks to me as though animals with instinct have exactly the kind of plan-like regularity he says we cannot get from humans, and humans with reason have exactly the kind of irregularity that he says makes a plan-conceived history impossible. If that is right, is the comparison a concession or an argument, and what is supposed to follow from it?

Third, the second passage presents what looks like a stark choice: keep the teleological principle, or else we have not a lawful but an aimless course of nature. Is that a genuine dichotomy or a rhetorical one? I want to know whether Kant thinks that dropping purposes means dropping lawfulness altogether, and what someone who denies any purpose in nature but still expects to find causal or statistical regularities in history would say to him. This is where the third passage puzzles me, because regularities of the weather and of the annual tables of births and deaths seem to be exactly the sort of thing one can have without any purpose at all, and I cannot tell whether Kant means those tables to be evidence for a plan or merely evidence that human affairs are not chaotic.

Fourth, and this is my central question: does the essay end up asserting a law of history? I have read somewhere that a philosopher named Popper objected that there are no laws of historical development, only trends, and that treating trends as laws is what leads people to prophesy about the future of mankind. If Kant is doing something like that, I want to see precisely where the offending step occurs. If he is not — if what he has are laws about human capacities, or about what a philosopher is entitled to presuppose — then I want to understand how the essay avoids the objection, and whether the notion of a purpose that by hypothesis no one involved can know is a genuine explanation or a way of refusing to explain.

Finally, please tell me which single sentence in the three passages above is load-bearing, in the sense that the rest of the argument collapses without it, and what the essay would have to look like if that sentence were false. I would rather have a plain-language account of the machinery than a survey of how the essay has been received, and if a term like "regulative," "teleological," or "empirical law" is needed to answer me, please explain it rather than assume it.
