I run a small AI safety reading group out of my living room — half the people are ML, half are philosophers — and we've spent the last three sessions re-fighting the same fight about worst-case vs. average-case alignment guarantees without ever getting anywhere. I want to stop arguing and just map the space, so please build me a decision tree / taxonomy of the positions that I can hand out.

How I want it built:

- Every branching node has to be a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Concretely answerable, no jargon-gated questions like "do you accept distributional robustness as the right frame?" If a question only makes sense once you've already picked a side, it's the wrong question.
- Leaves get the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody in particular holds it) plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment it has to swallow.
- Work from the logical space first, then locate actual views in it. Where a combination of answers is one nobody occupies, include it as a labeled gap and tell me whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's coherent but unattractive, or because it's merely unexplored.
- If two leaves come out identical on every question I can actually answer, say so rather than pretending the tree seperates them.

I want these distinctions to show up as nodes somewhere in the tree, since they're the ones we keep collapsing:

1. Who picks the input? Nobody (I've fixed a distribution in advance), an optimizer that can see the model, or an optimizer that also gets to pick the deployment environment the model ends up in.
2. How many shots does the adversary get? One query, a batch, or unbounded adaptive queries. I care about this one alot — a model that's fine under one adversarial query and folds after a million of them seems like a genuinely different position from one that's fine per query but drifts over a long conversation, and we keep treating those as the same thing.
3. Is the guarantee about the artifact or the process? ("this model won't do X" vs. "the pipeline would have caught it if it did.")
4. Is the claim about catastrophic outcomes specifically, or about expected harm?
5. Can the adversary also pick the training procedure, or only deployment conditions?
6. When someone says a bad case "isn't reachable," is that an empirical claim about the world or a claim about the model's inductive bias?

Leaves I need in there: full adversarial worst-case (and tell me if that's actually occupied or just a strawman), worst-case relative to a stated threat model, fixed-distribution average case, worst-case over a family of distributions, the inductive-bias / "bad cases are unreachable" view, the process-not-artifact view, the catastrophe-constraint view, and the deflationary "worst case is ill-defined because the quantifier is a free parameter" view. Also place one more thing for me: a view I'll call the credible-auditor position — worst case only up to the point where an auditor with realistic resources would actually have flagged the threat. I don't know where it lands and that's half the reason I want the tree.

Eight to ten leaves is fine. Please don't cite papers or give me a reading list — we're not doing a lit review, and honestly if you name specific work half the group will just argue about whether you've characterized it right, which is the opposite of what I want here.
