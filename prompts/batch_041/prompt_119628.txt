I am finishing a study of the abandonment of terraced agriculture in a Mediterranean valley between the middle of the nineteenth century and the middle of the twentieth, and in particular of one landowner who, in the 1870s, reorganised the irrigation of the upper slopes and introduced a cash crop that his neighbours copied within a generation. The terraces were gone by the 1950s. A colleague and I disagree about what such a study is for, and he has twice settled the disagreement by appealing to a picture that I associate with Braudel and that is, I concede, very common in historical sociology: history as a body of water. On this picture the seabed, the deep currents, the tides, and the film of foam on the surface correspond to four orders of historical reality — environment and geography; the slow structures of economy and society; the medium-term conjunctures of prices, demography, and taste; and, at the top, events together with the individuals who appear to make them. The event, on this account, is a crest of foam: the point of greatest agitation and least information, carried by movements it does not cause. From the picture my colleague draws the methodological conclusion he wants. Biographies of improvers, and accounts of single valleys, are studies of foam. The causal weight lies in the deep water. A historian who ignores the depths will mistake the surface for the substance. He adds that my landowner may have altered the timing of the valley's abandonment by decades, but that timing is itself foam.

A third colleague proposes replacing the sea with plate tectonics: slow plates, slowly accumulating strain, and sudden ruptures. On this version events are earthquakes — rare, violent, destructive, but caused by, and merely releasing, pressures that built up over centuries. She considers this a strict improvement on the sea, since it gives events both a cause and a function, and she thinks it strengthens the case against my kind of study. I am not persuaded, but I cannot say exactly why.

What I want is an audit of the picture, not a restatement of it. I know the three registers of time and I do not need reading recommendations or a survey of the literature. I want to know how far the sea picture holds: what corresponds to what, where the correspondence fails, which features of the sea the picture quietly transfers to history without warrant, and whether my colleague's conclusion — that events and individuals are foam, and that the study of them is therefore idle — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Please also say what would count as evidence against the foam claim, and whether the plate-tectonics variant improves matters or merely relocates the difficulty.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order, at roughly the stated lengths:

1. "The analogy and its burden" (~150 words): the picture as my colleague uses it, and the precise conclusion it is being made to carry.
2. "The correspondence" (~250 words): a table mapping source onto target, with any element of the picture that has no counterpart on the historical side flagged as such.
3. "Where the mapping breaks" (~450 words): the disanalogies, separating those that bear on the conclusion from those that are merely picturesque.
4. "What the sea picture imports" (~250 words): the features of water, tides, and foam that the analogy smuggles into the target, and what would have to be true of history for the import to be legitimate.
5. "The plate-tectonics variant" (~300 words): whether it is better, worse, or merely different, with reasons.
6. "Verdict and a better picture" (~300 words): whether the conclusion survives, what evidence would tell against it, and the best analogy available if neither the sea nor the plates will do — applied concretely to the valley and its landowner.
