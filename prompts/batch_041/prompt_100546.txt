I am a graduate student in philosophy of religion, and I have spent the better part of a year trying to write a seminar paper on a dispute that I have come to believe has genuinely stalled — not for any lack of cleverness on either side, but because the two camps seem constitutionally unable to reach one another. Please take on the role of a philosopher of language with deep expertise in the later Wittgenstein and in the epistemology of religious belief, and help me diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Work carefully and in stages: begin by stating, in your own words and as sympathetically as you can, the strongest version of each side; then give me your diagnosis; then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress.

The dispute concerns how to read Wittgenstein's scattered remarks on religious belief. On one influential reading, the believer who says that he believes in a Last Judgment is not asserting a hypothesis that evidence could confirm or disconfirm, but is giving expression to a commitment whose sense is fixed by a form of life; on that reading, to ask whether religious belief is rational or justified in the ordinary evidential sense is to smuggle a standard from one language game into another, and the question is a category mistake. On the opposing reading, this move purchases immunity from criticism at the price of making religious belief say nothing at all — it turns the religious language game into a wall — and it cannot be right, because believers themselves argue, defend, revise, and treat their claims as true or false, and because the position seems to entail that the believer and the atheist cannot even contradict one another. For decades the exchange has simply repeated itself: the defenders say the critics presuppose exactly the universal standard of rationality that Wittgenstein meant to put in question; the critics say the defenders are either saying something trivially true (people use words in different ways) or something incredible (religious disagreement is impossible).

A colleague has floated a hypothesis that I find tempting but cannot assess: that the two sides are not really disagreeing about Wittgenstein at all, but about the word "justification" — one side means justification within a practice, the other means justification of the practice — and that once that equivocation is exposed, most of the apparent conflict dissolves. I want to know whether that is the correct diagnosis or merely a dodge. So: is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of rationality, a verbal one about how "belief" and "rational" are to be used, two camps answering different questions under a single heading, or a sign that the question itself is ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would move the debate — a distinction, a counterexample, a change of question — or argue why nothing of the sort should be expected.
