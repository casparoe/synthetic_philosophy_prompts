Two doctoral students in my department, call them A and B, spent most of a seminar break arguing about G. E. M. Anscombe's claim that Truman's decision to bomb Hiroshima and Nagasaki was not merely a grave wrong but murder, because Truman chose to kill the innocent as a means to ending the war. I was the only other person in the room and took no part in it; what follows is my reconstruction of the exchange, which I have tried to keep fair to both.

(1) A said the verdict follows from a principle Anscombe held without exception: it is always wrong to intend the death of the innocent as a means, whatever good may come of it. Truman's end — ending the war — was good, but the deaths of tens of thousands of noncombatants were not an unfortunate side effect of the bombing; they were the instrument by which the surrender was procured. On that principle the bombing was murder, and talk of a hard call is evasion.

(2) B answered that the deaths were not the instrument at all. What coerced the Japanese leadership was the demonstration of a weapon of unprecedented power, and the destruction of the two cities was the evidence of that power. Suppose the same terror could have been produced by a detonation over an empty bay, with observers and a broadcast: the surrender might have come just the same. So the killing was foreseeable and possibly unnecessary, but not the means. B added a second point: if the deaths being the means is enough for murder, then the naval blockade, which knowingly starved civilians, and the area bombing of German cities, are murder too — so A's principle condemns much of the Allied war effort, which looks like a reductio.

(3) A replied that B's counterfactual is almost certainly false as history — the Japanese leadership was moved by the annihilation of two real cities, not by a rumour of power — and that B's second point confuses what is foreseen with what is intended. A commander who blockades a port to cut an enemy's supply and foresees that some civilians will starve does not intend those deaths; a commander who levels a city in order to break a government's will does intend the deaths of its inhabitants. The test is not whether people died, but whether the deaths were the route by which the end was reached.

(4) B then offered a sharper version of the objection. If the deaths being the route means anything, it should mean that the end could not have been reached had the victims lived — that the deaths were causally necessary. But by that test the blockade's dead were necessary too, since Japan was starved into submission; so A must either call the blockade murder or admit the test is not what A wants. Worse, B said, an act has no description of its own. The same raid can be described as securing surrender by shock, with deaths foreseen, or as killing civilians in order to coerce a government, with deaths intended. If which description we accept is settled by our prior verdict about the case, the distinction cannot be what settles the case.

(5) A conceded the redescription point had force but said it proved too much, since the same argument would dissolve the difference between negligence and malice. And A insisted the counterfactual test was too crude: a means is something the agent uses to bring the end about, and the blockade's dead were not used — they were what the agent was willing to let happen — whereas the bomb's dead were used, because their deaths were what was supposed to move the Japanese government.

(6) B said that A had simply restated the distinction under pressure rather than defended it: whether the deaths were used or merely permitted is exactly the question in dispute, and A's phrase about being willing to let it happen already encodes the answer.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general essay on the ethics of bombing. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and give a brief justification. Then say what the exchange has established about the underlying question: is the distinction between intending death and merely foreseeing it a genuine criterion that decides the Truman case, or is B's redescription objection fatal to using it that way? Finally, name the most important consideration neither of them raised, and say which of the two has the stronger overall position, and why.
