My little sister's wedding is in two weeks and I'm doing a speech at the recpetion, and I've backed myself into a corner with it. For a year and a half now our family has been needling her and her fiance about what this has all cost them — he sold his motorbike, he flew back and forth once a month for a year, she turned down a job in another city, and the wedding itself is more than anyone in our family has ever spent on anything. My uncle's line is that it's all a waste. I want to stand up and say it isnt a waste, and I want to do it without one drop of the usual "love is priceless" stuff, because that's cheap and everyone in the room knows it's cheap.

So what I've been reading about is the handicap principle — Zahavi, the peacock's tail thing. The way I understand it, the whole point of a signal is that it has to be expensive, because if it were cheap then anyone could fake it, so only the real thing can afford it. I want the speech built on that and nothing else. Rule I've set for myself: not one line can rest on cheap evidence. "Look at the way they look at each other" — out. "We all knew from day one" — out. Every nice thing I say about them has to be tied to something that actually cost them something.

Two things I half remember from reading around, and I want you to actually use them, not just nod at them. First, it's not really about the cost being a handicap in the wound sense — what matters is who else could afford it. The cost has to land harder on the fakers than on the genuine article. So the test I want applied to every line is "could someone who wasn't serious have paid this too?", not "how much did it hurt?". Second, I'm fairly sure there are versions of this where honest signals need no cost at all when both sides basically want the same thing — which I think means the sacrifices only prove something where their interests actually diverge. Like, he could have had most of what he wanted from her without ever giving up the bike. That's what makes the bike mean something. I also remember some distinction between a signal that literally can't be faked, like how tall you are, and one that's just expensive; if that's useful, use it.

The room: her half of it is people from her department — she did a PhD on bird song, so a good chunk of them know this biology better than I do and will notice if I get it wrong. The other half is our family, including my uncle, who thinks I overthink everything and will be three drinks in by then. Five minutes, funny where it fits, and I have to be honest — I'm not going to pretend the motorbike was a smart move, just that it counted. Don't explain the biology to the room; they'll either know it already or not care. It should be doing the work underneath.

When you're done, give me a small table so I can check myself against my own rule. Three columns: What I claim about them | What it cost them | Who couldn't have paid it. Six rows max, one line per cell, and if a row can't honestly be filled in, say so in the cell rather than inventing something.
