The topic is the standoff between the self-sampling and self-indication approaches to anthropic reasoning, and the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment that is the standard weapon against the latter. The background you may take as given, stated here so the proposal need not re-derive it: the self-sampling approach says you should reason as though you were a random sample drawn from the set of all observers in some reference class, which is what drives doomsday-style conclusions and saddles the view with a reference-class problem; the self-indication approach says that the fact that an observer with your evidence exists is itself evidence, and that hypotheses entailing more such observers therefore deserve proportionally more credence; and the presumptuous philosopher is the figure who, armed with the self-indication approach, claims to settle whether the cosmos is modest or enormous by armchair reasoning alone, since the hypothesis positing vastly more observers wins overwhelmingly on credence, which nearly everyone finds absurd. My half-formed idea, which the proposal should turn into something precise. I suspect the two views are answering different questions and that each is right about its own. One question is what to expect, in the sense of how the world is likely to be given that I am in it; the other is how to weight hypotheses when the hypotheses themselves differ in how many observers they posit and when the decision at issue has consequences that scale with the number of observers. My conjecture is that self-sampling-style normalization is the correct constraint on the first kind of probability and self-indication-style weighting is the correct constraint on the second, and that the presumptuous philosopher argument lands only because it reads an answer to the second question as if it were an answer to the first.
