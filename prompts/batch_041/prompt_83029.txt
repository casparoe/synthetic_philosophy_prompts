I teach a seminar on punishment, and I keep circling one puzzle from a Norwegian-style prison I toured last spring. The whole design is a single idea: life inside should resemble life outside, so the cells have kitchens, the men do their own shopping, health care and schooling are provided by the county's ordinary services rather than by the prison, and the officers are unarmed and mostly there to talk to people. My host kept saying the point is that they're citizens serving a sentence, not inmates. But the regime is compulsory -- you go to your job, you cook, you keep your room up, and refusing costs you the privileges. And the same staff who insist on all that also talk about crime as something deprivation caused, which sounds like it drains the responsibility out of it.

What does a place like this actually presuppose? I want a definite answer, not a survey of options. Tell me the theory of punishment it commits to (and whether it can honestly call itself punishment at all), what view of the person and of the state's standing to enforce a particular way of life the compulsory normalcy requires, and give me a verdict on wether the two halves -- caused by circumstance, addressed as a fully responsible citizen -- can be held together or whether one is doing all the work and the other is window dressing. Then say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined: which parts a retributivist or a deterrence theorist could sign on to without changing anything. If you think it's just incoherent, commit to that and tell me where it breaks.
