I teach a seminar on emotion and I keep running into a pattern I don't have a name for, so I'm hoping you can help me carve it up. Answer as someone who knows the philosophy of emotion cold -- I'd rather have the distinction sharpened than get a label slapped on it. Before you settle, brainstorm the neighboring ideas, say which ones tempt you and why, then commit, and be explicit about where your answer's edges are, including the cases that sit right on the line. I care alot more about the carving than the word.

Here's the thing. We grade emotions in two ways without noticing we're switching. Sometimes we ask whether the emotion has the world right: no dog in the yard, so the fear is off-target. Other times we ask whether the person was in a position to feel it: the dog really was there and snarling, so the fear was fair enough. Then there's a third thing, and the third thing is what I can't place.

Case one. A student tells me she distrusted her new roommate from day one, and she was right, he turned out to be a liar. I ask what set off the distrust. It was that he came from a town she'd decided was full of liars. So the suspicion landed on a real liar, but the thing that made him a liar isn't the thing that made her suspicious. She thinks the outcome settles it. I think something came out right and something went wrong, and they aren't the same something.

Case two. Another student feels awful about having snapped at his mother. He did snap at her, and it did hurt her, so the guilt is aimed at a real wrong, and he has decent evidence for it. But when I push, the guilt turns out to be mostly about wanting to be the sort of person who feels guilty about that sort of thing. Right target, fine evidence, but the reason he feels it isn't the reason the act was wrong.

Case three, maybe the same, maybe not. A colleague says her fear of flying is reasonable because planes do crash. They do. But the fear isn't tracking crash rates at all -- it's a feeling she has, and the crash rates are the story she tells afterward to make it respectable.

In all three, the emotion points at something real, and in all three it got there by a route that has nothing much to do with what makes the thing real. Its like the emotion is right and also not answerable to anything -- right for reasons that wouldn't survive being said out loud.

And this has to bear on the old question of what emotions are. If they're judgments they should be answerable to reasons; if they're perceptions they're more like the senses and answerable only to how the world is; if they're just feelings then none of this applies. My cases don't sit in any of those boxes, which is either a real point or me missing a distinction everyone else already has.

So: is there a standard name for an emotion that gets the value right but by a route that doesn't support it? What are the closest things already on the shelf, how do they differ from what I've described, and which fits best? If nothing fits, coin something and define it, and tell me what it leaves out -- especially whether case three belongs with cases one and two.
