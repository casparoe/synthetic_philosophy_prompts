Me and my mate Dan have been going round in circles for about a year now on what actually counts as art, and I've decided the only way to settle it is to write down every case where I'm sure of my answer and see if there's a rule hiding underneath. So here's my gut, one by one. The bower a satin bowerbird builds, with all the bits of blue plastic and bottle tops arranged in front of it: not art, however gorgeous it is, and I don't care how long he spent on it. A lump of driftwood my aunt pulled off a beach and screwed to her kitchen wall because she liked the shape: art, even though nobody made it and nobody would call her an artist. A pebble I put in my pocket on that same walk because I liked it: not art. My nephew's drawing of a dinosaur, sellotaped to the fridge, done by a five-year-old who's never heard of a gallery: art. The paintings the elephants do at those sanctuaries, where the keeper loads the brush and the elephant drags it about: not art. A telly programme that was nothing but a train journey filmed from the front of the train for seven hours, no talking: art. A livestream of somebody's fireplace running all day: not art. A poem a chatbot wrote when I asked it for one: not art. Six of those poems, after I'd read about four hundred of them and picked those six and put them in a particular order and printed them as a little booklet: art. A tiler doing an ordinary bathroom but taking a proper pride in the pattern: not art. That same tiling, done on a wall in a gallery: art. A hand-thrown teapot that I actually make tea in: art. A birthday cake I spent a whole weekend on: not art, it's a cake, we ate it. A war memorial: art. A full-size replica of a war memorial built as a film set: not art, it's a prop. My uncle's garden, which he's been shaping for forty years: art, and he'd be furious to hear it. My mate's garden, which he mows every fortnight: not art.

The rule I keep reaching for is something like: it's art if somebody meant it to be looked at for its own sake rather than used, and the looking is the point. But that definately falls apart on the bowerbird (no intention — but then what about the driftwood, which nobody intended either, and the nephew, who never meant anything by it?) and on the teapot (which is for using). So then I try: it's all about being put in front of an audience — a gallery, a booklet, a telly slot — but that throws out the fridge dinosaur and my uncle's garden, and I'm not giving those up. Can you tell me what principle actually fits these, or the nearest thing to one, and be honest with me about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop? I'd also like to know what it says about three I genuinely can't decide: a forger's copy of a painting, which I think is still art even though what he did was fraud; a painting of mine that got ruined when the cellar flooded and honestly looks much better now, which I don't think makes the ruined version a new artwork; and a recording my cousin made of our gran just describing her kitchen, with no artistic intent anywhere in sight, which I'd still call art and can't explain why. Write it in British English please, and don't go easy on the ones I've got wrong.
