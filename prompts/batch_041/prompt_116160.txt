I'm a retired entomologist — forty-odd years of IPM work, a lot of it extension, standing in growers' fields with a beat-up sweep net — and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy, slowly and badly, mostly to figure out what my colleagues and I were actually claiming all those years when we sounded so sure of ourselves. So please take on the role of a philosopher of science who also knows a fair bit of toxicology and regulatory risk assessment, and think this through carefully rather than giving me the polished summary.

Here's the thing I want to unpick. Last month I sat through a county hearing on whether to restrict a neonicotinoid seed treatment, in the middle of the usual fight about whether the residues turning up in nectar and pollen are doing real harm to pollinators. A representative for the manufacturer stood up and said, with enormous confidence, that the dose makes the poison — everything is toxic at some dose, nothing is at none — and that since the measured field residues sat below the levels that kill bees outright in acute lab tests, there was simply nothing to worry about and the proposed restrictions were unscientific. A few people nodded. I've said that sentence myself probably a hundred times and it has always felt more like a truism than a move in an argument, so I couldnt put my finger on what was wrong with the way he used it, and I've been stewing about it ever since.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict on the slogan as a whole: start by brainstorming every distinct precise claim that one-liner could be making, from the trivially true to the genuinely radical, and then tell me for each whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical thesis, or plainly false. That means asking what "dose" would have to mean for the claim to bite — what the animal or the grower is dosed with, versus what gets absorbed, versus what reaches the target site — and what "poison" would have to mean, whether a chemical kind, a legal category, or an effect. Does the slogan assert that a threshold always exists below which there's no effect at all? That harm rises monotonically with dose? That timing and duration of exposure don't matter, only the total? That individuals and species don't vary much in susceptibility? That one chemical considered alone is the right unit of analysis? I've got in the back of my mind things like endocrine disruption, sensitization and allergy, developmental windows, and hormesis, and I want to know which of the readings those actually threaten and which they leave untouched. Then the part I really care about: say which reading the manufacturer's man must have been relying on for the sentence to do the work he needed it to do — closing the argument — and whether that reading survives scrutiny. If the honest answer is that the slogan is true in some boring sense but useless in the sense he needed, or that he quietly slid from one reading to another mid-sentence, say that too, and tell me what a genuinely careful version of his point would have had to establish before it could carry any weight about the restrictions.
