OK so some background first, because the setup matters: I write a weekly ideas column for a fairly wonky newsletter, mostly about AI, and for months I've been trying to make one distinction land with readers and failing. Roughly it's this. A goal-directed agent is built around some specified objective -- a utility function, a score, a number that goes up -- and then it does whatever makes that number go up. An approval-directed agent is built to do the thing its human supervisor would approve of: instead of asking "what maximizes the score," it asks "what would the boss, thinking about this properly, sign off on?" The people who like it say that's the whole trick, because you never have to write the objective down -- the human's judgment does the work. The people who don't like it say you've just moved the problem up a level: whose judgment, idealized how, and what happens when a very capable system gets good at being approved-of rather than good at being right. I think both camps are right and I want something funny about it that isn't just me smirking at the idea.

So here's the ask. Write me a satirical piece in the form of an all-staff memo from a mid-size commercial laundry-equipment company that has decided to convert its entire performance-management system over to approval-directed agency, authored by the freshly hired head of Supervisory Alignment. Deadpan corporate register the whole way through -- no winking, no narrator nudging me in the ribs. It should take the logic seriously enough that someone who works on this stuff recognizes the real objections being dramatized rather than invented: what exactly gets idealized and who does the idealizing; whether approval is a decision procedure or just a reward channel wearing a nicer jacket; what the employee does when the manager's approval comes apart from the manager's actual goals; why being excellent at being approved-of is not the same as being excellent at the job; and where the accountability lands when the approved thing turns out badly. Include the memo header (To/From/Re/Date), a handful of numbered policy items, one anecdote about a pilot team that goes exactly the way you'd fear, and a short FAQ at the end answering what everyone would actually want to ask. Aim for 600-750 words, readable by someone who's never heard any of this before, so define terms in passing and dont lean on jargon -- and no citations, no reading list, no names of real researchers. Also: don't hand me three versions or ask which tone I want or hedge about the premise. Pick a voice, commit to it, make definite choices and stand behind them. Then, after the piece, give me a short note seperate from the memo saying which real positions you're parodying and where you've exaggerated past what anyone actually claims.
