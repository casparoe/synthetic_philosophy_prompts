I'm a third-year philosophy student, and the global justice unit of my political philosophy course requires each of us to bring a study instrument to the seminar. I'd like a decision tree — a flowchart, not an annotated list of -isms — that takes a reader from a starting commitment to a named position on the question of whether compatriots have claims on us that strangers do not.

Please build it from the logical space first: identify the distinctions that actually divide the positions, then locate the positions within that space, and then tell me which combinations of answers are left unoccupied and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended. Roughly a dozen branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves.

These are the distinctions I want the tree to turn on, because our seminar keeps collapsing them:

- whether the special claim is a permission (one may favour compatriots), a duty (one must), or a weighting (their interests count for more), and whether these can come apart within a single view;
- whether the ground of the claim is the relationship itself (the association is good in its own right, so partiality is a response to a good), shared coercive institutions, a fair-play or reciprocity condition, or a cultural fact about who one is;
- whether the claim is conditional on the institutions being just to everyone inside them, and what becomes of it when they are not;
- whether the claim covers distribution only, or also membership and exclusion at the border, and whether the tree can house a view that answers these two differently;
- whether it is individuals or states that are permitted to be partial, i.e. a division of labour between personal and institutional morality;
- whether compatriot claims have lexical priority, operate only once a global floor has been met, or merely break ties.

Every node should be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing where it leads — nothing like 'are you a nationalist or a cosmopolitan?'. The tree should separate views that are routinely conflated: for instance, a position that licenses cultural partiality but not distributive partiality, and one that licenses distributive partiality but not exclusion.

The leaves should include the familiar options — liberal nationalism, constitutional or civic patriotism, cosmopolitan egalitarianism, coercion-based state-centred views, global sufficientarianism, associative-duty views, and an impartialist option — but add any leaf the logical space requires even if it has no familiar name.

Then test the tree against the following cases, saying which leaf each one pushes a reader toward: a long-term resident who lacks citizenship; a compatriot who is a net contributor to a scheme that is unjust to a minority of compatriots; a compatriot living abroad who has opted out of the scheme; a young child or a severely cognitively disabled compatriot, who cannot reciprocate; a compatriot whose advantage rests on a historical injustice done to foreigners; and future members of the political community, who will bear costs we impose now. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and finish by naming the single node that does the most work in the whole instrument.
