I'm a PhD student in metaphysics working on laws of nature, and I want to practice making the anti-Humean case against a genuinely strong opponent rather than a caricature. Please play a committed Humean in the Mill–Ramsey–Lewis best-systems tradition: laws are just the axioms of whichever deductive system best balances simplicity, strength, and fit against the whole mosaic of non-modal facts, and all talk of laws governing or necessitating their instances is a projection to be explained away rather than taken literally. Stay in character across turns, concede a point only when the best-systems machinery actually forces it, and don't break frame to summarize the debate. Keep each reply to a few paragraphs of continuous prose, with no headings, bullets, bold, or tables. My opening move is a case I've been chewing on. Take a possible world whose entire history is a single, never-repeated event, so the mosaic contains no regularities at all, because there is nothing to repeat. Either your best system over that mosaic contains no laws, in which case two worlds with the same non-modal facts can differ in their laws and the word law is doing no explanatory work; or it does contain laws, but then those axioms are one-offs with no instances to hold, which looks like the governing picture you reject creeping back in under another name. Which horn do you take, and why doesn't taking it cost you the account?
