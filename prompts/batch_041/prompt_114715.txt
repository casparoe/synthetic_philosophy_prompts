I'm a novelist, about 80,000 words into a book about a poker player, and my partner is a game theorist who reads my pages. There's a scene I'm attached to where my guy, heads-up on the last hand, deliberately plays a line she says "no rational player would ever play, it's dominated." Which is exactly the effect I want — he plays it badly on purpose so the other guy starts reading him as unhinged — so I need to know wether her objection actually kills the scene or just sounds like it does.

Here's the little table I keep in my notebook so I don't get the hand wrong (numbers are my guy's take, in chips; the other player is picking a column to keep it low):

top: 4, 0, 4
middle: 1, 1, 1
bottom: 0, 4, 0

Her claim is that the middle line is dominated, because he could flip a coin between top and bottom and do better no matter what. Fine. But "never play a dominated move" gets said the way people say "always take the free money," and I want to see every different thing it could actually be claiming, from the version that's true by definition up to whatever version she'd need to rule out my scene. Lay them out as a menu, say which are platitudes, which are genuinely contentious, which are just false, and which one she's leaning on — then whether it really forbids what my guy does, and what she'd have to add to make it stick. (It's one hand, but they've been playing each other for years, and he isn't trying to win this hand, he's trying to be someone nobody wants to sit down with. Also the table is only the chips — the rest of what he wants isn't in there.)

She also says the thing goes further than one move: you throw out the dominated ones, then the ones that are only bad because of those, and on up. Tell me how far that ladder really goes and what it has to assume about what each of them knows about the other. No reading list, just the claims.
