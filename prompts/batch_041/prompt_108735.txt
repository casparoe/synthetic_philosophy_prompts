I am preparing a ninety-minute session for a departmental reading group — mostly philosophers, plus two cognitive scientists — who are fluent with Bayes' rule and ordinary conditionalization but have never met a case in which the evidence arrives as a conditional. The case I mean to build the session around is this: an officer is dropped somewhere she cannot survey, into a region divided two ways at once, into a red half and a blue half and, independently, into an area assigned to the second company and an area assigned to headquarters, so that she begins with four equally likely quadrants. By radio she is told that the operator cannot say whether she is in the red or the blue, but can say that she is in either the red region or the second-company area. What should her credences become? The session is not about the arithmetic; it is about the discovery that what she was told does not by itself settle the answer — that someone will want to conditionalize on the disjunction, someone else will insist a message silent about red versus blue cannot move those odds, and these reactions cannot both be right.

Do not explain the problem to me; plan how I should teach it. Give me the order in which the pieces must come so that the indeterminacy arrives as a conclusion rather than an announcement — what has to be in place before the constraint can even be written down, and what depends on what; the single case or variant that should carry the session, and what it must have that the officer's case perhaps lacks; the misconceptions the group will bring or form — I expect "there is one correct posterior and we merely lack the rule" and "being told a conditional is just conditionalizing on it" — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; the question I should put at the end to tell whether they have understood why the problem is a problem rather than a puzzle with a solution; and what I should say I am deliberately leaving out at this level. Commit to one plan with definite choices rather than surveying alternatives, in under 600 words.
