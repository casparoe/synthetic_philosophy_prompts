You are an expert in decision theory and game theory who also writes comedy for audiences of philosophers, effective altruists, and rationalists. Think carefully before you write. Begin by brainstorming three or four concrete scenarios in which the unilateralist's curse could play out, weigh which of them has the best comic potential, and then write a single joke — setup plus punchline, at most a short paragraph — based on the one you chose.

So that the joke is built on the right structure rather than on the name, here is the concept in plain terms: the unilateralist's curse is the tendency, in a group of agents who share a common goal, for an action to be taken even when the available evidence suggests it would be better if no one took it. Each agent decides independently whether to act, and each acts if their own estimate of the action's value clears the bar. Because the estimates are noisy, the action is triggered whenever any one agent happens to hold a sufficiently optimistic estimate — so a moderately harmful action becomes more likely to occur, not less, as more equally well-meaning and equally well-informed agents are added. Nothing here depends on anyone being selfish, uninformed, or unfaithful to the group's goal. The usual proposed remedy is a principle of conformity: act only if you judge it better that you act than that some other agent, drawn at random, acts instead.

Constraints on the joke:

1. The punchline must turn on the mechanism that makes the curse distinctive — dispersed estimates plus independent unilateral action, with the most optimistic agent supplying the trigger. A reader who knows the concept should recognize the target immediately and should not be able to substitute a generic someone-else-will-do-it joke.
2. It must not collapse into a tragedy-of-the-commons, free-rider, or coordination-failure joke. If your draft would still be funny when every character is purely selfish, or when the problem is that nobody acts, throw it out and start again.
3. Invent the scenario yourself. Avoid the stock settings that circulate in discussions of this idea; prefer a small, specific, slightly absurd situation — a household, a hobby club, a village, a committee — over anything resembling a technology or policy case study.
4. Do not explain the concept inside the joke, and do not append a moral. The humor should come from the situation, not from the vocabulary.
5. If it helps, you may make the punchline depend on the way the curse gets worse as the group grows, or on the gap between what a principle of conformity would have each agent do and what each agent actually does.

After the joke, add two or three sentences explaining which feature of the curse the punchline depends on, and why the same joke would fail if the characters were defecting rather than cooperating in good faith. This is your check that the joke is aimed at the right concept.

Do not include citations, references, reading lists, or attributions; write as though the concept has no literature behind it, only its structure.
