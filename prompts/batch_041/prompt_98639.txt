I am a second-year doctoral student in history, and I have been sitting in on a historiography seminar that has left me with a problem I cannot get straight. The seminar spent several weeks on the objectivity question and settled into three positions: Ranke's ambition to let the sources speak and to show the past as it essentially was, with the archive as the tribunal that settles disputes; Carr's insistence, in What Is History?, that the historical fact is not found but made, since the historian's questions arise out of his own present and it is the question that turns a fact of the past into a fact of history; and Hayden White's argument in Metahistory that the historian's constitutive act is not explanation but emplotment, the same chronicle of events being capable of being shaped as romance, comedy, tragedy or satire, with nothing in the record dictating which. I was struck, incidentally, that White's own table of affinities files Ranke himself under the comic mode, which is hardly how Ranke would have described what he was doing. What none of us could agree on was what any of this looks like when it is pointed at an actual public fight over the past rather than at the philosophy of the thing, so I should like you to treat the Historikerstreit, the West German historians' dispute of the mid-1980s, as the test case.

So that you need not rely on your recall of the details, here is the shape of it as I understand it, and you may take it as given. The dispute was touched off by the Bitburg ceremony of May 1985, at which Reagan and Kohl laid a wreath at a German military cemetery containing Waffen-SS graves, and by the surrounding talk of normalising the German past. In 1986 Ernst Nolte argued in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung that the Nazi past ought to be allowed to pass, that the crimes of the Soviet Union and those of Nazi Germany admitted of no moral difference, and that the Nazi annihilation of the Jews was in some sense a defensive reaction taken in fear of the Bolshevik threat. Andreas Hillgruber wrote of the German catastrophe of 1945 in a way that treated the fate of the German nation as a proper object of identification, and Michael Stürmer, a close adviser to Kohl, argued that a nation lacking a usable past would be politically weakened. Jürgen Habermas replied that this amounted to an apologetic rewriting of the past, and the exchange broadened into a general argument about whether the Holocaust was singular, whether it could legitimately be compared with other mass crimes, and whether historians were in the business of furnishing a nation with a consoling identity. Nobody in the dispute alleged that documents had been falsified; the quarrel was over what the same body of evidence was evidence of.

What I want from you is a case analysis that runs in both directions, and I do not need a bibliography or a reading list, as I know the literature well enough. First, work the theory through the case: say what each of the three positions predicts about this dispute, and who by the lights of each is the one being objective. Which features of the dispute does each position explain well, and where is each simply silent — what, for instance, can Carr say about a quarrel in which both sides share a present and a politics, and does White have anything to say about the fact that one of the emplotted narratives is being advanced by people who were themselves implicated in the events? Second, run the case back through the theory and be willing to find the theories wanting. Does the Historikerstreit vindicate White's claim that the choice of plot answers to the historian's moral and aesthetic commitment rather than to evidence, or does it embarrass that claim — and what should we make of White's own insistence that he is no relativist, since a literary portrayal of past events does not contradict their reality? Does the episode suggest that objectivity is better located in the historian's procedure than in the finished narrative? And I should like you to test a criterion of my own, which may well be a bad one: distinguish three things one might mean by objectivity in history — fidelity to the surviving record, fidelity to the questions of one's own present, and fidelity to the shape of the events themselves, that is, the claim that some sequences of events have a determinate narrative form and others do not. Tell me which of the three each side in the dispute was actually defending, whether any of them could be adjudicated by evidence, and whether the third is coherent at all. I would far rather have a disciplined mapping that admits where the framework goes blank than a tidy verdict.
