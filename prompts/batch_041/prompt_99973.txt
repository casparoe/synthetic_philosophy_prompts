I sit on the evidence appraisal committee of a medical specialty society. We grade each guideline recommendation strong or conditional, and the manual defines strong as "evidence adequate to support the recommendation" — that's the entire rule, no numbers attached. Here's my problem. Our next guideline bundles 42 recommendations, each backed by its own trial or meta-analysis, each of which cleared the usual bar (p<.05, or an interval that excludes no effect). Our methodologist says: if each finding is, say, 95% likely to be right, and they're roughly independant, the chance that all 42 are right is around one in ten. So a strong grade on the bundle is almost certainly wrong somewhere, and she's threatening to vote the whole thing down. The clinicians say that's absurd — nobody reads a bundle as a claim that every single finding is true, and patients need the guideline out. (She's also assuming the findings are independant, which is dubious: several of these trials share patients, endpoints, and probably a common bias.)

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete course — issue the bundle as one strong recommendation, split it into 42 separately graded ones, downgrade the bundle, or attach some kind of fallibility clause the way a book carries a preface admitting the author is only human — and it should say what the manual ought to require for compound recommendations going forward. Give me the reasoning, not just the verdict, and make the strongest case for whichever side you end up rejecting. In particular take seriously that the methodologist may have made a category mistake, since a recommendation is a directive, not a belief that can be true or false.

And please keep straight what's actually settled here versus what people who know this literature still fight about. I don't want the memo telling me the received view has decided it if the pros genuinely haven't. I'd like to know where the live disagreements sit — whether the fix is to drop the rule that lets you conjoin claims you've each accepted, to give up the idea that rational belief is closed under deduction, to stop talking about flat-out belief and work with graded credences instead, or something else entirely.
