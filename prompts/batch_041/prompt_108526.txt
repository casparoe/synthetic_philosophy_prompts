I am presenting a paper at a department colloquium next month and want a Q&A preparation sheet for the discussion that will follow. My thesis is heterodox, and I would rather it be attacked properly than received politely.

My position, in brief. Assertion is governed by exactly one constitutive norm, and that norm is hearer-facing rather than speaker-facing. Call it the reliance-answerability norm: one may assert that p only if one is prepared to answer for the reliance that the assertion licenses — that is, only if one is prepared to bear the costs, to that audience, of the reliance going wrong. Three commitments follow. First, what an assertion licenses, and therefore whether it may be made, depends on what the actual audience is entitled to rely on, not on the speaker's own epistemic or doxastic state. Second, knowledge is consequently neither necessary nor sufficient for permissible assertion; it matters only instrumentally, as evidence about what reliance a speaker can bear. Third, the familiar speaker-side norms — knowledge, truth, justified belief — are best reconstructed as empirical generalizations about which utterances tend to be indemnifiable within a given testimonial economy, not as the norm itself.

The room I will be facing is not sympathetic and not uniform. Four or five people work on dynamic semantics, presupposition, and the conversational scoreboard; they regard talk of norms of assertion as at best a redescription of update potential and will want to know what my norm does that the scoreboard cannot. Two epistemologists work on testimony and think some version of the knowledge norm is basically correct, though they disagree with each other about lottery cases and will not be able to resist re-litigating that. One colleague came to philosophy from law; he will hear my indemnity clause as a warranty and will want to know precisely what kind of obligation it creates, to whom, and whether it is strict or fault-based. Three or four graduate students work in experimental pragmatics and will ask what would count as evidence for or against my view. No one in the room works on the metaphysics of norms, and no one will fight me with the literature.

Please draft the sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from these people, phrased the way they would phrase them, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do if I fumble it. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in roughly ninety seconds, written as I would say it aloud, followed by one sentence on where that reply is weakest. Tell me separately what I should concede in my opening remarks rather than defend, identify the single exchange that has to go my way for the paper to survive, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Please do not give me citations, references, or suggestions for further reading; I need the arguments, not the literature.
