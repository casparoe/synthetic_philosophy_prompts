I am a novelist, and I have given one of my characters — a theoretical physicist who has arrived, with some relish, at a settled fatalism — an argument that I can neither fault nor accept. I would like you, as a philosopher of physics with a specialist's command of the foundations of special relativity, to find its seam. Before you write anything, take a moment to enumerate to yourself the several distinct senses of "present" and of "real" that such an argument might be sliding between; then commit to a diagnosis.

The argument my character puts on the blackboard, which I understand to be the one commonly associated with Rietdijk and Putnam, runs as follows.

(1) An event is real if it is present — if it lies on some observer's now.
(2) Special relativity grants no observer a privileged now. Simultaneity is frame-relative: for any event E, there is some inertial observer whose plane of simultaneity contains E, so that E is present to that observer.
(3) Hence every event lies on someone's now; and by (1), every event is real.
(4) If past, present, and future events are alike real, then nothing is genuinely open; the whole of history is as fixed and finished as a completed manuscript.
(5) No one can alter a finished manuscript.
(6) Therefore no action alters the future — the future is as settled as the past.

(He illustrates (2) with the case, which I owe to Penrose, of two people passing on the street who place the outbreak of a war in Andromeda, respectively, in the settled past and in the still-open future.)

Step (6) is intolerable to me. It empties the novel: if nothing anyone does makes a difference, my characters have no agency to dramatize and the book has no stakes. I suspect, too, that it is not what the physics actually says. Yet I cannot see where the reasoning bends. Is one of the steps invalid as written — and if so, which one, and what equivocation or slide does it conceal? Or is the argument valid, so that the real task is to decide which of (1)–(5) is least costly to abandon, and to say what each abandonment would cost someone who wants to keep the physics?

I am reluctant to surrender (2), which I take to be settled, and I confess I want to keep (3) and (4), since the four-dimensional picture is the one my character finds beautiful; (6) is the thing I cannot have.

Answer in no more than 550 words, and begin with the single step you would reject, or the single premise you would give up. Then price the alternatives — including, if you can, what the block-universe theorist must say to make room for the difference our choices make.
