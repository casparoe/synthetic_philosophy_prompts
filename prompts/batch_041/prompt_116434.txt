Last night I lost an argument to a friend — an entomologist, and rather a good one — about whether an ant colony can have a mind, and what galled me was not that she disagreed but that we could not find the observation that would have decided it. She said that everything a colony does is done by ants; I said that everything she does is done by cells, and that this settles nothing either way; and by two in the morning we were simply describing the same three experiments in two vocabularies and calling each other names. I should like to have the argument out in fiction instead, and I want your help with it. Please write a short story of about three thousand words in which a colony may or may not have a mind, and in which the interesting action is procedural: what a laboratory would actually have to do in order to find out.

The setting, so that we are working from the same materials. A converted glasshouse at a university field station, where a single harvester-ant colony has been kept for the better part of thirty years and has a name — Wren, say — and a pronoun, which the technicians use without irony and the visiting philosophers cannot abide. The lab keeps one logbook under three standing conventions, and I should like you to gloss each of them briefly the first time it appears. The first is the keep test: a mental state may be attributed to the colony only if the attribution predicts something the ant-level account does not, and any attribution that fails is struck through and annotated, so that the log is full of struck-through lines and marginal notes about what the strike was meant to rule out. The second is the one-ant veto: a single worker, by standing in the wrong place or dragging a pheromone trail the wrong way, can reverse an apparent decision of the whole, and the lab has never settled whether to record this as the colony changing its mind or as the colony being interrupted. The third is the fission protocol: the station is to be sold and the colony must be moved, and there is a proposal to move it in two vans as two halves, which raises, without anyone having wished it to, the question of what we are counting — colonies, or minds, or both.

The constraints matter to me more than the plot. The story should turn on at least three of the following four scenes, all of the sort the log would really contain: one in which the intentional description and the ant-level description each make a prediction that the other misses, so that neither vocabulary can simply absorb the other; one in which the colony follows a trail to a feeder abandoned a decade ago, a route no ant now alive has ever walked, and the humans must decide whether to say that the colony remembers it; one in which the workforce is replaced, a cohort at a time, over the course of a season, and nobody can say at what point, if any, Wren stopped being Wren; and one in which the two halves meet again and either recognise one another as nestmates or do not, with the humans arguing about what the answer means. Do not let any character win by authorial fiat, and do not let any of them deliver a lecture on functionalism, emergentism or the intentional stance — I want people arguing about ants. If the story has to end somewhere, end it on a description of the colony that can be read either way, and let the last word go to the technician who keeps the log, who is neither a believer nor a sceptic and who is, I suspect, the only one of them who has actually watched the thing for twenty years.
