Answer with reference to Dennett's own use of it.' And here's what my classmate wrote:

'Dennett's heterophenomenology is the method he proposes for making consciousness a scientific topic. We obviously can't climb into another person's head, so we start from what they say. The investigator records the subject's utterances and treats the whole lot as a text; then, adopting the intentional stance, she attributes beliefs and desires to the subject and builds up what Dennett calls the heterophenomenological world — the world as the subject takes it to be. This world is a fiction, rather like the world of a novel, except that it's grounded in real verbal behaviour: the subject is its author. Since the reports are the data, and it's the subject who actually has the experience, the method has to treat the subject as the authority on what they experience; the theorist's job is to explain why the subject says these things, not to second-guess the reports themselves.

The contrast Dennett is drawing is with two failed extremes. Classical introspectionism took first-person reports at face value and tried to build a science straight out of them, which never worked; behaviourism reacted by ignoring them entirely, which Dennett thinks threw away the most valuable data we have. Heterophenomenology is the middle path — it takes the reports seriously without endorsing their content. This is his version of Husserl's epoché, and he compares it to an anthropologist studying a tribe's belief in ghosts, who records the belief without having to decide whether ghosts exist. So the method itself is neutral on the metaphysical question of whether the things people report are really there. And because the method is neutral, Dennett's own conclusions are purely methodological: he never actually argues that phenomenal consciousness doesn't exist or that qualia are unreal, he only insists that we shouldn't try to build a science on ineffable, unreportable inner qualities. The upshot is that heterophenomenology can't be accused of begging the question against consciousness, since it leaves the existence of experience completely open — the hard problem is simply set aside rather than denied.'
