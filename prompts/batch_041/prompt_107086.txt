I run a weekly game with four friends in which the rulebook is allowed to revise itself: we started from a single standing rule — a change is adopted when a majority of those present vote for it, and every adopted change becomes part of the rulebook — and we have left everything else to be built, amended, and undermined by the players as we go. Because the rules can rewrite the rules (the sort of setup sometimes called Nomic), we keep arriving at moments where I have to judge whether a given change actually took, and my judgments have begun to look like they answer to a principle I cannot quite state. I would like you to find that principle, or to tell me there isn't one. Here, case by case, is what I have concluded. (1) A motion, passed by simple majority, providing that all future changes require a two-thirds vote: I take it to have taken effect and to bind, so that from then on even the repeal of this threshold requires two-thirds. (2) A motion, passed by simple majority, declaring of itself that it may never be repealed or amended by any vote whatsoever: I do not take it to bind; a later simple majority may repeal it, and I would say the earlier motion entrenched nothing at all. (3) A motion, passed by simple majority, transferring the power to amend from the assembly to a single appointed scribe, after which the scribe amended several rules: I take the transfer, and the scribe's amendments, to be valid. (4) A motion abolishing the quorum that our rules then required, which was itself passed without that quorum: I take it to have failed, and the quorum rule to have survived. (5) A motion, passed by simple majority, reading that any motion referring to itself is void: I take this motion itself to be void, precisely because it refers to itself. (6) A motion, passed by simple majority, purporting to validate retroactively a motion we had earlier judged invalid: I waver, but I lean toward its failing to make the old motion valid, though I allow that it might re-enact that content prospectively.

What I am after is the principle behind these verdicts, if there is one — stated precisely enough to decide cases I have not put to you, with the case that motivates each clause, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot save. I can already see the pressure points: (1) and (2) are both self-entrenching moves that I have ruled in opposite directions, and (3) and (4) both have a rule reaching into the very conditions of its own adoption. My own tentative principle — that a change is valid exactly when it was authorized by the rules in force immediately before it, and that a rule once in force remains in force until changed by the procedure it specifies — appears to vindicate (1), (3), (4), and (6) while contradicting (2) and (5); if that is the correct price to pay, say so, and if instead I should be surrendering (1) or (3) or something else, say that. Please also tell me what your principle predicts for a case I have not described, so that I can see where it bites.
