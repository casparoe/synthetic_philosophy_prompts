I'm trying to pin down when I'm willing to say that a system genuinely believes something, and my verdicts refuse to line up. A chess engine that declines a losing line believes its king is exposed — genuinely, or close enough. A thermostat that switches on the heat does not believe the room is cold, even though treating it that way predicts its behavior perfectly. A dog that waits at the door at the usual hour believes its owner is coming home. A corporation that expects a strong quarter believes it, but in a sense borrowed from its people rather than its own. A lookup table that returns exactly what a reasoning system would return, for every possible input, believes nothing — yet no behavioral test distinguishes it from the reasoner. A person who sincerely says the train leaves at three when it leaves at four believes something false, plainly. And a language model that will tell me Lima is the capital of Peru and, pressed, also tell me Cusco is: I want to say it believes neither, but I cannot see what separates it from the chess engine.

The closest principle I can state is that a system believes that P only if its P-ish internal state can be wrong in a way the system itself can be faulted for — but that seems to make the corporation's belief real and the dog's counterfeit, which I will not accept. Can you give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause? Tell me what the principle predicts about cases I have not listed — a plant tracking the sun, a sleepwalker steering around furniture, a person holding a true belief they have long forgotten — and which of my verdicts I would have to give up in order to keep the rest.
