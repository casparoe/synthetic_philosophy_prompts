I run a small compliance team and we do unannounced spot checks on our field offices. My boss has gotten obsessed with the surprise exam paradox (he read about it somewhere) and has now decided that we can never tell people a surprise check is coming, because the announcement cancels itself out. His version is that saying there will be a surprise check is a bit like the sentence that says of itself that its false -- it's self-referencing, so it can't be true, and the only way to actually keep a check a surprise is to never say anything about checks at all, ever, to anyone. I think he's overreading it but I can't articulate why, and I need to push back before he kills our whole announcement policy (staff already complained that not knowing checks exist feels unfair).

So a few things I'm trying to sort out. First, whats the right way to word the announcement so it stops undercutting itself? Should we announce the window but not the day -- something like "we'll check each office once, sometime in the next five working days" -- or does naming the window just make it worse, the way the teacher who says "one day next week" seems to hand the students the rope? Second, is there a fix that operates at a higher level, like an announcement about the announcement -- "we'll only ever tell you about a check after its happened" -- that gets us out of the self-reference loop? Third, does it matter who's in the know? My boss wants to tell our own auditors the schedule and only keep the offices in the dark. Does the paradox care which people are being surprised, or is it indifferent?

What I really want is to understand whether the paradox actually shows that a surprise can't be announced, or whether it shows something narrower that I could explain to him. And if announcing is fine, is there a way of putting it that a non-philosopher will accept?
