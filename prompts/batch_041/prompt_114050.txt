Next month I am giving a work-in-progress talk to my department on a paper I have been writing about Hume's argument concerning miracles, and I would rather spend the remaining weeks preparing for the discussion than for the talk itself. The thesis of the paper is this. The now-standard Bayesian reconstructions of the argument — the ones that treat it as a claim about the prior probability of a law-violating event, or as a likelihood-ratio point about the evidential weight of a report — are answering a question Hume was not asking, and the real force of the argument lies somewhere else. What I claim is doing the work is a point about the evidence base rather than about probability: in order to know how much weight a report of a law-violating event deserves, one would need to know the error rate of such reports, and the only way to learn that error rate is to have independent access to whether the reported events occurred — which is exactly what one lacks when the event is of a kind that violates a law. I call this the calibration problem. It has two consequences I am fond of: the strength of the argument turns out to be insensitive to how well confirmed the law in question is, and the argument generalises to reports of singular, unrepeatable events in any domain where we have no independent check on the reports. I will have twenty-five minutes to present and the better part of an hour for questions.

The room is mixed, and I know roughly what each part of it will do to me. Two people in the audience work on formal epistemology and the epistemology of testimony; they will want everything stated in terms of priors, likelihoods and error rates, and I expect them to treat my thesis either as a restatement of the likelihood-ratio reading in different words or as a confusion of the probability of the evidence with the probability of the hypothesis. One is a historian of early modern philosophy who has written on the reception of the Enquiry and who, I am fairly sure, regards the whole dispute as an artefact of reading Hume through twentieth-century confirmation theory. There is a philosopher of religion in the department who is careful and entirely unsentimental and will want to know whether my argument proves too much. The rest are generalists and graduate students who will ask whatever is left over. The paper is also under review at a volume, so I would rather discover a fatal objection in my own department than in a referee's report.

What I want from you is a preparation document rather than an exposition — I know the text, and I do not need a survey of the literature or a reading list. Give me the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do if it landed, each phrased the way the person who will raise it would phrase it, with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is thin. Tell me what I should concede early and cheerfully, which single objection could sink the paper if I fumble it, and — listed separately at the end — the question I am hoping nobody in the room asks.
