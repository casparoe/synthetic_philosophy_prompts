I'm grading a midterm for an early modern ethics course and I want a second opinion on one student's answer before I hand the grades back. Here's the exam question and the answer. Give me a letter grade plus point-by-point comments (what's right, what's off, and how much each problem should cost them), not just an overall impression. American English please.

Exam question (short answer, 25 points): Explain Leibniz's formula that justice is the charity of the wise. What work does the wisdom half of the formula do, and why does Leibniz insist that justice is grounded in reason rather than in the will of a superior? Then lay out the three degrees of natural right and say how Leibniz's theodicy handles the lazy sophism.

Student's answer: Leibniz defines justice as the charity of the wise. Charity, for him, is universal benevolence, and wisdom tells us how to aim it: since love is taking pleasure in another's perfection, the wise person's benevolence seeks to increase others' perfection and happiness. The wisdom half matters because plain benevolence can be short-sighted or partial. He grounds justice in reason because he wants to block the voluntarism he associates with Hobbes and Pufendorf, on which right and wrong depend on the command of a superior; if that were so, God could have made cruelty just. Instead the good is discovered rather than decreed, following from the eternal ideas in God's understanding, which are not products of his will. This does have the consequence that God's justice is measured by a standard prior to and independent of God, which is why Leibniz belongs with the natural law tradition that Grotius started. Leibniz then gives three degrees of natural right: the lowest is piety, which bids us live honorably; the middle is equity, giving each their due; the highest is strict right, which forbids harming anyone. Full justice unites all three, and it is the same in God and in us, except that God's wisdom is infinite. Finally, the lazy sophism is the fatalist objection that if God's decree already settles everything, effort is pointless — if I'm predestined to recover, I can skip the doctor. Leibniz answers that God's decrees are conditional rather than absolute, so the means aren't fixed in advance and deliberation keeps its point.
