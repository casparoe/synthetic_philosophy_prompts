I'm in a formal epistemology working group, and we've spent several sessions on van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the idea that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition, conditional on the proposition that her future credence in it will take some particular value, should equal that value. The rough intuition is that you should not now expect to disagree with your later self, since conditionalizing on the prediction that you will later be at some credence just is adopting that credence now. We keep re-deriving it, we keep producing the same counterexamples, and we keep ending the session in the same place. The counterexamples are familiar: the agent who knows she will be drunk or drugged or worn down by the end of the evening, who knows her future self will assign a high credence to something she now regards as unlikely, and who, it seems, should not move an inch; the agent who knows she is about to lose information or memory; the agent who knows her future self will be reasoning from a thinner evidence base or a worse mood. The defenders say that in such cases the agent is not merely predicting a future credence but is also in possession of facts about why that credence will arise, and that once the full information is conditioned on the principle is untouched; or they say these are cases where the future credence is not the output of good reasoning and so not the kind of future self the principle addresses; or they reformulate the principle so that it constrains only credences about future credences of agents whose epistemic situation is at least as good. The critics reply that the reformulations either trivialize the principle or smuggle in the idealization that does all the work, and that the original principle, read literally, is simply false.

What I want is not another round of counterexample and patch. I want a diagnosis of why this has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is. Is it verbal — are the two sides attaching different content to the principle, one reading it as a synchronic coherence constraint on a single credence function and the other as a diachronic norm of deference, so that they are not really contradicting each other? Is it substantive and about a hidden question — for instance, whether a future self counts as an expert about the proposition in question, or whether self-trust is a requirement of rationality at all rather than an optional ideal? Is it a disagreement about which cases count as data, with one side treating the drunk case as decisive and the other treating it as outside the principle's intended domain? Or is the principle doing two jobs at once — constraining conditional credences at a time, and also claiming something about how an agent should respond to information about her own future epistemic states — so that the impasse is a sign that one formulation has been asked to do both? My working hypothesis, held loosely, is that the deepest issue is whether rationality requires deference to one's future self or merely permits it, and that the counterexamples are not really about credences but about the conditions under which a future self is authoritative for the present one; if that is right, the debate will not be settled by more formal work on the principle but by a theory of when an agent's later epistemic state is one she should now defer to. Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, using what each side actually says rather than giving me a survey, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a principled restriction of the principle's scope, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed. Be explicit about how confident you are in the diagnosis, and say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
