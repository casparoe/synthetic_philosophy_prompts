I want an essay on cyclical theories of history, but not the usual tour where you list the anakyklosis, Ibn Khaldun's asabiyya, Spengler's seasons and Toynbee's challenge-and-response and then sort of shrug. Here's the specific controversey I want you to go after.

Loads of people defend cyclical views by pointing at the fact that civilisations keep forgetting the same lessons: institutions rot, elites get entitled, and the tacit know-how that made the whole thing work dies with the generation that built it, so it has to be painfully relearned from scratch. Call that the "institutional memory" reading of cyclical history.

The question is whether that reading actually supports a cyclical view of history, or whether it quietly collapses into a fairly boring linear story about knowledge being lost and regained, where nothing has to genuinely repeat and "cycle" is just a metaphor for forgetting. Take a side and defend it.

If you think the memory reading rescues cyclicality, say precisely what has to be true about history for that to work — does it need a fixed repertoire of regime types, some ceiling on what a society can learn, a hard limit on how long institutional knowledge can be stored, something else? If you think it kills cyclicality, say what's left of the cyclical tradition afterwards — is it just a moralising mood, or is there a real empirical claim underneath that survives the collapse?

Use Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler and Toynbee as and when you need them, but don't let it become a parade of Greatest Hits — I care about the arguement, not a reading list. No fence-sitting at the end. Proper essay prose, not bullet points.
