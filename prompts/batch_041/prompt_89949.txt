I'm in this small philosophy of mind reading group and next week I have to circulate a short essay on Dennett's multiple drafts model and the Cartesian theater. I've got a rough draft but I'm to close to it, and I'd really appreciate your comments. The essay is supposed to be somewhat original, not just a summary, so I'm trying to push on a distinction I haven't seen made explicitly.

Here's the gist. People often say the Cartesian theater is the idea that there's a single place in the brain where everything comes together and consciousness happens, and Dennett's multiple drafts model denies that there's any such place. But I think that's a bit of a strawman. The real target is not spatial centralization but the assumption that there is a single, determinate, finished narrative of what happened, and that this narrative is what an inner observer reads off. You could have a fully distributed system with no anatomical theater and still have a Cartesian theater in this functional sense, as long as there's a final, globally ordered version of events that the system settles on. So I want to compare it to consistency models in distributed databases. The Cartesian theater is like linearizability: every operation appears to take effect at a single instant, and there's a global order that all nodes agree on. Multiple drafts is more like eventual consistency: different nodes can have different local orderings, and there's no global now. The question is whether consciousness requires something like linearizability. Dennett seems to say no: the sense of a single finished narrative is just a useful fiction, a post hoc consensus that the system reports. But I want to argue that reportability itself may reintroduce a kind of virtual linearization. If the system can say I saw the light before I heard the tone, then it has produced a global order, even if that order was never represented anywhere as a single timestamped stream. Maybe that's not a theater, but it's a commitment to a single story, and I'm not sure the multiple drafts model can avoid it. I also want to say that the feeling of being the reader of the drafts is not just an illusion; it's a functional constraint that any system that can report on its own states has to satisfy. Is that just the hard problem in disguise? I can't tell.

I'd love comments on three things. First, am I getting Dennett's actual target right, or is the spatial reading of the Cartesian theater the standard one and I'm just attacking a strawman? Second, is the linearizability versus eventual consistency analogy apt, or does it mislead because databases have external clients and consciousness doesn't? Third, does my objection actually amount to anything more than the familiar complaint that Dennett explains the easy problems and leaves the hard problem untouched? And if you think the whole essay is uninteresting or too clever by half, please say so and tell me where it goes wrong.
