In a graduate seminar on famine, a participant closed off a disagreement about how to characterise the Bengal famine of 1943 with the remark that famines don't happen in democracies, and drew from it the conclusion that famine is a political crime rather than a natural disaster — that where people starve en masse, some government has failed them, and that relief is therefore a matter of political accountability rather than charity. I have heard the claim credited to Amartya Sen's work on entitlements, and I want to know how much argumentative work that one sentence can actually be made to do.

Please set out, as a menu rather than a verdict, the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and say for each what it commits its holder to. The dimensions I take to matter are at least these: what counts as a famine (mass starvation deaths, a mortality spike above baseline, a collapse of entitlements to food, an event officially declared as such); what counts as a democracy (competitive elections, a free press, an opposition that can make political hay out of a crisis, effective voice for the poor); and what kind of claim is being made at all — a universal empirical generalisation, a probabilistic tendency, a causal claim about what prevents famine, a normative claim about responsibility, or a definitional claim that makes famine under democracy impossible by construction. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false or unsupported, and say which reading the seminar participant most plausibly had in mind — and whether that reading actually licenses the conclusion they drew.

The cases in play are the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward. For each reading, indicate whether those cases tell for or against it and whether any of them is a genuine counterexample. One participant objected that Bengal had an elected provincial government at the time, and another that Ireland was then part of a state with a parliament and a vigorous press; I do not know what to make of either objection, and I would rather have the distinctions than a ruling on them.

I am not asking for a literature review, and citations would not help me. Please say how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the point you are least sure of.
