In 2012 the EU's subsidiarity early warning system produced its first successful objection: enough national parliamentary chambers submitted reasoned opinions against a Commission proposal on the relationship between the right to take collective action and the freedom to provide services across borders, and the Commission ultimately did not pursue the proposal. Under the procedure, each chamber holds a fixed number of votes, and once objections cross a share of the total, the Commission must review the draft, though it remains free to maintain, amend, or withdraw it; nothing in the mechanism requires the objecting chambers' reasoning to be correct, and the mechanism has never escalated to the higher threshold that would give the legislature a formal route to kill a proposal outright. Treat this as a test case for subsidiarity itself, understood in three competing ways: subsidiarity as a presumption that competence sits at the lowest level unless a higher level discharges an affirmative burden of justification; subsidiarity as a comparative-effectiveness test, which is closer to the treaty's own formulation asking whether member states could not sufficiently achieve an objective and whether the Union could do better by reason of scale or effects; and a republican reading on which the point is not efficiency but non-domination, so that decisions should be taken at the level where those affected are not subject to a will they cannot contest.

Which reading does the early warning system actually operationalize, and where does that diverge from what the treaty's own test commits the Union to? What does the mechanism structurally fail to register — cross-border spillovers, economies of scale, races to the bottom, and above all the interests of non-nationals who hold no vote in any objecting chamber — and is that blindness a defect of the mechanism or of the principle? Given that chambers plausibly object for domestic partisan advantage, and that assembling the threshold is itself a collective action problem, is the system better described as a signalling device than as a subsidiarity test, and does that make it a faithful or a debased realization of the principle? Consider two redesigns — reversing the burden so the Commission must affirmatively justify Union action, and making the objection binding — and say which reading each serves and what each would predict about which proposals get blocked. Finally, what does the episode show about the theory: is subsidiarity determinate enough to be administered at all, or is its only stable content procedural, and does the case favour the republican reading over the efficiency reading or the reverse?
