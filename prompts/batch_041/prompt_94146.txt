I'm doing the after-dinner speech at our criminal bar association's annual dinner in three weeks and I need you to write it for me. Seven or eight minutes, no more — the plates are still going round and there's wine on the table.

Picture the room. About half are old-school abolitionists who've been at this since the eighties and could give the speech themselves. A decent chunk think the death penalty is defensible in principle for the very worst cases and will be listening with their arms folded. There are two victims' families at the top table. There's a consultant anaesthetist on my left and a High Court judge on my right, so anything that sounds like medical ethics or like contempt of court will get noticed. I can't preach to the choir, and I can't do the thing where you make the other side sound like monsters, because they're not, and they're sitting right there.

Here's the hook. Parliament's just seen a private member's bill introduced — not to bring back the death penalty. It's to let people on whole-life sentences request what the bill calls a 'voluntary judicial death': they ask in writing, a doctor administers it, no pain, no last-minute panic, safeguards up the wazoo. It's being sold, sincerely, as a humane and autonomy-respecting reform. And some of my abolitionist mates are half-persuaded, which is what's got under my skin.

I want Camus's essay against the death penalty to be the engine of this speech, not a quote at the top of it. And here's what I actually want the speech to work out. As I read him, his objection was never really that the condemned man suffers. It was that the thing is premeditated, that the state does it coldly and deliberately and then goes home to dinner, and what it does to the rest of us to authorise it. If that's the right reading, then a painless, voluntary, requested death might be the case he'd have hated *most* rather than the one his arguments can't reach. Or it might be the one case his arguments simply don't bite, because there's no murder, only a request. I genuinely don't know which way it falls, and I want the speech to come down on one side and defend it.

So the structure should follow from that reading, not be bolted onto it. If the wrong is in the premeditation and the state's cold authority, then the bill's kindness is beside the point and the speech can't rest on how painless it is — say so, and then say what it *can* rest on in front of a room that includes people who think the bill is a mercy. If the wrong is really in the suffering, then the bill partly answers Camus and I want the speech to admit it rather than wriggle. Either way the doctor and the judge have to hear something they recognise as addressed to them, not to an audience of philosophers.

A few things to avoid. Don't put any direct quotations from Camus in it — neither of us can check them and I'd rather have the argument than a line I can't trust on the night. Don't name any scholars or books at me; I'm not citing anyone at a dinner. And don't hedge: the whole point is that I stand up and take a position, so take one, with reasons, and let it cost something.

Write it in British English, and make it sound like a person talking, not an essay read aloud.
