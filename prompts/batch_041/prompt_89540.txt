So I've been running a small rationality meetup for about four years — twenty-odd people, we do a double crux night twice a month — and somewhere along the way I became the guy people ask to sit in when two members have a disagreement they can't shake. Twice now it's been two people about to go into business together, which means real money, and last month it was a couple disagreeing about whether one of them should quit his job. So the stakes are occasionally high, I get 45 minutes and a whiteboard, and nobody in the room is going to compute anything.

What I want from you is a procedure I can actually run in the room, built on the Aumann agreeing-to-disagree stuff and the common prior assumption underneath it. Not an explanation of the theorem — I've read the Sequences twice, I think I get it. A procedure. The questions I ask in what order, what a sufficient answer to each one looks like, the point where the procedure stops and says "you two just disagree, go home", and — this is the part I care about most — where the procedure stops being math and starts being me making a judgment call, so I can say that out loud instead of laundering my gut through a theorem.

Some things it has to handle, because these are the ones that actually come up:

- Half the time what people bring me isn't a disagreement about credences at all. It's about what to do, or about values, and I don't think the theorem has anything to say about that. The procedure needs to catch that early and hand it back, not grind on it.
- The gap between two people's numbers is usually a pointer to private information. I want the procedure to treat it that way, with actual questions for digging it out, rather than just "state your reasons."
- Different priors. If they don't share a prior, the whole thing is void, and I need to know how to tell whether that's what's happening and what's left to do at that point.
- Nobody in that room is an ideal Bayesian and none of us can compute. So a small gap should probably be treated as noise and the procedure should say when to stop fussing.
- People can lie about their numbers, or anchor each other, or be wrong about their own credences. Build in whatever ordering avoids that.
- And under no circumstances should the output be "Aumann says we have to agree, so you're being irrational." I've seen that move used as a club and it's the worst thing to come out of this literature. If the procedure's honest conclusion is that one of us is probably making an error, it should say so plainly rather than dress it up.

Then run it on two cases, worked all the way through the steps, and make the third one a case where I'm a party and not the facilitator — where the procedure tells me to move most of the way to the other person's number and I hate it. If the honest answer there is "there's no way to tell who's right, so move anyway," I want that said plainly, and I want you to say whether that's actually what the theorem licenses or whether you're just being diplomatic.

Structure it like this:

**What the theorem buys you** — about 150 words, the minimum I need in my head to run the rest, and specifically what it does not license.

**The procedure** — the bulk of it. Numbered steps in the order I'd actually ask them, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure halts, and where I'm the one deciding. Keep the questions short enough that I can ask them out loud without sounding like a seminar.

**Two dry runs** — two disagreements taken through the steps end to end, including the third one where I'm a party. Show me what the procedure does to me.

**Where the math stops and I start** — a short list of the steps that are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, and what I should say out loud when I hit one so the other person knows it's me talking and not the theorem.

**What this can't do** — short. The kinds of disagreement this procedure will quietly fail on, and how I'd know I'm in one.
