Two colleagues of mine spent most of a long train ride arguing about the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework for comparing causes, and I got off the train genuinely unsure who had the better of it.

She said neglectedness earns its place as a separate criterion because it tracks whether an additional dollar does marginal work. Where a cause is already crowded, a new contribution is likely to substitute for funding that would have arrived anyway; where it is thin, the same dollar is more plausibly the marginal one, and so buys more. Importance and tractability alone cannot capture this, she said: a cause can be enormous and genuinely solvable and still be a bad place to put money, if it is already saturated.

He replied that this is a story about how much good the next unit of effort does, which is precisely what tractability was supposed to mean. If tractability is the marginal productivity of effort, then a well-funded cause simply has lower marginal tractability, and neglectedness is redundant. What the criterion actually does, he said, is smuggle in a taste for contrarianism: a cause can be neglected because everyone who examined it concluded it was unpromising.

She granted that it might be marginal tractability under another name, but insisted the point survives the relabeling. Marginal tractability is not readable off the size of the problem or the amount already spent, and neglectedness is the cheapest available proxy for it — a proxy, she said, not a substitute for thought.

He then raised a complication he took to cut against her: funding is not always a substitute. An extra dollar in a crowded, momentum-rich cause may attract further funders, build a movement, or shift policy, so that the dollar is leveraged rather than wasted; in a neglected cause the same dollar may simply sit there. If the sign of the crowding effect can run either way, he said, neglectedness is not a reliable proxy for anything.

She answered that this makes the criterion harder to apply, not empty. You would have to ask what your money displaces or attracts in this cause at this moment, which is exactly the question the framework is meant to force you to ask.

He closed by objecting to the scoring: multiplying the three factors together requires an exchange rate between importance and tractability that nobody can defend, so two causes can flip order depending on whether importance is scored in lives, in dollars, or in QALYs.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point rather than as a verdict on the framework as a whole: for each of the five moves, say how much of it is right, where it is overstated or incomplete, and what it would take to settle it. I am also interested in what both of them are missing.
