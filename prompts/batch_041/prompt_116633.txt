I chair the animal care and use committee at a mid-sized university. A private foundation has offered us an endowment large enough to fund a research centre and two endowed chairs. The offer is conditional. We must amend our animal care policy so that its stated rationale becomes what the foundation's charter calls the 'automaton premise' — that the capacity for thought, and not the capacity for sensation, is what grounds moral standing, so that procedures on beings incapable of thought need no justification of the kind owed to thinkers — and so that birds and cephalopods, which our current policy places on a schedule of species requiring extra justification, are struck from that schedule.

Two features of our policy are in play. The schedule was adopted after a review of behavioural and neuroanatomical evidence; it is not a list of favourites but a record of where the committee judged the evidence of a capacity for suffering to be strong. Separately, the policy contains a precautionary clause: where the evidence about a being's capacity for suffering is genuinely inconclusive, we require the more protective justification, not the less. Our provost wants the money and argues that a stated rationale is only a rationale — that we can adopt the foundation's premise as our official reason while continuing to require what we already require, so that no animal is worse off. Two members reply that a committee which resolves doubt in favour of the animal cannot at the same time profess that only thought matters, and that the amendment would abandon a policy we adopted on evidence. A colleague in early modern philosophy tells us the premise's name is doing rhetorical work it cannot bear: the popular picture of Descartes as the philosopher who denied animals any feeling is, she says, at least partly a caricature, and what he denied them may have been thought rather than sensation. A protocol on corvid caching, involving food restriction and temporary social isolation, is scheduled for review at the same meeting.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — accept, decline, or accept with conditions — and state the specific motion and next step, with a time frame. It should engage the strongest case for the position it rejects, the provost's included, and should not simply restate our existing policy. Because the premise is put to us as Cartesian, the memo must say what that attribution can and cannot carry: distinguish what is settled about Descartes' views on animal minds from what is genuinely contested among historians of philosophy, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. If the premise is a position Descartes can be read as holding, say what is most attractive about it and what its best contemporary defence would be; if it is not, say what the real argument for the condition would have to be, since the money is on offer either way. Mark clearly where the memo itself takes a contestable line.
