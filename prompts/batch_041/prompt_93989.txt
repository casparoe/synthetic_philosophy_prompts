I chair the science curriculum review for a mid-sized school district. Each adoption cycle brings a stack of proposed units, and more of them now arrive dressed as science — data tables, references, the word "study" — from fields our committee cannot agree on. We have to vote on every unit within a single meeting, most members have no background in philosophy of science, and whatever we approve stays in classrooms for years. I need a decision procedure, not an essay: the questions to put to a unit in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure can stop with a clean "this is not science," and the point at which it must hand the call back to the committee because the remaining question is about values rather than facts.

Ground it in the best accounts of how to draw the science/non-science line — falsifiability and its refinements — but make it survive the standard objections rather than ignore them. Any hypothesis can be rescued by adjusting a background assumption or adding an ad hoc rider, so "is it falsifiable?" alone will admit almost anything; some respectable science (statistical claims, existence claims, historical reconstructions) is not falsifiable in the naive sense; and a hypothesis gains nothing from predicting a result that any arbitrary addition to it would predict just as well. The procedure should also distinguish a line of work that keeps generating new testable successes from one that only patches holes, and it should be explicit about where it is applying a test of falsifiability versus a test of truth.

Run it on three units: (1) a young-earth unit that argues from geology and scripture and makes specific, checkable claims; (2) a unit teaching that students have fixed "learning styles" that teachers should match, supported by the usual studies; (3) a cosmology unit on an unobservable multiverse, drawn from mainstream physics, with no direct evidence. Tell me where the procedure returns an answer the committee will not like. Close with what the procedure cannot do — the steps that are judgment in method's clothing, and the cases where I should simply tell the committee it is voting on values.
