I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-odd years doing experimental physics, and since then I've been teaching myself philosophy in the evenings — reading whatever people hand me, annoying my old colleagues with questions. Somehow that got me appointed as the "technical/public" member of our regional health authority's priority-setting committee, which meets Thursday, and I've been asked to write the committee's opinion on something that's been eating at me.

Here's the setup. We get a fixed pot each year, and right now two programs are competing for the same money. Program A treats a fairly small group of people who are, by our own quality-of-life questionnaire, pretty badly off — chronic, disabling, scoring low — and it lifts them partway back, not to normal, but a real and lasting improvement. Program B treats a much bigger group who are basically fine but bothered by something annoying and persistent; each of them gains a small but genuine improvement, and because there are so many of them, if you simply add up the gains, Program B wins by a mile. (The woman who chairs our patient advisory group happens to be in Program A's population, which I don't think helps my objectivity.)

Our stated rule is embarrasingly simple: buy the most total health gain per pound spent, and every unit of gain counts the same no matter whose it is. My gut says that's wrong in this case — that we should weight the people who are worst off more heavily — and the chair half-agrees; she's floated adding a severity multiplier, something like 1.5x for anyone below a cutoff on the questionnaire. But whenever I try to defend it out loud I run straight into my own training. In physics, "more" and "less" only mean anything once you've fixed a scale and a zero. I have no idea what it means to say patient X is twice as badly off as patient Y, or that her gain is worth one-and-a-half of his — and yet the instant we start ranking people as "worse off" we seem to be making exactly that kind of claim.

So please draft me the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. I need a concrete recommendation I can actually put to a vote — adopt the severity multiplier or don't, and if we do, how to specify it — with the reasoning spelled out. Take the strongest version of the side I'd be overruling seriously rather than knocking down a straw man, because half the committee will be on that side. And if the real answer turns on some distinction I'm blurring, tell me which one, since I suspect the whole thing hinges on something I'm not seeing. Keep it short enough that I can read it aloud in ten minutes without losing the room. Thanks.
