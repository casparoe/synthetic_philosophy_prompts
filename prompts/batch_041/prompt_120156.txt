I've been arguing with a friend about qualia for like six months and I want to stop arguing and write her a poem instead. I'm on the quining side of it -- qualia are supposed to be ineffable, intrinsic, private, and immediately given, and the argument I keep making is that nothing can have all four of those and still do any work, so whatever's actually doing the work is really a disposition: a fact about what I notice, what I'd do, what I'd remember. She thinks thats a loss and I think it's a discovery. I want a poem that doesnt take either side.

Voice: someone who's just started a medication (you pick, make it plausible) and swears the food has changed. Their partner says the food is the same and their receptors are what changed. The doctor shrugs. The speaker wants to say something's been taken from them, but every time they go to say what, it comes out as a description of something they do -- they reach for the salt now, they leave half of it, they dont order it anymore, they cant remember why they liked it. The partner is basically my friend, so the speaker is losing an argument without knowing it.

Shape I want: four quatrains, one for each of the four marks, and the fourth line of every quatrain is the same sentence with exactly one word changed from the time before. In each quatrain the first two lines should assert the mark flat out -- no words for it, nobody else can check, it belongs to the thing and not to me, I just know it -- and the last two should hand over evidence anyone could check: a test, a receipt, a lab slip, the partner eating the same dish and shrugging. By the fourth time the refrain comes around, the word that named the experience should be gone and in its place a word for something the speaker does. And the speaker shouldn't notice the deflation happening to them.

Hard constraint, and it's kind of the whole point: no sensory words anywhere in the poem. No bitter, sweet, metallic, flat, bright, dull, loud, sharp, warm, cold. The reader has to reconstruct what the food is like purely from behaviour and circumstance. If the thing that got taken can only be reached through the stuff around it, fine, thats the argument; if it turns out you can't get there at all, thats also the argument, so dont panic and sneak a "sour" in.

Two things not to do: dont let it turn into a sermon about how it was all just neurons, and dont let it end on a punchline. I want it to sit where I actually sit, which is not knowing whether anything was lost.

Just give me the poem. Start with the first line -- no title, no preamble, no notes about what you're up to. If you want to say something about why the refrain slides the way it does, put it after the last line.
