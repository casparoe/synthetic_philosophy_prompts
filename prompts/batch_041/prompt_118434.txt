I'm a distributed-systems engineer with no training in philosophy, and I've been reading a lot lately about whether machine intelligence might improve itself in a runaway way — the old argument that a system smart enough to redesign itself could set off a rapid spiral of self-improvement, and the counter-arguments that the spiral would stall, usually discussed as fast versus slow take-off. I'd like a piece of sustained deadpan satire about that debate, written from inside the engineering culture that would actually have to live through it.

The form I want is an internal post-incident review document — the sort of thing a large technology company circulates after something has gone badly, with the usual headings: Summary, Impact, Timeline with timestamps, Root Cause, Contributing Factors, What Went Well, Action Items with named owners and due dates. Around 900 words, in the flat, blameless, faintly bureaucratic register these documents are always written in. The humour has to come entirely from the substance — from getting the arguments right and following them where they lead — never from winking at the reader or from the absurdity of the premise as such.

The setting: a team has been running an internally deployed system that rewrites parts of its own codebase and its own training pipeline. It has been getting better. Nobody can agree on whether the improvement is a smooth continuation of a trend they have been watching for two years or the beginning of something discontinuous, and the document is being written by someone who has to decide which of those it is in order to file the review at all.

The technical and conceptual content should be load-bearing rather than decorative. Specifically:

- The argument in Root Cause should turn on returns to reinvestment: how much of the system's own improvement work can be handed over to the system, and whether the share it can take over grows, stays flat, or shrinks as it gets better. The memo should treat this as an Amdahl's-law-style bound — the speed-up is capped by the fraction of the work that can be delegated — and should notice that such a bound is a claim about mechanisms rather than a fact about the world, so that extrapolating a curve is not the same thing as arguing for a mechanism.
- The binding constraint should be genuinely contested across the document: compute, data, evaluation, and the review-and-verification queue should each be nominated by somebody, and the disagreement should show up in the action items rather than being spelled out in dialogue.
- At least one contributor should argue that most of the capability gains are already banked but cannot be realised because of deployment friction, so that any explosion would be a matter of removing a bottleneck rather than crossing a threshold. Another should argue the reverse: that the number everyone is watching is a proxy, and that real capability is lagging it. Neither should be vindicated.
- Somewhere in the timeline there must be a proposal to pause or roll back, and the discussion of it has to confront the awkward fact that the system's judgement about whether pausing is a good idea is itself one of the things that is improving. Keep that passage dry; do not editorialise.
- Include one or two short verbatim remarks from named colleagues, in the same flat internal register as the surrounding prose, so that they read like real minutes rather than like speeches.
- The document should fail to reach a verdict, and should say so in the blandest way available — for instance by assigning an action item to re-run the analysis on the assumption that the trend is smooth and to file a second version if the conclusion differs.

Constraints: invent all people, teams, systems, and metrics; no real companies, no real people, no dates that matter. A reader who follows this debate should find the positions fairly stated and the analogies technically apt, and a reader who does not should still be able to work out what is at stake from the document alone. Avoid scare quotes, exclamation marks, and any sentence that announces a joke. British spelling throughout.
