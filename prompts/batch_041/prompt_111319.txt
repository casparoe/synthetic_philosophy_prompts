I'm giving a talk to our hospital's clinical ethics committee next month. My thesis is that advance directives shouldn't be treated as binding orders from a past self but as defeasible testimony from someone who used to know the patient, and that a patient who now rejects her earlier directive can override it only if she can offer what I call a narrative repair: a story that makes the change intelligible as a development rather than a conversion, with the burden of proof rising as the discontinuity gets sharper. The committee is mostly clinicians, two geriatricians, a palliative care nurse, an ICU internist, the hospital lawyer, a chaplain, and one philosopher who works on personal identity. They care about bedside decisions, liability, family fights, and not being made to do ethics seminars at 3am. Prep me for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get, in their vocabulary, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one I'm hoping nobody asks. My rough argument is that the earlier self has standing because it did the deliberating, but not authority, because it won't bear the outcome, and that narrative repair is how we tell a genuine change of values from someone just rationalizing what they want now.
