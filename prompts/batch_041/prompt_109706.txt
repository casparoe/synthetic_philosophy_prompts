Having spent thirty-five years as an experimental physiologist before retiring into philosophy, I have been trying to make sense of the mental causation debates, and I keep finding that the arguments move too quickly past the question of what a theory is supposed to deliver. I am convening a small reading group of former lab colleagues—people comfortable with controlled experiments but not with metaphysical vocabulary—and I want to open with a specification rather than a survey. Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of mental causation must meet. I mean the full list: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results from the physical and biological sciences it cannot contradict. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and mark whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; a model without web access should be able to answer from the specification alone.

To make the task concrete, here is a case I have been turning over. An experienced surgeon is about to make an incision. Her conscious decision to proceed occurs at a certain moment, and the incision follows a moment later. A recording electrode shows that, had she not so decided, a calibrated pulse of stimulation would have produced the same incision at the same later moment; in the actual sequence, the stimulation is absent. The theory must tell me whether the decision caused the incision, whether it merely failed to be preempted, and what difference the decision made to the physical outcome. It must also respect the distinction between a mental event causing a physical event and a mental property being causally relevant, and it must say whether mental causation is a relation among events, properties, or facts. It must accommodate multiple realizability and supervenience without turning mental causes into idle byproducts, and it must not contradict the causal closure of the physical domain or the completeness of physical explanation.

Finally, if some subset of these desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied—if, for instance, causal closure plus the non-overdetermination of physical effects plus the distinctness of mental properties makes mental causation impossible—say so plainly, because that negative result is the part I most want to see. Tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the most popular positions, and which requirement is most often smuggled in as an assumption rather than argued for. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I can work through the scorecard with the group.
