I am assembling a specification before reading further on conditionals, and what I want is the desiderata rather than a survey: what any adequate theory of the indicative/counterfactual distinction must deliver — the data it has to cover, the judgments it has to preserve, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it has to respect — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The target is the distinction itself rather than the semantics of conditionals in general, and I want the list built around the following fixed points.

First, the core contrast. There are pairs of conditionals sharing antecedent and consequent that differ only in whether the speaker presents the antecedent as an open possibility or as settled against; competent speakers take them to differ in ways that a purely truth-functional reading of if cannot capture, and in some instances treat the two as inconsistent while in others treat them as compatible. The theory must state what these judgments demand of it, and must commit on whether the difference lies in truth conditions, in presupposition or conventional implicature, or in the norms governing assertion.

Second, a constructed case, to be treated as data rather than as a curiosity. Consider a language, Karst, with a single conditional particle and no dedicated counterfactual morphology. Karst speakers may attach an optional clitic marking the antecedent as settled — true or false — as of the time of utterance, and use the bare particle when the antecedent is unsettled. What English encodes in mood, Karst encodes in settledness marking plus discourse context. Requirements that follow: the theory must say whether the indicative/counterfactual contrast survives as a semantic distinction in Karst or reduces to a pragmatic one; must predict how a bare Karst conditional is rendered in a mood-marking language, and what if anything is lost in the other direction; and must not rest on a distinction that a learner could not recover from Karst input alone.

Third, antecedents that are neither settled nor open. Both Karst and English apply their conditional forms to antecedents whose status is undetermined at utterance time — future contingencies, unresolved lotteries, propositions the speaker regards as probable but not certain. The theory must assign these a place and must say whether the settled/unsettled boundary is binary or graded.

Fourth, settled-true antecedents. A conditional whose antecedent the speaker takes to be true, used to draw a consequence rather than to hypothesize, is not obviously either indicative or counterfactual as those terms are ordinarily drawn. The theory must classify it and explain why it patterns as it does in the judgments above.

Fifth, logical interaction. Karst has a construction for conditionals with disjunctive antecedents that its speakers uniformly accept as entailing the conditionals with each disjunct taken alone, whereas English speakers are divided about the analogous counterfactual. The theory must say what this shows about how the indicative/counterfactual distinction interacts with the logic of conditionals, and whether the two forms can share a single logic.

Then give me the deliverable: a numbered list of requirements, each stated with the case behind it; a mark of which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps; any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, stated as such with the reason; and the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate theories. Close with a brief assessment of how the main families — truth-functional, possible-worlds, premise-semantic, suppositional or no-truth-value, and epistemic-modal-base accounts — fare against the list. I do not want citations, a reading list, or names; I want the criteria.
