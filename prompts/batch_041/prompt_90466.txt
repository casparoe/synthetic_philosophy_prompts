I am preparing an episode of a small interview series on formal models of life and mind. My guest is a philosopher of biology who has spent years reconstructing and extending the typogenetics thought experiment and who now defends a strong reading of the DNA–Gödel isomorphism. On her view, the isomorphism is not a literary analogy or a heuristic but a constraint that any physically realized self-replicator must satisfy, because self-replication requires a formal description that can be copied and executed independently of the physical substrate that carries it. She also argues that the standard objections to the isomorphism—that the genetic code is redundant, that proteins are not syntactically structured like typogenetic enzymes, and that the system is too artificial to teach us anything about biology—all miss the point, since they treat the isomorphism as an empirical claim rather than as a claim about the logical form of any replication process. I want the interview to press her on the details rather than let her restate the thesis. Draft ten to twelve questions that do the following. First, ask her to spell out exactly which parts of the typogenetics system are supposed to map onto which parts of molecular biology, and where the mapping is supposed to be exact rather than merely suggestive. Second, probe the role of the self-referential twist: specifically, whether the DNA–Gödel isomorphism is supposed to be a case of Gödelian self-reference or only of a more modest description–execution distinction, and what turns on the difference. Third, ask how her view handles the fact that the genetic code is degenerate and that the mapping from codons to amino acids is not one-to-one, without allowing her to retreat to the claim that the isomorphism is only about the logical possibility of a code. Fourth, press her on whether the isomorphism, if true, would rule out any actual proposed alternative to DNA–protein life, such as RNA-world scenarios, autocatalytic sets, or lipid-based replicators, and if not, what explanatory work it is doing. Fifth, ask what a falsifiable prediction or formal theorem would look like on her view, given that she says the isomorphism is a constraint rather than an empirical hypothesis. Finally, end with a question about whether the typogenetics model, precisely because it is so artificial, might reveal more about the limits of our concept of replication than about replication itself. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, especially if she tries to deflect by saying that the isomorphism is a research program rather than a claim. Do not ask about her biography, her influences, or the history of the field.
