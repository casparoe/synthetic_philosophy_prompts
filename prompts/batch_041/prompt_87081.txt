I am a novelist, some two-thirds of the way through a book whose protagonist is a conductor — a woman who has spent thirty years in the pit of a provincial opera house and who has lately taken to reading Schopenhauer, badly but sincerely. I want to give her one short speech about his philosophy of music, and I want it to be the kind of speech a person who has read a difficult book alone, at night, and half understood it would actually give: not a lecture, but not nonsense either. I read The World as Will and Representation myself, years ago, and I no longer trust my memory of it. So I asked a friend — a music journalist who took a master's in philosophy before he turned to criticism, and who has never in his life said I don't know — to send me the argument in brief. This is what he wrote:

'Schopenhauer's view is that music is the summit of the arts. The other arts — painting, sculpture, poetry — have the Ideas as their object, the eternal forms in which the will objectifies itself. Music is different: it copies the will itself, and it does so directly, without passing through the Ideas at all. That is why it moves us more deeply — the other arts show us shadows, and music shows us the thing itself. And because it is so immediate an image of the will, music is not bound to the phenomenal world; it could, in a manner of speaking, have existed even if the world had not. Its parts answer to the grades of the will's objectification: the bass is the lowest grade, the blind striving of unorganised nature, and the melody is the highest, the intellectual life of man. And because music grasps the universal rather than the particular, it never depicts this or that joy or sorrow — its universality is the same as the universality of concepts, which is why Schopenhauer treats it as a kind of abstract language, and why he likens the composer to a man who tells the deepest truths of the world in a state he cannot account for when he wakes. The conclusion he draws is that music is the only art that gives us knowledge of the thing-in-itself — the one art that hands us metaphysics rather than a picture of it.'

And then, because he cannot leave well alone, he added his own gloss:

'So the upshot is that for Schopenhauer the best music is the most absolute — the fugue, the symphony, pure instrumental music — and anything that sets itself to words or tries to depict something, opera and song and programme music alike, is a falling-off; and since music puts us in touch with the thing-in-itself, listening to it is a way of knowing reality, so music could in principle do the work that metaphysics does.'

I have my doubts about the sentence on universality — it has the smoothness of something half-remembered — and about the closing gloss, which sounds like the sort of thing said in a green room rather than found in a book; but I could not tell you what is wrong with either, and my conductor will be saying these things as though she believes them. Please go through his thumbnail sentence by sentence. Which claims would a specialist in Schopenhauer's aesthetics accept without fuss, which would make them wince as a simplification or a conflation, and which are simply false? Where he has gone wrong, tell me what the right version is, and why the difference matters. Then tell me whether his gloss follows from the parts of the thumbnail that survive, or whether it quietly needs claims Schopenhauer never held. I don't need citations; I need to know which sentences I can safely put into her mouth.
