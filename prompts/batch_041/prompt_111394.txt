I am preparing an application for a small internal research grant — six months, part-time, one investigator, no laboratory component — and I need you to turn a rough idea of mine into the actual proposal document. The area is the ethics of insecticides and pest control. I think that area has been badly served by the way its central question is posed, and the proposal should be built around fixing the question rather than answering the usual one.

The usual question is some version of: when is it permissible to kill a pest? I want to argue that this is malformed, and that the real puzzle lies elsewhere. Nearly every justification offered for a pesticide application presupposes what I would call the specificity assumption: that the set of organisms the practice kills and the set of organisms its justification cites are, near enough, the same set. The assumption is almost never stated, and I have not found anyone who defends it. My half-formed suspicion is that it cannot be defended, because the mode of action of most insecticides is deliberately non-specific and because pest status itself is a relational, interest-relative category — an organism is a pest by reference to human agricultural or convenience interests, not by any property it has on its own. If the specificity assumption fails, then the familiar justifications (the pest as aggressor, the application as an exercise of self-defence, the economic threshold as a proportionality calculation) are answering a question about a set of organisms that is not the set actually being killed.

The sharpest consequence, and the one I want the proposal to be organised around, is a translation failure between two levels of description. Pest management is conducted and justified at the level of populations and species: one sets an action threshold at the point where a pest population would otherwise cross an economic injury level, one aims to suppress or hold a population, one tolerates a residual population. Moral status, whatever else it is, attaches to individuals. So every pest-management recommendation embeds an implicit exchange rate between population-level outcomes and individual deaths, and that exchange rate is essentially never made explicit, let alone defended. My working conjecture is that when it is made explicit, a broad class of recommendations that currently look obviously acceptable — routine prophylactic spraying, seed treatments, broad-spectrum applications justified by an economic threshold — turn out to require either an individual-level moral weighting that no plausible view grants to a single aphid or beetle larva, or a doctrine of permissible side-effect harm so permissive that it would license outcomes nobody is willing to license in the human case.

I want the proposal to do four things. First, state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and make clear that it is not the question of whether insects are moral patients — that question can be left open, and the project should be written so that it can be left open. Second, explain why the existing work does not already settle it; my suspicion is that pest-control ethics is written almost entirely by people whose unit of concern is the agricultural system, and animal ethics almost entirely by people whose unit of concern is the individual animal, so the translation step falls between two stools and is nobody's problem. Third, lay out a method. What I have in mind is constructing a small number of stylised but realistic pest-management scenarios, deriving for each the individual-level death profile it implies, and then asking which principles of permissible side-effect harm, and which per-individual weightings, would have to hold for the recommendation to pass. Fourth, say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing.

Two cautions for the writing. I want the proposal to be honest about a real risk: that the whole exercise is an elaborate way of rediscovering that if you assign tiny moral weight to each of a very large number of individuals, the aggregate can still be substantial — in which case the interesting finding is not the arithmetic but the fact that pest-control practice and its justificatory conventions are wholly insensitive to it. The proposal should be framed so that landing there counts as a result rather than as a failure. And I do not want the proposal to depend on any particular empirical figure. I can assemble insecticide tonnage and insect population estimates myself; write the method so that it takes those as free parameters and asks what follows for any plausible value, including values I would have to defend.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I will supply those. Keep the scope honest: six months, part-time, one person. Tell me explicitly what I would have to cut, and what the smallest version of the project is that would still be worth funding. Aim for roughly 900 to 1200 words, and write it as a finished document rather than an outline.
