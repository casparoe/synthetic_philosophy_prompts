Okay, I've fell down the statue-and-the-clay rabbit hole and I want to run my own take by you before it takes over my whole week.

The setup, as I understand it: a lump of clay sits there, then it gets shaped into a statue, and now it sure looks like there are two things in exactly the same place at the same time — the clay and the statue. That can't be right, so I figure either they're really one thing wearing two names, or one of them isn't a thing at all.

My take: a statue isn't a thing. Its a phase of the clay, the way ice is a phase of water. The clay is the real object; "statue" is just a name for a temporary arrangement the clay happens to be in. Squash it and you haven't destroyed an object, you've just changed the clay's phase, same as letting an ice cube melt. And once I got there I couldn't stop — I think this goes for everything. A chair is a phase of some wood and glue, a body is a phase of matter, you are a phase of your body. Nothing we name is really a thing; they're all phases of stuff.

So what are the most important implications of a view like this? I don't want a survey of the five theories of material constitution, I want what actually follows for stuff I care about. In particular:

1) I do pottery and I've been reusing the same block of clay for years — make a pot, hate it, mash it back down, make another. By my own count I've made maybe fifty pots but I've only ever owned one lump of clay. So did I make fifty things or one? And what did I destroy each time I squashed one?

2) If a person is just a phase of their body, then what exactly dies? What do I tell my sister, who's convinced the person and the body are two things and that this matters alot for what happens when someone dies?

3) If the statue isn't a thing, is it even possible to steal a statue, or do you only ever steal clay? And if the museum's statue is really just a phase of its clay, what is the conservator actually doing when she fills in a chip?

And please, if the view forces me to give up something I obviously want to keep — like "there are two things on that pedestal, not one," or "my grandmother's vase is the same vase I dusted as a kid" — say so plainly instead of papering over it.
