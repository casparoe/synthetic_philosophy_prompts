You are a philosopher working in the epistemology of testimony and social epistemology, with a firm grip on the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate and the positions that have grown out of it (assurance and second-personal views, default-entitlement views, transmission-of-justification views, and the newer literature on group and machine testimony). I want you to reconstruct the principle behind a set of verdicts of mine. Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm several candidate principles, note what each buys and costs, and only then argue for your recommendation. Do not simply survey the literature; I want your best construction and your honest assessment of whether my verdicts can be made consistent at all.

Background: I have no theory of when it is epistemically all right to take someone's word for something. I have gut verdicts, and I keep suspecting they do not hang together. Here they are.

1. A stranger on the street tells me the museum is two blocks up and then left. I believe her and go. Fine — I need no corroboration.
2. A car salesman tells me this car has never been in an accident. Not fine to just believe; I should check.
3. A friend who is shrewd about people but knows nothing about medicine tells me a supplement cured her cousin's migraines and that I should take it. Not fine to believe.
4. The same friend tells me which of two nearby restaurants is better. Fine to believe.
5. One credentialed nutrition scientist says a diet works; another, equally credentialed, says it does not. Not fine to believe either on their say-so alone.
6. An AI chatbot tells me the population of a city. I should not believe it without checking, even though it is usually right — there is nobody there.
7. A well-sourced encyclopedia article, produced by many anonymous editors, tells me that same city's population. Fine to believe; I trust it more than the chatbot.
8. My thermometer reads 101 degrees Fahrenheit. Fine to believe I have a fever.
9. A friend tells me that a friend of hers, whom I have never met, saw that the museum was closed. Fine to believe, though a bit weaker than hearing it from someone who saw it herself.
10. A stranger tells me something against his own interest. I believe him.
11. Someone describes her own pain. I believe her fully.
12. An anonymous online review of a product. Not fine to believe.
13. A published scientific consensus that I could not evaluate myself. Fine to believe.
14. I catch myself inclined to doubt a stranger's account of her own neighborhood because of how she talks and dresses. I think that inclination is unjustified and that I should believe her.

My hunch — which I do not trust — is that everything turns on whether the speaker is answerable to me, whether there is someone who can be held to the claim. But the thermometer and the encyclopedia seem to break that, and so does the fact that I trust a stranger about directions but not about medicine. There is also one case I keep going back and forth on: a doctor tells me that an AI flagged something on my scan and that she has checked the finding herself. I cannot tell whether I am believing the doctor, the machine, or both, or whether that distinction even matters.
