You are asked to reconstruct, as faithfully as you can, what Daniel Bernoulli would have thought about a decision problem that arose long after his death. Do not supply citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; reason from the positions described below and from Bernoulli's standard commitments. Write in American English.

Bernoulli is best known in decision theory for distinguishing expected monetary value from expected utility and for proposing that utility is a concave function of wealth, standardly modeled as logarithmic. He introduced this distinction in response to the St. Petersburg game, in which the expected monetary payoff is infinite, yet rational agents will pay only a modest sum to play. His explanation was that the marginal utility of money diminishes, so the game's expected utility is finite and small.

Consider now a different game, sometimes called the Pasadena game. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) 2^n / n dollars. The expected monetary value of this game is the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, but only conditionally: the terms can be rearranged to converge to any real number or to diverge. Consequently, the game's expected value is not well-defined in an order-independent sense. Unlike the St. Petersburg game, the Pasadena game involves both gains and losses, so Bernoulli's logarithmic utility for positive wealth does not directly apply, and applying a concave utility function does not automatically remove the indeterminacy.

A colleague proposes a decision rule she calls truncation-first expected utility. When an option's expected value is undefined because of conditional convergence, compare options by the limit of their expected utilities under a sequence of truncations—for example, truncate the game after N tosses and let N tend to infinity. If those limits exist and differ, choose the option with the larger limit. If the limits do not exist or are equal, choose the option that is weakly stochastically dominant in the limit; if there is no such option, treat the choice as rationally underdetermined.

Reconstruct Bernoulli's likely response. Address each of the following:

1. Would Bernoulli regard the Pasadena game as a genuine counterexample to expected utility theory, or as a problem his own distinction between expected value and expected utility can absorb?
2. What would he say about the colleague's truncation-first rule? Would he accept it as a natural extension of his method, or reject it as ad hoc or arbitrary?
3. Does the Pasadena game put pressure on the idea that rational choice requires a well-defined expectation? If so, what alternative would Bernoulli likely have endorsed, given his mathematical commitments and his willingness to idealize rational choice?
4. Identify one point at which your reconstruction is uncertain, and explain what further evidence about Bernoulli's views would resolve it.

Aim for a self-contained, polished essay of roughly 800 to 1200 words.
