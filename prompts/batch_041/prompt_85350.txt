A health technology assessment body decides whether to fund a treatment by comparing its cost per quality-adjusted life-year against a fixed threshold, applied to every patient alike except for an adjustment for the severity of the condition. A safety regulator prices small changes in mortality risk by a single value of a statistical life, applied across the whole population without regard to the income of those protected. The economists who supply both numbers describe what they measure as willingness to pay — a money-metric representation of each individual's own preferences — and deny that any comparison of one person's utility with another's is involved. What philosophical commitments does this practice actually carry? Does a uniform threshold presuppose interpersonally comparable utility, or only the comparability of a good (health, risk of death) that is stipulated to matter equally for everyone? Can the money-metric defence survive the transfer of one figure across people whose marginal utility of income differs sharply, or does it quietly borrow a comparability it officially disowns? And separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: could a thoroughgoing ordinalist keep the practice intact and simply reinterpret it, or would rejecting interpersonal comparison force the practice to change — and if so, in which parts?
