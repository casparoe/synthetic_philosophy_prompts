I've been trying to get straight on when it's OK to bind your future self, and my gut keeps handing down verdicts I can't turn into a rule. Some of them feel obvious. A guy who knows he'll blow his paycheck on slots and sets it up so his wages get diverted before he can touch them: fine, do it. A friend who asks me to hold her keys when she's been drinking: fine. Someone who writes out, while she's lucid, that if she goes psychotic again she wants to be restrained and medicated and not let out on her own say-so: I think we should honor that, even when the person in the episode is begging us to release her — honoring it is the whole point, that's what she was buying. Ulysses lashed to the mast: obviously fine. Now the ones I resist. A man who signs a contract to work for one employer, no pay, for five years, to clear a loan: I don't think that should be enforceable, even though he signed it sober and clear-headed. A teenager who takes irrevocable vows in a religious order: I feel she should be able to walk away, vows or no vows. A guy who, mid-fight with his father, signs something binding that he'll never speak to him again: no. And a legislature that passes a balanced-budget rule a future legislature can't undo, or a constitution that freezes a specific pension age for a century — against it, though I'm strongly in favor of entrenching a bill of rights, and I'm aware those are structurally the same move. My tentative rule is that you can bind yourself in the service of your own deciding, but not in a way that hands your deciding over to somebody else or cancels it — ropes, not chains. Problem is I can't get the psychiatric directive and the teenage vows onto opposite sides of that line without cheating. In both, a past self overrides a present self who's objecting. Maybe the difference is whether the later objection is the very thing the earlier self was guarding against, but that feels like I'm just picking whichever self I happen to approve of, and the whole point of the directive is that you *can't* trust the later self's judgment. There's also a distinction I keep reaching for and I'm not sure it's real: something that's expensive to undo versus something that can't be undone at all. The savings account has a penalty; the vow has no exit. Does that line do any work, or is it just a price on a dial?

So — is there a principle, or a short list of them ranked, that gets most of my verdicts, and which one does it make me give up? I'd especially like it run against two cases I haven't listed. First, a friend of mine writes smart contracts, and he's built one where you stake money that gets destroyed if you back out of a commitment — no human being can release you, there's nobody to appeal to, the chain just does it when the deadline passes. Ropes or contract? Second, the mirror image: a precommitment that binds other people rather than yourself, like a founder writing into a company's charter a rule no future board can ever change. And if the honest answer is that nothing single survives all of this, fine, just say which of my verdicts is the one that has to go, and why that one rather than the ones next to it.
