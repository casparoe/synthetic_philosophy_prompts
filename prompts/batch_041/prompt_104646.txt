Next term I am running a graduate seminar on the politics of the archive, and I want to open with a session on what a theory of archival selection has to deliver before we start adjudicating between rival positions. The recurring fight in my field is between the older ideal of the archivist as a neutral custodian who keeps what the records happen to contain, and the newer insistence that every archive is an instrument of power that decides whose lives become legible and whose quietly vanish from the record. The two sides argue past each other because they never agree on what the theory is even supposed to do, so I want the specification settled first, in my hands before the term begins.

Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of what an archive should preserve — and, by extension, whose lives get recorded — must be able to do. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. I want the requirement that selection is inescapable, so the real question is never whether to exclude but on what grounds and by whose authority; the requirement that a silence be treated as a different object depending on whether it is a mere omission, the positive product of a decision to destroy or suppress, or the byproduct of a classificatory scheme that had no category for the people in question; the requirement that the theory account for the claims of the people inside the records as against the claims of those kept out of them; the requirement that it say whether the archivist can be neutral at all or only honest about a stance; and the requirement that it reconcile fidelity to a record's origin with making that record answerable to the communities it describes. Tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are really just commitments of one camp dressed up as criteria, and flag any pair you think cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want, because if two of the demands pull against each other then the list itself is a result. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to rule out the familiar positions.

One case to test the list against, if it helps: a county archive is offered a trove of police surveillance files that document both the targeting of a local movement and the private lives of dozens of uninvolved people swept up in the same dragnet. The donors want it preserved whole and unredacted, the movement wants it kept as evidence of what was done to them, and several of the surveilled want it destroyed. Whatever you write should let me say, on the theory's own terms, what is demanded here — and if the list cannot decide the case, say so, and tell me whether that is a failure of the theory or a sign that the question was never the theory's to answer.
