I'm writing the final for my final-year seminar on the socialist calculation debate (it runs as a political-economy/history-of-thought course, mostly econ and PPE students, a few philosophers). Closed book, no devices, one long essay question worth 20 marks, 60-70 minutes. The problem is that everything I draft can be answered by narrating the standard story: Mises says it's impossible, Hayek says knowledge is dispersed, Lange says a planning board can adjust prices by trial and error the way a market does, done. They've all read that story already, so I want the question built so that reciting it gets you a C at best.

So I've built the question around a stipulation I want them to actually take seriously. Give them this setup: a planning board that has (a) a perfect preference-elicitation technology, so it holds every household's complete ordering over every good, (b) unlimited computing power, and (c) managers who carry out instructions exactly - nobody shirks, nobody misreports their inputs, no principal-agent slack anywhere in the system. So the obvious objections are stipulated away by construction. The question should ask them what, if anything, of the calculation critique survives that setup, and - this is the bit I care about - to say which strand of the critique each part of their answer is answering.

What I want back from you: the question as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme. For the scheme I want five must-hit points, each with a mark value that adds up to 20, and next to each one a one-line note on how students typically blow it. Put a short note at the top of the scheme on what separates an A from a B - the specific moves an A makes that a B doesn't.

Two things I need the scheme to do. First, it has to keep apart the different things people mean by "calculation": the equation-solving / how-much-number-crunching reading, the dispersed-and-tacit-knowledge reading, and the logical reading about there being no common unit for weighing alternative uses of capital goods once there are no capital-goods prices. Half my students collapse all three into "the planners don't have enough data", and I want the marks to punish that seperately from the other errors. Second, I want it to catch the misattribution where students treat the incentive/principal-agent story as the heart of Mises's and Hayek's case rather than a strand that later critics developed, and the one where they describe Lange as an advocate of fully centralised planning with no prices at all.

Last thing: the students can't look anything up, so dont build the question on dates or titles they'd have to recall. It has to be answerable from the ideas alone.
