You are a historian of early modern philosophy who also works on the philosophy of mathematics, and you have a taste for the hardest genre in that trade: the counterfactual reconstruction. I want you to say what Hobbes would have made of mathematics that came after him, and I want the reasoning to be the interesting part of the answer, not the verdicts.

Hobbes died in 1679. He never saw non-Euclidean geometry, the algebraisation of the subject he cared most about, the arithmetisation of analysis, the foundational crisis, the incompleteness theorems, or machine-checked proof. Your job is to say, development by development, what he would have said, what he would have been unable to say, and where the question has no determinate answer because his framework simply does not reach it.

Before you write anything, brainstorm. In your thinking, lay out the commitments that bear on the question, note where they pull against each other, and only then start committing to predictions. Do not produce the finished text as a list of verdicts with reasoning retrofitted.

Here is the material to build on, so that you are not working from vague recollection. Hobbes treated geometry as the model of demonstrative knowledge: in his system mathematics was subordinate to geometry, and geometry to kinematics, the science of bodies in motion. His definitions were nominal, and on one influential reading the certainty of geometry rests for him on the fact that we construct its figures ourselves — the same point he used to argue that civil philosophy could be as demonstrative as geometry, since we make the commonwealth. He held that reasoning just is a kind of computation, adding and subtracting ideas. He was hostile to the symbolic algebra that was then becoming the engine of mathematics, and his long quarrel with John Wallis, which ran for decades and dragged in the Royal Society, was fought over the priority of geometry over algebra, over whether infinite quantities were legitimate objects (he saw them as opening the door to a revival of scholasticism), and, underneath the mathematics, over clerical and university authority. He also repeatedly claimed to have squared the circle, and was wrong each time. Treat this summary as a starting point rather than as authoritative; if you think part of it is a misreading, say which part and argue for your alternative.

Now the developments. Take these five:

1. The discovery of non-Euclidean geometries and the eventual treatment of geometry as a family of axiomatic systems with many models, none of them privileged as the description of space.
2. The ascendancy of algebraic and analytic methods, up to the formalisation of mathematics in symbolic logic in the Frege–Russell–Hilbert line and the arithmetisation of geometry through coordinates.
3. The rigorous rehabilitation of infinitesimals in nonstandard analysis, alongside the ordinary calculus that Hobbes's contemporaries were building on exactly the infinite quantities he rejected.
4. The incompleteness theorems, and the collapse of the programme of proving consistency by finitary means.
5. Machine proof: the computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem, formal verification projects that check whole bodies of mathematics, and current systems that generate proofs no human being reads in full.

For each, give a verdict and defend it. Say what he would have welcomed, what he would have denounced, and what he would simply have failed to understand — and be honest that these three categories come apart.

Keep three kinds of claim visibly separate: what his stated principles commit him to, what his temperament and his polemical situation predict, and what is genuine guesswork. Where his texts underdetermine the answer, say so and say what further evidence would settle it. Do not invent quotations or attribute views to particular works unless you are confident about the attribution; when you are unsure whether you are reconstructing Hobbes or paraphrasing a modern commentator, flag it. Watch for anachronism in both directions. Do not credit him with anticipating intuitionism or constructivism if what he says is weaker, stronger, or aimed at something else; and do not assume he must have been wrong about everything just because he was wrong about the circle.

One further thread to pull: Hobbes read disputes about mathematical method as entangled with authority — ecclesiastical, academic, political. Ask whether he would have read the later triumph of formal, symbol-driven mathematics the same way, and what he would have made of the fact that mathematical knowledge in the twentieth century came to be warranted by institutions and machines rather than by constructions any individual could survey.

Structure your answer as follows: a short reconstruction of Hobbes's mathematical commitments as you are using them; a section per development, each with a verdict and its argument; one paragraph on the places where he would turn out to have been prescient, and whether that is a real anticipation or a coincidence of vocabulary; a closing note on the two or three predictions you would defend most strongly, the ones you would defend least, and what the exercise shows about the limits of predicting a dead philosopher's views on questions he never faced.

No citations needed; I am after the argument, not a literature review. Aim for something substantial — this is for a graduate seminar, so roughly 1500 words is right.
