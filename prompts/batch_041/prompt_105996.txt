Write a short story, of roughly two thousand words, built around the distinction between a quantity of matter and the thing that quantity of matter presently constitutes. Set it in a society that has taken this distinction literally in its institutions: clay and the vessel it makes up, metal and the tool it makes up, cloth and the garment it makes up, are separately ownable, insurable, taxable, and destructible, and its courts have long been in the habit of declaring that one has perished while the other survives. The story should turn on a single contested object and a single dispute in which the parties agree about every physical fact — composition, shape, provenance, causal history — and disagree only about what exists. No one is ignorant or mistaken about the matter; what divides them is what each takes the persistence conditions of the thing to be.

The following constraints matter more than the plot:

1. Choose an artifact that is not a work of fine art, so that the sculpture and its clay are not simply re-enacted. Prefer something whose material is itself contested or valued — a scarce metal, a consecrated substance, a recycled feedstock — so that both claimants have something real to lose.

2. Do not make gradual part-by-part replacement the central device; if it appears at all, keep it in the background. The interesting divergence is not the slow swapping of pieces but a single event after which one party says the thing is gone and the other says it is still here.

3. Include a scene in which a character unschooled in any of this presses the obvious question: if the matter and the artifact consist of exactly the same parts in exactly the same arrangement, in what does their difference consist? Let the character receive no satisfying answer, and let the story continue without one.

4. Let at least one consequence of the plot depend on the two candidates having different modal profiles — on something the matter might have been but the artifact could not have been, or the reverse. Make it concrete: a counterfactual that would count as a mere alteration in the matter but as a destruction of the artifact.

5. Keep the metaphysics out of the narrator's mouth. No lectures, no named positions, no character who is a philosopher by trade expounding the literature. The distinction should surface through insurance schedules, customs declarations, criminal charges, inheritance, ritual, or grief.

6. Do not dissolve the puzzle at the end — no revelation that there was really only one object all along. A decision may close the story, but it should read as a stipulation the characters impose rather than a discovery they make.

Write it straight: no satire, no metafiction, no allegory with the labels left showing.
