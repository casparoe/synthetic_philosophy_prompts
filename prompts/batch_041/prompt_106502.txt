Two colleagues of mine in a materials lab have been at loggerheads for weeks over a specimen they call X-9, and they have asked me to settle it. X-9 is an alloy that, in its ordinary state, fractures when subjected to a load above a certain threshold. It has, however, been engineered with an embedded additive that, at the instant a load above that threshold is applied, triggers a phase change which renders the alloy ductile, so that it deforms rather than fractures. In every test anyone has run, X-9 never fractures. Nor can the additive be separated out: it cannot be removed without destroying the specimen.

(1) Anna says X-9 simply is not brittle. Brittleness, she maintains, just is the disposition to fracture under sufficient load; since X-9 never fractures under any load, it is not brittle, full stop. (2) Ben says it is brittle all the same. The brittleness is real, he says, but is being masked: the phase change is an interfering factor that prevents the disposition from manifesting, and the proof is that the additive had to be designed in precisely because the underlying alloy is brittle. (3) Anna replies that this turns brittleness into something that can never show up anywhere or be detected by anyone, and that a property which makes no difference to any possible test is doing no work. Ben answers that it plainly makes a difference: the additive is a response to it, and a rival alloy without the additive shatters. (4) Anna then offers a case she thinks breaks him. Take a fuse. A fuse is made of a material that conducts, yet the very current it would conduct destroys it, so it never conducts for more than an instant. By Ben's way of talking, the fuse's conductivity is also 'there but prevented from manifesting'. But nobody thinks a fuse is a conductor in the way that copper is. (5) Ben says the fuse is different: in the fuse the disposition is destroyed by the very circumstance that would manifest it, whereas in X-9 the disposition survives and is merely prevented from manifesting; and he insists that this distinction matters. Anna says both cases look like the same move — 'the behaviour never shows, but the disposition is still there' — and that Ben is now multiplying distinctions to save the theory.

Please adjudicate this dispute move by move, rating each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justifying each rating. Then say, separately, which of the distinctions drawn are genuine and which are ad hoc; what each party is getting right and what each is missing; and who is more right overall. Address in particular whether the fuse case is really of a piece with X-9 or genuinely different in kind, and whether the dispute between Anna and Ben is a substantive disagreement or a merely verbal one. Please answer in British English.
