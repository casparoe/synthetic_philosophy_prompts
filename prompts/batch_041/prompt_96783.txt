Ok, so I keep running into a pattern and I don't know if it has a name. Honestly the name matters less to me than the concept, so if there's no term, coin one -- but I want the carving, not the nearest label.

The pattern shows up in big sweep histories. Sapiens, Guns Germs and Steel, The Dawn of Everything, and basically every "history of everything" chapter in popular science. They're all built on the same skeleton. There's a long stretch where nothing much happens, and then a named transition that does all the causal work: the cognitive revolution, the agricultural revolution, the birth of the state, the origin of life as a single event, the scientific revolution. And the transitions are load bearing. If you pulled one out and replaced it with a slow drift spread over thousands of years, the rest of the book wouldn't work. Later claims depend on the hinge having been real and sharp.

Heres the thing that bugs me: the hinges are exactly where the evidence is thinnest. Everyone agrees more people were farming later than earlier; nobody agrees when it flipped, why, or whether it was one thing at all, and the author needs it to be an event, because the story needs a verb. The naming also seems to happen before the dating -- the transition gets a name and a chapter long before anyone can say when it happened, and once it has a name it's very hard to argue it away.

And the critics do the same thing, they just move the hinge. The Dawn of Everything isn't against hinges, it refuses the one it doesn't like and installs its own. So it isn't a flaw of one politics.

I know the ballpark of what people might call this: conjectural history, just-so stories, critical junctures, punctuated equilibrium, Whig history. But each seems to be about something a bit different -- Whig history is about reading the past as a march toward us, which doesn't fit a book arguing we've been going downhill. So what I want is: is there an established concept for this specific structure (the narrative's explanatory pivots being its least supported joints, and the narrative being unable to survive their dissolution)? How does it differ from each of the above? Is it even a vice -- some hinges really are sharp, like the Cambrian explosion or the industrial revolution, and I don't know what exempts them. Boundary cases welcome: does it apply when only one hinge carries the whole book? Is a well evidenced sharp transition exempt?

One more thing, since you can't go look anything up: please sort your answer into what's actually settled and what's contested among people who know the field. If historians disagree about whether the thing I'm describing is even a real pattern, tell me that instead of handing me one camp as the consensus, and flag where you're extrapolating.
