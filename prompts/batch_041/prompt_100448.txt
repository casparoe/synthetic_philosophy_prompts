I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting an essay on heritability for our ideas section. I'd like you to act as an expert in quantitative genetics and the philosophy of biology — someone who knows the ACE model, the gene-environment literature, and the standard critiques — and to help me before I file. Please begin by brainstorming, in a few lines, what you take my central thesis and my supporting claims to be, so I can see whether the draft reads the way I think it does; only then comment.

Here is the draft:

For most of the last century, arguments about nature and nurture have been fought with a single number, and the number has usually been misunderstood. Heritability is a ratio. It takes the variance in some trait across a population and asks what share of that variance is attributable to genetic differences among the individuals in the population. Three consequences follow immediately, and none of them is intuitive to a general reader.

First, a heritability estimate is a property of a population, not of a person and not of a trait in the abstract. There is no fact of the matter about how heritable height or intelligence is, full stop; there is only how heritable it is in a given population, at a given time, across a given range of environments. Change the population or the environments and the number changes.

Second, because heritability is a ratio, the denominator can move. Narrow the range of environments — make schooling, nutrition, and parenting more uniform — and the genetic share of the remaining variance goes up. This is why the same trait can look more heritable in a society that has worked harder to equalize environments, and why estimates drift across times and places. It is also why almost anything is heritable in the technical sense: if everyone in a sample owns roughly one television, the differences in how many televisions families own are still partly explained by genetic differences between them. The statistic describes the variance that happens to exist, not the variance that could exist.

Third, heritability is not destiny. A trait can be nearly entirely heritable and still be reliably changed by changing the environment — the textbook case being a metabolic disorder that is fully genetic and fully manageable by diet.

Where the number is genuinely abused is in the leap from within-group to between-group. Take a bag of genetically varied seeds and plant them all in one plot: the differences in their height are highly heritable. Now split the same seed lot between a rich plot and a poor one. Within each plot the differences are still highly heritable, but the difference between the two plots is pure environment. A high heritability coefficient tells you nothing about what causes the gap between two groups. That is the structure of the fallacy whenever someone notes that a trait is highly heritable within some population and concludes that the differences between populations must be genetic. The inference does not go through, even if the estimate is right.

Two technical worries remain. Heritability is not measured; it is inferred from a fitted model, usually a twin or family design that assumes something like equal environments for identical and fraternal twins and no gene-environment interaction, and real families violate those assumptions in ways that are hard to bound. And the environment is not exogenous: children help shape the environments they receive, so what looks like a clean genetic effect can be a genetic effect routed through a chosen environment. I suspect these two problems together make most popular heritability figures close to uninterpretable for policy purposes — though I want to be careful about how strongly I put that.
