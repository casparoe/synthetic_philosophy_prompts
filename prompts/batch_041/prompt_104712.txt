I am working on a short paper defending Dennett against Searle on original versus derived intentionality, and I would like detailed comments before I revise.

My argument has three parts.

First, I claim Searle's objection to Dennett turns on an ambiguity in the notion of derived intentionality. Searle treats derived intentionality as observer-relative: a map or a sentence has content only because someone interprets it that way. But there are at least three distinct things one might mean by calling a system's intentionality derived. (a) It is conferred by an actual interpreter, so that if all interpreters vanished, the content would vanish too. (b) It is ontologically dependent on design or evolution — it exists only because of facts about how the system was produced — but it does not require any ongoing interpreter, as with a thermostat or a bacterium's chemotaxis. (c) It is a real pattern that supervenes on the system's dispositions and is picked out by a predictively useful stance, in Dennett's sense. I argue that Searle's objection only clearly lands against (a), and that the two-bitser case is designed precisely to show that (a) cannot be the whole story, since the machine's behavior is more predictable from the intentional stance than from the design stance once it is off the assembly line.

Second, I reconstruct the two-bitser as a case about misrepresentation. Dennett's machine accepts quarters, and we ask whether it believes that a brass slug is a quarter when it accepts one, or believes that quarters are slugs, or has no beliefs at all. Dennett's point, as I read him, is that there is no fact of the matter fixed independently of our explanatory interests. Searle's reply, as I read him, is that the machine cannot genuinely misrepresent, because misrepresentation requires a content that is fixed independently of the interpreter, and the machine has none.

Third, I introduce a variant. Suppose the two-bitser has an internal coin-recognition subsystem whose outputs feed a calibration routine: when slugs are accepted repeatedly, the routine adjusts the tolerance until they are rejected. The subsystem now has states whose functional role includes being corrected, and whose correctness conditions seem to be fixed by that role rather than by our interests. This looks like it gives Dennett a determinate content without original intentionality. But I worry it cuts the other way: if functional role is what fixes content, then Searle can say the subsystem has derived intentionality in sense (b), and that this is exactly what brains do too, so the original/derived distinction collapses rather than being vindicated. I am not sure whether this is a problem for my defense or an unexpected asset.

I would like comments on four things. (1) Have I characterized Dennett's and Searle's positions fairly, or have I built a strawman on either side? (2) Is the three-way distinction among senses of derived intentionality sound, and does Searle's objection really only land against sense (a)? (3) Does the learning variant help Dennett, hurt him, or leave the dispute where it was? (4) Where is the argument weakest, and what is the most serious objection I am not addressing?
