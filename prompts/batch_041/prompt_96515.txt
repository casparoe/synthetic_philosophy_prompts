I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and animal welfare law, and I keep encountering a practice whose philosophical commitments I cannot fully reconstruct. In both legislation and research ethics, the operative test for moral standing is sentience, and sentience is in practice inferred from neuroanatomy: a centralised nervous system, a pallium or cortex, a sufficiently large number of neurons, or some measure of brain size relative to body mass. The same inference appears in public argument, where the case for protecting octopuses or birds, or for excluding insects and crustaceans, is often made by pointing to absolute brain size or neuron count. Yet the science delivers a gradient — corvids and cephalopods have unusual architectures, some small-brained animals behave in ways we find hard to explain, and encephalisation quotients vary wildly across taxa — whilst the law and the ethics committee need something closer to a threshold. A colleague has suggested that the practice therefore presupposes a distinction between a substrate threshold and a substrate gradient, and that it quietly treats brain size as a proxy for sentience and sentience as a proxy for moral status.

I should like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments that this practice actually presupposes. What view of moral status does it require: that status is a function of computational capacity, of phenomenal consciousness, of belonging to a kind, or of something relational? If capacity is doing the work, is the relevant capacity absolute brain size, relative brain size, neuron number, neural integration, or architecture — and does the practice commit itself to any one of these, or does it merely fit several? Does it presuppose a threshold or a scalar, and does it treat brain size as constitutive of moral status or only as fallible evidence for it? Which rival views does the practice rule out, and which does it leave untouched? Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a utilitarian, a Kantian, and a biocentrist endorse the same legal line for different reasons, and what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its implicit view with a straightforwardly capacity-based criterion, or with a relational one? Please answer in British English, and do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the conceptual reconstruction.
