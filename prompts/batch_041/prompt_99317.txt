I'm a novelist — third book, currently stuck — and I've been re-reading Tolstoy's What Is Art? mainly in order to be annoyed by it. Put on your philosophy-of-art hat (or at least the hat of someone who's actually read the book rather than the blurb) and do me a proper ranking: take the criteria Tolstoy keeps returning to — that the artist has to genuinely feel what he's transmitting (sincerity), that the feeling must be communicable to everyone, peasants included, so no wilful obscurity, that the feeling itself should be morally elevating rather than just any old feeling, and his four tells of counterfeit art: borrowing, imitation, effectfulness, diversion — and sort them from most to least worth listening to as working advice for someone actually writing a novel. Say in a sentence or two why each lands where it does.

Then slot in three of my own candidates and tell me where they'd go relative to his: (1) the sentence-thrill test — does the prose give you a small physical pleasure on the second reading, when the plot can't be doing the work any more; (2) the re-read test — does the book survive being read again by someone who already knows how it ends; (3) the translation test — does it still land in a language the author couldn't read, which I take to be the honest test of whether the feeling was in the words or in the reader. I want a definately ordered list, not a menu of considerations, and I want you to be willing to say he's simply wrong about one of them. Think it through before you answer, and flag anywhere you're leaning on a contested reading of what he meant by sincerity, because I've seen people fight over that.
