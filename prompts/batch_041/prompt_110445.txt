I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on philosophy of mind and cognitive science — 16 students, one 75-minute session, no budget, no tech beyond a projector. I want to run a session where they generate a pattern of judgements themselves and only find out at the end that it has a name and a literature. Can you design it for me?

On the role: treat yourself as a philosopher of mind who's also taught experimental philosophy and run alot of classroom demos, so lean on what actually works with a room of restless undergrads rather than what looks tidy on a syllabus. Think it through carefully before you commit, and brainstorm a few different framings of the activity before you pick one and build it out fully.

The thing I want them to feel: there's a family of cases where someone acts for one reason, knows a side effect is coming, and honestly doesn't care about the side effect. Ask whether they brought that side effect about *intentionally* and people's answers slide depending on whether the side effect is good or bad — they're much readier to say yes when it's bad. I dont want to explain any of that up front. I want them to fill in the ballots, see the split in the room, and then have to explain why their own standard for 'intentionally' just moved.

Here's where I need you to go past the textbook version, because half of them have heard the textbook version. Build the session around four variants:

- the straight bad/good pair, as a baseline where they can see the split;
- a case where the side effect is bad but bad *for someone they'll be rooting against*, so that the students themselves approve of it — this is the one I most want, because it prises apart 'the outcome is bad' from 'I disapprove of the outcome';
- a case where the agent isn't a single person at all but a committee or a company, to see whether the asymmetry even gets off the ground when there's no one mind to point at;
- an epistemic version of the same cases, where instead of asking about intention you ask whether the person *knew* the side effect would happen. Some people in the field claim the same asymmetry shows up there. Don't build it assuming they're right — build it so it actually tests it.

Deliverables: a run of show with timings; the exact vignette texts for the cards, written so nothing tips them off about what's being measured and no card uses the word 'intentional' before the question that asks about it; how to collect answers without them anchoring on each other (ballots rather than hands, at least on the first pass); what I should put up on the board at the reveal; and then a debrief that maps each moment onto the underlying issue — whether people's concept of intentional action is itself morally loaded, versus the rival thought that they aren't reporting a concept at all but expressing blame or something conversational, versus the possibility that asking about other people's answers, or about money, would have pulled out something different.

Tell me honestly which feature of this the activity can't reproduce and that I'll just have to tell them — I assume the real work is far more controlled and answered in isolation than anything I can stage, but say what else I'm papering over. And what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how do I handle that without derailing the room? Finish with a one-page handout I can photocopy for them.
