I'm a journalist — I write a weekly science-and-ideas column, which in practice means I spend most of my week interviewing researchers and then trying to make their work sound interesting to people who'd rather be doing anything else. Along the way I've started collecting a pattern in how successful people get treated, and I've never found a name for it, so I'm hoping you can either find me one or help me build one.

Here's the shape of it, with a few instances so you can see what I'm pointing at. A researcher in a small department wins a big international prize. The local coverage is warm, but in the corridors something shifts — people start saying the work was really the group's, that he'd always been a bit pleased with himself, that prize committees go for a certain type. Nothing anyone could be called out for, mostly delivered as jokes. Then, months later, the same community is cheerfully claiming someone who made it abroad: an athlete winning for a foreign club, an emigrant who comes home to open a factory, and there's no resentment at all, the boasting just moves into the plural — 'one of ours'. But someone who makes it at home, in front of everyone, who takes the chair or the promotion or the telly slot and then stays in the room with all the people who didn't, gets it far worse than any outsider would. My working hunch is that the norm isn't against success, and isn't really envy either, since the people sniping at the local prizewinner will cheer the emigrant to the rafters. What seems to be policed is who gets to own the achievement — whether it reads as a property of the group or a property of the person — plus something about distance, because it's much easier to celebrate someone who isn't standing next to you. The candidates I've been offered are the tall-poppy thing they talk about in Australia and New Zealand, the law of Jante, the crabs-in-a-bucket idea, and ressentiment (a friend reads a lot of Nietzsche). None of them sits right for me, because they all seem to be about levelling as such, whereas mine seems to turn on credit and proximity. My sister, who works in a lab, says it's just what happens whenever people work in teams, and I don't think that's the whole story, but she may be onto something about the team-credit bit.

I'd like you to answer as a social anthropologist — or at least someone who knows the anthropology of egalitarian societies and the social psychology of in-group norms, and who's read enough moral philosophy to keep envy, resentment and justice seperate. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm a list of candidates and say in a sentence or two why each one does or doesn't fit, then give me your answer to the following. Is what I've described one phenomenon, or two or three bundled together? What's its nearest established relative, and what exactly separates the two — where does the neighbour apply and mine not, and vice versa? Where are the edges of mine: does it need a small face-to-face community, does it need the successful person to stay put, does it survive anonymity or a big city, does it work the same way when the success is inherited rather than earned? And if there genuinely isn't anything that fits, coin a term for it, define it, and tell me what your definition rules out — I'd much rather be told that I've muddled three existing things together than be handed one tidy label that hasn't earned its keep. Take your time with it and don't feel you have to be diplomatic about the bits of my description that don't hold up.
