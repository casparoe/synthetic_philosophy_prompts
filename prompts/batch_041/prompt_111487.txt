I retired about six years ago after thirty-odd years as a field ecologist, mostly soil work and long-term plots, and since then I've been reading philosophy pretty much nonstop. It's been a joy and also kind of maddening.

Here's what I want help with. Somewhere along the way I became a moral realist, and I'm fairly sure it's because of my old job. In ecology you learn to talk about a degraded watershed, a stressed population, an organism that's thriving versus one that's barely hanging on, and it never felt like I was just expressing my preferences. There are facts about what a given organism or community needs in order to do well, those facts can be hard to find out, and you get them wrong constantly and then the data corrects you. I've ended up thinking morality is continuous with that: there are facts about what helps and harms creatures like us and about what a decent human life requires, and they get discovered roughly the same way, by careful observation and by learning from people who've seen more of the world than we have. Not by consulting our feelings, and definately not by voting.

I know this is a position with a name and a long history of objections and I've read some of them. What I want is the honest map of what would count against it. What would have to be true, or what would have to happen, for me to be wrong? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be rather than just listing them.

In particular I want the ones that aren't about morality at all but about the machinery I'd be borrowing. My view seems to lean on there being real facts about what an organism is for and what it needs, and I gather philosophers of biology don't agree about what makes a function claim true. If the story is that a trait's function is what it was selected for, does my moral realism inherit whatever problems that story has? If instead function is just a matter of what a part happens to do in a system, does morality become relative to whichever system you draw the boundary around? Does my position stand or fall with that dispute, and would I even notice if it did?

Two more specific things I've been chewing on. First, I've read that if our moral attitudes are products of natural selection then that undercuts realism. But the same style of reasoning seems to undercut our confidence in our own reasoning, and I can't tell whether that's a reductio of the argument or just me being sloppy. Second, a case: imagine a species built so that what makes its members flourish is, by our lights, monstrous. Do I say they're morally mistaken, or that their flourishing facts are simply a seperate subject matter from ours? I can't tell which answer my own view forces, and I suspect the answer reveals whether I'm really a realist or a relativist with extra steps.

A few last things. What does my view predict that the rivals don't, anything I could actually catch it on? What would a committed defender of this kind of view themselves admit counts against them, since I don't only want the critics' list? Is there a version of it that's been quietly patched into unfalsifiability, and if so is that a real problem for a view of this kind or a sign I'm asking the wrong question? And the thing I'd personally find most persuasive, since I'm a scientist at heart: if the moral facts I believe in turn out to do no explanatory or practical work whatsoever, so that every case where I thought they were pulling their weight can be fully described without them, I'd take that very seriously. Tell me how much of the view survives that.

Also, if what I've described isn't really realism but some other thing with its own name, say so now. And I have a sneaking suspicion that one of my defeaters is just the old complaint about getting an ought out of an is, dressed up. Tell me whether that's right or whether I'm flattering myself.
