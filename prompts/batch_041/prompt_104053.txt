I'm in a reading group on teleology and the philosophy of biology, and we keep returning to a disagreement I can't settle to my own satisfaction. The topic is what our group has been calling free-floating rationales: reasons that figure in a genuine explanation of why some system does what it does, but that no one — not the system, not its designer, not any observer — ever actually has or entertains. The stock cases are familiar enough: a vine's tendril coils around the nearest support because coiling secures more light; a coastal grass grazed by herbivores spreads laterally because spreading escapes the grazer; an enzyme's active site has the shape it does because that shape binds the substrate. In each case we can state a rationale — do this rather than that, because it serves the end — and the rationale seems to do real explanatory work, yet there is no reasoner anywhere in the story. Half the group thinks this is a straightforward category error, that we are reading agency into a world that has none and should simply stop; the other half thinks the explanatory work is real and that refusing to call it reason-talk is just anthropocentric squeamishness. I want to know which of them is right, argued rather than asserted.

I've come to think the whole dispute is confused, and that the confusion is worth a careful argument. My view is that free-floating rationales do exist and are not a category error, but that the paradox only seems to arise because we run together two different things. Call one an explanatory rationale: a reconstruction an observer can give of why the system's arrangement serves an end, which is genuinely counterfactually robust — alter the relevant feature of the world and the system would have been arranged differently, in a way that tracks the end. Call the other an avowable rationale: something the system itself could, in principle, cite as its reason. My claim is that explanatory rationales do not require avowable ones, that the demand for an avowable occupant of the reasoner role is precisely the extra premise that generates the supposed paradox, and that once we drop that premise the free-floating rationale is a perfectly ordinary explanatory posit rather than a mysterious half-agent. What I want from you is the full argument for that position, worked out in the abstract and against at least one concrete case — not a survey of what other people have said about the topic, and not a reading list. Give me the reasoning, and give it to me in a form where I could see exactly where it might break.

Please write it in five named sections, in this order. First, "The claim," about 150 words, stating my position precisely enough that it could turn out to be false — including what I am and am not committing to when I say the rationale explains. Second, "The case for," roughly 600 words, giving the argument: at least two independent lines of support, one drawn from the explanatory practice of biology and one from the metaphysics of what it takes for an explanation to be correct, and working at least one of the cases above in detail rather than gesturing at it. Third, "The strongest objection," about 250 words, presenting the best version of the category-error view, stated so fairly that its proponents would accept the statement as their own. Fourth, "Reply," about 300 words, answering that objection and saying what, if anything, it forces me to give up. Fifth, "Verdict," about 100 words, with the bottom line up front and the single consideration that would most change my mind.
