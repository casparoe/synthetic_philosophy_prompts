Write a poem of twelve to sixteen lines, no fixed form, in the voice of the hitchhiker in Parfit's desert case, spoken to the driver who has stopped for him. The poem is a promise to pay on arrival, and it should carry the whole paradox in its bones rather than explain it: when the promise is made it is worth nothing to the driver unless it is backed by the sort of person making it, and when it comes due there will be nothing in the situation to make good on it. So let the poem be built almost entirely out of grounds the driver could use to trust the speaker -- evidence about what he is, rather than assurances about what he will do -- with the promise itself appearing once, plainly, and let the closing lines step outside the ride and out of the second person: they have to show the reckoning honestly, that arriving costs the speaker nothing and gains him nothing by paying, and still leave the payment resting on a reason that was settled before the poem began and cannot be re-decided inside it. It will open a seminar meeting on the case, so end on the line the room can argue about. Do not use the words rational, irrational, disposition, or consequentialist, and do not name the case or its author inside the poem; someone who knows the example should recognize it at once, and someone who doesn't should still have a good poem about a stranger, a long drive, and a debt. If it rhymes at all, the rhyme it must not dodge is owing with knowing.
