I'm in my third year and taking a metaphysics module that's just finished a unit on dispositions, and my study group has built an argument that I'm fairly sure proves far too much, only none of us can find the joint. It leans on the old thought that calling something fragile is just a way of saying that if it were struck, it would break. The chain goes:

(1) A disposition ascription is true only if some corresponding counterfactual of the form 'if C, then M' is true.
(2) For any way you fill in C and M for a given disposition, we can describe a case where C happens and M doesn't, because something interferes. The interference either strips the disposition away before the moment of manifestation (a fink: the wire goes dead the instant it's touched), or leaves the disposition perfectly intact and merely blocks its manifestation (a mask: the glass is struck inside a foam cradle, the poison is drunk alongside an antidote that does nothing to the poison itself).
(3) So no counterfactual of that form holds without exception.
(4) Therefore no disposition ascription is true.

What bothers me is that this doesn't just sink the conditional analysis, it sinks the dispositions along with it: salt isn't soluble, wires aren't live, arsenic isn't poisonous. And once you've said that, nothing that cites a disposition explains anything, and a huge amount of ordinary talk about ordinary objects comes out false. Nobody in the group thinks arsenic is harmless, so something has slipped, and I suspect it's in the move from (2) to (3), but I can't put my finger on it without sounding like I'm just denying the fink cases, which I don't want to do. My instinct is to keep (2) and give up (1) if I have to, and one of us has already tried patching it by adding a clause to the effect that no interfering factor is present, which I pointed out is either circular (interfering just means whatever stops the manifestation) or else quietly packs in a theory of which factors count as relevant. I'd like to know which step you'd reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what the alternatives cost — if I keep the counterfactual and repair it, what exactly am I taking on, and if I drop it and treat dispositions as primitive, what does that buy and what does it leave dangling? Also say how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure of, since I have to defend this in a seminar next week.
