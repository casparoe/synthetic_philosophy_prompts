I run a small funding programme — five people, three years each — for conceptual work on how you get from what someone does to what they actually want. Inverse reinforcement learning, the economics of revealed preference, the philosophy of preference, that whole junction. Deliberately not empirical ML: no new benchmarks, no engineering, they won't be training anything. They'll be thinking and writing.

The problem is that the selection panel has about nine candidate questions on the whiteboard and we can afford to back maybe two of them properly. So: which open questions in this area are actually worth the years, and in what order? I care much more about the reasoning than the list — why each one is upstream of other work, whether it's tractable for philosophers rather than ML people, and what would move it up or down the ranking over the next couple of years. And tell me which ones you'd drop outright because the answer, whatever it turned out to be, wouldn't change much downstream.

Here's what's been thrown at me, so you can see the shape of it. Don't feel bound by the list, and say if something obvious is missing or should be binned:

- What exactly is the object of inference? Even with perfect data, reward functions seem to be pinned down only up to transformations that leave the behaviour unchanged, which makes me wonder whether the project is really about recovering preferences at all or just about predicting choices more cleverly.
- How do you tell the difference between someone being noisy or suboptimal and someone having genuinely different values? Every practical method seems to need a knob for how rational the demonstrator is assumed to be, and that knob is doing an enormous amount of quiet work. Is that a technical inconvenience or a conceptual hole?
- Whose preferences? The person demonstrating, some aggregate of everyone affected, the person they'll turn into in ten years. And whether a group's preferences are even well-defined enough to serve as a target, or whether we're chasing an average that no one holds.
- A colleague keeps pushing something I genuinely can't tell is profound or trivial. Roughly: if the person knows their behaviour is being used to work out what they want, then being modelled changes what they do — and possibly changes what they want, since finding out what you're like is itself informative. So the thing being inferred isn't sitting still while you infer it. She wants to know whether the right target is a fixed point rather than a fact, and whether that makes the whole enterprise self-fulfilling rather than measuring anything. Related, and maybe seperate: people seem to have preferences about how their preferences get inferred — about being legible, about being read charitably, about not being modelled at all — which would mean the inference procedure can't be a neutral instrument.

No need for a reading list or a bibliography, I'm not going to check your citations. I want the argument for the ordering, including the criteria you're using and where they pull against each other.
