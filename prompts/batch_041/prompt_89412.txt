I am not a philosopher or an economist, but I have been keeping a decision journal for a few years and have become convinced that the usual way of writing down my uncertainty — a single probability, or a probability with a confidence interval — is missing something important. A friend who studied Keynes told me that Keynes distinguished between the probability of a proposition and the weight of the argument for it, and that he thought uncertainty in the economic sense is not just low probability but a lack of the kind of evidence that would make probability even measurable. I want to put that idea to work, but I do not know which of the following notations for recording a belief is most faithful to it. Please rank them from most to least faithful to the Keynesian distinction, and for each one say in a sentence or two what it captures and what it flattens. Treat them as ideal types, not as existing software.

1. A single probability, such as 70% chance of rain.
2. A probability plus a separate weight-of-evidence score, such as 70% chance, weight 3 out of 10.
3. A set of probabilities, such as between 50% and 90%.
4. A probability plus a list of unmodeled factors, such as 70%, but I have not considered the political situation.
5. A probability plus a verbal hedge, such as probably, but I would not bet on it.
6. A probability plus a second-order probability, such as 70%, with 60% confidence in that estimate.
7. A refusal to assign any probability, with a note about what would need to be known, such as no meaningful probability; I would need to know X and Y.

I am less interested in which notation is most useful for decision-making than in which one best preserves the difference between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty. If you think the distinction itself is unstable, say so, but still give the ranking.
