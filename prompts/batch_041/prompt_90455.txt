Right, so I've been chewing over this thing people call Goodhart's law — I think it comes from a British economist at the Bank of England back in the seventies, the idea being that any number you start steering by stops being a good number — and I've ended up with a theory I can't shake off. Everyone says the engine is pressure. You push on a proxy hard enough and it buckles. I don't buy that. I think the engine is publicity: a number tracks the thing fine while it's a by-product of people just doing the thing, and it dies the moment it becomes common knowledge that the number is what's being counted. Two warehouses measure the same thing, one of them puts the scores on a whiteboard by the door, and only that one rots. If I'm right then half the usual stories about this are blaming the wrong cause.

What I want is a decision tree I could actually walk a colleague through — them answering questions, me ending up at a named position on *what does the damage*. Sort by what the person thinks is doing the work, not by which story they've heard of.

Root it in something everyone in the conversation already accepts: that a metric can stop tracking what it was meant to track. From there, each node should be a question someone can answer without knowing where it leads, and phrased as a concrete case or claim, not as a label. Don't ask anyone whether they believe in the adversarial story or the causal story, ask them what they'd expect to happen in a specific setup.

Some distinctions I care about, because they're what my own view lives or dies on:
- whether the rot is located in the metric itself, in the people being measured, or in the world moving underneath both;
- whether knowing that the number counts is what breaks it, or whether it would have broken anyway once the pressure went up;
- whether the failure is gradual and predictable or a cliff edge;
- whether more than one metric, or a rotating one, actually fixes anything or just relocates the problem.

Leaves I want to see, at minimum, whatever you end up calling them: (a) my publicity thesis, that common knowledge is the trigger; (b) the plain pressure thesis, that intensity of optimisation is the trigger and awareness is neither here nor there; (c) the statistical one, that it's mostly regression to the mean and measurement noise and there's no deep mystery; (d) the causal one, that the proxy was never on the path to the real goal and more data wouldn't have saved it; (e) the adversarial one, that the measured agents are gaming you and the interesting question is how they find the seam; (f) the deflationary one, that the whole thing is near-tautological and says nothing about metrics in particular; (g) the institutional one, that what breaks is accountability rather than measurement, and the number is just the stick the blame travels down. Add any leaf you think belongs that I've missed, but mark which names are yours rather than terms of art.

Cases you have to use at least once each: a hospital waiting-time target; a school league table; a call-centre target for calls handled per hour; a reward model trained on human approval in a machine-learning setup; a line-of-code count used to judge programmers; a wildlife population count used to decide whether a species is recovering; and my own invented one, a coffee shop that starts counting loyalty cards handed out rather than repeat customers. If a case can't separate two leaves, say so instead of forcing it.

Size: no more than ten nodes, no more than eight leaves. Where two leaves can't be told apart by anything in the tree, flag the pair and say what extra case you'd need to split them. Where a path through the tree leads to a combination nobody actually holds, note it and say whether you think it's incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored — I'd rather see the empty slots than have them papered over.

Give it back to me as JSON, British English please, with exactly these fields:

- "root": the id of the starting node
- "nodes": a list, each with "id", "question" (the thing put to the person, in plain speech), and "answers" (a list of objects with "answer" and "goes_to", the latter being a node or leaf id)
