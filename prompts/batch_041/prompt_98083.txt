I'm a software engineer by trade and have no philosophical training beyond a stubborn aquarium habit — for the past six years my main tank has been home to about forty cherry shrimp, and I have spent far more evenings than I care to admit watching them contest a single algae wafer. Through a volunteering connection I have been asked to give the after-dinner speech at a regional aquaculture conference, a "sustainable production" dinner of roughly a hundred and fifty people: mostly farm managers and processing-plant staff, plus a few vets, some feed suppliers, two journalists, and one or two academics who will be quietly checking whether I know what I am talking about. I have twelve minutes, and a two-line toast to the outgoing chair to fit in at the end.

What I want to argue is that the industry's running argument about whether prawns, crabs and lobsters feel pain is the wrong argument, and that a second question keeps going unasked. The distinction I want the speech built on is between a threshold view of moral status, on which a creature either counts or does not, so everything hangs on a fact about its nervous system, and a graded view, on which what you owe a creature scales with how probable and how intense its experience is, so everything hangs on a decision about how to act under uncertainty. I want to argue, without ever asserting that crustaceans definitely suffer, that the graded view is the one that binds them — and that on it a handful of cheap and unglamorous changes are already owed: stunning or chilling before killing rather than dropping animals into boiling water, handling that avoids the eyestalk ablation still routine in parts of prawn hatchery work (the practice of severing a broodstock female's eyestalk to trigger spawning), and not splitting crabs while they are alive. That distinction should do real work in the speech rather than decorate it: it should stop me from claiming settled science, make me concede honestly what nobody yet knows, and still land the conclusion that uncertainty is a reason to act early and cheaply rather than to wait.

Please keep the evidence claims general and hedged — no named studies, researchers or figures, since I cannot check them and would not be able to defend them from the podium. The room is the hard part: thin margins, a word like "welfare" that many there have only ever heard used as a stick, and an audience that will switch off the moment it thinks it is being told its livelihood is cruel. So the speech needs to be warm and funny enough to hold a dinner crowd, generous about what farmers already do well, and it must not turn into a lecture for the two academics in the corner. I would like it to sound like something a person with no philosophy training could actually deliver out loud, and to leave the audience with one sentence they could repeat to a colleague the next morning.
