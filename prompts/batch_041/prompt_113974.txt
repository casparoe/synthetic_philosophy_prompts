Question: In Fear and Trembling, Johannes de Silentio asks whether there is a teleological suspension of the ethical. Explain what this means, how it differs from the sacrifice of the tragic hero, and why Silentio says he cannot understand Abraham. Then assess whether the book commits its author to moral antinomianism, understood as the view that a private divine command can override ordinary ethical requirements. Be careful to distinguish the pseudonymous narrator's claims from Kierkegaard's own views, and explain the roles of the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith.

Student answer: Johannes de Silentio argues that the ethical is the universal, which applies to everyone and can be mediated in language. The tragic hero, like Agamemnon, sacrifices his daughter for a higher ethical end, the good of the state, and so his act remains within the ethical; it is tragic because he must violate a particular duty for a higher duty. Abraham, by contrast, is not sacrificing Isaac for the sake of the state or any higher ethical good. His act is for God, and so the ethical is teleologically suspended: it is not abolished, but it is set aside relative to a higher telos, the individual's absolute relation to the absolute. This is why Silentio says Abraham is either a murderer or a knight of faith; from within the ethical, the act looks like murder. Abraham's silence follows from this: the universal is the medium of speech, so he cannot explain himself. He cannot make himself understood, and this is his distress. Kierkegaard's central claim is that faith requires a leap beyond the universal, and that the knight of faith is the one who, having made the movement of infinite resignation, believes by virtue of the absurd that he will receive Isaac back. The knight of infinite resignation gives up everything and is reconciled to loss; the knight of faith goes further and believes the impossible. Abraham is therefore not a tragic hero but a knight of faith. As for antinomianism, Kierkegaard does not simply say that any divine command overrides ethics. The suspension is not a general rule; it is a paradox that cannot be mediated. Silentio repeatedly says he cannot understand Abraham, and he does not present him as a moral example to be imitated. However, the logic of the suspension does imply that when an individual has an absolute relation to God, the ethical can be overridden, and so the book does leave open the possibility that a private divine command can justify what would otherwise be murder. That is why the book is so unsettling. But this is not moral relativism, because the telos is God, not the individual's preference. The knight of faith is not the same as the knight of infinite resignation; the latter is a preliminary movement, while the former adds the absurd belief that Isaac will be restored. So the teleological suspension is not a rejection of ethics but a paradox that faith alone can inhabit.
