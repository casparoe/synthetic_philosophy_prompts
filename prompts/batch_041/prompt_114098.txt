I did thirty-odd years in a biochemistry lab and retired a few years back, and since then I've somehow ended up helping run a U3A philosophy group — sixteen or so of us, mixed ages, a couple who did a bit of philosophy at uni forty years ago, the rest just curious and quite sharp. I've just read Taurek's paper on whether the numbers count and it's been needling me for a fortnight in a way that no paper on enzyme kinetics ever did. I've volunteered to do the next session on it, and I don't want to stand at the front and talk at them, because the whole thing turns on whether you can actually feel the pull of it rather than just nod along.

So: design me an activity. One room, ninety minutes, no projector, no laptops, tea at the break. Materials I'm happy to make: index cards, a bowl, a whiteboard, a handful of counters, a coin. Nothing that needs real money — though I do want something genuinely at stake for the people who lose, even if it's a small thing.

What I want them to come away having felt, in their own deciding and not in my summary: that the move from "each of these six people has an equal claim" to "so save the five" is not a move the equal claims make for you; that the counting comes in from outside, from where the chooser is standing; and that once you've noticed where it comes in you can't quite un-notice it. Then I want them to feel that choosing by an equal chance is a serious answer rather than a cop-out — and then feel the standard objection to it land on them.

My half-formed plan, which you should feel free to gut:

Round 1 (10 min). Everyone is a lone rescuer with one vial of something that will save exactly one person, and six people in front of them who'll die without it. (Invent your own framing — I don't want the trolley.) Each person writes their reason on a card, anonymously, and posts it in the bowl before any talking is allowed. Then I read the cards out and sort them on the board. My guess is that nearly all of them say some version of "because there are five of them".

Round 2 (20 min). Now everyone draws a card from the bowl: six cards name one of the six victims, the rest say rescuer. Each of the six gets sixty seconds to argue for their own life, in the first person, and the rule is that nobody may use a number — no "five", no "more", no "most", no "the others". I write the six statements up side by side, and they come out almost identical in form. Then the rescuers vote. The people who lose have to sit out the rest of the session in the corridor and watch through the window — that's the stake: real, small, and the closest I can get to a payoff without money.

Round 3 (15 min). The reveal: before the vote, each rescuer had a one-in-six chance of being the lone one. Second vote, same rules. Then actually toss the coin and implement the result. On the board I put what each of the six has, in chance of surviving: under "save the five" it's 100,100,100,100,100,0; under the coin it's 50 all round. Then I ask them to do the sums and see who gained what, and from whom.

Round 4 (15 min). The survivors have to justify the outcome, face to face, to whoever sat in the corridor. Then a round of "who was wronged, and how many times?" — each of the losers gets a seperate card reading "you lost your life", and the group has to point at the person who lost five lives. Nobody can.

Then the debrief (20 min) should map each of those moments onto a piece of the arguement: where the counting entered; why every first-person claim came out the same; what the "greater loss" is supposed to be a loss to; why the coin toss looks like equal respect and why it costs the five so much; and what Taurek is and isn't claiming. Tell me where I should let them push back rather than defend him for them.

Two more things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what to do about it — I'd rather hear it now than find out in front of sixteen people. I'm especially worried that the moment I mention a lottery they'll all be in favour of it because it sounds fair and modern, and the whole second half will go flat. Second, be straight with me about what the activity can't do and what I'll have to say out loud myself: I've a suspicion the room will mostly save the five at the end and feel fine about it, and that the feeling I'm chasing is a wobble rather than a conversion. And if Taurek's own version of the case has a friend of the rescuer in it — check that before you run it — then the partiality question is doing a lot of work, and my scenario has no friends in it at all.
