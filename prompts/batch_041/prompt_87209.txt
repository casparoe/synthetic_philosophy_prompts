My roommate's doing a master's in finance and I did philosophy, and we were up til like 1am arguing about whether you can learn anything just from the fact that someone wants to trade with you. He kept invoking some theorem he only half-remembers — something about how if two people share the same prior and it's common knowledge that they're both rational, they can never agree to a trade, so the mere existence of a trade must mean something's off: either someone has information the other doesn't, or they don't really share a prior, or they're trading for reasons that have nothing to do with the price. I don't remember the name and I don't think he does either, so don't just tell me the name, I want the actual content. Anyway, I gave him my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and now I want to know what principle is actually driving them, because I suspect I'm being inconsistent. Here they are. Me buying into an index fund at the market price from some anonymous seller: tells me nothing about whether the price is right — could be a pension fund rebalancing, somebody paying tuition, a robot. My old boss, who still works at the company, offers to buy my shares in that company well above the market price: I'd update hard, he knows something, that trade is pure information. A farmer selling wheat futures and a bakery buying them, both of whom think the current price is roughly fair: totally unremarkable, neither one is telling me where wheat is headed, they're just moving risk off their own books. Two people who've read the exact same analyst report, both sharp, one wants to buy the stock and the other wants to sell at the same price: that feels wrong to me, like at least one of them is missing something, or they don't actually disagree about the stock so much as about their own situations. A stranger who's obviously a poker player offers to bet me even money on a game I only half understand: I say no, and I take the offer itself as evidence I'm about to lose. I win an auction by being the highest bidder: I should feel worse about the thing I bought than I did before the auction, since winning means everyone else valued it less. And a stock I own drops 8% on a day when I can't find any news: I update hard — somebody out there knows something, the price is talking to me.

But hold on, the last case and the first case are the same thing, aren't they? An anonymous counterparty on the other side of a trade at the market price. In one I shrug, in the other I say the price is talking to me, and the only difference is that the price moved. Same with the farmer/bakery case versus the two-people-same-report case — one I shrug at, the other makes me suspicious, and the only difference is that the farmer and the baker have different risk exposures while the two stock pickers supposedly don't. So my half-baked principle is this: a trade is evidence about the thing being traded only when I can't explain the counterparty's willingness to trade by facts about them — their other holdings, their risk appetite, their cash needs, their priors. And a disagreement between two people with the same evidence is a real disagreement only if their priors differ; otherwise somebody's just being irrational and the disagreement itself tells me nothing about who's right.

Is that a principle that actually gets all seven, or do I have to give something up? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and why that one rather than the others? And what does it say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a guy at a flea market sells me a watch he claims is rare, cheap — do I take the cheapness as evidence it's fake, or as evidence he just needs cash? Those are the same observable thing and I genuinely can't tell which way I lean, so tell me what my own principle implies and whether I should trust it there. Also, my friend who's just a more optimistic person than me and has no private information whatsoever offers to buy a stock off me at the current market price because he has a good feeling about it — do I take his money, and does his willingness to trade mean anything at all, or is that just the same case as the farmer and the bakery again?
