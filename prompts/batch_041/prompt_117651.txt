I am in the second year of a joint English and Philosophy degree, and this term I have ended up taking two modules that keep colliding: a philosophy of language course that has just finished its unit on Austin and Searle, and a Shakespeare paper for which The Merchant of Venice is our set text. In the seminar I floated the idea that the whole play is an argument that Austin underrates uptake, and the tutor said this was either a very good observation or a category mistake and declined to say which, so I am bringing it to you. The trial scene reads to me like a set piece for everything we have been taught about performative utterances. The bond is a paradigm speech act — the right parties, a recognised procedure, an outcome constituted by the words rather than described by them — and the courtroom is a chain of utterances that do things: an accusation, a plea for the law, a judgment, a sentence, a pardon. But the scene also presses on the theory precisely where the theory is most confident. Portia is not a doctor of law; she arrives in disguise with a letter from a cousin who never appears on stage, and yet the Duke and the court treat her ruling as valid, when by Austin's own requirement that the persons and circumstances be appropriate this ought to be a misfire and the act void. The sentence that ends the scene requires Shylock to convert to Christianity, an act extorted rather than sincere and therefore, on the same scheme, an abuse rather than a misfire — yet everyone proceeds as though it were a completed and binding act. And the bond itself, about as felicitous an act as the play contains, turns out to be impossible to execute, because the words grant a pound of flesh and are silent about blood.

What I want is a proper reading of the play through speech act theory, of the kind I would write if I had another month: one that takes the play and the theory equally seriously rather than using either as an illustration of the other. I would like the trial worked through in detail — the bond, Portia's reinterpretation of it, the Duke's sentence, the forced conversion — and I want you to say whether the play is showing that felicity is not a property an utterance has at the moment of utterance but a social achievement that later speech acts can confer or withdraw, or whether I am overreading a courtroom scene that is simply about legal fictions. I am also unsure what to make of the dispute about reading, since Shylock insists on the letter of his bond while the Christians appeal to mercy and to the spirit; is that merely the distinction between sentence meaning and speaker meaning that we did with Grice, or does the play stage a case the distinction cannot hold? And I would like the final act brought in, because the ring plot — a promise made, a token given away, a promise broken, and a wife who then interrogates her husband about the letter of his word — strikes me as a case the theory has trouble placing. I half remember that Austin's list of infelicities includes a slot for participants failing to conduct themselves subsequently in the way the procedure required, but I cannot tell whether that covers a broken promise or only an insincere one. It also occurs to me that the casket test is a constitutive rule in Searle's sense, a procedure that creates the fact it appears to report, and I would be glad of a paragraph on it, though the trial is my main interest.

Where the reading stops working I would much rather you said so than forced it: if there are scenes the theory cannot capture, or if applying Austin's machinery to Shylock's bond distorts a scene that is really about power, law, and antisemitism rather than about the philosophy of language, that belongs in the essay. I do not need a bibliography or extended quotation — I have the text and the module reading list in front of me, and I would rather have the argument than the apparatus. Please end by saying what the play contributes to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not: whether Shakespeare dramatises a problem about performative utterances that Austin and Searle leave untouched.
