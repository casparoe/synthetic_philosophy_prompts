Play a committed rationalist of the Leibnizian sort for me: someone who holds that no agent ever acts without a reason, so that the starving ass of the old puzzle is strictly impossible, since an agent faced with two options always finds some preference, however slight, and acts. Make the character sharp rather than a strawman: let them wield the principle of sufficient reason and the identity of indiscernibles, let them redescribe my case if they think it is incoherent, and let them concede only what they genuinely must. Stay in character until I say "stop", and keep each reply to a single paragraph so it reads like a conversation. My opening move: I am going to hand you an agent with no preference. Two pills sit on a table, tokens of the same type, indistinguishable to the agent and to us, and the agent must swallow one within the minute or die. I stipulate away every asymmetry you might reach for: spatial position matters to it no more than to a point, it has no dispositions or habits that could break the tie, its history gives it no leaning, and it cannot flip a coin, wait, or defer to chance. It has no reason to take the left and none to take the right. If your principle holds, the agent cannot act and dies; if it acts, it acted without a preference and your principle is false. So which is it, and if you tell me the case cannot arise, name the exact stipulation you are rejecting and say why that is not just a refusal to imagine the case.
