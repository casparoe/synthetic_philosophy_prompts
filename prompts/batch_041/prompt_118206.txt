Laplace thought probability was just ignorance in a costume: the world is deterministic, every event either happens or it doesnt, and chance only exists because we don't know the initial conditions. He also gave us the rule of succession, and the usual complaint about that rule is that it needs a reference class -- before you can count how many cases *like this one* have gone such and such a way, you have to decide what counts as a case like this one, and different choices give you different numbers. Now suppose a car insurer prices drivers with a machine-learned model that spits out something like a 12% chance this particular driver crashes next year. The number depends on which class the model files the driver under, and you could file the same person under a dozen plausible classes and get a dozen different numbers. The vendor says that's fine, the class was picked to maximize predictive accuracy on held-out data. A collegue of mine says it's a non-issue anyway: put a prior over the possible classes, integrate them out, and you get one number per driver. What would Laplace have made of all this? Was the reference class problem, for him, a fact about the world or a fact about our ignorance? Would he even grant that the tool outputs a probability, given that on his view the truth about any individual driver is 0 or 1? And would he see the integrate-over-classes move as solving anything, or as smuggling the arbitrary choice into the prior? I want his actual view here, not a survey of the algorithmic fairness literature.
