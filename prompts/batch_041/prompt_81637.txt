Two weeks ago a former student of mine, now a science journalist, sent me the draft of a chapter on consciousness for his first trade book, along with a note asking whether I would let my name appear on the jacket. The chapter is built around what its author calls illusionism, and while the exposition is brisk and mostly competent, something in it kept catching at me on a first read without my being able to say precisely where the trouble lay. The relevant stretch of the draft runs as follows:

'Illusionism is the view that consciousness does not exist. On this picture, the feeling that there is something it is like to see red or taste coffee is a trick the brain plays on itself — a "user illusion," in the same sense in which the icons on a computer desktop are not real files but convenient fictions that let you operate the machine. Illusionists point out that we never catch consciousness in the act; all we ever observe, from the inside or the outside, are brain processes and the reports they produce. They therefore expect that as neuroscience matures, talk of experience will be replaced by talk of information processing, much as talk of vital spirits was replaced by biochemistry, and that the puzzle of consciousness will come to be recognised as a question about why we say the things we do rather than about what is really going on. The obvious objection — that a trick requires someone to be tricked, and so the illusionist must be presupposing the very consciousness he denies — is, they reply, merely a confusion.'

I have no interest in policing the man's prose, and I am plainly not the audience for the book. What I want is a clear picture of what I would be putting my name behind. Please take the passage claim by claim and sort it three ways: what a competent defender of illusionism would accept without hesitation; what is the sort of simplification a defender would want amended in a footnote; and what a reader would be actively misled by. Where the passage runs two distinct theses together, I would like them separated and labelled, since I suspect at least one of the joints in it is doing quiet work. I am especially curious about two things. First, whether the closing dismissal of the objection is one that an illusionist can genuinely make, or whether the objection returns in a sharper form once the dismissal is spelled out — and if it returns, in what form. Second, whether the prediction in the middle of the passage, about how the vocabulary of experience will fare, actually follows from the metaphysical claim, or is a separate and considerably stronger commitment that the illusionist need not take on. Assume I know the standard vocabulary of the philosophy of mind and the shape of the debate; I do not need a survey of the literature, a history of the problem, or a reading list — only an audit precise enough that I can decide what to do about the blurb.
