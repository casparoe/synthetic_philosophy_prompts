And here's what the student wrote:

The rhyme is usually read as saying that enormous events hang on trivial ones — the nail, the shoe, the horse, the rider, the battle, the kingdom. The question is whether that shows history is contingent, and I think it does, though not for the reason people usually give.

First, determinism and predictability come apart. A system can be perfectly deterministic and still unpredictable, because of sensitive dependence on initial conditions: two starting states that differ by less than we can measure can end up in completely different places. Weather is the standard case. So unpredictability doesn't prove indeterminism, and determinism doesn't guarantee that we can forecast. That's the right way to read the rhyme — the kingdom falls because of the nail, and nobody in that stable had any way of knowing it.

Second, this is where path dependence does its work. Once the horse is gone the sequence is locked in: no rider, no battle, no kingdom. Early accidents get amplified and then entrenched, which is why the same story turns up in economics with QWERTY and railway gauges, where an early and essentially arbitrary choice becomes permanent because everyone else adopts it. Path dependence therefore means history is inefficient: we get stuck with bad arrangements that no rational planner would have chosen, and we can't get out of them. The nail is the arbitrary choice, the lost kingdom is the lock-in.

So the rhyme does show history is contingent. Contingent here means the outcome wasn't necessary given the initial conditions — a butterfly's wingbeat in Brazil really can be the cause of a tornado in Texas, and a careless hand in a stable really can be the cause of the fall of a kingdom. If the farrier had been more careful, the kingdom stands. Because tiny causes can have enormous effects, the past could have gone otherwise, and that's what licenses counterfactual history: asking what would have happened if the nail had held is not idle, because nothing in the setup forced the outcome we got.

The obvious objection is that if the world is deterministic then the kingdom had to fall. But that only bites if you confuse determinism with predictability, which we've already separated.
