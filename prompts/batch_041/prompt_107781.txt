Design a 90-minute activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on moral uncertainty, about 18 students, that makes them feel the force of the aggregation problem rather than hearing it described. In the setup, each student is an advisor holding one moral theory with a stated credence, and the group must issue a single recommendation among four concrete options in a policy dilemma, earning points rather than money depending on which option is chosen and on which theory a final reveal declares correct. Build in two features and leave them to be discovered. First, several assigned theories are near-duplicates, subtly different formulations of the same underlying view, so that any procedure counting theories rather than weighing how sharply each one discriminates among the options can be gamed by splitting one view in two. Second, two of the theories are to be compared on a shared cardinal scale whose zero point and unit the group must set for itself, and their choice of scale alone flips the recommendation. Give the group the option to spend one round buying additional evidence at a cost, so that deferral has a felt price.

Provide the roles and materials, the payoff scheme, a timeline, and a debrief mapping what happened onto maximizing expected choiceworthiness versus following one's favorite theory, intertheoretic comparison, theory individuation, weighting each theory by how much its verdicts actually vary across the options, and the alternative of treating theories as parties that negotiate a joint policy instead of voting. State what will predictably go wrong the first time — students collapsing into ordinary majority voting, someone declaring the exercise incoherent, the near-duplicate trick being spotted in the first ten minutes — and how to handle each. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: it stages disagreement among people where the philosophical problem concerns a single agent's uncertainty, and it stipulates the scale rather than solving it. Say what the facilitator must supply in place of each.

Write in American English.
