I'm a TA for a second-year course on practical rationality, and we spend a week on money pumps — the transitivity case, the diachronic case where the agent's preferences shift across time, the objection that the pump only shows exploitability and not irrationality. The students get the mechanics quickly and then run out of things to say, so I want a satire they can argue with. Please write a mock guidance document, roughly 750–850 words, in the flat numbered-section register of a consumer-protection regulator: a fictional agency of your own invention (not a parody of a real one, and please don't put words in the mouth of anyone living) instructing retailers on how they may and may not profit from customers whose preferences cycle. The piece should take entirely for granted, without ever defending it, that a customer with intransitive or time-inconsistent preferences is a proper object of regulation, and then work out the administrative consequences in deadpan: what counts as the pump itself, given that each individual trade is endorsed by the customer at the moment of making it; what a compliant countermeasure looks like, with commitment devices, cooling-off periods, and price schedules that charge for undoing an earlier commitment treated as equivalent instruments; how to classify the customer who simply refuses the second trade; whether the merely incomplete customer — no cycle, just gaps — belongs in the same protected class or a different one; and finally the question the agency cannot dodge, which is whose preference it is protecting, the customer at the till or the customer who will be at the till next Tuesday, and by what authority it prefers one to the other. The target is the habit of treating pump-vulnerability as decisive proof of irrationality, together with the paternalism that quietly follows from it; the joke should be that the regulator is forced to be a resolute chooser on the customer's behalf, which is exactly the controversial move. Humour should come from the machinery rather than from jokes about bureaucrats.

A few specifications. Keep it legible to someone who has never opened a decision theory text — every clause should make sense on its own, with the technical bite available only to those who know it. Use the familiar three-way distinction between the chooser who anticipates the future reversal, the one who doesn't, and the one who has decided in advance not to be re-optimised at the later node, but treat them as three regulatory categories with different rights and disclosure duties, since I think that is the funniest available use of the taxonomy. Include at least one worked example in full — I'd like a loyalty programme or a vending machine that charges the customer a fee for each step of a cycle that returns her to where she started — and one provision covering the case where each increment is acceptable on its own and the endpoint is not, where the agency has to decide whether it intervenes at the increment or only at the endpoint. Cite the agency's own prior guidance and its internal dissent where it helps; invent document numbers, effective dates, and a form to be filed. No winking, no editorial asides, no telling the reader what is being satirised.

After the document, break the register and add a plainly written note of about 150 words. In it, say which premises the piece leans on that are genuinely contested by people who work on this — for instance whether the pump's normative force depends on assumptions about what preferences are or about the counterparty's existence — and how the satire would read if the other side had it right. Then state your own view of whether vulnerability to a money pump is a defect in a preference or merely an opportunity for someone else, and name the argument or result that would change your mind, along with the point in your reasoning where you'd expect it to be weakest.
