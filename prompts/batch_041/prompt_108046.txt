I've just started sitting on a small grant panel (astro/particle-adjacent stuff), and I keep noticing that we all reach for the same move without ever defending it: a theory that called its shot — predicted something nobody had seen yet — gets treated as confirmed in a way that a theory which merely explains what we already knew doesn't. String theory gets dinged for never coughing up the novel prediction; Einstein gets the halo for Mercury's perihelion, even though that was a known anomaly he was trying to account for. And we routinely say a lot of proposals are just curve-fitting. So what epistemology is this practice presupposing, and what are we implicitly commited to? Does it presuppose that old evidence literally can't confirm a theory, or only that novel prediction is better evidence of something else — that the theory wasn't cooked up to fit, or that it's tracking truth? Is the panel being inconsistent when it celebrates the perihelion and sniffs at curve-fitting, or can one story cover both cases? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it just fits comfortably with, and tell me whether any coherent probabilistic account licenses the practice as it stands, or whether we're all quietly committed to something we'd deny if anyone asked us straight.
