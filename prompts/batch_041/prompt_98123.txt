A drug regulator has adopted a rule that a compound may be sold over the counter only if its potential for serious interaction with common medications is low. Because the drafters could not agree on a numerical cutoff and wanted to avoid the appearance of arbitrariness, the rule approves a compound only when its interaction risk is determinately low and routes every penumbral case to an expert review board whose decisions are final and are not treated as precedents. A member of the drafting committee now objects that the design collapses under either reading of vagueness: if a vague predicate has a hidden sharp boundary, then "determinately low" is just "low" and the review board is decorative; if there is no boundary and truth is a matter of supertruth across precisifications, then the approval condition is either empty or arbitrary and the board is doing the real work under a different name. Please describe the most important implications of the disagreement between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness for whether this objection lands and for how the rule will actually behave. I am especially interested in:

1. Whether the two views agree that there is a fact of the matter about any given borderline compound, and what follows from each view for whether an applicant rejected on a penumbral case has been wronged.
2. What each view says about the review board's authority: is it discovering a determinate but unknown status, constructing a precisification, or deciding something the rule genuinely left open?
3. What each view implies about higher-order vagueness — compounds that are neither determinately low nor determinately not low, and where it is itself indeterminate whether they are determinate — and whether the rule's two-tier structure survives it.
4. What each view implies about the choice between this rule and a bright-line numerical cutoff: is the vague rule a defect to be repaired, a legitimate delegation of authority, or something the regulator is not entitled to enact?
5. The error profile: which view predicts more false approvals, more false rejections, or more unpredictable decisions, and on what reasoning.
