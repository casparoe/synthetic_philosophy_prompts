I am a professor of philosophy and I chair the algorithmic-fairness review board at a state human-services agency. The board signs off on the predictive models the agency intends to deploy — at present a screening tool that ranks families for child-welfare investigation and a model that triages disability-benefit applications — and it meets once a month for ninety minutes. Its members are a clinician, an agency lawyer, a statistician, two community representatives, and me. None of them can be asked to reconstruct a proof. The director signs off on our recommendation, and a bad recommendation is a real harm to real people.

I know the formal literature well enough to see the trap we keep falling into. Vendors and program offices arrive proposing to make a model fair by adjusting it, as though fairness were a single dial. I know that the standard statistical criteria — parity in approval rates across groups, equal false-positive and false-negative rates, and calibration within each group — cannot in general hold together when the groups differ in base rate, and that even the meaning of a fair outcome depends on what the model is actually predicting, which is frequently a convenient proxy rather than the thing we care about. What the board needs is not another seminar on the impossibility results but a procedure it can actually run.

You are an expert in fair machine learning, in the philosophy of measurement, and in the design of institutional decision procedures. Write that procedure. Address me as a philosopher who already knows the theory, and give me something I can hand to a board that does not. Before you draft it, brainstorm the cases the procedure has to survive and the ways a procedure of this kind typically goes wrong, and think carefully about the difference between what the impossibility results forbid outright and what they merely make expensive. Then produce, in order: the questions the board should put to every proposed model; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure returns a no; and the point at which it must stop and hand the decision to a named human being because the remaining question is one of values rather than mathematics. Mark plainly which steps are genuine constraints from the impossibility results and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and state what the procedure cannot do.

Then run it on three cases: (1) the child-welfare screen, where the recorded target is a later investigation rather than the maltreatment we actually care about, base rates differ across groups, the vendor claims the model is calibrated, and the community representatives demand equal error rates; (2) the disability-benefit model, where the agency wants equal approval rates but the underlying need is genuinely unequal; and (3) a case of your choosing in which the procedure returns an answer the agency will not like. Be specific about who signs what, and say what the board should do when it runs out of time before the questions are exhausted.
