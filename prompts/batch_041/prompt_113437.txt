I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course in decision theory, and I am grading the essay section of the final examination. I would like a careful assessment of one student's response. Please judge the substance of the argument rather than the fluency of the prose, and work through the answer part by part.

The examination question (20 points) read as follows:

> **Newcomb's problem.** You are shown two boxes. The first is transparent and contains $1,000. The second is opaque and contains either $1,000,000 or nothing. You may take either both boxes or the opaque box alone. A predictor, whose predictions in recorded trials have been correct in the overwhelming majority of cases, made its prediction before you entered the room: it placed $1,000,000 in the opaque box if and only if it predicted that you would take the opaque box alone. You know all of this, and you know that the prediction has already been made.
>
> Evidential decision theory (EDT) and causal decision theory (CDT) give different verdicts in this case.
>
> (a) State the criterion each theory uses to evaluate an act, and show how each criterion yields its verdict. [5 points]
>
> (b) Does either verdict depend on the assumption that the predictor is less than perfectly reliable? Explain. [5 points]
>
> (c) Does EDT's verdict require backward causation? Explain. [5 points]
>
> (d) Assess the following argument: "Whatever the theories say, the people who take one box walk away with a million dollars, and the people who take both walk away with a thousand. A theory of rational choice that recommends an act leaving its followers poorer is not credible." Say what a causal decision theorist should reply. [5 points]

The student's answer:

> (a) Both theories evaluate an act by its expected utility; they differ over which probabilities enter the expectation. EDT evaluates an act by its expected utility conditional on the act's being performed. For each available act A one compares the quantity Σ P(O | A)·U(O), where the probabilities are conditional on A. In Newcomb's problem, taking the opaque box alone raises the conditional probability that it contains $1,000,000 to near certainty, so the conditional expected utility of one-boxing is roughly $1,000,000, whereas that of two-boxing is roughly $1,000. One-boxing is therefore required. CDT evaluates an act by the expected utility of its consequences, where the relevant probabilities are those of the outcomes the act would bring about: the act is assessed under the supposition that it is performed, while everything the act does not cause is held fixed. The contents of the opaque box were settled before the choice, and the choice cannot cause them to change, so two-boxing is worth $1,000 more than one-boxing in either state of the world. CDT therefore requires taking both boxes.
>
> (b) Yes. The two theories part company precisely because the predictor is not assumed to be infallible. The causal decision theorist is willing to bet against the predictor: even a predictor with a long and impressive record may be wrong on this occasion, and taking both boxes guarantees the extra $1,000 whatever it predicted. The evidential decision theorist declines that bet and treats the prediction as settled by her choice. The disagreement is thus about how much confidence it is reasonable to place in a fallible predictor, not about the structure of rational choice. (It should be noted that EDT's verdict does not require perfect reliability; any correlation between choice and prediction better than chance will give one-boxing the higher conditional expected utility.)
>
> (c) Yes. EDT's verdict requires backward causation. To one-box, the theory must treat the present choice as bringing about an earlier event: only in this way can a present act raise the probability of a prediction that has already been made and of contents that have already been fixed. The evidential dependence that EDT invokes is therefore a disguised causal dependence running from the choice to the predictor's act, and anyone who rejects backward causation must reject EDT's verdict in this case.
>
> (d) The argument is weak, though not for the reason the causal decision theorist usually gives. One-boxers do end up with more money, and this is not an accident: it reflects the predictor's accuracy. But the argument moves from a fact about the relative fortunes of two populations to a conclusion about which act a given agent should perform, and the populations differ in exactly the respect that matters. The causal decision theorist replies that the difference in fortunes is explained by the predictor's having sorted the agents in advance: those who one-box are rich because they are the sort of agent the predictor expected to one-box, not because one-boxing made them rich. Given the contents of the boxes, which are already fixed, taking both boxes always leaves the agent $1,000 better off. This reply does not show that the argument is worthless — that one-boxers prosper is a real datum that a theory of rationality ought to be able to explain, and it is the point at which CDT is most often thought to be uncomfortable — but it does show that the argument cannot settle the question on its own.
