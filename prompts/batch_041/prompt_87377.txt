I'm running the last session of a reading group thats been on perception all term — mostly disjunctivism vs. the common-factor picture — and I want to close it with a poem I can hand out. Write me one.

Form: two panels, same length, 10-12 lines each. The second panel is the first panel rewritten with only a handful of small changes — a word or two, a line break, punctuation — so that on a fast read its just the same poem twice. Panel one is someone seeing something real; keep it ordinary (a kitchen counter, a street at dusk, a friends face), nothing dramatic. Panel two is the same scene hallucinated. The reader will get one line of setup from me at the top saying which panel is which, so dont spell it out inside the poem.

What the form has to do: the disjunctivist point is that not being able to tell the two cases apart from the inside doesnt mean there is a shared inner state that both cases are instances of. So the repetition should be the trap — the words come back almost identical, but the second pass has to read as a different kind of event, not the same event with a different cause stapled to it. If a common-factor theorist could say "great, youve written the same experience twice", the poem has failed. And dont let panel two collapse into an absence; a hallucination is an episode, not a blank.

Two things I want folded in, and I care about them being right more than about them being named:

- The argument from illusion isnt the argument from hallucination, and the poem should know it. Bent oar in water, whatever you use — the thing is there and being seen, so the argument that runs off that case has a different shape and a different weak joint from the one that runs off a total hallucination. Bonus if the poem makes you notice that a lot of the force of the illusion version leans on one reading of 'looks', the phenomenal one, when 'looks' has other readings where nothing metaphysical follows at all.
- There are two ways a disjunctivist can treat hallucination, and I want the second panel readable as the more interesting one: not that the hallucination gives you no reason at all, but that it gives you a reason of a different kind, so the perceiver isnt just the hallucinator plus a bit of luck. Dont announce it, just make the poem compatible with it.

No names, no citations, no footnotes. Dont rhyme if the rhyme is going to make you say something false. Title optional, keep it plain if you use one. Then, outside the poem, four or five lines telling me which reading of 'looks' you leaned on and where a common-factor theorist would push back hardest on the piece.
