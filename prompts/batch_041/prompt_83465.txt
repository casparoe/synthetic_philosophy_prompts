Consider the recurring standoffs over the statutory limit on United States federal debt. Treat the crisis of 2011 as the paradigm case and the 2013 episode as a recurrence that confirms the pattern rather than an exception, and analyze them through the theory of Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment. I want the analysis to run in both directions.

The facts I have in mind, so that we agree on the case: the ceiling was created in 1917, when Congress stopped authorizing each debt issue individually and instead set an aggregate limit the Treasury could borrow up to; a limit on total federal debt dates from 1939. The limit constrains borrowing to pay obligations that earlier tax and spending decisions have already incurred; it does not constrain those decisions themselves. Congress can raise, suspend, or repeal it by ordinary legislation. In 2011 it raised the limit in tranches in exchange for spending cuts, made one tranche subject to a congressional motion of disapproval that the president could veto, and attached automatic across-the-board cuts if a joint committee failed to agree on a larger package; the credit rating of the United States was downgraded shortly afterwards. In 2013 the limit was simply suspended, and the confrontation returned within months. Executives have repeatedly been advised that they could prioritize payments, invoke the provision of the Fourteenth Amendment concerning the validity of the public debt, or mint a large platinum coin, and have declined to do any of these.

Now the theory, as I understand it, and where I want you to be exact. A Ulysses contract works because the agent removes an option from its future self, and because a third party — the crew, in the story — is under instruction to ignore the future self's subsequent pleading. On this picture, bindingness requires an enforcer who is not the bound party, whose power is adequate to hold the rope, and whose incentives run with the present self rather than the tempted future one. Keep distinct the following: option-removal versus cost-imposition versus delegation to an agent with different preferences; renegotiation-proofness, meaning the device survives only if at the moment of temptation nobody with the power to undo it wants to; and the Schelling-style hostage or burnt bridge, in which one deliberately surrenders control in order to gain leverage.

What I want from you:

1. A careful mapping. Who is the present self, who the future self, who the crew, and what are the ropes? Is the ceiling an option-removal, a cost-imposition, or a delegation — or is it, as I suspect, a device with the temporal structure inverted, biting after the binding decision rather than before it, which would make it a postcommitment rather than a precommitment?

2. A verdict on whether it is a precommitment at all. If the bound party is also the enforcer, and the enforcer can unilaterally untie the knot, then on the standard account the device is not binding; yet it plainly has effects. Say what it actually is — a hostage, a bargaining chip, a costly signal, a ritual of fiscal seriousness, or something else — and defend the choice against at least one rival.

3. The reverse direction. What does the case show about the theory? Does it confirm that precommitment needs an external enforcer, or does it show that a non-binding rule can still do work through the costs it makes salient? Does the theory have any account of a device that must be renewed repeatedly rather than entered once, and of a bound self whose preferences are not stable but are re-formed with each election? Where is the theory simply silent, and where does it misdescribe?

4. One counterfactual test. Would a supermajority requirement for raising the limit, or a constitutional balanced-budget rule, be a genuine precommitment — renegotiation-proof, enforced by someone other than the bound party? What would it cost in terms of the ability of later majorities to revise, and is that cost the dead-hand problem or a legitimate form of entrenchment?

Be disciplined about the mapping rather than gesturing at parallels, and tell me where the theory runs out instead of extending it by analogy. No literature survey and no citations.
