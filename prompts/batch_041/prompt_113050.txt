My nine-year-old came home from school last week in floods of tears because her new science teacher told the class that Pluto isn't a planet, whereas her old teacher had drummed the nine-planet thing into them, and she's concluded that one of them must have been lying to her. I said something useless like "they're both sort of right", which pleased nobody, and then I had a proper arguement about it at dinner with my brother-in-law, who reckons the whole business was just astronomers being fussy about a word. It's realy been niggling at me since, and it turns out to be a much stranger question than I expected, so I want a proper run at it.

Take the role of a philosopher of language — someone who works seriously on Wittgenstein's later philosophy and on rule-following, not a science communicator and not a historian of astronomy. Think carefully, brainstorm a bit before you commit to a line, then give me the analysis. British English, please. And please no reading lists, no citations, no "see so-and-so's book on this" — I can't check any of it and it isn't what I'm after. I want the argument itself, made properly.

Here's the theory, and I'll put it in my own words so we're definitely talking about the same thing. The Kripkenstein reading of the rule-following remarks: take any finite history of my uses of a word — all the sums I've ever actually added up, say. That finite history is compatible both with the rule I think I'm following and with any number of deviant rules that agree with it on every case I've ever actually met and diverge on cases I haven't. So what fact about me — about my past behaviour, my dispositions, my mental life — makes it the case that I mean plus rather than the deviant thing? The answer is supposed to be: no such fact exists. And the way out isn't to hunt harder for the fact, it's to see that the demand was misconceived — correctness isn't grounded in a fact but in a shared practice, so I'm going right when I do what the community does, and the sceptic's request for a fact that makes my use correct was the mistake from the start.

Here's the episode. On 24 August 2006, in Prague, the International Astronomical Union voted to define "planet" for our solar system so that a planet has to (roughly) orbit the Sun, be massive enough to be pulled into a nearly round shape, and have cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit. Pluto fails the third, so it was reclassified as a dwarf planet rather than a planet. The pressure had been building for a while — the Kuiper belt objects, and then in 2005 a body out beyond Neptune, Eris, initially reported as slightly bigger than Pluto, so it looked as though either Eris was a planet too or Pluto wasn't. (I've also read that Mercury isn't actually in hydrostatic equilibrium and the definition just quietly keeps it on as a planet anyway — no idea if that's right, but if it is, say something about it.) And I gather the IAU's committee had floated three options: a structural one (round enough), a dynamical one (dominant enough), and a cultural one — in effect, a planet is whatever enough people agree to call a planet. They went with the dynamical one.

What I want, roughly in this order:

1. Run the theory over the case and show me where the joints are. Was there ever a fact of the matter about whether Pluto was a planet, or only a practice?
2. Was my daughter's first teacher wrong, or was she just playing an older game correctly? Is "wrong" even the right word here, and if not, what is?
3. If the communal answer is right, what makes the IAU the community that counts? Plenty of planetary scientists thought the vote was a shambles, the public never signed up, textbooks and school curricula are all over the place, and my daughter's class is now a community of its own. This looks like it should be the theory's easy case and I suspect it's actually the hard one.
4. Now push the other way. Eris is the awkward bit for me: a new object turned up and made the old practice unworkable, and that looks like the world constraining us rather than just us deciding. Does the theory have anything to say about that, or does "no fact of the matter" leave it unable to explain why reality gets a vote? Be honest if the theory is simply silent here.
5. The cultural option was genuinely on the table and they rejected it. Is that a problem for the community solution — the community declining to make its own practice the ground of correctness — or is it just the community deciding how to go on?
6. Compare it with a court doing the same job. There's an 1890s US Supreme Court decision holding that tomatoes count as vegetables rather than fruit for import-tax purposes, on the ground that that's the ordinary meaning of the words, whatever the botanists say. Same move, different authority. Does the analogy help, or does it show that what settles these cases is authority rather than meaning — which would be bad news for the community answer?
7. Finish with what the episode shows about the theory: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated. And then what you'd actually tell a nine-year-old.

Don't force the theory to speak where it can't. Where it goes quiet, say so.
