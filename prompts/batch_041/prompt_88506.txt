I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy — what I know of it I have picked up from reading on my own — and I have written out an argument that I can no longer decide whether to believe, because it seems to prove far more than it is entitled to. I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind and of value working at the intersection of the two, and to help me locate the fault. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by setting out, in your own words, the strongest version of the position the argument attacks, so that I can see you have understood what is at stake before you start dismantling anything.

The argument concerns hedonism — the view that pleasure is the good, or the one thing good in itself — and whether "pleasure" even names a single kind of thing:

(1) For any collection of things to constitute a single kind, there must be some one property that every member possesses and that accounts for its membership.
(2) The pleasure of a hot bath after a day of shivering and the pleasure of finally seeing why a proof works share no introspectable feature: what each is like, from the inside, is wholly different, and nothing is present in both.
(3) The only candidate for a shared feature is that in each case the person wants or welcomes the experience; but wanting is a relation the person bears to the experience, not a feature of the experience itself, and people want plenty of things that are not pleasant — a punishing workout, a bitter medicine.
(4) Therefore no single property is shared by all and only the things we call pleasures.
(5) Hedonism says that pleasure is the one thing good in itself. If there is no single property shared by all pleasures, then "pleasure" does not pick out a kind, and the thesis cannot even be stated.
(6) Therefore hedonism is not a coherent position.

What makes me suspicious is not the argument but its reach. I can run the same machinery on other categories. Fear, awe and nostalgia share no common felt quality; on this reasoning there are no emotions. Red and blue share no property beyond the bare fact of both being colors, which is just to restate the question; on this reasoning there are no colors. Believing that it is raining and believing that two and two make four have almost nothing in common; on this reasoning there are no beliefs. All three conclusions are absurd, so something has gone wrong — but I cannot find the joint.

I would like to keep hedonism a live option if I honestly can, and I am reluctant to abandon premise (1), because it looks to me like a truism about what it means to be a kind at all. But I am not attached to any particular verdict, and I do not want to be talked out of the puzzle by being told it is merely verbal.

So: which step or steps should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the fault is in (1), tell me what a kind can be unified by if not a shared property, and what that commits me to elsewhere — does the same move rescue the disunity of emotions, colors and beliefs, or does it leave some of them broken? If the fault is in (3), tell me what unifies pleasures instead, and whether the resulting view is still hedonism or has quietly become a different theory (a desire theory, say), and whether that matters. If the fault is in my parity reasoning — the claim that the same argument sinks emotions, colors and beliefs — tell me precisely where the disanalogy lies and why it is not ad hoc. Where more than one repair is available, rank them by how much each costs and say which you would pay.

One thing I keep circling: in my day job we routinely treat things as the same kind when they expose the same interface, even if their inner workings have nothing in common. I cannot tell whether importing that thought here would illuminate the problem or simply assume the answer.
