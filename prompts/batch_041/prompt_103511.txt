Daniel Dennett introduced the notion of a free-floating rationale to explain how there can be reasons in nature without any reasoner. The core idea is that natural selection can discover reasons for traits or behaviours—reasons that are not represented in any mind. For example, consider the alarm calls of certain birds. When a bird gives an alarm call, it warns other birds of danger. The rationale for the call is that it increases the survival of the caller's kin. But the bird need not intend to warn anyone, nor need it believe that a predator is present. The rationale is free-floating because it floats free of any particular psychological state.

Dennett contrasts this with the intentional stance. When we adopt the intentional stance, we treat an organism as a rational agent with beliefs and desires. But free-floating rationales are more fundamental: they are the reasons that natural selection has built into the organism's design. Indeed, Dennett thinks that the intentional stance is just a convenient way of describing these free-floating rationales, and that ultimately all talk of beliefs and desires is just a useful fiction. So free-floating rationales are not themselves real reasons; they are patterns we impose on nature for predictive convenience.

This view is similar to Dawkins's idea of the selfish gene, in that both emphasise that the ultimate reason for a behaviour is its effect on genetic replication, not the organism's conscious motives. However, Dennett's account is more sophisticated because it distinguishes between the proximate causes of behaviour (e.g., a bird's hormonal state) and the ultimate causes (the free-floating rationale). In conclusion, free-floating rationales are a powerful tool for understanding animal behaviour, but we should not reify them into genuine reasons.
