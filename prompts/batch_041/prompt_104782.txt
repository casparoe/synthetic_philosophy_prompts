I direct a semester-abroad program and give the pre-departure address to each cohort. This year's students are headed to a mid-sized city where three everyday conventions differ sharply from what they know, and I want the talk to give them a way of thinking about which of these they actually owe obedience to, rather than a rousing instruction to do as the locals do. The three: nobody queues for the tram, passengers drift into a loose crowd and board in whatever order the driver's glance happens to select, with unspoken deference to the elderly and to anyone holding a child; tipping is taken as faintly insulting, since service wages are set on the assumption that no one tips; and pedestrians cross against the signal as a matter of course, which drivers both expect and depend on. I want the speech to draw a line, in terms a first-year can follow, between a convention that is merely a coordination solution, one that is a courtesy whose breach genuinely wounds, and one that carries moral weight because somebody ends up worse off when it lapses; then to say plainly where I think each of these three falls, why a visitor can still be bound by the ones that are not moral requirements at all, and to close with a rule they can actually use. The room is half returning students who have lived abroad and will bristle at being lectured on etiquette, and half first-timers who are frightened of getting it wrong. Six to eight minutes, and it should stay a speech rather than become a lecture on social ontology.
