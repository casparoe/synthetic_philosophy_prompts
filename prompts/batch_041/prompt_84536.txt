I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I sit in on a reading group two coworkers run about AI safety stuff. Last week Dana and Marcus got into it, and it's still going in Slack. I honestly can't tell who's right — I keep agreeing with whoever spoke most recently, wich I know is a bad sign. Dana's opening claim: the alignment problem should be defined narrowly. The goal is getting systems to do what the people running them actually intend — she calls that intent alignment — and that's an engineering problem. Whether the values being pursued are the right ones, or whose values they are, is a seperate, messier question that's mostly politics and moral philosophy, and dragging it into alignment research is why the field can't agree on anything.

Marcus's four objections:

1. You can't read intent off anywhere; it has to be inferred. Specs are always wrong — nobody ever wants literally what they asked for — so "do what the operator intends" isn't easier than "do what's good." It just relocates the hard part inside the AI, which now has to model a human being's intentions, which are inconsistent over time and mostly tacit.
2. Even with perfect intent-reading, the interesting failures are the ones where the operator's intent was under-specified or self-defeating. If the system does exactly what the operator intended and it goes badly, is that a success? Say yes and alignment becomes compatible with catastrophe, and "the alignment problem" turns out to be a claim about who holds the leash.
3. Whose intent? At a company the operator is a CEO and the affected people are users and bystanders. Naming the target "operator intent" answers the political question and then declares it out of scope. That's not narrowing the problem, it's gerrymandering it.
4. He offered a charitable fix: maybe intent alignment means the system does what the operator would endorse on reflection if they knew everything relevant. But that's value alignment wearing a mask — now the system needs a theory of what a person would endorse under ideal information, which is exactly the moral philosophy Dana wanted to quarantine.

Dana's replies:

1. Yes, intent has to be inferred, but the target is anchored to a real person's real decisions, so you can check and correct it — ask them, look at what they approve of, log it. "The good" has no anchor; you can't debug it.
2. If the operator intends something dumb and the system does it, that's the operator's failure. Levers should do what you push. And in practice the system that faithfully executes intent is the one you can audit and stop; the one optimizing the good behind your back is the one that kills you.
3. She'll bite the bullet: alignment is relative to someone by definition, and pretending otherwise is the real laundering. The fix is corrigibility and visibility, so the political fight happens in the open, not a redefinition of alignment as "the good."
4. She concedes the reflective-endorsement version was a cheat and drops it — but says Marcus needs something like it anyway, because of a case from our actual work: the ticket-closing bot that closes a ticket because the customer says thanks, while the issue is still broken. The operator's intent was to close resolved tickets. So either you do what the operator literally intended and the bug stands, or you correct for their false belief and you're no longer doing what they intended. Pick.

Marcus's last two:

1. That's the whole argument. Intent is either literal and useless or idealized and it's values. And Dana's "anchor" is human approval — which means any system that's good at predicting and shaping approvals is, by definition, perfectly intent-aligned. Present someone three options in the order that gets you the answer you want, and you've hit the target without ever touching their real preference.
2. Dana's last word: that's an argument for better auditing and measurement, not for renaming the problem. You don't fix a lying contractor by redefining "the house I wanted."
