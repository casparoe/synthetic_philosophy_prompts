I am grading the final exam in an upper-level undergraduate course that includes a unit on the foundations of mathematics, and I would like a careful second opinion on one essay before I return the papers. The question and the student's answer are below.

Question 3 (essay). Assess the following argument: "Arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency; that is Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. But an inconsistent theory proves every sentence, so if arithmetic were inconsistent it would prove its own consistency too, and any consistency proof we obtained would be worthless. We would have no way of knowing that we are not in that situation, so our trust in mathematics rests on a leap of faith." Say what is right in the argument, what is wrong, and what the history of the subject shows about how mathematics responds when an inconsistency is actually found.

Student's answer: "The argument is right on two counts. Arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency: Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that no consistent, recursively axiomatized system strong enough to represent arithmetic can prove its own consistency, so a proof that arithmetic is consistent would have to come from outside arithmetic. And an inconsistent system does prove every sentence, so an inconsistent arithmetic would indeed 'prove' its own consistency, which is why such a proof would be worthless as reassurance.

But the leap-of-faith conclusion does not follow. That we cannot prove consistency from within arithmetic does not mean that we have no reason to believe it; we also have centuries of failed attempts to derive a contradiction, and that is evidence, even if it is not proof. And Hilbert's program was not refuted, as is often said: Gentzen soon gave the finitistic consistency proof Hilbert had asked for, so the program was carried through rather than abandoned. The Frege case makes the same point. Russell's paradox showed the system of the Grundgesetze to be inconsistent, and mathematics did not collapse; the faulty principle was identified and given up, and the discipline went on. If a contradiction were found in arithmetic, we would locate the axiom responsible, weaken or replace it, and keep the parts that applications rely on.

That said, the argument is pointing at something real, and the possibility of inconsistency is not closed off. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that arithmetic has true sentences it cannot prove, so we cannot assume that a contradiction, if there were one, would be something we had already stumbled on; it could lie among the sentences arithmetic cannot prove, in which case we would never find it."
