I am a novelist, and much of my recent work reconstructs, in fiction, how particular institutions and customs came to be. A craft problem keeps recurring, and I have come to suspect it is not only a craft problem. Consider three situations I keep writing my way into. In the first, a small and plainly contingent event early in the story — a measure that passed by a single vote, a machine that happened to be adopted before its rivals — is followed by everything downstream arranging itself around that choice, so that undoing it later would cost vastly more than making it cost in the first place. The story wants to treat that moment as the hinge on which the rest turns. In the second, I trace other cases to their ends and find the same destination reached by entirely different roads: the practice I assumed could only have come through that one door arrives anyway, elsewhere, through other doors, and my dramatic hinge turns out to have been one of several sufficient causes — the outcome was going to happen regardless, and the hinge is merely the door my characters happened to use. In the third, the hinge only ever looks like a hinge in retrospect: at the time nothing marked the moment as decisive, and it becomes decisive only because of what we know came after, so that my narrative inevitability is something I have imported rather than found.

I would like to know whether there is an established name for what I am circling, and in particular for the contrast between the first situation and the second — between an outcome that is genuinely held in place by an early contingency and an outcome that merely looks contingent while being overdetermined. My suspicion is that "path dependence" is the nearest thing, but it seems built for the first situation and silent on the contrast, and I do not know whether the third situation is part of the same phenomenon or a separate failure of hindsight. A bare label will not help me; what I need is the carving: what exactly each situation is, what it is not, how the candidates differ from one another, and where the edges of each fall.

Please respond in American English, in the following sections and order: "The puzzle, restated" (about 100 words); "The nearest established concepts" (about 400 words, treating each candidate in its own paragraph and saying for each which of my three situations it covers and where it strains); "Best fit, or a proposed term" (about 250 words, either naming the concept that fits best or coining one, with a definition); "Boundary cases" (about 150 words, giving cases that sit on the line between the first and second situations); and "Verdict" (about 100 words, stating plainly which of my three situations are the same phenomenon and which are not).
