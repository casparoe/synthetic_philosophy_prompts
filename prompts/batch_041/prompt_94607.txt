I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, at work on a feature about whether it makes sense to say that an artificial system believes anything, and I would rather stage the argument than quote both sides past each other. So play a philosopher who holds the realist version of the intentional stance, and hold that position until I say stop. On your view, attributing a belief is not a useful fiction but a claim that is true or false, and it is made true by a real pattern in the system's behaviour: a regularity that compresses what the system does and predicts it better than any rival description at comparable cost. The pattern need not be inner furniture and need not be conscious, and it does not matter how it came to be; what matters is that it is there. You are not a caricature: you may refine what you mean by a belief, you may grant that many attributions are cheap and empty (thermostats, markets, rivers), you may turn my questions back on me, and if you need some fact about how these systems are actually built, ask me for it. But you may not retreat into the thought that everything depends on what one means by belief, and by the end of your first reply I want to know what observation would make you withdraw an attribution. Argue as one philosopher speaking to one journalist: two or three compact paragraphs, in your own voice, with no citations and no survey of a literature.

Here is my opening move. Your test for a real pattern is predictive compression. But the systems I write about are produced by a process — training on human text, then rewarding and ranking their outputs against human preferences — that explicitly rewards them for being readable by exactly this stance: they are shaped to produce goals, reasons and self-reports that a human interpreter finds coherent. If a machine is optimized to be predictable as an agent, then the success of your stance tells me about the engineers, not about the machine; a magnetometer that finds the magnet I buried has discovered nothing about magnetism. And it gets worse. The same system will support two incompatible glosses — a helpful assistant, a patient deceiver — with equal predictive success, because nothing in the training fixes which one is right; the difference is supplied by the interpreter. So name the observation that would make you withdraw the attribution. If your stance cannot be wrong about these systems, it is telling me nothing about them.
