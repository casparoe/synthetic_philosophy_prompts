I'm a novelist — third book, due to my editor in the spring — and for the last couple of years I've been reading philosophy of technology on the side the way other people do crosswords, and I've ended up with a set of views I'd genuinely defend at a dinner party and that I suspect I can't hold all at once. Roughly in the order I'd say them: (1) artifacts have politics — designing a tool is deciding who gets to do what, and the decision lives in the thing, not just in how people happen to use it; (2) all the same, meaning is made in use — users reinterpret, repurpose, work around, so the same object can be an instrument of control on one street and of liberation on the next; (3) once a tool is woven into a practice it's practically irreversible — not that we can't imagine another way, but that the other way has stopped existing and the people who remember it are retiring; (4) the politics of a tool is invisible while it works and becomes legible when it breaks; (5) the only honest critique of a technology is to specify the alternative — if you can't describe the tool that would have been built instead, you haven't shown that anything was chosen; (6) there's a distinction I keep using with my editor that I think is doing real work, between a tool's spec and what I've been calling its carrying capacity — the range of ways of living it can hold up without falling over — and I think a tool's politics is really fixed at the point where the carrying capacity runs out, i.e. at the people it can't carry; and (7) at the level of the engineering nothing political is happening at all — a transistor is a transistor, and anyone who tells you otherwise is confused.

Here's the part that makes me nervous, and I'd rather you not be polite about it. The book is about a city that replaces its patchwork of local services with one civic app that allocates everything — repairs, clinic slots, school places — out of a single queue, and I've comitted to a rule for myself: the system never crashes, there's no villain reveal, no moment where the screen goes dark and everyone finally sees the truth. I want the reader to feel the politics of the thing while it's working perfectly, because that's how it actually happens to people. I can't tell whether that's a real artistic choice or just something I want to be true, and I'd like to know before I've written another two hundred pages around it. Don't send me reading — I've done enough of that to get myself into this mess.

So: audit this. Which of the seven are actually pulling against each other, which pairs only look like contradictions and dissolve once you draw the right distinction, and for the real ones, how bad are they and what's the cheapest thing that would have to give? I'd rather change my mind about one item than paper over three, so rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of flagging everything, and then tell me the single revision that buys me the most consistency — and say plainly which item you think is the one that has to move. Take a position, don't hand me a menu of options with the choice left to me. And lead with the verdict: first sentence, whether the package holds and which commitment you'd put your money against, then the reasoning, no throat-clearing and no restating my list back to me.
