I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and I've been sitting in on a historiography seminar in another department because a friend of mine teaches it. Last week after the session two people in the class got into it, and then kept going at the bar, and I've been chewing on it all week because I genuinely couldnt tell who was winning. They're both sharp and neither one was a strawman, which somehow makes it worse. Here's the exchange as close as I can reconstruct it — Mira is the she, Daniel is the he.

(1) Mira starts with: the whole idea of a global history is either incoherent or a Eurocentric lie. Any narrative has to select events and chop the past into periods, and there's no selecting and periodizing except from some standpoint. So "the past told from everywhere at once" isn't a view from nowhere, it's a view from somewhere pretending not to be, and in practice that somewhere is Europe — the periods are European (medieval, early modern, the Renaissance), the engines of change are European (industrialization, the Enlightenment), and everyone else shows up as either the recipient of those things or as scenery.

(2) Daniel says thats a false either/or. A global history doesn't have to be one master narrative running on one clock. You can write entangled history, where the basic units are interactions and exchanges instead of civilizations, and multi-centered history, where different regions are the protagonists of different stories, and then you don't need a single causal spine or a single timeline. He says the view from everywhere is a regulative ideal you approach by adding centers and tracing connections, not a standpoint anybody actually occupies.

(3) Mira calls that a cop-out — if you can't say what counts as getting closer to the ideal, it isn't a standard, it's a mood. And adding centers doesn't even touch the real problem, which is the choice of questions: as long as the questions are the ones European historiography made interesting (why did Europe pull ahead, when does modernity start), you're still centered on Europe even if the story is set in Canton and Bengal. The centering lives in the questions, she says, not in where the action happens. She also throws in that even if a fully decentered history were possible she's not sure you'd want it — a history with no center has no argument, it's just a chronicle, and explanation needs a standpoint; you can't explain everything at once.

(4) Daniel says fine, grant the question-selection point, that's a much narrower complaint, so ask different questions. But notice what she just conceded: the causal facts aren't the problem. Industrialization really did start in Britain, the silver really did move from Potosí to Seville. If the causal claims are true, then putting the causes in Europe is just history, not Eurocentrism. Eurocentrism is a vice of interpretation, not of geography, and you can fix the interpretation without giving up the true causal story. He offers a test: a history is Eurocentric to the extent that non-European actors only ever get reactive verbs — reacted, resisted, was colonized, declined — and not Eurocentric to the extent they get their own projects and purposes.

(5) Mira says the test is too weak, because you can hand everybody agency and still make Europe the thing that determines how it all turns out; a narrative where everyone has projects but only Europe's projects set the terms is still centered. And she says the facts/interpretation split is exactly the move she's suspicious of. "Industrialization started in Britain" isn't a neutral fact sitting there before interpretation, it's already a description under a periodizing concept, and the concept is what does the centering. So there's no pre-interpretive floor to retreat to, and his "just history" is rhetoric, not bedrock.

(6) Daniel says that last move proves too much. If there's no pre-interpretive layer, then you can't distinguish a Eurocentric history from a non-Eurocentric one at all — every history is centered somewhere, so the word "Eurocentric" stops picking out a defect and just means "history." But Mira clearly does think some histories are worse than others, so she owes an account of what makes the difference, and that account has to be able to say what a less Eurocentric history would look like.

That's where they stopped. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what? I don't need an overall winner so much as a breakdown — which of the six moves land, which ones are doing something sneaky, and what each of them is missing. I'm especially curious whether Mira's last move is as strong as it sounds or whether it wins by making the whole thing unarguable, but I've got no dog in this fight, I just want to see how the pieces actually fit together.
