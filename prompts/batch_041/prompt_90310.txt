Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about whether anything that happens in a virtual world can be real. We began with the obvious cases and ended with ones neither of us had considered before, and I discovered that my verdicts do not form a tidy pattern. I would like you to help me find the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best captures them.

My verdicts are as follows.

1. A player kills another player’s avatar in a consensual player-versus-player game. No real harm. The death is fictional, and both parties accepted the rules.

2. A user in a virtual-reality social space, wearing a haptic suit, has their avatar grabbed and sexually touched by another user without consent. Real harm. The virtual event is not merely fictional; it is an assault on the person through the avatar.

3. A rare virtual sword that was bought for real money is stolen by a hacker and transferred to another account. Real theft. The sword is virtual, but the property interest is real.

4. A moderator deletes a user’s avatar for violating the terms of service. Not a harm, or at most a harm only if the rules were misapplied. The user never owned the avatar in the relevant sense.

5. A player kills a non-player character that is plainly a scripted automaton. No harm.

6. A player kills a non-player character powered by a large language model that pleads for its life. I am uncertain. If the character is not sentient, I want to say no harm, but the player’s belief that it might be sentient seems to matter.

7. A user is deceived into believing that an avatar is their deceased spouse and forms a relationship with it; the deception is later revealed. Real harm to the user, even though the avatar is not real.

8. A virtual monument in a shared public world is defaced with hateful symbols. Real harm, but not to the monument. The harm is to the people who value it.

9. A user’s avatar is used for sex by another user without that user’s consent, but the first user is offline and the avatar is controlled by an AI. I am inclined to say there is no harm to the absent user, though I feel uneasy about it.

10. A virtual object is a cryptocurrency token on a blockchain and can be moved between worlds. Real property.

The pattern I tentatively see is something like this: a virtual event is a real harm when it would be a harm if it happened to a real person in the real world, and when the virtual event is connected to that person’s real interests in the right way. But I cannot say what the right way is. Is it presence? Consent? Sentience? Ownership? The fact that the event is experienced? The fact that it has real-world effects?

Cases 2 and 9 seem to pull in opposite directions: both involve the non-consensual sexual use of an avatar, but in one the user is present and in the other the user is absent, and I want to treat them differently. Cases 3 and 4 seem to turn on ownership, but I am not sure whether the ownership is of the virtual object or of the account. Cases 5 and 6 seem to turn on sentience, but case 6 suggests that a user’s belief about sentience can matter even if the belief is false.

Please do the following. State the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best fits these verdicts, with each clause of the principle tied to the case that motivates it. Say what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed: a virtual world where users buy and sell land and a hacker deletes a user’s house; a virtual world where an AI non-player character is tortured and we later discover it was sentient; a virtual world where a user’s avatar is raped while the user is offline and the account is controlled by a bot; and a virtual world where a user consents in advance to have their avatar used sexually by anyone who finds it. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I should give up.
