I've been reading the rationalist-adjacent blogs and comment sections for something like fifteen years, which means I've absorbed alot of mechanism-design opinions without ever sitting down and doing the math properly, and now I've got a pile of gut verdicts about strategic behavior in voting and other procedures that I can't get to line up with each other. Here they are, roughly in the order I hold them. Voting for the lesser evil in a plurality election instead of my actual favorite: completely fine, that's just coordinating with strangers, I do it every time. Ranking a compromise candidate first on a ranked/IRV ballot when my real favorite is first in my heart: not fine, I don't do it, it feels like writing a false statement on a form. Approval voting where I tick my favorite and the acceptable one and leave off someone I wouldn't mind: fine, approval is a threshold rather than a ranking so I don't feel like I'm lying, even though I know it's still manipulable and that when two candidates are both acceptable you're forced into a nasty guess about which one to tick. Shading my bid in a first-price sealed-bid auction: fine, that's the game, everyone knows it's the game. Bidding above my value in a second-price auction: not exactly wrong, more like a dumb bet. Buying extra votes in a quadratic vote: fine, that's expressing intensity, which is what I wish ordinary votes did. Logrolling with a legislator from another district — you vote for my dam, I vote for your bridge, though neither of us thinks the other's project is worth it: uneasy, feels like it shouldn't be OK, but I can't say why it's different from the lesser-evil vote. A hiring or tenure committee where I downrank a candidate I actually think is good because they'd be bad for my subfield: not fine, feels corrupt rather than merely strategic. The residency-match style setup where students submit honest rankings and hospitals submit strategic ones: students being honest is fine and the hospitals being strategic is the whole scandal. Single-peaked committee stuff where everyone's best move is to state their true ideal point: gold standard, I want more of this. And then the case that broke my brain when I heard it: a lottery that picks one random voter and does whatever that voter says. Totally strategy-proof, nobody can gain by lying, and obviously an insane way to run anything. Which makes me suspect the thing I treat as almost a dealbreaker when I'm comparing voting systems — strategy-proofness — is either not that important or not the thing I actually care about, and I gather Gibbard–Satterthwaite says I can't have it in general anyway with three or more options and no restrictions on preferences.

The pair I genuinely can't reconcile is the plurality compromise (fine) and the IRV compromise (not fine), since as far as I can tell they're the same act: reporting an order that isn't my true order, to get a better result, given what everyone else is likely to do. My best attempt at a principle is that manipulation is bad when it's a false report about my own private preferences, and fine when it's a bet about other people's behavior or a legitimate act of choosing — but the IRV case looks like a bet to me too, so either that verdict or the plurality verdict has to go. My fallback theory is expressive: strategy is bad when it makes the procedure mean something different (a committee becomes a faction fight, an election becomes a poll of guesses) and fine when the procedure is openly a game (auctions). That one has the weird feature of making the badness depend on something other than outcomes, which bothers me, because in a large election my vote moves the outcome with probability roughly zero, and yet I still feel the difference between the two ballot cases.

Can you work out whether there's a principle, or a small set of them, behind all of this? I'm not after a reading list or citations — I'll chase the literature down myself, I just want the conceptual machinery, and I'd rather have a definite answer I disagree with than everything hedged into mush. Please give me, in this order: 'Where my verdicts collide' (~150 words), naming the pair or pairs that can't both survive; 'Candidate principles' (~400 words), two or three principles stated precisely enough that I could apply them mechanically, each tied to the case that motivates it and each labeled as being about acts, outcomes, or meanings; 'New cases' (~300 words), running each principle against cases I didn't list — I'm especially curious about the random-voter lottery, about mechanisms that are strategy-proof only because they hand people a dominant strategy to do something they'd hate, and about whether 'everyone could do it symmetrically' ever rescues a case; 'Which verdict I should give up' (~200 words), being honest that probably one of my gut verdicts has to go, which one, and why the rest are worth keeping; and 'Bottom line' (~100 words) with the principle you'd actually endorse if you had to pick one.
