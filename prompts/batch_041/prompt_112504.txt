So I run a 75-minute session in our second-year philosophy of science course — 16 students, pretty mixed maths backgrounds, a couple who'll happily do the algebra and a couple who'll freeze the moment I draw a circle on the board — and I want to do something on the thing people say all the time, that if you've got no reason to think one way rather than the other then it's 50/50 and that's just what rationality requires. A friend in my cohort says this constantly and I think it's fine in some cases and pure hand-waving in others, and I want the students to run into the hand-waving themselves instead of hearing me assert it. The setup I've half-designed: I hand out a sheet with a circle and an inscribed equilateral triangle and tell them they can buy or sell a bet that pays out if a "randomly chosen chord" of the circle comes out longer than a side of the triangle, and before anything else happens they have to write down their own fair odds in secret and then compare — my hope is the room ends up with three or four different prices and everyone is certain the others have simply miscalculated. Phase two is me revealing that the chord will actually be produced by a concrete physical procedure (a bean thrown at the disk and the chord drawn through where it lands; a pointer spun twice for two endpoints; a distance pulled from a bag of slips), letting them re-price, and then playing it out for tiny stakes — a square of chocolate, nothing real — so the people who were loudest at the start have to sit with having been confident at a price that the device just doesn't honour.

What I want from you is the whole runnable thing: the rules, both (or all three) phases, the payoff scheme in non-monetary terms, a rough minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the underlying problem — namely that "give equal probability to cases you can't tell apart" isn't a rule that stands on its own, it quietly assumes a way of measuring the space of possibilities, and that the very same physical event can be read off different variables, so that "the same" outcome gets different probabilities depending on which variable you're treating as the random one. One of the postdocs in our group told me the whole disagreement is supposed to be settled by demanding that the answer come out the same under rotations, and that the real fight is then over which family of transformations you're allowed to insist on — I don't understand it well enough to teach it, but I'd like the debrief to point at it honestly rather than pretend the puzzle is closed. I'd also like them to leave with the thought that "uniformly at random" can't even be defined over something infinite or continuous until you've said what the measure is, which is the real reason the principle can't carry the weight people put on it.

Constraints: don't let the facilitator name Bertrand or the principle of indifference until the debrief, and make the activity do the teaching — the disagreement in phase one and the settling up at the end should be where it lands, not a slide. Assume cheap printable materials only (circle sheet, ruler, a bean, a spinner or pin, a bag of slips) and assume no projector, the bulb's been dead since October. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where students will dig in, where someone will accuse me of rigging the device, where I'll be tempted to just explain — and be honest about which part of the idea this game simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. I'd rather here all of that now than discover it in front of sixteen undergrads.
