I've been arguing for weeks with a friend in an online reading group, and I'd like help sorting out a slogan he keeps deploying, because I suspect he's smuggling something past me. The line he repeats is that "to call a claim true is just to say the claim over again — truth is not a real property, only a device of disquotation." He wheels it out whenever I press him on whether it matters that our beliefs are true, and his conclusion is that since "true" names no property of anything, there is nothing especially valuable about having true beliefs, and caring about truth is merely a verbal habit. I have no philosophical training — I studied accounting and read widely on my own — but I'm fairly sure that one sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and I want to know exactly what it could mean before I decide whether he's right.

Please take the role of an analytic philosopher of language and logic who works on theories of truth, and think this through carefully rather than giving me the usual correspondence-versus-coherence lecture. Begin by brainstorming the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, from the utterly uncontroversial to the radical, and then lay them out for me as a menu I can actually carry back into the argument — rather than picking one reading and defending it. For each reading, tell me what it commits you to, whether it is a platitude or a genuinely contentious claim, and whether it is defensible or simply false. I especially want to know which readings would actually support my friend's conclusion (that true belief is not valuable or worth caring about) and which would leave it untouched, since I suspect the reading that makes the slogan sound true is not the reading he needs. He also offers an analogy I can't assess: he says "true" is like the word "it" in "it is raining," a mere grammatical place-holder that stands in for a sentence the way a pronoun stands in for a name, and he thinks that settles the matter.

To make this concrete, here are the cases that convince me the slogan cannot be taken at face value. When I say "everything my grandmother believed about her garden is true," I have no idea what those sentences are, so I can't simply repeat them in place of "true." When a reporter says "what the witness said is true," she may be endorsing claims she cannot state. "The success of modern medicine is explained by the truth of germ theory" seems to use truth to explain something, not to repeat anything. "It's true that there are infinitely many primes" feels like it adds nothing to "there are infinitely many primes." And "that claim is neither true nor false," or "true for me but not for you," seem to use the word in ways a mere device of repetition could not. For each reading on your menu, say which of these cases it handles, which it strains on, and which reading my friend most likely has in mind; then tell me what the strongest version of his position would say in reply to the cases that resist it, and whether that reply costs him the conclusion he wanted.
