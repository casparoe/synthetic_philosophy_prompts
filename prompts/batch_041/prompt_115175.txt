I have been arguing about free speech with friends for most of a year, and I have reached the point where I no longer trust any of the slogans I used to argue with, including the harm principle as I once understood it. What I would like from you is the principle, or the small set of principles, that actually generates my verdicts — not a survey of what legislatures or courts have done, and not a reading list, since I am asking you this in a place where I cannot look anything up. I will give you the cases and my reactions to them, including the ones that make me uneasy.

First, a man stands in a public square and argues at length that a particular ethnic group is inherently criminal and ought to be stripped of citizenship and removed from the country; he belongs to a party with a published platform for exactly that. I think this must be permitted, and not only because banning it would be unpopular. Second, a newspaper prints an op-ed denying a well-documented atrocity, written to rehabilitate the perpetrators. Permitted, I think, though it is false and I have no doubt it does damage. Third, the same paper accepts a paid advertisement asserting that a named medicine cures a disease it does not cure, aimed at people who have that disease. I would restrict that without hesitation, and I am fairly sure the falsity is part of why. Fourth, a man who has been left by his partner recites to her, calmly and at conversational volume, entirely true statements — that he knows where she parks, that he will be outside her office at six, that he has been keeping a record of her movements — and also posts her daily schedule to a public forum. Nothing he says is false and nothing is a threat in the sense of a promise of violence, and I would restrict it and feel no conflict at all. Fifth, a writer with no connection to any industrial site publishes a detailed illustrated guide to defeating a particular safety interlock on a widely used type of installation, with video of himself doing it on a mock-up; he says his point is that the design is dangerous and ought to be changed. I want to say this can be restricted, but I cannot say why without catching a great deal of engineering writing I would want to protect. Sixth, at a rally a speaker tells an armed crowd that the time for talking is over, names a street, and says he will see them there; he issues no instruction, and the crowd has already discussed the plan among themselves. I would restrict that. Seventh, a party whose openly stated and actively pursued aim is to abolish elections and govern by decree campaigns for office, holds rallies, and publishes its programme. My gut says this is permitted, and the fact that they would use the office to end the game moves me less than I would expect it to; I recognize this is where people start talking about tolerance turning into its own opposite, and I want to know whether my principle can handle the case or whether I am simply being inconsistent. Eighth, two laws: one requires a paper that published a false claim about a named person to print a correction of a specified length, and the other requires a print shop to produce banners carrying a slogan its owners find abhorrent. I think the first is fine and the second is not, though both compel rather than restrict speech.

The closest I have come to a principle is something a friend named the ingredient test: an utterance may be restricted only when it is itself a constituent of a wrong — a move within a fraud, a solicitation, a conspiracy — and not merely a cause of a wrong that runs through other people's choices. That seems to get the first, second, third, fourth and sixth cases right as far as I can tell. But it gets the fifth and seventh exactly backwards: the pamphlet is nobody's ingredient and I want to restrict it, while the party's programme is arguably the first step in a plan to end elections and I want to permit it. Either the test is wrong or one of those two verdicts has to go, and I would like you to tell me which.

What I want is a principle, or a short list of candidates if one will not do, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen without writing to you again. For each clause, say which of my cases forces it and what work it does that the other clauses do not. Then apply the principle to two cases I have not described — choose ones that would embarrass it rather than flatter it — and state honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the truthful answer is that I need one clause for catastrophic technical information and another for imminent coordination, say so, but then tell me whether the result is a principle or merely a list of my opinions with names attached to the clauses.
