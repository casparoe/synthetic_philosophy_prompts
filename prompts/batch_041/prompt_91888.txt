I am rebuilding the first term of a one-year access-to-engineering course at a British further-education college. My students are adults returning to study after long absences; most of them arrive convinced that mathematics is a thing other people are good at. The awarding body fixes the syllabus and the assessment dates, the scheme of work is rewritten once a year and signed off by a head of department who has a fortnight and a funding target, and the students' progression to a degree depends on the outcome. So the decisions are frequent, consequential, and taken under a clock.

I have been reading Whitehead's essays on education, and his diagnosis lands on my students with uncomfortable precision: they were taught knowledge that was never put to any use, made to acquire technique before they had any sense of what it was for, and asked to be exact about things nobody had ever made interesting to them. His prescriptions, though, are pitched at institutions with years in hand and freedom to design their own syllabus. I have neither. I read a good deal of the rationalist blogosphere, and what I want is something operationalised rather than an essay in disguise: a decision procedure I can run module by module, and which is honest about where it stops being a procedure and becomes a value judgement. I would rather it announce that a question is not its to settle than quietly smuggle in an answer.

Take seriously the following claims of his, which I paraphrase and which you should correct if I have mangled them: that learning has a rhythm, moving from an initial vivid and unanalysed apprehension of a subject, through a stage of systematic acquisition and exactness, to a stage of return, application and fruition; that the same rhythm recurs at smaller scales as well, so that a term, or a week, or even a single lesson can each have its own; that freedom and discipline alternate, freedom belonging to the beginning and the end, discipline to the middle; that knowledge merely received, never used, tested or combined with anything else, is not neutral but actively harmful; that one should teach fewer things thoroughly rather than many superficially; and that entering the exacting stage too early, before there is any vivid experience to be exact about, is the characteristic failure of schooling. One further thing, which I think matters for how you write this: he treats those stages as describing the growth of a mind, and I am not sure I believe that. I want to know what the procedure looks like if I keep the rhythm purely as a design heuristic and drop the developmental claim, and which of his distinctions I would then be quietly abandoning.

Please give me the procedure itself, as a sequence of questions I ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which it terminates in a no, and the points at which it hands the decision to a person; then a one-page version I could put in front of the head of department; then run it on three cases. (a) A compulsory module on trigonometry and vectors that the awarding body examines in week nine, for which my students have no romance to draw on and no time to build any. (b) An optional 'engineering in the world' strand — site visits, a build project, some reading — which I think is the only place romance could actually start, and which the head of department regards as a luxury. (c) A cohort that is not uniform: a former mechanic who is already past romance in some topics, and a care leaver who is not yet in it in any. I expect (b) to go against me; if it does, say so plainly rather than softening it. Tell me what the procedure cannot do, where its behaviour will mislead me, and which of the three cases would best tell whether it is any good.

Please write your answer in American English.
