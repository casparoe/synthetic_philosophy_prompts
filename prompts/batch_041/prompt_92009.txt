Posting from a causal inference reading group in a philosophy department, so hopefully this is legible to somebody. We keep hitting the same wall and I want to know if it's a real wall or just a slogan doing the work. The line is "no causation without manipulation." I've now heard it pull three completely different jobs in one semester.

(1) My advisor uses it to shut down causal claims from observational data about things we can't randomize: you can't assign people a city's density, so stop saying density causes innovation.

(2) Somebody in the group used it to say that a sentence about a one-off historical event isn't just unproven, it's meaningless -- that "the cod collapse caused the town's decline" can't even be a causal claim, because you can't rerun the fishery.

(3) Somebody else used it to rule out a whole class of variables: if you can't assign it, it can't be a cause of anything, so "birth order affects personality" is a correlation with extra steps, not a causal claim.

Meanwhile we all agree the moon causes the tides and nobody can manipulate the moon, and we all agree that tapping a barometer doesn't make it rain even though a barometer is something you can manipulate. So at least one of those three uses has to be wrong, and I suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs under one name.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims "no causation without manipulation" could be making -- as a definition of what causation is, as a claim about how we can come to know it, as a constraint on which variables are allowed into a model, as a claim about when causal talk is meaningful, and whatever else it might be -- and for each one tell me whether it's trivially true, substantive and actually defensible, or just false? Then tell me which reading is in play when somebody uses it for jobs (1), (2), and (3), and whether that reading really gets them the conclusion they want, or whether they need a stronger reading that they haven't earned.

I'm not asking for a reading list, I want the menu of readings plus your verdicts, using the moon and the barometer as test cases where they help. Please be honest about which parts of this are genuinely contested among people who know the area versus which parts are just people being sloppy -- and if I've set the thing up wrong, like if nobody serious actually uses the slogan the way my advisor does, then say so plainly instead of humoring me. American English, please.
