I'm a science journalist — features, mostly, but for the last few months I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on decision theory for a piece I'm writing about how people (and, increasingly, institutions) handle uncertainty. Last week's session was on imprecise probabilities: the idea that a rational person's state of mind about some event can be a whole spread of values rather than one number — say, that the next ball out of an urn of unknown composition is red to degree somewhere between 0.3 and 0.7, and that this interval isn't a confession that you're unsure about your own opinion, it just is your opinion. I think I get the Ellsberg-flavoured motivation. But one of the other auditors, a formal epistemologist who collects "there ain't no such thing" arguments the way other people collect stamps, put this on the board and I genuinely can't get out from under it. I've typed it up as clean as I can:

(1) A rational agent's doxastic state, as far as the proposition p goes, is fully given by her credence in p.
(2) A credence is a degree of belief, and a degree of belief is a single real number in [0,1] — that's just what "degree" means.
(3) So when someone says her credence that the next ball is red lies somewhere in [0.3, 0.7], exactly one of two things is true: either (a) she does have some particular number in that range as her credence, and the interval merely reports how unsure she is about her own mental state, or (b) she has no credence at all about the colour of the ball.
(4) Option (b) is a failure of rationality: to have any opinion about whether p, you must assign p some degree of belief.
(5) Under option (a), the interval is not itself the credence; the credence is the single number buried in it.
(6) Therefore an "imprecise credence" is either a precise credence in disguise or no credence at all. Either way, there's no such thing as a genuinely imprecise rational credence, and the whole literature is chasing a phantom.

My gut says something has been smuggled in, maybe around (2) or the fork in (3), but every time I try to say what, I end up just reasserting the thing under dispute, which is not a great look in a seminar. I'm not attached to any particular fix; I'd just rather not hand in an essay that quietly assumes its conclusion. Keep it in plain English where you can — I have to turn this into something a general reader will follow, so if a term is doing real work, flag it.
