I'm preparing a piece on the moral status of computations, and I want the specification on the table before I adjudicate any of it. Write the desiderata a theory of which computations matter morally would have to satisfy: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the results it must remain consistent with, each with the consideration that motivates it as a requirement. Flag any pair that looks jointly unsatisfiable, since I suspect the list itself is where the argument lives.

I'd want at least the following on it, because I think these are where the theories strain. The theory has to be substrate-independent enough that a neuron-by-neuron replacement of a human brain with silicon chips doesn't make the lights go out, yet not so permissive that any physical system counts as implementing some computation and therefore qualifies as a candidate subject. It has to say whether status attaches to the abstract computation or only to a running, causally engaged realization: whether a program sitting unrun on a disk, or a frozen snapshot of a mind, is a subject. And it has to return a verdict on a system that reproduces a person's outputs by lookup table without computing anything, and on the same mind copied, or run at a thousandth of its usual speed, which forces it to say whether status duplicates and whether it dilutes.

Then tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidates. My hunch is the trivial-implementation constraint, but I want your reasoning, along with a judgment about which of these demands are genuine and which are parochial commitments of the functionalist camp. I don't need the scorecard of existing contenders; the criteria are what I'm after.
