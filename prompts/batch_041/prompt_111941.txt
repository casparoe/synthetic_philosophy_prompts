For a couple of years now I have been trying to defend a claim about the structure of value, and I have reached the point where I want to know what would count against it rather than collect further support. The claim is this: the relation 'at least as good as' is genuinely intransitive, and the intransitivity has a single source. Strict betterness, on my view, is transitive and acyclic; what fails to compose is the indifference-like relation, the 'about as good as' that holds between neighbouring items in a spectrum. Every spectrum argument I have worked through seems to share a skeleton — a chain in which each adjacent pair is plausibly about as good as its neighbour, bracketed at the two ends by a comparison that is plainly strict. On that diagnosis, the population spectra that end in the repugnant conclusion are not paradoxes to be dodged but predictions of the structure, and the self-torturer is not a puzzle awaiting solution but a description of what a value structure without transitive indifference looks like from the inside. A case I have been using to keep the discussion honest comes from conservation rather than population: a painting cleaned in imperceptible stages, each stage a marginal gain in legibility and a marginal loss of original surface, until the end state is a bright, over-cleaned thing that nearly everyone agrees is much worse than the grimy original, while every adjacent pair is about as good as its neighbour. I mention it not because I think it settles anything, but because it is a spectrum with two recognizable goods at the ends rather than one good and one horror, and I suspect that a view which survives only on population cases is a view in trouble.

What I want from you is a map of my exposure, and I would rather have it unkind than flattering. Which objections are fatal and which merely awkward? I am particularly concerned about the exploitation arguments: whether the pump can be built from a chain of trivial worsenings rather than from a strict cycle, whether my standing reply — that I can always refuse a trade, and that the sequence need not be completable — has any force when the sequence is one I would enter step by step under no external pressure, and whether I am committed to a decision theory without representation theorems, in which case say what happens to expected value, to choice under uncertainty, and to the very idea that some option is best. I also want to know whether my view is a substantive thesis or a redescription of something else. Does it collapse into the claim that betterness is vague and the adjacent judgments indeterminate? Into the claim that distant options are on a par rather than comparable? Into the claim that value is relative to the scale or the interest one is comparing under? If so, which of those rivals should I prefer, and on what grounds — and what does my view predict that they do not? What would a committed defender of it concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only critics say? Has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, given that any counterintuitive implication can be absorbed as one more non-composing comparison, and if it has, is that a defect or simply what a structural thesis about value looks like?

Assume I know the standard material, and do not give me citations or a reading list — I want the arguments, cases, and formal results themselves, ranked from what should make me give the view up to what I can live with. The thing that would move me most, and which I am not sure I could answer, is a demonstration that the intransitivity is an artifact of a coarse-grained comparative practice rather than a feature of value; if that is where the case against me is strongest, say so directly. And if the honest verdict is that the view risks nothing and explains everything, tell me plainly, and tell me whether that is a defect or just the price of a thesis at this level of abstraction.
