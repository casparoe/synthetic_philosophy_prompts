Under the law of armed conflict, a soldier who kills an enemy soldier in a lawful engagement commits no crime, and this holds whichever side was the aggressor: combatants on both sides enjoy the same immunity from prosecution, and the law asks only whether the killing observed the rules governing conduct in battle, not whether the war itself was just. The same body of law, however, makes the crime of aggression a leadership crime, chargeable only against those who shape or direct a state's policy of war and not against those who merely discharge it; and the conscientious-objection provisions that some states maintain typically excuse only those who object to war as such, not those who object to this particular war. So the ordinary soldier is treated as neither competent nor entitled to judge whether the war he is fighting is just — he is not punished for waging it — while being treated as fully competent and fully answerable for the lawfulness of what he does within it: he is punished for killing a prisoner, for feigning surrender in order to attack, for targeting civilians.

Reconstruct the theory of responsibility this pattern presupposes. Does it require the moral equality of combatants, the thesis that soldiers on both sides have the same permission to kill? Or can the same pattern be explained instead by a division of moral labor, in which the state answers for the war and the soldier for its conduct; or by an epistemic excuse, in which the soldier simply cannot be expected to know the facts that would make his war unjust; or by a purely conventional reading, in which the immunity is not a permission at all but a reciprocal bargain both sides keep because they benefit from it? Say which of these the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits, and whether the practice remains coherent if it rests on more than one. Press the epistemic reading in particular: a volunteer soldier in a state with a free press and a public record of aggression has ample evidence about the justice of the war. If the law would still excuse him, the excuse cannot be ignorance of fact, and the practice must rest on something else — say what.

Then test the practice against its own internal tensions. Combatant immunity is conditional on the combatant's own compliance: fighters who fail to distinguish themselves from civilians, or who violate the rules, lose the privilege and may be tried as common criminals. That makes the law look like a bargain, its protections bought by good behavior. Yet the same law forbids reprisals against civilians and prisoners of war, and insists that certain persons be protected regardless of what the enemy does — protections that cannot be explained as terms of a bargain, since they are owed even to a party that has broken it. Is the practice reciprocal or not, and can a single account of it cover both halves? Finally, suppose we replaced the underlying view with the revisionist one, on which an unjust combatant is not morally permitted to kill and may be liable to defensive force: state precisely which features of the legal practice would have to change, which would survive, and whether the surviving ones would still be justified.
