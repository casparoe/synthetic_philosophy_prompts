I am presenting a constructive paper at a departmental colloquium next month and need a Q&A preparation sheet. The paper defends what I call deliberative opacity: the distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias cannot be read off the first-person deliberative surface. A preference and a bias can be phenomenologically identical, equally articulate, equally endorsed at higher orders, and equally resistant to ordinary counterevidence, yet only one is a bias. The difference, I argue, lies in the disposition's causal history and its counterfactual profile—specifically, in whether it would survive the agent's own considered standards under an etiological counterfactual, a process in which she learns how the disposition arose and then considers whether to keep it. I call the failure mode the laundering problem: a bias can be laundered into a preference by the very reflective endorsement that is supposed to distinguish them, because the disposition shapes what the agent regards as a considered standard. My central case is one I constructed: D. has a stable, articulate, higher-order-endorsed moral aversion to eating meat. Her brother, a psychologist, reveals that the aversion was installed by a hypnotist during a diet program and would vanish under a drug; nothing in her deliberative experience changes. I use this to argue that the preference/bias distinction is real but source-sensitive and source-inaccessible. I also distinguish this from the familiar debunking argument: I am not saying the aversion is unjustified, only that its status as preference or bias is fixed by facts the agent cannot access from the inside. The paper is not about vegetarianism; the case is a device to isolate the symmetry.

The audience is a mixed colloquium: six moral psychologists who work on implicit bias and dual-process moral judgment, three normative ethicists (two Kantians and one consequentialist), and one decision theorist. They are not hostile to empirical work, but they will be suspicious of any account that seems to make the distinction either metaphysical or question-begging. Prepare the Q&A sheet I actually need. Phrase each in the vocabulary of the person most likely to raise it, give me a two-minute reply for each, tell me what to concede and what to hold, and mark where the reply is soft. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. No citations or reading lists—just the arguments, in plain terms.
