I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on Indian philosophy of action, and I'd like you to write the centerpiece. Please take the role of someone who actually knows the technical karma debates — not the watered-down "what goes around comes around" version, but the hard question of how an action can produce a result long after the action itself has ceased to exist.

Write a dialogue between two people, both of whom know exactly what they're talking about. One of them holds that karma is a genuine theory of moral desert: you get what you deserve, and the act itself leaves behind a kind of unseen potency or trace that ripens into a result later — a law built into the order of things, no divine bookkeeper needed. The other thinks that's incoherent, and their objections should be the sharp ones, not the easy ones. At minimum: (a) an impersonal causal law isn't a system of desert at all — you don't *deserve* a hangover the way you deserve a prison sentence, and the whole point of desert is that someone weighs what you did; (b) if there's no persisting self, then the person who suffers later isn't the person who acted, so the punishment lands on the wrong shoulders; (c) the unseen potency looks like a promissory note — posited to save the theory, impossible to check; (d) people are born into good and bad circumstances they never chose, and desert seems to need the agent to have been able to do otherwise.

Don't let the defender fold. They should come back with the strongest moves available — e.g. that debts can be inherited, or that a corporation can owe money even though none of its members is the same person as before, or that desert might be constitutive rather than administered by a judge. But the critic has to answer those moves in turn rather than repeating themselves. I want real back and forth, no talking past each other, and neither character should be a dummy or win on a cheap line. If it ends unresolved, thats fine.

Before you start writing, think through what the strongest version of each position actually is, then draft. Keep the whole dialogue under 700 words. No citations, no recommended reading — just the conversation.
