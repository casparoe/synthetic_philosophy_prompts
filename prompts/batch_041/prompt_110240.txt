Two colleagues of mine spent a lunch break arguing about whether the prosperity and order delivered by governments such as Singapore's or China's can justify their authoritarian character. Neither would concede anything and I could not referee. Here is the exchange as it went; I would like you to adjudicate it move by move rather than summarizing what the literature says.

(1) He said the proper test of a political system is whether it delivers security and rising living standards, and that on that test these governments pass while many democracies visibly struggle, so the absence of competitive elections cannot by itself settle the case.

(2) She said he was confusing why people comply with whether they are obligated to. Fear and satisfaction may produce obedience, but that shows the regime is effective, not that it has any right to rule; a well-run firm earns its employees' cooperation without acquiring authority over their politics.

(3) He replied that 'a right to rule' is liberal theology. Legitimacy, sociologically, just is the settled belief that a regime is entitled to rule; where a state has kept its side of a bargain for decades and the population genuinely believes it is entitled, the regime is legitimate, whatever a philosopher's intuition reports. To demand a warrant independent of what people actually accept is precisely the parochial move.

(4) She said that makes the view unfalsifiable: no matter how the prosperity was produced or how durable it is, a grateful enough population could launder any arrangement into legitimacy. She added that the justification is self-undermining, since a regime that teaches its people that authority rests on results has given them a reason to withdraw allegiance as soon as results disappoint, and so cannot supply the stable ground a theory of legitimacy is meant to supply.

(5) He answered that conditionality is no defect -- consent theories make authority conditional too -- and that the self-undermining charge proves too much, since any honest justification will license withdrawal when its own conditions fail.

(6) She came back with the point that 'order' is not a single outcome: order bought with pervasive surveillance is partly constituted by the loss of things people value, so the performance being credited is not one thing that can simply be weighed against its costs. He said that only works if you smuggle in the liberal values you claim to be testing the regime against.

Give each of the six moves a rating, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification; say what each party is right about and what each is missing; and identify any distinction the exchange turns on that neither of them has drawn. Then give me a definite verdict on which of them, if either, is closer to correct, and say whether the question as they have posed it is well formed.
