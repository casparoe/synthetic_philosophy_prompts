I teach a graduate seminar in moral psychology, and I need a teaching instrument that maps a debate my students keep flattening. The debate is about guilt and shame: which, if either, is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can choose between them.

The flattening takes a predictable form. One camp holds that shame is the deeper moral emotion because it concerns the self's standing in a shared evaluative world rather than a single act; the other holds that guilt is the more defensible one because it targets what you did rather than who you are, and because it admits of discharge through repair. Underneath both lies a mid-century anthropological contrast between 'shame cultures' and 'guilt cultures' — a contrast that was applied to Japan, to the Mediterranean, and elsewhere, that has been criticized for a long time, and that nonetheless keeps doing silent work in how the question is posed. My students also slide between three questions as though they were one: what the difference between the two emotions is, in what respect one might be better, and whether a culture can select for one. I want the instrument to hold those apart.

Please act as a philosopher who works on the emotions and on moral psychology, and who is in the habit of mapping a debate rather than summarizing it. Think the problem through carefully before writing: begin by enumerating the questions on which positions in this area genuinely divide, then the combinations of answers those questions permit, and only then place the actual positions in that space. Do not start from a list of names.

Deliverable: a decision tree — or a taxonomy whose every split is labelled with the question that separates the siblings — that takes a reader from a few starting commitments to a named or defined position. It should be usable as a two-page seminar handout: the tree, then the leaves, then your notes.

Constraints:

- Every branch node must be a question or claim a reader can answer without knowing which position the answer leads to. No node may be of the form 'do you think shame is the better emotion?'.
- Build the tree on at least these axes, though you may add others: (i) whether the guilt/shame distinction is a real distinction between two emotions, a difference of degree on a single axis, or an artifact of observers that keeps being redrawn for reasons of its own; (ii) where the difference lies, if it is real — in the object (act vs. self), in the audience (an internalized standard vs. actual or imagined spectators), in the standard (morality vs. social standing), or in the action tendency (reparation and confession vs. hiding, withdrawal, disappearance); (iii) what 'better' is being asked for — fittingness, motivational efficacy, respect for the person's standing, or fit with liberal and pluralist institutions — and you should show where a change of criterion moves a position to a different leaf; (iv) whether a culture can choose an emotion at all, and if so by what mechanism.
- At least two branch nodes should turn on concrete cases rather than on abstract formulations. Suggested material: a person who has done something irreversible, so that there is nothing to repair and no one to forgive; a person who has done nothing wrong but has been publicly disgraced; an institution that reintegrates an offender after public confession.
- These positions must appear as leaves, though you may split any of them further if it comes apart under pressure: shame-primacy; guilt-primacy; the division-of-labour view on which each emotion does a job the other cannot and neither is ranked; the view that the dichotomy is an artifact of the observer; the view that neither is a moral emotion in the relevant sense because both are heteronomous; the view that the unit of assessment is not the emotion but the practice of holding someone answerable; the view that the deliberate cultivation of shame yields something else — humiliation, or mere compliance — so that a culture cannot choose the emotion without replacing it; strong social constructivism; affect-program universalism; the middle view on which the emotion is universal but its salience and typical elicitors are selected by institutions; and the view that the debate conflates shame with imposed humiliation and therefore mis-ranks shame.
- Aim for between ten and fourteen leaves. Each leaf gets a one-line statement of its most awkward commitment.
- Mark combinations of answers that no one occupies, and say for each whether you take it to be incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, rather than papering over the gap, and say what further question would separate them.
- Assume graduate students who know the standard contrast and not the specialized literature. No citations, no reading lists, and no names of scholars unless you are confident about the attribution; define every leaf by its commitments rather than by its defenders, and define any familiar label you use. If you are unsure whether a position has a name, say so rather than inventing one.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or case would move a position to a different leaf, or lead you to redraw a branch, and where in the classification you are least confident — including any premise you are assuming that a reasonable reader might reject.
