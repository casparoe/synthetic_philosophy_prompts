For about a year now a reading group I'm in has been going round in circles over the centipede game, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of it. I have read enough of the decision-theory material to know the standard moves, so please don't rehearse them; what we are stuck on is a variant we built ourselves. Rather than playing the tree in real time, each player submits a program in advance; the two programs are given read access to each other's source and then play the tree mechanically. One half of the group says this dissolves the backward-induction paradox, because the choice is made once, over whole programs, and nobody is ever asked to act at a node they believe will not be reached — the counterfactual beliefs, the subjunctive dependence on what a player would do at an unreached node, never have to be supplied. The other half says it merely relocates the paradox one level up: the unravelling now runs over programs rather than moves, and whatever makes taking at the last node irresistible makes taking at the first node irresistible too.

My own hunch, offered as a hypothesis rather than a position, is that the two halves are answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking what an ideally rational agent who has common knowledge of rationality would do, the other is asking which decision procedure we should expect to be selected in a population that gets to read source, and neither question has much bearing on the other. Tell me whether that diagnosis holds up, and if it doesn't, what the real structure of the disagreement is.
