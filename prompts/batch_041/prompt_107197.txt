I write a column on science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about the split between approval-directed and goal-directed conceptions of artificial agency. Please act as a philosopher of technology and decision theorist, and think carefully before you answer. Begin by brainstorming the logical space: what questions genuinely separate the views, rather than merely relabelling them? Then give me a decision tree, not an essay, that starts from the bare commitment that an artificial agent can usefully be described as pursuing something, and leads to named positions. Treat approval-directed as the view that an agent should select actions by asking what a suitably informed supervisor would approve of, and goal-directed as the view that agency requires a fixed objective or preference ranking; but do not assume those are the only options.

Each internal node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing the labels. The branches should turn on at least these distinctions: whether the agent's standard is a target it represents or a standard to which it is held by others; whether approval is a terminal value, an epistemic proxy for an underlying goal, or a constraint on how a goal is pursued; whether the agent may revise its own objective; whether the relevant unit of assessment is outcome, intention, or process; and whether corrigibility is a goal, a side-constraint, or an illusion. Leaves should include pure expected-utility goal-directedness, approval-directed agency, a hybrid that treats approval as evidence about an underlying goal, intent-aligned agency, a process- or role-based view, and at least one unoccupied combination of answers. For each leaf, give a one-sentence characterisation and its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate. Do not cite any literature; coin descriptive labels where necessary. Keep the whole answer under about 700 words.
