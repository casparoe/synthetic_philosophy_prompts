I am a novelist at work on a book about a family whose fortune was made in the nineteenth century and whose present-day members are, in various ways, beneficiaries and critics of that fortune. One of my characters is a historian of political economy, and I have given her a scene in which she tries to explain to a skeptical cousin a passage from John Stuart Mill's Principles of Political Economy. The passage is from Book II, Chapter II, and it reads: "The institution of property, when limited to its essential elements, consists in the recognition, in each person, of a right to the exclusive disposal of what he or she have produced by their own exertions, or received either by gift or by fair agreement, without force or fraud, from those who produced it." A few sentences later, Mill adds: "If it be said, as it may with truth, that those who have inherited the savings of others have an advantage which they may have in no way deserved, over the industrious whose predecessors have not left them anything; I not only admit, but strenuously contend, that this unearned advantage should be curtailed, as much as is consistent with justice to those who thought fit to dispose of their savings by giving them to their descendants."

I want to understand exactly what Mill is claiming here, and how the two halves of his position fit together. On the one hand, he grounds property in the right of producers to what they have produced, and he treats the right to dispose of one's savings by gift as part of that institution. On the other hand, he says that the advantage of those who have inherited the savings of others is unearned and should be curtailed as much as is consistent with justice to those who thought fit to dispose of their savings by giving them to their descendants. My question is: what principle does Mill use to determine how far the curtailment may go? Is the right of bequest absolute, or is it subject to a prior principle of equal opportunity? And how does his distinction between the right to give and the right to receive — if he makes such a distinction — bear on contemporary debates about dynastic wealth and equal opportunity?

I would like help understanding the passage, not a summary of Mill's political economy as a whole. Please explain the argument in the passage, the distinction between the right of bequest and the right of inheritance, and what Mill's view implies about the justice of large inherited fortunes. I am particularly interested in how Mill could simultaneously defend the right of bequest and argue for curtailing inherited advantage — what is the reconciling principle? And how would that principle apply to a contemporary case of a perpetual trust designed to keep a fortune in a family for generations? I am writing a scene in which my character must articulate this principle in plain language, so I need to understand it well enough to have her explain it without jargon.
