My team lead recently announced a surprise code review. She said it would take place on one of the next five working days, and that on the morning of the day it happened we would not be able to know it was that day. Two details she stressed: she had already chosen the day and written it on a card sealed in an envelope before announcing anything, and she would not change it no matter what we said or concluded afterward. Two teammates then argued about whether her announcement can be honored, and I couldn't referee them.

Dana argued that it cannot. Suppose the review hasn't happened by Thursday evening; then only Friday remains, so we would know, so it wouldn't be a surprise — hence it can't be Friday. Take Friday away and the same step eliminates Thursday, and so on down to Monday. So there can be no surprise review, and the lead's announcement is simply false. She added that the sealed envelope changes nothing, because what defeats the announcement is what we can deduce, not what the lead has privately decided.

Sam argued that the induction proves too much. A piece of our own reasoning can't reach back and stop an event the lead fixed before she heard it, and the announcement promised only that we would not in fact know — not that we could not possibly know. Since we've now reasoned our way into believing no review can happen, we will in fact be surprised whenever it comes, Friday included. So the announcement is consistent, the envelope is exactly why it's consistent, and Dana's step "it can't be Friday" is where the mistake enters.

Dana replied that Sam's account makes the lead's promise depend on our being wrong: she isn't guaranteeing a surprise, she's counting on our confusion, and if "we will not know" can be secured only by our believing something false, then the announcement isn't the clean truth Sam claims. Sam answered that this is the same move once more, and that it produces a regress with no stable resting point — which he takes as evidence against the reasoning, not against the lead.

Please adjudicate this move by move: which of Dana's steps are sound, whether Sam has correctly located the flaw, and what each of them is missing — in particular, what work the words "surprise" and "not be able to know" are doing on each side.
