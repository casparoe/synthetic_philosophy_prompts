I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and I've somehow committed to writing a paper for a workshop on the canon and contemporary politics, which means I've landed on the most unpromising pairing imaginable: Bodin and the European Union. My angle is that nobody has actually asked what Bodin would make of the EU as it exists now, rather than as a footnote in a federalism-versus-intergovernmentalism lecture, and I want a first pass at his verdicts before I commit to a reading of him.

So here's the Bodin I'm working with — tell me first if any of this is off, because everything downstream hangs off it. Sovereignty is one and indivisible; a commonwealth can't really be mixed in its form of government, the mixed constitution is a confusion people talk themselves into; the sovereign is whoever can give law to subjects without needing anyone else's consent, and that's the mark you use to identify it; the sovereign is nonetheless bound by natural and divine law, by the fundamental laws of the realm, and by the covenants it makes; the rights of sovereignty (final judgment, the last word on law, coinage, taxation, war and peace, pardon) can't be split off and parcelled out permanently without the thing ceasing to be what it is; and magistrates below the sovereign hold only a revocable commission, not a slice of sovereignty itself.

Now the EU side, and this is where I need him to get specific, otherwise the paper is just a seminar exercise. Predictions please on:

1. The Court of Justice's doctrine that EU law has primacy over national law, including national constitutional law, against the German court's line that the Union is a derivative order of limited competences and that Karlsruhe keeps the last word on ultra vires acts. Where does he come down, and does he read this as two sovereigns staring at each other, or as one sovereign plus a committee that has got above itself?

2. The euro and the ECB. Twenty-odd members handed monetary policy to a body they don't directly instruct, on a mandate written into a treaty. Given that coinage is on his list of the marks of sovereignty, is that even possible on his view, or does it just show that these aren't properly commonwealths any more? And would he class the Commission and the ECB as magistrates on revocable commission, or as something with no real name in his scheme?

3. Ever closer union — the treaty language committing the members to an unspecified further integration with no terminus. Binding covenant, unenforceable aspiration, or a promise no sovereign can make on behalf of its successors? I genuinely don't know and I keep flip-flopping.

4. The democratic deficit. I have a hunch this is where he surprises people, because on his own account the scandal isn't that the European Parliament can't initiate legislation — it's something about where responsibility sits and whether anyone can be blamed. Am I right about that? What would actually offend him in the current institutional setup, and what would he just shrug at?

5. Article 50 and withdrawal. Does the mere existence of an exit clause settle the question of whether the members are still sovereign, or is he going to say something more annoying than that?

For each one: give me the prediction plus the argument he'd actually run, then, separately, tell me where his framework simply fails to determine an answer — he never faced a body that is neither a league of states nor a state, and I want to know when I'm watching him reason and when I'm watching you fill in for him. Then tell me the one commitment of his that he'd have to give up or fudge to keep the rest standing, because my suspicion is that indivisibility is doing all the work and that if it goes, the whole thing goes with it — but I'd like to be told that rather than assume it.

No reading lists, no citations, I know the literature well enough to be dangerous. And please don't give me the standard Bodin-would-be-a-Brexiteer move, anyone can do that in a paragraph and it's boring. I want to see him argue his way there, or not.
