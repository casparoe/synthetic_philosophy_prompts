I am the discussion leader for a monthly interdisciplinary reading group — the members are scientists, historians, and engineers, intelligent and argumentative but with no training in epistemology — and next month's topic is the bootstrapping problem and the neighbouring problem of easy knowledge. I do not want an explanation of either problem; I already understand the material. I want a plan for the session: how the explanation should be built, in what order, and around what.

So that we are working from the same framing, here is the material the session has to convey. The bootstrapping problem concerns a certain kind of track-record argument for the reliability of a faculty. I form an ordinary perceptual belief — that the object before me is red — and I also register how things appear, namely that it looks red. From the fact that in this instance my belief is correct, I conclude that my colour vision is reliable in this instance; and by running the same step across many instances I assemble a record that is supposed to show that my colour vision is reliable in general. The trouble is that the first step already depends on the very faculty whose reliability the record is meant to establish: I am entitled to the premise that the object is red only if my colour vision is working. The problem of easy knowledge is the second half of the topic. Grant me ordinary perceptual knowledge together with the principle that I can know the conjunction of things I separately know, and a single glance is then enough to give me knowledge that the table is red and is not a white table being illuminated by red light. That is knowledge won too cheaply, and it makes the sceptical alternatives too easy to wave away.

Given that material, produce the teaching plan. In particular:

- The order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit — what has to be in place before the next piece can even be stated, and why. Say which single idea, if it were not secured first, would cause everything after it to be misunderstood.

- The one example that should carry the whole session, and what it must have and must lack for the argument to come out cleanly. Tell me whether the red-table case is the right one to build on, or whether a different case would do the work better, and why.

- The misconceptions the group will predictably arrive with or form along the way, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I expect they will think this is merely the problem of induction, and that it is about how we actually acquired our concepts as children; I assume there are others I am not anticipating.

- The one question I should put to them at the end that would show whether they have understood the problem rather than merely followed the exposition.

- What I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how to say so without leaving them feeling that something was withheld.
