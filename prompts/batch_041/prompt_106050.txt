I run a free evening seminar at our community centre — no formal training of my own, just years of reading and arguing with friends — and this term we have been working through the question of which animals can be said to be conscious. About a dozen people come, most of them with no background either, but they write a short response each week and I mark it, which is turning out to be harder than I expected: the errors that matter are not the loud ones, and I keep catching myself rewarding a fluent paragraph that on a second reading does not quite hold together. I would like you to mark the response below and tell me what mark to give out of ten, along with the specific comments that justify it. I have to hand those comments back to the student, so they need to attach to particular sentences and say what is right or wrong with them, rather than leaving me with a general impression. I am not confident enough to be sure I am catching the real problems, and I would rather not give a high mark merely for clarity of writing. For what it is worth, the student is a retired veterinary nurse, writes very cleanly, and has been the most engaged person in the room.

The question I set asked them to keep three things apart: arousal (being awake and responsive rather than knocked out), sentience (having experiences that feel like something, especially pleasant or unpleasant ones), and self-consciousness (having some conception of oneself as a subject that persists over time). I also asked them to use two ideas we had built up together over the previous weeks. The first was the contrast between a threshold view, on which a creature either is or is not a subject of experience at all, and a graded view, on which sentience comes in degrees. The second was what we had been calling the marker strategy: instead of arguing from one mechanism, list several independent features — centralised integration of information, behaviour that flexibly trades one goal off against another, responses to injury that are altered by painkillers rather than left as plain reflexes — and see whether they turn up together in the same animal. The question was to say what could reasonably be claimed about insects, fish and cephalopods, what kind of evidence would move the judgement either way, and whether the difficulty of settling it is a metaphysical difficulty or an epistemic one.

Here is the response:

"To decide whether an animal is conscious we first have to say which of the three senses we mean, and I think most disputes dissolve once that is done. Arousal is clearly widespread and not very interesting: an insect moving about and reacting to the world is awake in that sense, and so is a fish. Nobody should doubt it. Sentience is the hard case. Following the marker strategy, I would look for several signs at once — a nervous system that integrates information rather than wiring reflexes point to point, behaviour that trades one goal against another, and responses to damage that change when an analgesic is given. Insects have a centralised nervous system and show quite flexible behaviour; bees can learn, and can apparently weigh effort against reward. So on the marker approach they are at least candidates, and the strategy is useful precisely because it does not pretend to hand down a verdict — it tells you which animals are worth taking seriously. But insects have no neocortex, and since the neocortex is where subjective experience is produced in humans, I think we can say that insects are not sentient; the flexibility is impressive but it is probably just very good machinery. Fish are a clearer case for sentience: they have nociceptors, and nociceptors are what allow an animal to suffer, so a fish that has been hooked and afterwards avoids the hook is feeling pain. Cephalopods are the strongest non-mammalian case. They have a large nervous system with most of the neurons in the arms rather than in the brain, which means their consciousness is distributed through the body rather than located in a head, and they solve problems in ways that look like curiosity.

On the second part, I would say the difficulty is metaphysical. When I ask whether a shrimp is having an experience, I am asking about the nature of its mind in a way that no amount of behavioural evidence could settle, because I can never get inside its head; that is the hard problem of consciousness, and it applies to other people just as much as to shrimps. If sentience is graded rather than all-or-nothing, the problem becomes less pressing, because we no longer have to find the exact point on the scale at which a creature starts to count, and in practice every animal we have discussed will be somewhere on that scale. Self-consciousness is a separate and much rarer thing, and since the mirror test is the standard way of testing it, most of the animals we discussed will fail it, which is roughly what one would expect."
