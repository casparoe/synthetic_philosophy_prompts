I teach a third-year seminar on practical reason and I'm writing the final exam. The last chunk of the course is the transitivity-of-betterness material — spectrum arguments, the repugnant-conclusion-adjacent stuff, whether 'better than' can fail to be transitive, all of it. I need one essay question for the exam, plus a model answer and a marking scheme I can hand to the two grad students who'll be marking with me. 30 marks, 90 minutes, closed book.

I want you to take the role of someone who's taught this material at MA level for years and has seen every lazy answer in the book. Before you write anything, brainstorm for a bit: what is the actual crux here, what does a lazy answer look like, and what would a genuinely good answer have to do that a lazy one can't fake? Think it through properly, then write the question and the supporting materials.

Here's the case I want the question built on. Outcomes are ranked by how well off the people in them are:

- O1: 1,000 people, all at 100.
- O2: 999 people at 100, plus 20 people at 60.
- O3: 998 people at 100, plus 40 people at 60.
- ... and so on, until you get to something like O50: 500 people at 100, plus 10,000 people at 60.
- From there the chain keeps going the same way but one level down — the remaining 100s get traded for larger numbers of 60s, then the 60s for larger numbers of 40s, then the 40s for 20s, and the 20s for 5s — until you reach O*: 100,000 people, all at 5.

Adjacent comparisons: each step looks better than the one before it (a few people lose a little, alot of people gain alot, or come to exist at a decent level). Endpoint comparison: O1 looks better than O* — 1,000 people flourishing vs 100,000 people scraping by. So you get O* better than ... better than O2 better than O1, but O1 better than O*. Something's gone wrong.

Things the question needs to do:

- It can't be answerable by reciting 'some philosophers think betterness is intransitive'. My students can all do that in a paragraph and then they're stuck.
- It has to make them take a stand on whether this is just a Sorites paradox in disguise. If they say it isn't, they have to say exactly what the difference is and why it matters; if they say it is, they have to say what follows for the argument.
- It has to force the formal point: if 'at least as good as' is both transitive and complete, then 'better than' comes out transitive for free, so something in the package has to go. I want them to say what they'd give up, and what it costs them elsewhere — e.g. whether it means admitting incomparability, or denying that 'not worse than and not better than' means 'equally good'.
- It should make them say something about the practical fallout: whether rational choice requires acyclic preferences, and if betterness is intransitive whether that's a reductio or something we can live with.
- Write it so a student who's memorised the standard moves but hasn't thought about the formal bit will run out of road about two thirds of the way in.

Deliverables: the exam question itself; a model answer of roughly 800 words that actually makes the moves rather than summarising the literature; and a marking scheme out of 30 with five or six must-hit points, the marks for each, a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong, and a sentence or two at the end on what separates a first from a 2:1 on this question. Don't worry about citations or a reading list — it's closed book and I don't want them padding with names.
