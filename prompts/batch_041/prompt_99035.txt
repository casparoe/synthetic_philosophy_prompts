I'm a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. For about a year I've been working out, on my own, an account of why buying meat, taking a flight, or voting can be wrong even though no single one of those acts makes a measurable difference to the harm, and I've been invited to present it to the ethics reading group that the philosophy department near me runs. My view, in short: a collective harm like factory farming or climate change is best modeled as a threshold structure, and the morally relevant feature of my act is not its marginal causal difference but the role it occupies in that structure -- whether it is the act that trips a threshold, one of the acts that would be redundant once a threshold is tripped, or one that sits inertly below it. I claim the moral status attaches to the role rather than to the difference, and that this is a third option between the marginal-difference view (which seems to make almost everything permissible) and the joint-action view (which seems to make us complicit in something we never agreed to).

The room will be roughly: a Kantian who works on universalizability and imperfect duties; a consequentialist who thinks in expected-value terms and is involved with effective altruism; a virtue ethicist; and one person who works on collective intentionality and joint action. Please give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight objections I will actually get from this room, phrased the way each of them would put it, ordered by how much damage they do; what I should concede up front and what I should hold; a short reply to each and a note on where that reply is weakest; and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.

I should add: I suspect I may be reinventing something that already has a name, or that my "third option" quietly collapses into one of the two views I say it's distinct from. If that's the case, say so plainly rather than handing me a tidy prep sheet that pretends my framing is sound. Keep the whole thing under 1,100 words.
