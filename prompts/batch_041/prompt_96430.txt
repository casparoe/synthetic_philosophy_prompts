Last night I spent the better part of three hours arguing with a friend who is finishing a dissertation in epistemology, and I came away with the uncomfortable suspicion that she had found a real fault in my position and that I had spent the last hour simply repeating it more loudly. I am due to present my own view on the value of knowledge at a reading group next week, and I would much rather locate the trouble now than in front of an audience. So I am setting down where I actually stand and asking you to audit it. Five commitments: (i) the Meno problem is a genuine problem — knowing that p is better than merely having a true belief that p, and better in a way that is not exhausted by the fact that true beliefs are more useful to have; (ii) the explanation is Plato's own image — a true belief is like a statue of Daedalus, admirable where it stands but liable to run off, whereas knowledge is tethered, and the tether is the believer's grasp of why things are so, the working out of the reason that the traveler with only a true opinion about the road to Larissa lacks; (iii) I am a veritist, in the sense that true belief is the fundamental epistemic good and everything else that is epistemically good — justification, reliability, tethering, whatever else one wants to add — is good because of its relation to truth; (iv) I accept the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief plus whatever fourth condition is needed to handle Gettier cases; (v) the other half of the road to Larissa lesson is that for practical purposes a true belief is as good as knowledge, since the traveler who believes truly arrives just as surely as the one who knows.

My friend insists that at least two of these five cannot be held together and that the difficulty is not merely one of emphasis. She would not tell me which, and her parting shot — that I was defending a position that was respectable in 1963 — struck me as a debating point rather than an argument, which is why I am bringing the matter to you. What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look as though they are; put the genuine ones in order, worst first, saying for each how much has to be surrendered to relieve it; and identify the cheapest single revision that would restore consistency, together with what it costs me — that is, which of the remaining commitments would have to be weakened or reinterpreted once I make it. If any of the five is a red herring, in the sense that nothing else on the list could possibly conflict with it, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a tension for the sake of symmetry.

Please commit. Tell me which one commitment you would give up, give your reasons, and defend the choice against the obvious alternative rather than presenting both and leaving the decision to me. Then tell me what the position looks like afterwards: whether the Meno problem survives in the version I have stated, or whether I have to restate it in a weaker form, and whether my (ii) is a fair reading of the Meno or a modern gloss I have quietly imposed on it.
