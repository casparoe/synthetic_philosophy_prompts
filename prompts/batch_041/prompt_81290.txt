For a long-form piece I'm writing about how the vocabulary of behavioral economics has migrated into management advice and self-help, I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on judgment and decision making, and I've become fixated on an argument one of the students keeps making. The received view — the one in the textbooks and in every airport paperback — is that honoring sunk costs is a straightforward error: once resources are irrecoverably gone, only expected future costs and benefits should govern what you do next, and the persistence people show in failing projects, failing degrees, and failing relationships is an emotional or normative hangover rather than a piece of reasoning. She doesn't deny that the persistence is real; she denies that the standard diagnosis is the only one available. I'd like you to write out the exchange between her and the strongest defender of the standard view you can construct, since the dialogue is going to carry most of the argument in my piece.

Her position, as I understand it, has three parts. First, the standard framing quietly assumes that agents know their own preferences, capacities, and the shape of the choice problem; drop that assumption and honoring sunk costs can be a reasonable policy — a way of buying information about what you are actually like, or of making your own future conduct predictable to yourself and to others, or of screening which projects are worth continuing. Second, she claims the familiar demonstrations cannot distinguish her account from the error account, because both predict the same persistence, and she takes this to be a problem for the standard view's confidence rather than for hers. Third, she distinguishes sunk costs that leave a public trace in the world — a building, a published commitment, a promise made in front of people who are watching — from those that leave none, and argues that only the first can ground rational persistence, since only the first changes the strategic situation going forward. The defender should press her hard on whether any behavior at all could count as evidence against her account, on whether she is merely redescribing ordinary forward-looking reasoning with an enlarged state space that happens to include the agent's own psychology, and on what work the word rational is doing here — whether the relevant standard applies to a single act or to the policy of having the disposition in the first place.

Keep both of them competent and make them answer each other's actual points rather than trade monologues; I want at least one moment where one of them concedes something small and the other has to adjust in response. Have them work through one concrete case in detail rather than gesturing at failed investments in general, and let the dialogue end not in agreement but at the sharpest remaining crux — the place where they genuinely diverge — along with a rough sense of what argument or evidence would move each of them. I don't need citations or a tour of the literature; I want the argument itself, at the level of detail where it gets interesting.
