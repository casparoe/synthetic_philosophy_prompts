You are a philosopher of science and decision theorist whose specialty is anthropic reasoning: the logic that governs how facts about your own existence, and about the number and distribution of observers, ought to constrain what you believe and what you choose. I am going to lay out a set of verdicts I hold about particular anthropic cases, and I want your help finding the principle, if there is one, that underlies them. Please begin by brainstorming rather than answering. Write out the several candidate principles that could generate a pattern like mine, including candidates I have not named; for each, say which of my verdicts it explains and which it strains; only then commit to a view. Reason in the open — I would rather watch you work than receive a verdict with the scaffolding removed. Do not give me a reading list or cite literature; I want the argument, not the bibliography. If a case seems underdescribed, state the assumption you are making and proceed rather than stopping to ask.

My verdicts, all first-person:

1. Doomsday. I think the Doomsday reasoning is basically sound. Learning that I am, very roughly, the hundred-billionth human being ever to have lived strikes me as genuine evidence that the total number of humans who will ever live is not vastly larger than that — that humanity is nearer its middle than its beginning. My birth rank seems informative, not a mere artifact of the fact that I had to be born somewhere in the sequence.

2. The presumptuous philosopher. And yet I find something absurd in the figure who is told that two cosmological theories fit all the evidence equally well and differ only in that one says the universe contains a few billion observers while the other says it contains vastly more, and who then declares the second to be enormously more probable, purely because it contains more observers for him to be. It seems to me you cannot read the size of the cosmos off the bare fact of your own existence.

3. Creation by coin. Suppose a fair coin is about to be tossed: on heads, exactly one person is brought into existence, and on tails, a billion people are. I then find myself existing, with no further information. I think it is overwhelmingly likely that the coin came up tails. I am aware this sits uneasily with verdict 2, and I cannot see how to make the two sit together.

4. Sleeping Beauty. On Sunday I am told that I will be put to sleep, woken on Monday, and — if a fair coin lands tails — put back to sleep with my memory of Monday erased and woken again on Tuesday, whereas if it lands heads I am woken only on Monday. Waking and not knowing which day it is, I put my credence in heads at one third.

5. Boltzmann brains. I am unwilling to conclude, from the mere fact that I am undergoing coherent experience, that I am probably a short-lived fluctuation in an otherwise disordered cosmos. The line of thought that would push me toward that conclusion strikes me as a reductio of something, though I cannot say precisely of what.

6. Quantum immortality. If the many-worlds interpretation is correct, I do not think I should expect to survive an indefinitely repeated round of Russian roulette, or to find that some branch always continues. Whatever the mathematics of branching says, the survivor in the branch that continues is not someone whose experience I should anticipate as mine.

Now: is there a single principle, or a small family of them, that captures this pattern? State it precisely, clause by clause, and motivate each clause by pointing to the verdict that forces it. Then tell me what the principle predicts for two cases I have not described — one in which the number of observers varies while nothing else does, and one in which my identity across branches or copies is at issue — and say how confident you are in each prediction. Finally, and most importantly, tell me which of my six verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and what I lose by abandoning it. If no single principle can do the job, give me the smallest set of rival principles, identify the verdict that separates them, and tell me which verdict I should give up and why. If you conclude that some of my verdicts are simply inconsistent with one another, say so plainly, and rank them by how confident you think I should be in each.
