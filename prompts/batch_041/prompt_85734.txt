Every few months my reading group devotes a session to de re modality, and this time I am the one assembling the discussion piece, so I would like you to write a dialogue for us. It should be between two philosophers of equal competence and roughly equal charity. One is a counterpart theorist of the Lewisian sort, who holds that each individual exists in exactly one world and that everything we say about what might have been true of a given individual is really a claim about the counterparts that individual has in other worlds. The other is a defender of transworld identity, who holds that when we say of a particular person that they could have done otherwise, we are talking about that very person, and that the person in the other world is not a stand-in for them but is them. Neither may be a strawman, neither may score a point by misstating the other, and each must answer the argument the other actually gave rather than retreating into a recitation of stock talking points; if one of them lands a hit, the other has to absorb it and adjust rather than change the subject. I have read enough blog threads on this to be tired of the way both sides get flattened into slogans, so I would also like them to resist the temptation to declare the whole dispute merely verbal: if one of them tries that move, the other should push back and the first should have to defend it. Roughly 1,500 words, and please no citations, no references to the literature, no reading list; they should argue from cases and from each other.

I want the exchange organized around two pressure points rather than a tour of the debate, since the stock versions of this disagreement are exactly the sort of thing a competent reader can recite without thinking. The first is a case in which the counterpart relation comes out non-functional. Suppose there is a world containing two people who are each, on the counterpart theorist's own account, a counterpart of one actual person of ours, and no fact about that world that settles which of the two, if either, is the privileged one. The counterpart theorist will say this is fine, that the relation was never supposed to be one-to-one, and that the demand for a unique counterpart is a prejudice imported from identity. The identity theorist will say that only one of the two can be our friend and the other is at best a duplicate, so the counterpart theorist has described a world in which our friend is ambiguous between two candidates, which is not a description of anything. Have them actually argue this out, and then have them run the mirror case, in which a single possible individual is the counterpart of two of our actual people rather than the reverse.

The second pressure point is the question of differences that make no observational difference. Ask them whether two worlds could differ only in which individual plays which role, with the two worlds qualitatively identical and no possible observation, memory, or inference able to distinguish them. I expect they will divide on this, but I do not want the division simply assumed: each should argue for a verdict and say what it costs the other view. In particular, can the identity theorist give any account of what makes the cross-world identity claim true other than its being brute, and can the counterpart theorist say what makes one counterpart relation the right one rather than one our interests or our descriptive vocabulary happen to favor? Let them draw out where that leaves things, and end with the live options laid out and a clear statement of what each side still owes, rather than with a handshake or a synthesis.
