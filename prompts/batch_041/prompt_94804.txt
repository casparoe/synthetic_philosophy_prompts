I run a monthly reading group whose members come out of the rationalist blogosphere: they will happily argue about Bayes and decision theory for two hours, but most of them haven't touched physics since a required intro course, and a couple of them are the type who pounce the moment they smell a hand-wave. Next month I've volunteered to do Maxwell's demon and the thermodynamics of information, and I want you to plan the session rather than explain the topic to me. Take the role of a statistical-mechanics instructor who has taught this exact material to humanities-trained audiences and has watched it go wrong. Work out the order in which the ideas have to come, and why each depends on the one before it: entropy as a bookkeeping device over microstates versus entropy as something in the world, the demon's procedure, the question of whether the second law is genuinely threatened, and only then the resolution. I want one carrying example to do the whole job, and I'd prefer the Szilard one-particle engine to a gas of many molecules, because I want the audience to feel that the demon's entire advantage is bought with a correlation it must acquire and then pay to forget; tell me what that example has to include and, just as important, what it has to leave out so that the arithmetic doesn't swamp the idea.

Here is the part I care most about. I do not want the session to end on the standard textbook fudge that the demon is defeated because measuring costs energy. My understanding is that the real cost lands at erasure, not measurement, and that plenty of popular treatments get this wrong. Build the sequence so that the audience sees why measurement can in principle be reversible and why the second law is saved only once the demon has to reset its memory, and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head off the misconceptions they will walk in with: that information is subjective or observer-dependent, that the demon creates order from nothing, that the fix is some quantum uncertainty or observer effect, that the second law must therefore be false, and the subtler one that the demon is impossible because it would need unbounded memory. Give me the check for understanding at the end, a single question that separates someone who has internalized where the entropy actually goes from someone who has merely memorized the punchline, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud. Before you draft the plan, brainstorm the dependency structure first.
