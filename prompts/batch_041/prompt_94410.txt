I'm working up an essay on consequentalist cluelessness and I'd like your comments -- mainly on whether the central argument actually goes through and whether the whole thing is interesting or just a repackaging of something obvious. Don't worry about prose. Here are my notes:

- The usual setup: any action of mine has enormous, chaotic downstream effects, so I can't know its expected consequences. If act-consequentialism needs me to compare expected consequences, it's unusable as a guide.

- My move is to split the cluelessness into two kinds. (a) Washout cluelessness: the far-off effects are unknown to me but effectively random with respect to my action -- they're symmetric noise, they cancel in expectation, so the local, knowable effects dominate the comparison. (b) Bias cluelessness: the far-off effects are systematically correlated with what I do -- not noise but a directional shift. This one doesn't cancel, and it's exactly the part I can't estimate.

- Thesis: only bias cluelessness is a genuine objection, and it's fatal, because we have no reason to think the systematic component is small compared to the local effects -- and some reason to think it's often much bigger (tiny local good, large systematic bad).

- I preempt the obvious reply, that over the long run things average out, or that the far future swamps everything and is roughly symmetric. That reply just assumes the systematic drift is zero, which is the thing in dispute. And decision-theoretic fixes -- act on the policy that looks best given your evidence -- don't help, since the bias is precisely what your evidence is silent about.

- Upshot: a dilemma. Either you bound consequentialism to some restricted domain of effects, which looks like smuggling in a deontology of scope, or you keep the unbounded domain and admit the criterion is defined over a region you're systematically blind to, in which case it can't guide anything.

So: is the washout/bias split doing real work, or does it collapse into the ordinary point? Is the dilemma a real fork, or is there an escape I'm missing -- does the symmetry argument have more force than I'm giving it? And does this land as a contribution or as a known point in new words? I don't need names or citations, just tell me where it stands conceptually and which step is weakest.
