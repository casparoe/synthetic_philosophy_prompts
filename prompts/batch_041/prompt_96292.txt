I am convening a graduate seminar on weakness of will, and before we begin assessing particular accounts I want the specification written out: what any adequate theory of akrasia has to deliver. Give me that list of desiderata, with the case or consideration that motivates each requirement, and let the requirements do the work; I shall handle the comparison of candidates myself unless you have a short line to add at the end.

Treat the domain as spanning both the traditional problem and the empirical literature on intertemporal choice, since our authors disagree over whether these are one phenomenon or two. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of a camp.

Cover at least the following.

The core case. An agent judges, at the time of action and all things considered, that she ought to do A; she does not do A; she does not retract the judgement; she regrets the omission and takes it to have been hers. A theory that cannot accommodate the regret, and the sense in which the act was nonetheless her own, has missed the target. Say, too, whether abandoning a resolution under pressure and never having formed one are the same defect or two.

Synchronic and diachronic weakness. Take an agent whose preference between two dated rewards reverses as the earlier option draws near: at nine in the morning she prefers a larger reward at two o'clock to a smaller one at noon, and by half past eleven she prefers the smaller one. Suppose she knows this about herself and deplores it. Is that one episode of weakness, two, or none? The theory must decide, and justify the decision.

Inverse cases. Take an agent who has absorbed, and sincerely endorses, a moral outlook we regard as mistaken, and who on one occasion acts against her own best judgement and does what we think right. We admire the act and not the judgement. The theory must therefore not identify weakness with failure simpliciter, and must say what, if anything, is weak here.

The impostor case. Take an agent whose acts never conflict with her judgements, but only because her judgements are quietly revised to fit whatever she was going to do anyway. That is not continence. A theory satisfied by mere act-judgement agreement has failed a case.

The boundary with compulsion and incapacity. An agent who fails to act on her judgement because of a tic, a paralysis, or a compulsion is not thereby weak-willed. The theory must locate the boundary, say what does the work there (control, reason-responsiveness, or something else), and survive the possibility that the same sub-personal machinery sits on both sides of the line.

Precommitment. An agent who arranges in advance to be restrained from what she will later be tempted to do, and who is then restrained, has exercised no self-control at the moment of action. The theory must say whether she is continent, weak, or neither, and how self-binding can work at all.

Empirical adequacy. The theory must fit the robust findings that people discount future rewards at a rate that declines with delay, so that preferences over a fixed pair of options can reverse as time passes, and that people will bear real costs to restrict their own future options. It must also say whether these findings describe a defect at all, since a time-inconsistent preference structure is not obviously an irrationality.

Distinctions it must not collapse: weakness of will against a change of mind; against self-deception; against weakness of desire; not trying against trying and failing; and the first-personal deliberative stance, in which the agent takes herself to have been able to do otherwise, against the predictive stance, in which the failure is the output of a discounting process.

Finally: flag any pair or subset of these requirements that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which has to give. That is the part I most want. Note which single requirement excludes the most otherwise popular accounts, and add a short line on how the judgement-centred, will-centred, attention-centred, and preference-theoretic families, and the eliminativist who denies that there is a distinct phenomenon, fare against the list. I need the theory to serve two purposes, explaining and predicting ordinary self-defeating behaviour and telling us when blame is apt; if those purposes pull the requirements apart, keep them separate in the list rather than fudging. No reading list, no citations, no attributions: if you invoke a position, name it in a clause and move on.
