I chair the selection panel for a small foundation's flagship fellowship, and we score every application against a published rubric. Two or three times a year we end with two finalists whose scores are identical and whose files resist further discrimination, and the panel then does one of two things badly: it deliberates past its deadline, or someone invents a tie-breaker that was never in the rubric. Please write a procedure the panel can run whenever this happens, built on what is actually known about choice without preference — the Buridan's-ass problem at institutional scale. It has to separate the four situations that get conflated: indifference, where the options leave us cold; parity, where they are comparable but neither is better and the difference matters; uncertainty, where we do have a ranking but lack the facts; and a preference we hold but have not admitted to ourselves. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, when to stop collecting information and act, whether a lottery or coin toss is ever permissible and what would have to be true for it to be, and why a decision made without a preference can still be rational rather than a confession of failure. Mark the points where the procedure hands the matter to a person — the chair, the board — because the question is one of values, not facts. Then run it on two cases: a genuine tie between two equally strong finalists, and a tie that dissolves once the panel admits what it is actually weighing. Include one case where it gives an answer I will not like, and say what the procedure cannot do.
