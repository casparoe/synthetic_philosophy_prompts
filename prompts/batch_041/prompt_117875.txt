Two people I know — both historians, one in the middle of a book, the other a sceptic by temperament — have been arguing for a week about a footnote, and they have asked me to referee, because I was at the dinner where it started and I have no stake in either side. I can follow the argument but I cannot settle it, so what I want is a verdict on the argument as they actually made it, move by move.

The claim in dispute is that a certain abbey in the region was sacked in the 1140s. Nothing in the surviving sources from that decade mentions any such event. The earliest reference to it comes from a chronicle written some two hundred years afterwards. That is the whole of the evidence, and everything below concerns what it licenses.

(1) Marta: the silence is decisive. The chronicles that do survive from that decade were written within a day's ride, by men whose stated business was to record precisely this kind of outrage, and they set down far smaller things. Had the abbey been sacked, they would have said so; they did not; so it did not happen.

(2) Devin: Marta is treating two surviving manuscripts as though they were the archive. What survives is a small and accidental fraction of what was written. The later chronicler plainly had something to work from, whatever it was, and that something is now lost. A silence in the surviving remnant tells us almost nothing about what occurred.

(3) Marta: Then nothing can ever be concluded from a silence, and the same fragmentariness you invoke would dissolve every positive claim you are content to make. You do not doubt that the abbey was standing in 1140, though the evidence for that is no thicker. You cannot trust the record when it speaks and refuse to hear it when it is quiet.

(4) Devin: Those cases are not symmetrical. A positive report has to survive a short chain of failures: that the writer was placed to know, that he had no reason to distort, that the text reached us unaltered. A silence has to survive a much longer one — that he knew, that he thought it worth setting down, that he set it down, that the manuscript survived, and that no later copyist had a reason to drop it. I cannot put a figure on any of those links, and neither can you.

(5) Marta: You reason from silence whenever it suits you. The sources are silent about the ordinary fabric of the world you take for granted, and silent about a great deal that you are quite sure never happened. So the argument from silence is not illegitimate in principle; you want to apply it only where it does not inconvenience your source.

(6) Devin: I reason from silence whenever the silence would be genuinely surprising — that is, whenever a surviving source is of a kind that could hardly have failed to mention the thing. That is the whole test: how much more probable the silence is if nothing happened than if something did. What we are really disputing is whether this event falls into that class, and you have simply asserted that it does. The two founders of the genre already disagreed on exactly this: one sets down what is said and leaves the judging to his reader, the other claims to give you only what he could test. You want the second's authority on the first's evidence.

Please go through the six moves and say, for each, whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why — not merely which of the two is more persuasive overall. Then tell me what both of them are missing, and what a defensible way of reasoning from silence would actually look like. Both of them will read your answer, so it has to be fair to the strongest version of each side and specific about where each one overreaches.
