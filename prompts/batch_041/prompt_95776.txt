I serve on the academic priorities committee of the board of a small graduate faculty. A deceased alumna, who spent her career as a research assistant to a mid-twentieth-century medium, has left the faculty a substantial bequest with a codicil. The money is to establish a Sidgwick Fund for the Study of Psychical Phenomena, and the first year’s disbursement is to be decided by our committee under a three-part test she attributes to Sidgwick’s own methodological writings. As she states it, the test requires that any funded project (1) produce results that are independently corroborated or repeatable under conditions that rule out ordinary sensory leakage and fraud; (2) bear on a question of philosophical consequence rather than mere curiosity; and (3) be pursued with the same public, adversarial scrutiny that attaches to any other empirical claim. The codicil names two competing proposals for the first year: a psychology professor’s preregistered telepathy study with a local medium, using sensory deprivation and blinded judging, and a historian’s proposal to digitize and annotate the early archives of the Society for Psychical Research, including the cross-correspondence files. The committee must decide whether to accept the bequest at all, and if so, how to allocate the first year’s funds. Some members argue that accepting the money legitimizes pseudoscience and will embarrass the faculty; others argue that refusing it would be a dogmatic prejudgment of a question Sidgwick himself thought it a duty to investigate.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action, with a timeline and any conditions you think the committee should attach, and it should give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. In doing so, engage Sidgwick’s actual position: he helped found the Society for Psychical Research and served as its first president, yet he was famously cautious about evidence, insistent that psychical claims be weighed by ordinary canons of proof, and inclined to treat the question of survival as bound up with the rational authority of morality rather than as a merely technical curiosity. The memo should also say whether the codicil’s three-part test is a fair reconstruction of Sidgwick’s own stance or a selective reading, and what difference that makes to the decision. Do not rely on citations or a reading list; reason from the principles and the case as stated. Finally, say what the committee cannot decide on the evidence available and what it should therefore delegate to a standing faculty review body.
