I'm a novelist by trade — I write fiction, mostly — but the department ropes me in every year to run a small upper-level seminar called Metaphor and Mechanism in the Life Sciences, which is really just a philosophy-of-biology elective wearing a nicer coat. This term we've spent three weeks on the idea that DNA is a code, and I want to end with a final essay question that my students cannot get through by reciting the usual transcription-translation-central-dogma litany. The knot I want them to confront is this. Everyone in the room agrees that the mapping from codons to amino acids is arbitrary in roughly the sense Saussure meant when he said the sign was arbitrary — there is no chemical reason GCU should code for alanine rather than anything else — and then they slide from that to saying DNA carries information, and from that to saying the genes tell the cell what to do, and I want a question that forces them to prise those three steps apart and say what, if anything, licenses the move from one to the next. I'd also like the parity thesis in there, the argument that DNA is just one developmental resource among many and no more informational than the cytoplasm or the maternal environment, because they find it outrageous and I think outrage is pedagogically useful; but I want them to have to state what the defender of the code metaphor must actually show in order to answer it, rather than just asserting that DNA is obviously special. And if you can work in the awkward fact that the standard code looks rather well optimised against the effects of point mutations and mistranslation — which is a bit of a problem for anyone who wants to lean hard on arbitrariness — so much the better, because half of them will have noticed it and half won't.

Could you draft the essay question — 90 minutes, one question, they see the paper a week in advance but bring no notes — together with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme? Build the marking scheme round five must-hit points with marks out of 20, and for each point give me a one-line note on the way students typically miss it; I'm especially keen on the ones who write but the code is degenerate, so it isn't really a code, as though that settled anything, and the ones who treat arbitrariness and aboutness as the same thing. One more thing, and I'd hold you to it in the model answer and the notes alike: keep apart what is actually settled among people who work on this — I take it that nobody seriously denies that the mapping is chemically arbitrary — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether information talk in biology should be cashed out in a Shannon sense, in a teleosemantic sense, or dropped altogether; don't let the model answer present any one of those camps as the consensus view, and flag where it is leaning on a contested reading. Write the whole thing in British English, please — programme, colour, that sort of thing — and pitch the model answer at the level of a good third-year who has done the reading but is not yet a specialist.
