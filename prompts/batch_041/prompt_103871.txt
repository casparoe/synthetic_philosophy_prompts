In the foundations reading group I belong to, a colleague has been pressing an analogy he takes to be decisive. Zorn's lemma, he says, is simply the infinite counterpart of an elementary finite fact: begin with any element of a finite partially ordered set and repeatedly move to a strictly larger element; since the set is finite, the climb must halt, and where it halts you have an element that cannot be enlarged, a maximal element. The infinite case, on his telling, is the same climb carried on transfinitely — you simply keep going — and the hypothesis that every chain has an upper bound is nothing but the assurance that the climb always has a limit to arrive at. From this he draws a conclusion he puts to work in argument: a proof that invokes Zorn's lemma is bookkeeping on top of a triviality, so it incurs no genuine debt to the axiom of choice, and anyone who treats such proofs as non-constructive, or as importing a strong set-theoretic assumption, is overreacting.

I would like the analogy audited rather than waved away. Lay out explicitly what is supposed to correspond to what — the finite set, the climb, the element at which you halt, the work done by finiteness, the work done by the chain hypothesis, and whoever it is that does the climbing — then say where the correspondence fails, and separate the failures that bear on his conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. His conclusion, that Zorn's lemma costs nothing beyond finite induction, either survives the disanalogies or it does not; say which, and if the analogy is a poor one, say what would be a better one. Concrete partially ordered sets are welcome wherever they make a failure vivid; no citations or literature are needed.

Give the response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences: one item for the correspondence, one for the failures, one for which failures matter for his use, one for the verdict on his conclusion, and one for the better analogy, with no introduction and no conclusion.
