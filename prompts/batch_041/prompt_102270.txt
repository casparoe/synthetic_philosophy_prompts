I teach a seminar on political ethics and every year the dirty-hands unit turns into a shouting match, so I've been trying to nail down my own gut verdicts on the cases we use and see whether they add up to anything. Here's what I think. A mayor who quietly lets a chemical plant stay open, knowing it modestly raises local cancer rates, in order to keep two thousand people employed — I think that's permissible, though she should lose sleep over it. A prime minister who authorizes a strike on a house where a bombmaker is staying, knowing it will kill a family of bystanders too, to stop an attack — also permissible, same bad taste in the mouth. A legislator who breaks an explicit campaign promise to her own constituents to get a child-health bill through — that's fine by me, but she owes them an explanation and an apology, and it would be wrong for her to feel nothing about it. Now the two that trouble me. A candidate who spreads something that's literally true but deeply misleading about a rival, to beat that rival, who really would be dangerous in office — I can't get myself to call that permissible, even though the stakes are high and the statement is true. And a minister who lets a corrupt old ally keep his post so the ally won't bring down the government before a budget vote that would help millions — that one feels wrong to me, not just dirty but actually out. But then I look back at the strike and the plant and think, what's the difference? All four trade some people's rights or welfare for a larger good, and I've said yes to two and no to two.

I've got a half-baked principle I'd like you to stress-test and repair. Roughly: an officeholder may do the dirty thing only when the harm it averts is a genuine catastrophe that can't be averted any other way, and only when the wrong she does falls on people who've put themselves in the way — the bombmaker, the extortionate ally — rather than on the public she's meant to serve, the voters she lies to or the rival she smears. And the act stays wrong even when it's permitted, so it leaves a remainder: she has to carry it, own it where she can, and make it up to whoever she can. Does that get all four verdicts, or do I have to give one up, and which one? What does it predict for a case I haven't listed — a governor who, during a flood, lies to the public about how bad things are so they won't panic and block the evacuation routes? I've gone back and forth on that one for years. Also tell me honestly if the principle is doing real work or if I'm just dressing up a consequences calculation, because I suspect the remainder clause is where all the weight is actually sitting and I'm not sure it can carry it.
