I have been reading a trade book on decision theory and self-control that devotes a chapter to precommitment, and I would like to know how much of its central characterization I should trust. I am not asking who said what first, or where to read more; I want to know what is true. Here is the gist, reconstructed as faithfully as I can manage:

"Ulysses contracts take their name from the story in which Odysseus has himself lashed to the mast so that he can hear the Sirens without steering onto the rocks. The point of the story is that the later self — the self who will actually be in the situation — cannot be trusted to do what the earlier self knows to be best, so the earlier self arranges matters so that the later self simply cannot do otherwise. The defining feature of a genuine Ulysses contract is that it removes the option: it is not a promise, a reminder, or a financial penalty, but an arrangement that makes the unwanted action impossible. A constitution is this same device applied to a whole people, a Ulysses contract writ large, which is why we speak of entrenched rights and the separation of powers as precommitments. The familiar objection — that the later self has as good a claim as the earlier one to be the 'real' self, so that binding it amounts to self-tyranny — is a genuine difficulty for the story, but the constitutional case escapes it: since the people can always amend their constitution, they are never really bound, and the whole business is simply a matter of making change slow and deliberate. The metaphor earns its keep because it explains how a sovereign people can be legitimately constrained by its own past decisions, and it dissolves the puzzle of precommitment once we notice that the binding is voluntary and revocable."

I am inclined to accept the amendment point, since it matches my sense that a democratic people cannot be permanently bound, though I am not sure it is doing the work the book wants it to do.
