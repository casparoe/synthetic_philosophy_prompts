I am a novelist, and each spring I teach a small seminar in the writing programme here — nominally on the essay, in practice on what happens to an argument when you press on it — and I have just finished marking the mid-term. The students were asked, in a page, to set out Hempel's raven paradox, to say what Hempel took its lesson to be, and to assess whether the Bayesian treatment dissolves it or merely relocates it. The answer reproduced below is one I have now read three times without being able to make up my mind about it, which is usually a sign that I am being persuaded by fluency rather than by argument; I am the kind of marker who lets a graceful sentence stand in for a step that does not follow, and I would rather not do it here. So I would be grateful if you would grade the answer and, more usefully, write the comments that would justify the grade: go through it claim by claim, say where it is right, where it is right but thin, and where it goes wrong, and finish with a single mark out of ten together with a sentence explaining what the mark is for. One piece of background. A note in the margin of the student's earlier draft, from the colleague who runs the companion logic section, raised the suggestion that a green shirt might actually lower the probability that all ravens are black, on the ground that it makes it likelier that there are no ravens at all; the student has taken that thought into the final version, and I would like your view both on what the student does with it and on how much the thought itself can bear.

The question was as follows. "Set out Hempel's raven paradox. What did Hempel himself take its lesson to be? Does the Bayesian treatment dissolve the paradox or only relocate it? You may assume familiarity with the equivalence condition, Nicod's criterion, and the likelihood-ratio account of confirmation."

The answer reads: "Hempel asks us to consider the hypothesis that all ravens are black. This is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, that all non-black things are non-ravens. Now take a green shirt. It is non-black and it is not a raven, so it is an instance of the contrapositive, and on Nicod's criterion, which says that a generalization is confirmed by its instances, it confirms the hypothesis about ravens. The paradox is that evidence with no apparent bearing on ravens nevertheless bears on them, and that one could in principle do ornithology from an armchair by cataloguing the contents of one's wardrobe. Hempel's own response is to accept the conclusion rather than explain it away. On his account, confirmation is a matter of an object satisfying a hypothesis, and since the two formulations are logically equivalent, whatever satisfies one satisfies the other. It follows that the green shirt confirms 'all ravens are black' to exactly the same degree as a black raven does: the satisfaction relation runs symmetrically between the two formulations, and this is simply the price of a purely qualitative theory of confirmation. The Bayesian reply does not dispute the equivalence; it disputes the significance. Confirmation goes by the likelihood ratio, and a green shirt raises the probability of the hypothesis only to the extent that non-black objects are likelier to be sampled when the hypothesis is false than when it is true. Since non-black objects vastly outnumber ravens, the increment is negligible, effectively zero, and the paradox is a pseudo-problem produced by treating a vanishing quantity as though it were evidence. There is a further consideration that I take to settle the matter. Observing a green shirt makes it more likely that there are no ravens at all, and a hypothesis about ravens tells us nothing about the world if there are no ravens for it to be about. So the green shirt is not evidence for the hypothesis but evidence against it, and once we read 'all ravens are black' as also asserting, and not merely presupposing, that there are ravens, the paradox never gets started."
