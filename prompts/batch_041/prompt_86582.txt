For most of my working life I designed and interpreted measurements, and since retiring I have been reading economics and the philosophy of it with a growing suspicion that I am missing a distinction my old trade would never have let me get away with. In the laboratory we were punctilious about separating a quantity defined operationally — here is the procedure, here is what the number means — from a quantity posited as a theoretical term and estimated indirectly through its effects. Those two carry very different burdens of proof, and confusing them is how you end up arguing past someone for an afternoon. I cannot work out which of the two an economist is offering when he writes down a utility function. Sometimes it looks like nothing more than a compact way of recording a pattern of choices, in which case any ranking that reproduces the pattern will do equally well and the question whether it is the right utility function is malformed. Sometimes it looks like a hypothesis about something going on inside the person, of which choices are an imperfect symptom. And sometimes it looks like a normative construction — the ranking the person would have if she were better informed and thinking straight — which is a third thing again.

I would like you to build me a decision tree that sorts the positions on what a utility function is and what it is for. Start from the commitment I actually hold, as a man who spent forty years with instruments: that everything we have to go on is behavior, verbal reports included, since saying something is a kind of doing. Let every branch be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and let each branch question be a concrete case rather than an -ism. For instance: the man who takes the same option every time but reports no preference and says he is only following a habit; the person whose choices cycle and who, shown the cycle, insists the options really are like that, each better than the next in some respect; the person who chooses one lottery in a straight choice and then, asked what he would sell each lottery for, prices the other one higher; the person who wants the wine tonight and wishes he did not, and is relieved when the wine is taken away; two people whose choices never conflict but whose reports of how well their lives are going differ sharply; the person whose preferences shifted after the choice was made, so that what he chose now looks to him like what he always wanted. Each of these should split the tree somewhere, and I want you to say where and why, and to say if any of them turns out not to separate anything.

The leaves should include at least these: the pure representation-theorem view on which utility is a bookkeeping device for a choice pattern and nothing more; the view that utility names a mental quantity of which choice is evidence; the idealized-preference view; the view that preference satisfaction is a proxy for something else that is what really matters; the decision-theoretic view on which utility is whatever quantity makes the person's betting behavior come out as expected-value maximizing; the view that utility is a modeling convenience with no claim to represent anything at all; and the view that treating preferences as given data begs the question, because preferences are made rather than found. Add any leaf you think belongs that I have not named, and tell me which leaves are reachable only by giving up my starting commitment.

I want the empty rooms named rather than skipped: for the combinations of answers that no one occupies, say whether they are vacant because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered. Flag any pair of positions your tree cannot in fact separate, instead of letting a final question pretend to do it. And finish with a paragraph on where the unfalsifiability worry bites: since any finite pattern of choices can be rationalized by some ranking, tell me which of these positions add enough structure to be tested and which are content to be untestable, and whether the untestable ones are thereby worthless.

A dozen branch questions and seven to nine leaves would suit me. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables and no bold, the way you would write it out in a letter to a colleague — even where a tree seems to want a diagram, do it in sentences. Six or seven hundred words of connected argument is worth more to me than a schematic, and please do not cite anyone; I have no library at hand, and I would rather have the distinctions in your own words than names I cannot check.
