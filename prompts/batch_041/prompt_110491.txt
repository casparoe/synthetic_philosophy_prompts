I am an undergraduate currently taking a course on concepts, categorization, and analogy, and I also have a background in type design: I have drawn letterforms, set type, and I am fluent in the working vocabulary of the craft — bowl, stem, counter, aperture, terminal, spine, crossbar, ear, tail, stress and modulation, contrast, weight, x-height, overshoot, sidebearings, kerning, tracking, optical correction. My cognitive-science vocabulary is much weaker; I can follow the readings but the machinery is not second nature to me.

The position I would like translated is the Letter Spirit account of letter identity and style (Hofstadter and McGraw's program, discussed in the Letter Spirit chapter of Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies). As I understand it, the claim runs roughly as follows. Underlying every 'a' there is an abstract letter skeleton, an armature that specifies what it is to be an 'a'; style is a coherent family of deformations applied to that armature, applied consistently across the whole alphabet, so that style is a property of the set of letters rather than of any single letter. The program's two central tasks are to extend a given style to letters it has not seen, and to judge whether an unfamiliar shape still falls inside the category 'a'. Category boundaries are treated as graded and somewhat fluid, shifting with context, rather than fixed by a definition.

Please restate that position, claim by claim, in the vocabulary I actually use as a designer, and then give me a residue report. For each claim, tell me what maps cleanly onto something a type designer would say, what survives only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in the craft at all. Keep the translation honest: do not quietly import a designer's assumptions and present them as part of the original position, and do not let the original position borrow credit from the craft where the correspondence is only verbal.

The residue is the part I care about, so please address it concretely rather than in generalities. On the craft side, consider at least these candidates: the 'a' may be single-story or double-story, which looks like a discrete structural switch rather than a deformation; designers describe themselves as shaping the counters, the white rather than the black; spacing and rhythm belong to the word or line rather than to the letter; and a letter's identity is partly differential, since an 'a' is what it is in part by contrast with the 'd', 'o' and 'q' it must not be mistaken for. Which of these can the Letter Spirit account absorb, and which are simply not expressible in it? On the other side, is there anything the account asserts that cannot be said in a designer's vocabulary — the graded boundary, the ur-letter beneath all styles, the requirement that a style cohere across an entire alphabet rather than letter by letter?

Finally, I want a verdict on what the residue means. Is it a defect of the craft's vocabulary, which is a body of precedent and practical maxims with no theory of what makes a category a category? Or is it a sign that the original claim is thinner than it appears — that the essence of an 'a' comes down to whatever a competent designer would accept as an 'a', which is close to circular? If the essence does not survive the translation, say what does survive and whether that residue is still a genuine explanatory claim. I do not need further reading; I need the translation and an honest account of what it fails to carry.
