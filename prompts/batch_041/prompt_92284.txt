Two months ago, at my grandmother's funeral, I heard the same family anecdote told four times, in four versions, each narrator certain that theirs was the original — and I recognized, not for the first time this year, that I have begun thinking about culture in frankly Darwinian terms. It started when a joke I made at a wedding came back to me three weeks and several group chats later in a form I barely recognized and would not have told myself; it had evidently survived by becoming more transmissible, not more accurate. Since then I have been reading in the literature on cultural evolution, and I have come to think that the meme-as-replicator picture is indispensable as a heuristic and, taken literally and then institutionally, a trap.

I have promised a piece for a small volume of "in-universe documents" — texts written as though from inside a world where a contested claim has been settled and then bureaucratized. Mine is to be sustained deadpan satire, and I would like you to draft it.

The premise: memetics won, not as a research programme but as an administrative one. A public body — call it the Bureau of Memetic Standards, or invent something better — regulates the circulation of cultural items under a statute whose central concepts it cannot operationalize. What I want is the Bureau's official guidance to its own inspectors.

Specifics:

- Form: a numbered official guidance document, roughly 800 words — scope, definitions, thresholds, procedures, a compliance schedule, at most one short appendix. Give it a title and an effective date.
- Voice: entirely deadpan. No winking, no jokes flagged as jokes, no narrator stepping outside the document to tell the reader it is satire. The comedy has to come out of the machinery itself — the definitions, the thresholds, the exemptions, the forms to be filed.
- That machinery should be the actual machinery of memetics, pushed one institutional step: fidelity, fecundity and longevity as compliance criteria; the meme's-eye view taken literally enough that the Bureau has no mandate to protect hosts, only vehicles; memeplexes as regulated entities that are more than the sum of their members and also, inconveniently, exactly the sum of their members; and the borrowed epidemiological apparatus — a basic reproduction number, super-spreaders, something like herd immunity to a joke.
- At least one section must concede the standard objections from the inside, as procedural clauses rather than as arguments: that the Bureau cannot say how many memes a given utterance contains; that its definition of fidelity cannot distinguish copying from reconstruction; that cultural transmission is Lamarckian and so the analogy leaks; that no observation could count as disconfirming an attribution of fitness, since failed predictions get reclassified as drift.
- Include a short worked example tracing one concrete item — a children's clapping rhyme, a chain letter, a corporate slogan, or the like — through the Bureau's categories, with the counting breaking down in plain view.
- Include a dissenting footnote or minority note from an invented official who holds the rival view that cultural items are reconstructed rather than copied, and who is thereby, on the Bureau's own rules, out of order.

Constraints: do not quote real texts, real scholars or real legislation, and do not attribute views to named real people; every voice in the document should be invented, and any quotation marks should enclose words spoken by your invented officials. A reader who has never heard of memetics should still find it funny as bureaucracy. A reader who works on cultural evolution should recognize the objections as the real ones and should not feel strawmanned — the target is the replicator picture and its administrative ambitions, not quantitative cultural-evolution research as such.
