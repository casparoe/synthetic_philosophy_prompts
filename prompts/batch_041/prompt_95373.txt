A colleague in my department — a philosopher who works mainly on ethics but reads a good deal of decision theory, and who is the person we all ask about this sort of thing — circulated a short write-up of risk-weighted expected utility as background for a reading group I am about to join. I know the theory is a serious proposal and a genuine rival to expected utility theory rather than a fringe idea, and I know my colleague is competent, so I take it that the paragraph is mostly sound. What I want to know is which of it I can repeat and which of it I would be embarrassed to repeat. Here is what she wrote:

"Risk-weighted expected utility is a repair of expected utility theory rather than a rejection of it. The expected utility maximizer evaluates a gamble by multiplying the utility of each of its outcomes by that outcome's probability and adding up the results. The risk-weighted maximizer adds one further ingredient between belief and desire: a risk function of her own, which rewrites probabilities before they are used. She takes each probability in the gamble and adjusts it in the light of how she feels about the outcome it attaches to — a risk-averse agent discounts the probabilities of the outcomes she welcomes and gives extra weight to the probabilities of the outcomes she dreads — and then does the ordinary expected utility calculation with the adjusted probabilities. So a fifty per cent chance of the good outcome is treated as worth less than fifty per cent, and expected utility theory turns out to be the special case in which the risk function does nothing. This is why the theory can accommodate the Allais preferences that embarrass expected utility theory: the agent who takes the sure sum over the long shot is not making an arithmetic mistake; she is applying her own attitude to risk, which belongs to her rational profile alongside her beliefs and her desires. Formally, the view is the normative twin of prospect theory: take Kahneman and Tversky's finding that people are risk-averse about gains and risk-seeking about losses, strip out the psychology, and ask what a theory of rationality looks like once a non-linear weighting of probabilities is treated as legitimate rather than as a bias to be corrected. The price of the view is that the risk-weighted maximizer gives up the independence axiom, and with it any guarantee that her preferences will be transitive, or that she will never choose a gamble that is worse in every state of the world than the alternative on offer. The moral the theory draws is that the expected utility maximizer of the textbooks is not an idealization of perfect rationality but simply mistaken."

Two things make me uneasy, though I could not defend either uneasiness. The first is the sentence about transitivity and dominance: I have a vague impression that the point of the theory is that it is better behaved than the descriptive alternatives it is often mentioned alongside — that it is meant to keep the elementary requirements and give up only something narrower — so a theory whose selling point was supposed to be its discipline ought not to license cycles, and I doubt that anyone would propose a view that lets you choose a dominated gamble. The second is the comparison with prospect theory, which feels too neat to me; I thought the two views disagreed about something fairly deep, though I could not say what.

What I want is an audit rather than a summary. Go through the paragraph and, sentence by sentence, tell me: what a decision theorist who works on this topic would accept as a fair statement of the view; what is the sort of simplification a competent popularizer is entitled to make, and at exactly what point the simplification begins to mislead; and what is a genuine error, and of which kind — a conflation of two things the view keeps apart, a modest thesis inflated into a strong one, a technical term used in its everyday sense, an objection to the view presented as the view's own conclusion. Where my colleague has gone wrong, tell me what a defender of the view would say in reply, and whether that reply is any good or merely restates the position. Finally, the closing sentence draws a moral from everything that precedes it: does that moral survive once the errors are corrected, or do the accurate parts support something weaker, or something different in kind? If one small example — two or three gambles, with numbers — would make the difference between the corrected account and my colleague's account visible, give it and say what it shows. I am not asking for further reading: I will not have time to chase down primary sources before the session, and I would rather understand the paragraph.
