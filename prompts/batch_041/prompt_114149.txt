I'm writing a novel and I've hit a wall, so I'm hoping you'll do the philosophy for me.

The setup: the afterlife is a bureaucracy. Every harm a person does in a life gets logged by a clerk, converted into units of suffering, and paid back in the next life — one unit for one unit, exactly. Nobody remembers their previous life. That's the whole system: pure desert, no mercy, no grace, just a ledger.

My editor says the premise is incoherent and honestly I think she might be right, but I'd rather not rewrite the whole book. So argue for the position that the ledger is a genuine system of moral desert — that when my character gets hit by a truck in life four for something she did in life one, she really does deserve it. And I mean deserve in the strict sense: it's fitting, good in itself, that she suffers — not that it's useful or deterent or whatever. A defender of desert would have to sign off on it.

You'll have to get past at least these, and I'd rather you not just wave at them:

- she doesn't remember the thing, and if she did remember it she'd be a different person by now;
- in my draft souls sometimes split, so one life's debts get handed to two successors who then live seperate lives — I'm not even sure who the debtor is;
- and the ledger never closes, because the souls keep cycling. If everyone's ledger ends up unbounded, in what sense is anyone's suffering deserved rather than just the next entry in an infinite series?

Give me the strongest version of the pro-ledger argument, not a tour of what other people have said about karma. I don't need citations, I need to know whether the thing holds up, and where you'd say it actually breaks.
