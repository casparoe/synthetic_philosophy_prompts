I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I read popular neuroscience and philosophy of mind in my spare time, and I am putting together a small collection of jokes about philosophical problems. I would like one of them built on pain asymbolia, which I suspect is richer comic material than anything I have seen used.

Treat the following as the factual basis for the joke, and correct me in a sentence or two if any of it is wrong before going on. Pain asymbolia is a condition in which pain is experienced without its unpleasantness. It has been reported after brain injury, including damage to the insula, and also after certain psychosurgical procedures and in some patients receiving morphine. Its signature is the pattern of what survives and what is lost: patients can locate the painful stimulus, distinguish sharp from dull, withdraw a hand from a flame, and report the sensation as pain, sometimes as intense pain, yet they are not distressed by it and say it does not bother them. The usual gloss is that the sensory-discriminative dimension of pain is preserved while the affective-motivational dimension is missing.

What I find philosophically loaded about this is a dilemma with no comfortable branch. If pain is by definition unpleasant, if hurting is part of what the word means, then these patients are not in pain at all, and their reports are false or mistranslated, so the phenomenon is really the absence of pain with an odd residual sensation left over. If instead the sensory signal is what makes a state pain, then there is pain that is not bad for the person who has it, which breaks the usual link between pain and the desire to make it stop and makes the badness of pain seem to come from somewhere other than the sensation. The second horn interests me most, because it pulls apart this hurts from this is bad for me.

Please write me a joke that turns on this material. Requirements:

1. Keep it short. A one-liner, a riddle, or a few lines of dialogue, not a sketch.
2. The punchline must turn on the content above: the survival of intensity and location alongside the loss of distress, the dilemma over whether the patient is in pain at all, or the split between hurting and being bad. A joke that would work just as well for any strange neurological condition, or that lands only on philosophers being unable to agree about definitions or doctors being cold, does not satisfy this.
3. It should be funny to someone who knows roughly what pain asymbolia is, and it should not need any vocabulary that such a person would not already have.
4. Do not make the patients the butt of the joke. The comedy should come out of the concepts rather than out of people with brain injuries.
5. Give me one joke, then two or three sentences explaining which clause of the material the punchline depends on and what would have to change about the condition for the joke to stop working.

One frame I have not been able to finish, which you may use, adapt, or discard: a hospital pain questionnaire that asks patients to rate, on separate scales, how much it hurts and how much it bothers them, and an asymbolic patient who answers ten and zero. If nothing funny can be built out of any of this, say so plainly and tell me which part of the material resists comedy.
