I'm a novelist, and the county historical society has asked me to give the after-dinner speech at their annual dinner this fall — it's the bicentennial of a duel fought in the meadow behind the old courthouse, nobody died, which is why we get to eat over it. Half the room are real historians, half came for the chicken, and the society's president still thinks the whole thing was noble. Write me the speech, five minutes, and don't let it turn into a lecture.

Here's what I actually want it to argue, and I'd like you to commit to it: I dont believe these things end because somebody finally makes the argument. The argument against dueling was sitting right there the whole time. Same with slavery. So my hunch is that both practices died when the people who cared about status found a cheaper way to signal it — dueling stopped being a flex once the men who mattered started caring more about the bank than about the insult. The speech has to make that case and still be fair to the honor story (Appiah's version) and to the plain economic-interest story, because if I'm wrong about the cheap-signal line, I'm probably wrong there.

One more thing to build it around: my great-great-uncle got challenged in 1871 and simply didn't show. I want the speech to turn on that — what does it mean that the last duels are the ones nobody bothered to show up for? Then, at the end, a short note meant for me and not for the room: where the speech is fudging the ideas, which parts are genuinely contested, which reading of the honor material you're leaning on, and what would actually change your mind about the cheap-signal line.
