I teach a first-year animal ethics unit and I want to build one session around Charlotte's Web, which I've read aloud to my own kid maybe forty times now and which is definately doing something I can't name. What I want from you is a reading of the book, not a summary of it, and I want the lens to be wild animal suffering.

By that I mean the thought that if you actually count up the sentient lives on this planet, the overwhelming majority are not farm animals but wild ones, and their lives are largely a matter of being eaten alive, starving, freezing, or hosting parasites; and then the live argument about whether anyone should do anything about that at all, or whether nature is the one thing we're supposed to keep our hands off, and the further complaint that animal ethics gets very worked up about pigs and cows and stays oddly silent about insects and spiders, who are most of the number.

Charlotte's Web is a gift for this because the two halves sit right next to each other. Wilbur is the mammal we're told to care about; Charlotte is a predator who eats flies; and the book's most beautiful image, the web, is a killing device. I don't want a smug "actually this book is about murder" reading — the friendship has to be taken seriously — but I also don't want you to look away from the fact that the barn is a place where things get eaten and nobody, narrator included, thinks twice.

Some things I'd like it to reach. The opening, with the ax, and then the rescue: Wilbur is saved by being made worth something to the farmer, which is a welfare deal and not an abolition one, and it's paid for with fame — words in a web, "Some Pig", "Terrific", "Radiant", "Humble" — plus a bribe to Templeton, who helps only when he's fed. That looks to me like a very sharp joke about how concern for animals actually works, and I want it followed through. Fern, the only human who can hear them, grows out of it and starts mooning over Henry Fussy, so the book quietly files moral attention to animals under childhood. And I want the arithmetic: a spider who kills insects by the thousands to save one pig, a farm that runs on feed, which pulls in the arguments about how many field animals die in a grain harvest and about beef versus chicken, one animal versus many. The book's sympathy seems allocated by charisma and I want to know whether it knows that. The ending too, where Wilbur keeps Charlotte's daughters around and never quite loves them the way he loved her — I don't know what to do with that and I'd like you to.

Be honest where the reading strains. There's a real chance the book is just a story about a friendship and isn't making an argument about any of this, and if so I'd rather you say so than force it. Finish with what the book does better than the philosophy does. My hunch is it's something about how loving one particular animal is the actual engine of moral concern, and that the arguments never capture that.

Don't quote the book at me or give me a reading list or citations — I know the book and I won't check the sources. Just write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way you'd write me a long email.
