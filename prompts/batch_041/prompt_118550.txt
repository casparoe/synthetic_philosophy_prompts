So I'm in this philosophy club at my high school and we read a short thing about a puzzle by some philosopher named Kavka, I think it's called the toxin puzzle, where a billionaire tells you he'll give you a million dollars if you genuinely intend right now to drink a poison tomorrow. The poison won't kill you, it just makes you really sick for a day, and he pays you in the morning before you actually have to drink it. The catch is that once the money is in your account you have zero reason to drink the poison, so how could you intend to do it in the first place? I brought it to my friends at lunch and I feel like I solved it but the club president says I didn't, so I wanted to check with someone.

My thinking is that this is basically the same thing as Ulysses tying himself to the mast so he can hear the sirens without jumping in the water, you just need a commitment device. So before you ever talk to the billionaire you make a deal with your friend Jordan where you put a thousand bucks in escrow and Jordan keeps it unless you actually drink the poison. Now drinking it is worth it, so you can honestly intend it, you collect the million, you drink the poison, and yeah your sick for a day but your rich. Or even simpler, you could just tell yourself that your the kind of person who always follows through and that your pride is worth more than feeling gross for a day, and then the intention comes naturally.

So my question is, since the puzzle is really about willpower and self control, and the whole point is that you should set up your incentives ahead of time so your future self can't weasel out, why do philosophers act like its this deep paradox instead of just a lesson about precommitment? Is there a mistake somewhere in the setup, like maybe the billionaire couldn't actually tell what you intend so the whole thing is impossible, or is the real takeaway just that commitment devices are powerful and I should start using one on my phone at night?
