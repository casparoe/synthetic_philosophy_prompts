Some friends and I are doing a film night on Memento and I want to come at it through the cogito, but not in the lazy Matrix way. Here's what's bugging me. Descartes's move is supposed to hand you an indubitable self -- whatever else can be doubted, there's an I doing the doubting. But two things in the literature make that feel too fast. First, the worry (Lichtenberg's, and it resurfaces in a lot of later stuff) that all the cogito really earns you is that there is thinking going on, with the I smuggled in on top -- you're entitled to the process, not the person. Second, the reading where the cogito isn't an inference at all but a kind of performance: it's not that you deduce your existence from a premise, the saying of it is what does the work. Fine, but then what does it perform, and does a performance need a continuing performer?

That's where Leonard fits. He's got the stream of experience but no grip on a self that lasts, and he keeps rebuilding one out of tattoos, Polaroids and notes -- and then the film turns the screw and shows him editing that self on purpose. So is Memento a reductio of the cogito, a demonstration that Lichtenberg was right and there's only thinking without an I? Or does the movie actually need the I -- Leonard can't act at all without pretending there's a stable someone who wrote the notes? And I want the two moves kept apart: the certainty-of-a-moment claim and the persistence-over-time claim are different claims and I think peple conflate them.

Give me a reading that takes the film's plot and structure seriously (the backwards thing, the Sammy Jankis reveal, the way the tattoos are both memory and forgery) and doesn't just use it as an illustration of a thesis. Where does the film push back on the philosophy, and what does it add that the arguments alone don't? No reading list needed, just the argument.
