Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student, half in a philosophy of mind lab, half in an ML lab, and my officemate and I have been having basically the same argument for three months now — we both gesture at Dennett, we both think we won, and then we go back to our laptops. I want to see the thing actually argued out on paper.

Write me a dialogue between two grad students on opposite sides of this question: does the intentional stance actually earn its keep when the target is a large language model? I.e., is attributing beliefs, wants, intentions to a model a real explanatory move that buys you predictive grip, or is it a shorthand that's either redundant with the design stance or straight up misleading? One of them should be pro-stance, one against, both of them should be smart, and neither should get the dummy version of their own position. Real back-and-forth — they should respond to each other's actual points, concede small things and then come back at it, maybe interrupt. You can give them names and put them over coffee, I don't care, just keep it mostly about the argument.

Some things I definately want them to fight about, not just mention in passing:

- Whether the intentional stance predicts anything the design stance can't. Get concrete: generalization to prompts nobody trained on, behavior under fine-tuning or distribution shift, sycophancy, the model acting different when it's "told" it's being evaluated. If the pro-stance one claims a prediction, the other should press on whether a purely dispositional description ("given input X it emits Y") would have gotten you the same prediction for free, and the first one should push back on that.
- The self-report problem. Models are trained to say they don't have beliefs, feelings, or intentions. Is that a datum for the stance or against it? I keep thinking there's something genuinely weird here: if you take the stance seriously, you end up with a system whose best behavioral description includes it believing it has no beliefs. I want them to actually wrestle with whether that's a reductio or just... fine.
- Underdetermination. If two different belief-ascriptions fit the model's behavior equally well, is there a fact of the matter about which one it believes? One of them should say no and think that's close to fatal; the other should say that's true of people too and it never stopped us.
- Safety. One of them should point out that deception work and dangerous-capability work basically have to talk this way or you can't say anything at all. The other should point out that this is exactly the anthropomorphism that gets people to over-trust models and over-regulate them, and that "the model believes P and wants Q" smuggles in a whole agent that isn't there. Neither of them should get to win this one by just asserting it.

No citations, no reading list, I don't need anyone's name dropped. Around 1300 words.

And don't let it end on "well, I guess it depends what you mean by belief" — I want one of them to land a real blow and the other to have to deal with it. Then after the dialogue, in a short paragraph, tell me which side you'd actually bet on and why. Take a position, don't hand me the options.
