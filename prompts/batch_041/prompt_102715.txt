I'm working through Frederick Douglass's 1860 speech on whether the US Constitution is pro-slavery or anti-slavery, and there's a stretch near the start that I keep getting tangled up in. Here it is:

"Thus, for instance, the American Government and the American Constitution are spoken of in a manner which would naturally lead the hearer to believe that one is identical with the other; when the truth is, they are distinct in character as is a ship and a compass. The one may point right and the other steer wrong. A chart is one thing, the course of the vessel is another. The Constitution may be right, the Government is wrong. If the Government has been governed by mean, sordid, and wicked passions, it does not follow that the Constitution is mean, sordid, and wicked."

And a bit later:

"Again, it should be borne in mind that the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States. It should also be borne in mind that the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution."

I think I follow the broad move -- he wants to split the document off from the government so he can say the Constitution itself is clean even though the country has been run as a slaveholding one -- but the ship-and-compass analogy doesn't really land for me. A compass pointing right while the ship steers wrong, sure, but isn't the whole point of a constitution how it gets read and steered? And the second passage muddles me more, because he says only the plain text counts and not what the framers secretly intended, but then he also says we should respect the framers' intentions so far as they're plainly stated in the text. Isn't that just letting intentions sneak back in through the window? He also makes a big point of the convention meeting behind closed doors and the debates not being published for decades -- I genuinely can't tell if that's meant to help his case or the Garrisonians who think the thing is a slaveholding instrument.

Can you walk me through what he's actually arguing here, and whether it holds together? I'm not after the period history so much as the logic of the passage itself -- what work the ship/compass analogy is doing, and whether the textualist move is consistent or whether it's quietly inconsistent. Also, please tell me how confident you are in your reading and point to the one thing you're least sure about.
