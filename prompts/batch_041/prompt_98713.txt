And here's what this student wrote:

"Dennett's intentional stance is the strategy of predicting what a system will do by treating it as an agent that has beliefs and desires and that acts rationally to get what it wants. You don't need to know anything about the insides. If the strategy predicts well, that's the whole justification, and Dennett is happy to apply it to things nobody thinks are conscious, like thermostats and chess programs. Harbor fits: when the engineers say 'it thinks this customer is about to churn,' they aren't making a mistake, so long as treating Harbor that way predicts which tickets it escalates better than anything else on offer.

The stance is predictive, not introspective, and it's a last resort in Dennett's scheme: you only adopt it once the physical and the design stances have failed to predict. With Harbor you can usually stay at the design stance — retrieval, threshold, template — and only reach for belief-talk when the mechanical description gets too unwieldy.

On original vs. derived intentionality, which Dennett works with: Harbor's states are derived through and through. Their content comes from us — the engineers who chose the retriever, labelled the tickets, wrote the prompt. Nothing in the system has original intentionality, so we can use belief-talk about it without committing ourselves to it having a mind.

That's why Dennett's fictionalism is the right label here. He doesn't think there's a fact of the matter about whether Harbor believes anything; 'Harbor believes the ticket is urgent' is true if it predicts well, and in this domain that's all truth comes to. Hence no embarrassment in saying a thermostat believes its surroundings are too warm.

Where I'd push back on the question is here. If treating Harbor as believing the ticket is urgent reliably gets the escalation decision right, then Harbor must have an internal state that stands in for the ticket's urgency and that can be wrong about it — otherwise the predictions wouldn't keep working. So (i) does license (ii), at least in Harbor's case."
