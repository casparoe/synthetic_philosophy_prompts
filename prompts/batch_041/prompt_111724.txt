My uncle Ray has been finding water with a forked stick for going on fifty years. No theory, gets cranky if you ask him how it works, and he's right often enough that three different farmers in the parish wont dig a well anywhere he hasnt walked first. My dad is a hydrogeologist and it drives him up the wall — not because Ray is wrong, but because Ray cant say why he's right, and dad reckons thats just luck wearing a good coat.

I want a poem to read at Ray's 70th. 16 to 24 lines, free verse is fine, I dont need a form with a name. But I want the shape of it to do the arguing for me. Pick a short refrain, five or six words, and bring it back three or four times over the poem — except let it come back a little different every time: a word swapped, the tense slid, a clause dropped, so it never sits still long enough to be the same line twice. The first time it appears is the true version. Then in the last stanza bring it back exactly as it was that first time, word for word, and end the poem on it, sitting there, not moving. That is the whole thing I am after: a true belief that nobody has tied down wanders off, and the account is the rope. I'm the one speaking, not Ray — he would never talk like this — so let it be a nephew half persuaded by his dad and half not.

Please dont use the words justified true belief, epistemic, or propositional, and dont explain the poem inside the poem. If someone has read the Meno they should feel those statues that wont stay put on their pedestals without ever being told thats what they are. If they havent, it should still be a decent poem about my uncle and a stick.
