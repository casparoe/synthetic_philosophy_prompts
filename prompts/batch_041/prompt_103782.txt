I am a final-year undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar on the epistemology of assertion, and the assignment I have been set is unusual: rather than an essay, I am to submit a sustained piece of parody. I would like your help drafting it.

The piece should be a chapter from the employee handbook of an invented firm — let us call its department the Office of Assertion Compliance — which has adopted, as a matter of workplace policy, the thesis that one may assert only what one knows. It should be written in the flat, unctuous register of a genuine compliance manual, organised as a set of numbered rules with brief commentary, and run to roughly 750 words.

I want the comedy to come from the epistemology itself, so that my professor — who is, I should say, a committed defender of the knowledge-first programme — laughs rather than winces. With that in mind, the manual should:

- present the knowledge norm as settled policy while allowing its rivals to survive only as embarrassing residues: the truth norm as an old rule still honoured in the marketing department, the belief norm as a concession to the sales floor, the justification norm as the working position of anyone who has ever had to file an expenses claim;
- turn on the fact that the Office can audit an employee's evidence and sincerity but not, in any operational sense, their knowledge, so that its safe-harbour provision for statements prefaced by 'I believe' or 'I think' does not do what the manual believes it does — the reader should watch the regime quietly collapse into one of the norms it claims to have superseded;
- address the difference between asserting something and merely implicating it, since the manual is obliged to forbid the second while insisting that it regulates only the first;
- include a grievance procedure that must adjudicate a complaint of the form 'I was disciplined for saying that the shipment had arrived while admitting that I did not know that it had', and a second procedure for employees who merely repeat what they have been told by someone else and so cannot, by the manual's own lights, know it themselves;
- never state the objections to the knowledge norm outright, but leave a reader who knows the literature able to reconstruct them.

Please keep it deadpan throughout — no winking, no breaking frame to explain the joke — and make sure it remains legible to someone who has never opened a book of epistemology.
