I sit on my faculty's research integrity committee, and at next week's meeting we must dispose of a case that has split us down the middle. A doctoral researcher in a theoretical group contends that the central move in a paper published by their supervisor — a reframing of an old problem that suddenly made it tractable — was first proposed by the student, in a whiteboard conversation and then in a shared draft. The supervisor reworked the idea, proved the results with two other co-authors, and credited the student only in the acknowledgements. The supervisor's defence is that ideas are not property, that the student's sketch was one input among several, and that what the paper actually contributes is the articulation and the proofs, which the supervisor supplied. The student wants authorship, or failing that a formal note of conceptual contribution attached to the published record.

A friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about this and got nowhere. Her line was that what the student lost was not a thing but an opportunity — that the currency of scholarship is priority, not ownership — and that a committee which confuses the two will either invent property rights over thoughts or leave real injuries without a remedy. My worry is the opposite: that if a remark in conversation can ground an entitlement, nobody will think out loud again, and the seminar and the corridor, where most ideas are actually made, will go quiet.

Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete disposition — including what, if anything, the supervisor must do, and by when — and give the committee reasons it can act on. Engage the supervisor's position seriously rather than dismissing it as self-interest; say plainly what is strongest in it and why it should or should not carry the day. Say also how the rule you propose would bear on the ordinary case, where an idea is genuinely in the air and two people reach it independently, since I suspect the committee will want to know that before it signs. Where the decision turns on contested facts, tell me what further finding we must make first. Keep it short enough to be read aloud at the meeting.
