My reading group just did Weil's little essay about abolishing political parties, and the loudest person in the room keeps insisting that a party is basically a church. His list: a creed you have to profess, a priesthood, converts, missionaries, excommunications, and the fact that you can't really be a member in good faith while disagreeing with the doctrine — you can't call yourself a Catholic and deny the resurrection. His conclusion is that Weil's real target is creedal politics rather than the party form as such: swap the dogma for a modest set of policies and all her worries about conformity and manufactured passion evaporate, and her proposal that candidates stand as individuals with no party label on the ballot is just nostalgia for a world of independent gentlemen. I think he's wrong but I can't say why, and I'd rather have the arguement done properly than keep waving at it. Can you audit the comparison: give me the correspondence table first (what in a party plays the role of the creed, the priest, the congregation, the heretic, the schism), then say which mappings actually hold and where the picture breaks down, and seperate the breaks that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just noise. What does the church picture quietly smuggle in that a party hasn't got — a soteriology, a hierarchy, a claim on the whole of life, the idea that disagreement is sin? Weil's own line, as I read her, is that the trouble lies in the party as a form of organisation, since its business is to generate a shared passion and get its members to think as one; so I especially want to know whether the church picture supports that claim or undercuts it, because a church seems to be defined by its creed in a way a party isn't, and I suspect the analogy may be working against him. If it turns out to be a bad analogy, tell me what a better one would be. British English throughout please, and no reading lists or references — I just want the analysis.
