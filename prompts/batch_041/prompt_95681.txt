I am planning a session for a cross-faculty reading group I convene — a dozen people, mostly philosophers and historians, with two economists who will keep trying to jump ahead to the axioms — on the Ellsberg choice problem and ambiguity aversion. I do not want the explanation itself; I want the teaching plan. Seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, no formal decision theory presupposed, though I ought to be able to lean on the economists towards the end. Set out the order in which the components must come and why each depends on the one before: my instinct is that the audience needs a firm grip on the ordinary known-odds case, and a clear distinction between not knowing a composition and having no basis at all for a probability, before the ambiguous urn can be posed as a choice rather than a riddle; then the awkward step at which the two preferred bets turn out to be jointly unrepresentable by any single subjective probability, so that the audience must choose between convicting the chooser of irrationality and convicting the standard model of incompleteness; and only then the normative question of whether the aversion is a mistake. Say where in that sequence each of these belongs, whether my instinct about the order is right, and whether I should build the comparison as two urns or as the single urn whose four bets expose a tension with the sure-thing principle.

For the carrying example I had thought of building the whole session on a device of my own devising. A fair coin is tossed behind a screen; on heads the urn is filled with thirty red and sixty black balls, on tails with sixty red and thirty black. The audience bets on red, can see that the bet is a fifty-fifty one, and is comfortable. I then remove the coin stage and set the identical urn before them with the composition merely unknown, and their comfort evaporates. Should that carry the idea, or does it smuggle in precisely what is in dispute — the assumption that any ambiguity can be integrated away into a known chance, so that the audience learns to treat the second case as a defective version of the first rather than as a distinct situation? If it will not carry it, tell me what example should, and specify what it must have and must lack in order to do the work; if it will, tell me how to sequence the session around it so that the two-stage urn functions as a diagnostic rather than a giveaway.

Finally, map the misconceptions for me. I expect the conflation of ambiguity aversion with ordinary risk aversion, the slide from having no basis for a probability to assuming fifty-fifty, the reading of the paradox as a contradiction rather than an incompleteness result, and the conviction that preferring the known urn is plainly irrational because a book can be made against it — usually advanced without noticing that the same argument would indict risk aversion as such. Tell me where in the sequence each is most likely to form and at which point I should head it off. Then give me the single question I should put at the end that would show whether the idea has landed rather than merely been recognised — something requiring them to construct a preference pattern, or to state what someone who prefers the ambiguous urn in the loss domain has thereby committed themselves to — and tell me what I should openly leave out at this level: the axiomatic repairs (non-additive capacities, sets of priors), the accounts that explain the aversion by comparison with other people's ignorance, and the experimental literature. I would rather name those as omissions than have half the room suppose I have never heard of them.
