I'm a backend engineer — distributed systems, queues and consensus protocols, no philosophy training at all — and I've been reading some philosophy about "shared intention" and doing things together on my commute, and honestly I think the whole thing is overblown. My view: when two of us intend to do something together, that's just each of us having our own intention to do our own part, plus each of us knowing the other has theirs, plus something that keeps it from falling apart — a commitment, a habit, a reputational incentive, whatever. It's a handshake protocol. The "we-intention" isn't a seperate ingredient in the world, it's a handy way of talking about a bundle of individual states plus mutual knowledge. Same for "joint commitment": that's just each of us being disposed to stick to the plan and to be annoyed if the other one bails, which is exactly what you'd expect from a repeated game with reputations.

What I want is the case against this, sorted by how much it should actually move me. Concretely: what can joint action do that individual intentions plus mutual knowledge (plus some incentive structure) can't? Where does my protocol analogy break? Things I'm suspicious of, and I'd like you to take them seriously rather than wave them away:

- If the story really needs mutual knowledge, that's a strong requirement. I know the two-generals problem — acks alone never get you certainty that the ack got through, so the regress never bottoms out. Does joint action actually survive when nobody can be sure everyone is on board, or does the philosophical story just quietly assume perfect communication?
- Is there a normativity leftover? If we've jointly committed to something and I change my mind and bail, you think you're entitled to be upset with me — not merely that I made your life harder. Can that entitlement be derived from my individual intentions plus our incentives, or does it have to be smuggled in as a primitive?
- Cases where the "we" seems to do work no individual state does: two people carrying a table, a quartet improvising, a crowd moving as one, a couple who've been together so long they don't plan anything out loud. And the reverse direction: stuff that looks like individual intentions plus knowledge but where you'd want to say there's no joint action at all — two of us walking to the same place by coincidence, or me following you without knowing where we're going.
- Does any of this turn on actual findings, or is it all intuition-pumping? If it's the latter, just say so.

And be straight with me about the meta-question: is my reduction even the kind of claim that can be wrong? If it can absorb every counterexample by tacking on one more belief or one more disposition, then it's unfalsifiable and I should stop pretending it's an empirical thesis about what exists. If it can't absorb everything, tell me which case it can't. I don't know the literature, so skip the names — describe the positions in plain English. Also, if my protocol analogy is doing work it can't support, I'd rather know that than get a polite "interesting parallel."
