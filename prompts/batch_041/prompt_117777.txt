I've been going to a reading group at the library since I retired (forty years in industrial chemistry, so I'm about as far from a philosopher as you can get, though I'm enjoying it enormously), and last month someone forwarded me a long essay on just war theory — an excerpt from a book, I think, or maybe a magazine piece, I've lost track of where it came from — and I'd like to know how much of it I should trust. The gist was this: just war theory is built on the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants, which holds that in a war there is no morally right side — the two armies are on the same footing, so the German soldier and the Soviet soldier were each as justified as the other in shooting at each other, and neither of them was doing anything wrong, because whether the war should have been fought at all is a question for statesmen and not for the men in the trenches. That, the essay said, is why we don't prosecute soldiers for fighting in an aggressive war, only for atrocities. It went on to say that the doctrine depends on keeping the justice of going to war completely seperate from the justice of how you fight, and that a school of "revisionists" has now shown this to be false: if you are knowingly fighting for an unjust cause then you aren't defending yourself, you're the aggressor, so you're liable to be killed and you're doing something seriously wrong. The conclusion was that the classical doctrine is untenable, and since the moral equality of combatants is the linchpin of the whole theory, the theory goes down with it.

My instinct is that the essay is basically right — it's hard for me to see how a man who volunteers to fight for a plainly unjust cause isn't doing something wrong, and the "everyone's equally justified" line has allways smelled to me like a truce between nations rather than a moral claim. But I've learned that my instincts are a poor guide in this field. Every time I think I've got hold of a distinction here (wrong versus blameworthy, permissible versus excused — I still can't keep them straight) it turns out to be doing far more work than I assumed. So please go through the essay's characterization of the doctrine and tell me what a careful just war theorist — not a revisionist, but not a hawk either — would accept as a fair summary, what they'd wince at as a popularizer's simplification, and what is simply a misstatement of what the doctrine says. I'm especially interested in whether the essay's conclusion follows from the parts that are accurate: if the revisionists are right that unjust combatants are doing something seriously wrong, does the classical theory really collapse, or does something of it survive the correction?

And at the end, please be candid about the limits of your own audit: which of your verdicts are you least confident about, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about whether some piece of the essay is a misrepresentation rather than a harmless simplification? I'd much rather have you flag your uncertainty than sound more decisive than the material allows.
