I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book, and I've written myself into a corner that I'm now pretty sure is a philosophy problem wearing a plot's clothes.

My protagonist is a forensic psychiatrist — very loosely based on a woman I sat next to at a residency dinner years ago — and she's a full Szasz person. The line she keeps saying, at least four times in the current draft, is "mental illness is a myth." And I have her do two things with it that my editor swears can't both be true. Scene one, she's on the stand arguing that her patient is fully responsible for what he did and shouldn't get anywhere near an insanity defense — it's a myth, he's not sick, he knew exactly what he was doing. Scene two, same character, later, trying to get that same man out of an involuntary hold — you can't keep a man locked up for a disease that doesn't exist. My editor read it and wrote in the margin, in caps, YOU'RE HAVING IT BOTH WAYS. Which, fair. But I have a nagging feeling the sentence is doing different work in the two scenes and that she's right in both, and I can't articulate why, which is a bad place to be when you're the one who has to write the next chapter.

So don't tell me what Szasz "really thought" — I don't care, my character isn't him and I'm not writing a dissertation, no citations please. What I need is the menu: every distinct thing that one sentence could actually be asserting, sorted from the boring to the wild, with your honest read on which of them are true, which are just restatements of something nobody disputes, and which are the kind of thing only a crank would say. Then tell me which reading makes my psychiatrist coherent and which reading makes her a hypocrite, because that's the whole question.

Please lay it out like this:

**Bottom line** — four or five sentences, up top. Is she contradicting herself or not, and under which reading of the sentence. I want to know where I stand before I read the rest.

**The menu** — this is the bulk, take your time, say 800–1000 words. Numbered list of the readings. For each one give it a short label, one sentence stating exactly what it asserts, a one-line verdict (trivial platitude / substantive and defensible / probably false / not even coherent), and a note on who tends to mean *this* one when they say it. If a reading is so vague it can't be judged true or false, say so and say why — I'd rather know a reading is mush than get a fake verdict on it.

**Which reading does which job** — two short paragraphs, one for the witness-stand scene and one for the release scene. For each, which readings actually support the move she's making, which readings quietly undercut it, and then the part I really want: whether any *single* reading supports both moves, and if so which one, and whether that reading is one a real person could hold without flinching.

**What I'm writing next** — one paragraph, your call. Given all that, what should she be made to mean on the page, and is there a line I can give her that commits her to one reading instead of wobbling between them.
