I retired a few years back after thirty-odd years building instruments for atmospheric remote sensing, and I've been trying to make up for lost time by reading philosophy — badly, probably, but enthusiastically. Last month a former colleague of mine, who went the other direction in life and now works on autonomous systems for a defense contractor, was holding forth at a reunion dinner about loitering munitions. His system keeps a human 'on the loop' rather than in it: a classifier flags a target, and the operator has something like three seconds to veto before the thing commits. Somebody at the table asked whether that's truely a human decision at that speed, and he waved it off with the old line, 'guns don't kill people, people kill people.' The algorithm, he said, is just a sight — a fancier rifle sight. I've been chewing on this for three weeks and I'm fairly sure the slogan is doing about four different jobs at once, but I can't get them seperated.

What I want from you is a menu, not a verdict on the slogan as a whole. It sounds like a truism, and I suspect most of its force comes from sliding between a reading that's trivially true and a reading that's doing real work — something like 'no artifact is ever a moral agent' versus 'for every harm an artifact causes there is some human decision that is its sufficient cause' versus 'moral responsibility for any harm can always be assigned, without remainder, to some person.' Are those the right cuts? What am I missing — and is there a reading about causation specifically, versus a reading about agency, versus a reading about who we should blame or regulate? For each reading, tell me whether it actually gets my colleague his conclusion, namely that the operator (or the commander, or the engineer who picked the training set) is the killer and so nothing morally novel is happening, or whether it only gets him there with extra premises he hasn't stated. The case I actually care about is the misfire: the classifier engages something it shouldn't have, and no human decision looks like the cause — only a training distribution and a confidence threshold. Which readings survive that case, and which quietly assume it away? I'd also like to know which reading people like him usually have in mind when they say it, since I don't think it's the one that's true.

Give it to me as a JSON object so I can paste it into my notes. Schema: the object has a key "readings" holding an array, where each element has "label" (a short name for the reading), "precise_claim" (the reading stated as a single precise sentence), "scope" (what it quantifies over — which artifacts, which harms, which human acts), "status" (exactly one of "trivial", "defensible", "contentious", "false"), "why" (one or two sentences of justification), "gets_him_his_conclusion" (exactly one of "yes", "no", "only with extra premises"), and "extra_premises_needed" (a list of strings, empty if none).
