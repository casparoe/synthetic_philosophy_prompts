I am a retired experimental physicist who, since laying down the lab notebook, has been reading philosophy in the evenings — slowly, and with the humility of a man who spent forty years trusting instruments more than arguments. Next month I am to give the after-dinner speech at a small interdisciplinary gathering marking the retirement of my oldest friend and former collaborator. He began in condensed-matter theory, spent two decades as a quantitative analyst at a fund, and has ended his working life as an autodidact in the philosophy of economics. The room will be split almost exactly in half. One half consists of economists, together with a few philosophers of science, who will know the no-trade theorem intimately and will wince at any clumsy statement of it — the result that agents who share a common prior, are commonly known to be rational, and begin from a Pareto-efficient allocation cannot generate trade merely by acquiring private information, so that every observed exchange must be paid for out of something else: differing priors, differing risk tolerances, or motives that are not epistemic at all. The other half are family, neighbours, and a handful of physicists who have never heard of any of this and for whom an unexplained theorem would be an ordeal rather than a gift.

My friend had a private way of putting the result that I want to use as the spine of the speech. He treated informational trade as a conserved quantity — something that cannot be conjured from nothing, a conservation law for markets — and insisted that any actual trade be audited for the 'motive term' that paid for it. What I want the speech to do, without ever becoming a lecture, is turn that idea on our friendship. We agreed, as far as I can tell, about almost everything that mattered, and argued about nearly all of it for forty years; on his own account of markets, that lifelong disagreement cannot have been an exchange of information, since we held the same priors and each took the other to be rational, and so it must have been financed by the motive term. I want the speech to arrive at the thought that the argument was the point and not a defect — affectionately, and without pretending that he ever won anything, or that I did.

Please write it as a speech of roughly six to eight minutes, in a register that a physicist can deliver and a non-specialist can follow: introduce the theorem by analogy with conservation of energy, cash the analogy out plainly, and anchor it in one concrete occasion — a dinner, say, in the late 1990s, when he tried to explain a bond arbitrage to me across a table and I kept asking where the energy came from. Do not quote from any text, real or invented, and do not lean on the names of the people who proved the theorem; if a claim about markets or about our friendship cannot be made without a citation, make it without one or leave it out. And be committed: take a definite position, and defend it within the speech itself, on whether his conservation-law reading of the no-trade theorem is a genuine insight or a physicist's metaphor that flatters a result it does not quite fit — a theorem about what equilibrium permits is not obviously a law about what dynamics conserve, and I would rather the speech take a side than politely entertain both. End with three or four lines, clearly set apart from the speech, telling me where it takes liberties with the theorem or leans on a contested reading of it, so that I know what I am vouching for when I stand up.
