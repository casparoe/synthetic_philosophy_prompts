My friend group has a standing Sunday-night argument club — someone picks a topic, someone else brings a bottle, and we see who can annoy whom the longest. Last week the topic was what counts as alive, and it got alot more heated than I expected: two of them, Mira and Dan, went at it for an hour over viruses, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, so I said I'd get an outside opinion. (1) Dan opened: a virus isn't alive, full stop — outside a host it's inert, it doesn't eat, doesn't burn energy, doesn't do anything a growing crystal doesn't do; it's a chemical with a delivery mechanism, and 'life' has to mean an ongoing self-maintaining process, not a structure that happens to have been built by one. (2) Mira said that can't be right, because a seed, a spore, or a tardigrade dried out in a drawer is also completely inert — no metabolism, no energy use, sometimes for decades — and nobody says it stopped being alive; what matters is belonging to a lineage that grows, copies itself and changes, and viruses do that as well as anything. (3) Dan came back: 'capacity' is doing all the work there — the spore has the machinery on board, just switched off, while the virus has to borrow an entire cell. And if 'reproduces and evolves' is the test, then self-copying code that gets selected counts, and so does a crystal with heritable defects; meanwhile reproduction can't be necessary either, since a mule and a sterile worker ant are alive and never reproduce. (4) Mira: mules and worker ants are alive because they're parts of lineages that reproduce — same as a virus strain, which has been replicating for millions of years — and anyway some microbiologists say the living form of a virus is the infected cell, with the particle being more like a spore; she'd happily grant that a self-replicating program is 'alive' in a thin sense and deny the crystal. (5) Dan's last shot: then the lineage test is either pure history or it's nothing, because take two chemically identical systems, one grown from a lineage and one assembled from scratch in a lab, and you'd have to say they differ in aliveness on the basis of where they came from, which is absurd — he thinks the whole thing is a verbal dispute about a word that works by family resemblance.

I don't need the usual tour of definitions of life; I've read those and they all feel like they pick a side in advance. What I want is someone to go through the actual exchange and tell me who's right, point by point. Skip the reading list and the citations, I'm not going to look anything up — I want the reasoning. Format: (1) 'Move by move' — each of the five moves, rated from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating; this should be the bulk of the answer. (2) 'Talking past each other' — brief, where they're using 'alive' in different senses or quietly answering different questions. (3) 'What would settle it' — what kind of discovery or conceptual move, if any, would actually break the tie. (4) 'Bottom line' — one paragraph on who's more right overall and what both of them are missing. Please don't just tell me they both have a point.
