I am an undergraduate in a seminar on Plato's Meno and its afterlife in epistemology, and I have been asked to lead one of our weekly sessions rather than to present a paper. The group is about eighteen students, the session is seventy-five minutes, and my brief is to design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of what we have been calling the Meno problem: the question of why knowledge should be worth more than a true belief, given that when it comes to action, a lucky true belief gets you down the road to Larissa just as surely as knowledge does. I would like them to run into this tension as something felt, before anyone names it.

Please design the activity. I think it needs to have two phases that the participants do not see coming. In the first phase, two participants or two teams should be placed in a position where one of them knows the relevant thing, in the sense that they can derive it, explain it, and re-establish it under questioning, while the other holds the same true answer without being able to give any account of it. The rules and the payoffs should be arranged so that in this first phase the two do equally well, so that the natural verdict is that there is no difference between them worth caring about. In the second phase, the same two positions should be perturbed so that the untethered true belief comes apart from the knowledge: the merely-true believer should lose the belief or be brought to doubt it under pressure, whether from a confident but misleading interlocutor, from a re-run of the case in a new guise, from a demand to justify, or from having to transmit the answer to a newcomer who cannot tell the lucky guesser from the reliable one, while the participant who has an account should be able to rebuild the same answer from scratch. The payoffs in this second phase should make the divergence matter.

I need rules, materials, a scoring or payoff scheme that does not require real money, a distribution of roles, and a timeline for the seventy-five minutes. Then a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the Meno's own answer, that true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus, which run away unless they are tied down, and that what ties them down is an account of why the thing is so. The debrief should also surface the objection the activity will naturally provoke: that a true belief which happened to be stable would be just as good, in which case the tethering answer may explain the fragility of true belief without yet explaining why knowledge is more valuable.

Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, since I am worried that the second phase will feel rigged, and be honest about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
