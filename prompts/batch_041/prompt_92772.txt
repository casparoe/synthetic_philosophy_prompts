I serve on the grantmaking committee of a small private foundation that supports conceptual and technical work on AI safety, and in a few weeks we have to commit our next three-year budget — enough to fund three or four research programs and no more — which has forced the question of which lines of work genuinely deserve the money. What makes the discussion hard is that my fellow trustees keep reaching for the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax as though those two ideas settle the matter. Someone observes that no lab will unilaterally absorb a safety tax while its competitors are racing, and the room immediately treats that as an argument for funding only work that lowers the tax for everyone, or for abandoning technical work in favor of governance, or for concluding that the race is lost and funding nothing at all. I have a nagging sense that these slogans are being made to carry far more weight than they can bear, but I cannot yet say which precise claims they are entitled to and which they are not, and I would rather not spend the foundation's money on the strength of a phrase.

So what I want from you is not a survey of the field but a defended agenda: given a portfolio of this size and a horizon of about three years, which open questions about the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax matter most, and in what order should we take them up? Please make the ordering explicit and argue for it on stated criteria — how many other questions depend on a given one, how tractable it is for a small group working on concepts and theory rather than frontier models, how neglected it is, and whether settling it would actually change what any lab or regulator does — and then tell me which trade-offs between those criteria you are making, and what evidence over the next two or three years ought to move a question up or down the list. Among the candidates I would like you to place are: work that makes safety cheaper to implement (lowering the tax directly); work on monitoring and verification that makes defection from a common safety floor detectable; work on coordination and governance mechanisms that could bind competitors; and conceptual work on whether the competitive situation is really a race to the bottom or better modeled as a repeated game in which reputation and credible commitments do real work. Our program officer has also floated a distinction I find suggestive but unproven — between alignment taxes that are fixed, paid once to reach some compliance standard, and taxes that are marginal, a per-unit-of-capability drag that grows as models improve — and she claims the competitiveness constraint only truly bites on the marginal ones. I would like you to say whether that distinction is load-bearing for how we should allocate the money, or whether it collapses under pressure.

Finally, tell me plainly which of these questions you consider overrated, or answerable enough already that funding them would be a waste, and which single question you would bet the portfolio on if you could fund only one. Please write your answer in American English.
