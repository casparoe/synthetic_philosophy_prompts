Next month I'm giving a paper at a two-day workshop on skepticism and dreaming, and what I want back is a Q&A prep sheet, not a pep talk. The thesis: the butterfly passage that closes the second chapter of the Zhuangzi is not a skeptical argument. Zhuang Zhou wakes and cannot say whether he was Zhou dreaming of being a butterfly or is now a butterfly dreaming of being Zhou, and the chapter calls this the transformation of things. I argue the passage targets the stability of the first-person perspective rather than the reliability of the senses: the "I" is a role in a transformation, not a substance that survives it, so "which one am I really?" is malformed rather than merely unanswered. My reconstruction leans on the mid-century ordinary-language claim, associated with Malcolm's Dreaming and contested ever since, that dreams are not experiences at all — that nothing is undergone while asleep and that a dream is a belief formed on waking. If that claim holds, "am I dreaming now?" cannot even pose the Cartesian doubt, and the usual proto-Cartesian reading of the passage is anachronistic in a specific, diagnosable way.

The room is mostly analytic epistemologists who know the dreaming argument from Descartes and Moore and will go straight to closure-based skepticism, plus two historians of early Chinese thought who are allergic to reading the Zhuangzi as proto-Cartesian and will cite the chapter's other dream lines — the great awakening, everyone dreaming, Confucius and you — against me. I work in translation and cannot adjudicate character-level disputes. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging, phrased the way each faction would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Commit: tell me which reading you would stake the talk on, which objections I should concede outright, and whether any of them should make me change the thesis rather than defend it. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. American English.
