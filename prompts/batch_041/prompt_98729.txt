For the past few weeks my comparative-philosophy reading group has been circling the idea of karma as a theory of moral desert — the thought that the world is arranged so that agents eventually receive what they are owed. We keep catching on two snags. The first is the gap: if the payoff is proportional to what you did, and most of what anyone does goes unpaid within a single life, then either the debt falls due to someone else in a later life (which starts to look like vicarious punishment rather than desert) or the whole scheme is an unfalsifiable promissory note. The second is the owner: the traditions that take karma most seriously also tend to deny that there is any persisting self to carry the debt across lives, so it becomes unclear who, exactly, is supposed to deserve anything. Near the end of the last session, one of us floated a way out — stop picturing karma as a ledger, an account held in a person's name, and picture it instead as a law of nature, something closer to weather: deeds leave traces that reshape the conditions of the future, and those reshaped conditions happen to someone, but there is no bookkeeper and no account-holder. The catch, which nobody could get past, is that this seems to rescue the mechanism by giving up the desert, since weather does not owe anyone anything.

For our final meeting I would like to open with a poem that holds all of this together instead of settling it. Please write a pantoum of six quatrains. I am choosing the form on purpose: in a pantoum the lines return in a revolving pattern and the closing quatrain is assembled out of the opening lines, so the same utterance comes back wearing a new context — which is the exact shape of a deed returning into conditions it did not choose. I want the form to carry the argument, not merely decorate it. Two conditions. First, the repeating lines should not be anchored to a single persisting "I": if there is a first-person voice, let it be handed off, borrowed, or left unfilled as the stanzas turn, so that by the end a reader feels the return without a returner, the debt without an account-holder. Second, the poem should let both pictures breathe — the ledger reading, in which something really is owed and someone really is shortchanged, and the weather reading, in which the causal chain grinds on indifferently and no one is keeping score. Do not let the final line adjudicate between them; if the poem has to stop somewhere, let it stop on the gap itself. It should be accurate enough that someone who has wrestled with no-self and the delay between act and consequence recognizes the problem, and readable enough that someone who has not still gets a real poem out of it.
