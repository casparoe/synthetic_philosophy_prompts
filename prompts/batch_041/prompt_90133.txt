I am assembling a specification for population axiology — the theory of how good a state of affairs is when it contains some number of lives whose welfare can vary in level and in distribution — and I want the desiderata set out before I commit to any particular proposal. Please write that list: the cases the theory must get right, the puzzles it must dissolve or explain, the distinctions it must respect, and the structural results it must be consistent with, giving the consideration behind each requirement. Mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp, and identify any subset that looks jointly unsatisfiable. The impossibility, if there is one, is the part I care about most.

The specification must be able to handle at least the following, and you should add whatever else the cases force:

1. The familiar pressure toward enormous populations of barely-worth-living lives when welfare is summed or averaged, together with the mere-addition reasoning that generates it, and the symmetric pressure toward a small number of lives in extreme suffering that some ways of blocking the first pressure produce. State precisely what each case shows and which requirements they pull apart.

2. A case the standard treatments tend to skip: two populations with the same total welfare but very different distributions of that welfare across the length of each life. One consists of a vast number of very short lives that are intensely good; the other, of fewer lives that are long and mildly good. Say whether an adequate theory must be sensitive to duration as such, or only to welfare per life, and what turns on the answer.

3. A case in which welfare is partly positional: each person's level depends on how they compare with the others, so adding or removing people changes the welfare of those already present. Ask whether the theory must treat individual welfare as independent of population size, and whether it can.

4. A case involving welfare we cannot measure or predict — future people, or beings whose good we have poor access to — and what the theory owes us under that uncertainty.

5. The distinction between making someone worse off and failing to make them better off, and the distinction between a life worth living and a life worth creating. Say which requirements force the theory to keep these apart and which tempt it to collapse them.

Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that motivate them; I will do the scoring of candidate views myself. If a requirement is really just a prejudice of one school dressed up as a constraint, say so and say why. End with the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate theories, and a candid verdict on whether the full list can be met at all.
