Question: Hobbes treats geometry as the model of demonstrable knowledge. Explain why he thinks geometrical reasoning is demonstrable, and what follows from this for the status of (a) civil philosophy and (b) natural philosophy. Be specific about what, on Hobbes's view, makes a science demonstrable rather than merely probable.

Answer: "Hobbes thinks geometry is demonstrable because we construct its objects ourselves. When we draw a line or a circle we aren't discovering something that was already sitting there, we're making it, and because we made it we can know its causes — the demonstration is just unfolding the consequences of our own construction. He runs the same argument for civil philosophy: the commonwealth is something we make, so we can have demonstrative knowledge of it, which is why he thinks politics can be as certain as geometry even though it looks like a much messier subject. Natural philosophy is the opposite case, since we don't make natural bodies and so we only ever encounter their effects without getting at the causes from the inside. This is also why Hobbes insists that the axioms of geometry are self-evident and universally agreed on, and why his long fight with Wallis was really about whether those starting points could be doubted at all — Wallis wanted geometry to lean on algebraic symbols instead of the obviousness of the first principles. The overall upshot is that the sciences where we are the makers are the ones where genuine proof is possible, and everything else is conjecture."
