PROBLEM

An agent's beliefs about a finite sample space are represented by a credal set: a set C of probability distributions, understood as the agent's belief state. For any event A, define the lower probability as the infimum of P(A) over P in C and the upper probability as the supremum of P(A) over P in C. Say that events A and B are credally independent when P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) for every P in C.

Two coins are to be tossed once each. Coin 1 is fair: its probability of heads is 1/2 under every P in C. Coin 2 has an unknown bias, and the agent's credal set is C = {P_b : b in [1/4, 3/4]}, where under P_b the two tosses are independent and the probability that coin 2 lands heads is b. Let H1 be the event that coin 1 lands heads, H2 the event that coin 2 lands heads, and M the event that the two coins land the same way, that is, both heads or both tails.

(a) [4 points] Compute the lower and upper probabilities of the event that both coins land heads, with reasoning.

(b) [6 points] Compute the lower and upper probabilities of M, with reasoning.

(c) [3 points] The agent then learns that coin 2 is not fair, that is, that b is not 1/2, and learns nothing else. Recompute the four quantities from (a) and (b).

(d) [7 points] A classmate maintains that a credal set is a notational variant of a single distribution: since C is convex and contains the distribution P with b = 1/2, nothing is lost if the agent replaces C by that single distribution, equivalently by the uniform mixture of the distributions P_b over b in [1/4, 3/4], which on this sample space is the same distribution. Evaluate the classmate's claim. In doing so, say what the interval from (a) represents, whether the agent's uncertainty about an objective chance or the agent's imprecise degree of belief or something else, and what the credal-set representation commits the agent to.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) Under P_b the two tosses are independent, so P_b(H1 and H2) = P_b(H1) times P_b(H2) = b/2. As b ranges over the interval from 1/4 to 3/4, b/2 ranges over the interval from 1/8 to 3/8. Hence the lower probability is 1/8, attained at b = 1/4, and the upper probability is 3/8, attained at b = 3/4. The two events are credally independent, and here the product rule delivers the exact extremes because the probability of H1 is the same under every admissible distribution.

(b) M is the disjoint union of the event that both coins land heads and the event that both coins land tails. For disjoint events, the lower probability of the union is at least the sum of the lower probabilities, and the upper probability of the union is at most the sum of the upper probabilities. By the same computation as in (a), the probability under P_b that both coins land tails is one half times (1 minus b), which also ranges over the interval from 1/8 to 3/8, with the lower bound at b = 3/4 and the upper bound at b = 1/4. Therefore the lower probability of M is 1/8 + 1/8 = 1/4 and the upper probability of M is 3/8 + 3/8 = 3/4. So the agent's credence that the two coins match is imprecise, spanning the interval from 1/4 to 3/4. The width of the interval reflects how little the agent knows about coin 2.

(c) To learn that coin 2 is not fair is to learn that b is not 1/2. Under every admissible distribution P_b this has probability 1, so conditioning leaves the credal set unchanged and all four quantities are as before: the interval from 1/8 to 3/8 for the event that both coins land heads, and the interval from 1/4 to 3/4 for M. The excluded value b = 1/2 was interior to the range of b, so removing it does not move the extreme values.

(d) The classmate's claim is essentially right. Each admissible value of b yields a conditional probability for the event that both coins land heads, and the interval simply records the range of those values; it is a set of precise hypotheses, and a set of hypotheses is not itself a belief state. Since the agent has no grounds for weighting one admissible bias more heavily than another, the natural single distribution to use is the uniform mixture, which has marginal bias b = 1/2 and assigns probability 1/4 to the event that both coins land heads and probability 1/2 to M. Nothing of substance is lost: 1/4 lies inside the interval from 1/8 to 3/8, and 1/2 is the midpoint of the interval for M, so the mixture faithfully summarises the agent's state. The interval is therefore best read as the agent's uncertainty about the objective chance rather than as a distinct kind of credence.
