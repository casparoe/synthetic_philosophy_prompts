I am a graduate student working at the seam between philosophy of mind and applied ethics, and my department has spent the better part of a year consumed by an argument between two colleagues. The first, whom I will call the pan-sentientist, holds that if experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the physical world — as panpsychist and Russellian-monist views propose, on the grounds that physics characterizes matter only through its relational and dispositional structure while leaving its intrinsic nature unspecified — then it follows that there is something it is like to be a field, a particle, or a quantum of energy, and that the ethical consequences are very large: any physical process that realizes the relevant intrinsic states is a candidate site of morally considerable experience, so that our accelerators, our industrial chemistry, and perhaps even ordinary collisions are events we have reason to regard as possible bearers of harm on an almost unimaginable scale. The second, whom I will call the structuralist, does not deny that the metaphysics might be true; she denies that it carries the ethical implications claimed for it. Her position is that the inference from fundamental experience to morally relevant suffering requires at least two further premises that the pan-sentientist has not supplied — first, that fundamental experience is valenced, i.e. that there is a way it feels which can go better or worse for whatever has it, and second, that it belongs to something with enough unity or self-relation to constitute a subject capable of being harmed — and that since neither premise follows from the metaphysics of experience alone, panpsychism is ethically inert until those further claims are independently defended, which at present they are not. The two of them have long since stopped hearing each other, and the rest of us are tired of refereeing.

I would like to produce, as a neutral third party, the document that a real adversarial collaboration between them would have generated. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims — what both can sign without qualification about the metaphysics of experience, about the logical gap (if any) between experience and valence, about the combination problem and whether it threatens the existence of unified subjects, and about what our current evidence does and does not tell us. Then a table of the residual disagreements, with one row per disputed question and columns giving, for each side, a formulation of its own position that that side would actually accept as its own wording; the crux or sub-question on which the disagreement turns; the case, argument, or observation that both sides agree would genuinely bear on that crux; and each side's prediction about how it would come out, written down in advance so that neither can move the goalposts later. I am particularly interested in three things. First, whether the two of them are really disagreeing about the world or only about the meaning of words like suffering, harm, and subject, and if the latter, exactly which words and how the dispute would look once that is fixed. Second, whether the pan-sentientist's position, once its extra premises are made explicit, is best understood as a claim in metaphysics, in moral theory, or as a wager under deep uncertainty — and whether both sides can agree on which of these it is. Third, a concrete test case: take a single isolated particle held in a trap, and require each side to state what it predicts about that particle's moral status, what would count as evidence against its own prediction, and whether any observation we could in principle make would distinguish the two views. Where the collaboration can only produce a conditional agreement, say so plainly rather than papering over it.
