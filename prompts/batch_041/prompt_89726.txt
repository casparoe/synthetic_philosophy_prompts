I retired last year after thirty-odd years in geochemistry and have been filling the time with philosophy, mostly the free will stuff, which I somehow never got to as a student. I keep circling back to the Frankfurt cases — the ones where somebody would have been pushed into doing the thing if they hadn't already decided to do it on their own, so they couldn't have done otherwise, and yet we're meant to say they did it freely and are on the hook for it. I get the shape of the argument. What I want from you is a short story that does for that idea what the good science fiction does for other philosophical puzzles — makes me feel the bite of it instead of nodding at a diagram.

But I want the story built around a wrinkle I havent seen handled well, and I'd better describe it carefully, because the wrinkle is the whole point.

In the usual setup the intervener is a stranger. A neuroscientist, a demon, some foreign force waiting in the wings, and that's exactly what makes the case feel like a cheat to me: the guarantee comes from outside. So I want my protagonist to have installed the backstop herself, deliberatly, years earlier, precisely so that she would be unable to do otherwise. A commitment device, the way people set up automatic transfers so they can't talk themselves out of saving, except the thing she's guarding against is a decision she doesn't trust her future self to make. Then the night arrives when she does the thing — does it herself, wanting to, no forcing required — and the device sits there armed and never fires.

Second wrinkle, the one I actually want the story to earn: the device is old, and she no longer knows whether it would have worked. The mechanism may have degraded. She may have a nagging sense it was never reliable to begin with. Maybe there's a scene where a technician tells her it's been nonfunctional for a year. So the whole case flips depending on a fact she can't check. If it was armed and reliable, she had no alternative, and yet she seems responsible. If it was broken, she could have done otherwise, and the case collapses into an ordinary choice. I want that flip to feel vertiginous rather than clever.

Some things I'd like it to contain, but write it as fiction, don't lecture me:

A second character who designs these devices for a living and has a professional's view about what it even means to say the thing would have fired, and who is unimpressed by the protagonist's scruples.

At least one moment where she is doing the thing and the narration lingers on how she does it — the hesitation, the order of her movements, the fact that she might have done it sloppily or gracefully. I have a suspicion this is where the friends of freedom want to plant their flag, and I want to see whether it can carry weight when it's dramatized rather than asserted.

A scene afterwards where she learns, or half-learns, what was true about the device, and has to decide what to tell people about what she did.

No brain implants, no neuroscientists, no demons. Keep it domestic and plausible — a person with a mortgage and a car that needs work.

Don't resolve it for me. I don't want the story to tell me whether she was free. I want to be left unable to decide, but for the right reasons, feeling exactly where the two descriptions of what happened come apart. As a chemist I'm used to counterfactuals only having well-defined answers relative to a model, and I suspect that's what's itching me, so if the story can make that itch sharp, good. Length: whatever it needs, but if it runs past 1500 words I'll assume it's padding.
