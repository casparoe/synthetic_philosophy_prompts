I am a supervisory patent examiner at a national patent office, and I spent most of last Saturday arguing with an old friend who now teaches philosophy of language, about something I do roughly twenty times a week. Our statute instructs us to refuse a claim when the claimed invention would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art at the time the application was filed, and my office has a long internal apparatus for that judgment: the scope and content of the prior art, the differences between the prior art and the claim, the level of ordinary skill in the field, the presence or absence of a reason to combine references, evidence of unexpected results, and so on. My friend's suggestion, which I resisted for an hour and then began to half-accept, is that this apparatus misdescribes what my examiners are actually doing. Obviousness is a vague predicate: there are combinations that no competent examiner would hesitate over in either direction, and a wide middle in which the factors simply do not settle the matter. On the view she called supervaluationism, the honest description of that middle is not that we do not yet know the answer but that there is no answer until someone sharpens the predicate; a claim is determinately obvious only if it comes out obvious under every admissible way of making obviousness precise, determinately non-obvious only if it comes out non-obvious under every such way, and otherwise indeterminate, in which case my examiner's refusal is a stipulation about where this office draws a line rather than a discovery about the world. She then pressed the rival view she called epistemicism, on which there is a sharp fact of the matter in every one of those middle cases and we are merely unable to know which side of it we are on, and she insisted that the two views, though they agree about all the evidence, have different practical consequences for how I train my examiners and how they should write up a refusal.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my examiners can actually run, in the order they should run it, when a case is close enough that they are tempted to write that the claim is obvious and move on. Fix the following constraints. My examiners are chemists, engineers and computer scientists, not philosophers, and they have between two and four hours per case; whatever you give me has to be usable by someone who has never heard of either of the views above, and it has to produce a written refusal that will survive review by a three-member appeal panel and, eventually, a court that will ask what our reasoning was. The procedure should say what question to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with a no, and — most importantly — where it must stop and hand the case to a person because the remaining question is a matter of institutional policy rather than of technical fact. It should be explicit about the difference between a case that is vague and a case in which we simply do not know the facts, since my examiners constantly confuse the two: a claim whose fate turns on an untranslated reference in a language nobody on my staff reads is not a borderline case, and the procedure should send it down a different path. It should also say what role precedent plays: as I understand the supervaluationist picture, an appeal panel decision does not discover where the line already was, it narrows the set of sharpenings that are now admissible, and I would like the procedure to make that mechanism visible rather than leaving it as folklore. And it should contain a short section, addressed to me rather than to my examiners, on what would change if the epistemicist were right instead — whether the procedure I am asking for would have to be rewritten, or merely relabelled.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show me the output, including the written form of the refusal where there is one. First, a claim that combines two known mechanical elements whose joint behaviour is predictable, where the prior art contains both elements but no reference suggests putting them together. Second, a claim to a numerical range that overlaps a range disclosed in the prior art only at its edge. Third, a claim to a software-implemented data structure whose analogue exists in an unrelated field, where the examiner's honest technical view is that the step is routine but no reference says so. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like, and I would rather you tell me which one and why than quietly soften the procedure to avoid it. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and mark plainly the points where it is doing nothing more than dressing up a judgment call as a test.
