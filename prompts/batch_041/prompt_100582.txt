My brother-in-law Dan is 52 and got diagnosed last year with a genetic thing that slowly eats away at the memory centers of his brain — his doctor says he'll probably lose most of his long-term memory within a decade, though physically he'll be fine. He's been offered a spot in a trial that, as far as I can follow it, doesnt cure anything but works around it: over about two years they do six surgeries, and each one swaps out a chunk of the affected tissue for a chip that does the same job. After the sixth he'd be, functionally, himself — same memories, same personality, same terrible jokes — just running partly on hardware. His wife is all for it, and honestly I think I'd sign up too.

The part that's making everyone squirmy is the fine print. Each surgery is only authorized if the person who wakes up from the previous one signs off on the next, which means at some point the thing consenting to be replaced is already partly the thing being replaced. And Dan found out from another patient in the trial that the chips don't just copy memories, they sort of re-save them, like a photo getting compressed every time you move it to a new phone. The other guy says his memories of his kids growing up feel more like summaries now than like being there. So Dan asked me, since I'm the one friend he trusts not to just tell him what he wants to hear: if he goes through with all six, is the guy at the end still him, or is it a very good copy his wife is going to spend the rest of her life with? And does the answer change depending on whether it's the chip doing the compressing versus him just getting older and forgetting things the normal way? I keep going in circles — I can feel that the "gradual" part is doing something sneaky to my gut reaction but I can't put my finger on exactly what, and I don't know how to give him a straight answer.
