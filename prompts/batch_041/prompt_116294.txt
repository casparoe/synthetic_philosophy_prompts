I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and I am grading the final examination for a graduate survey course in the philosophy of religion. The students have had two lectures on modal treatments of the ontological argument, including Gödel's; the question below was the last of four on the paper. I would like you to assess one student's answer and produce the comments I will return with it.

The question was:

Explain Gödel's modal ontological argument. In your answer, (i) define the property of being Godlike and explain the role played by the notion of a positive property; (ii) identify the modal principle or principles on which the derivation depends, and say what work each performs; (iii) state precisely what the argument concludes and what it does not establish; and (iv) identify what you take to be the most serious objection to the argument, and explain why the others are less serious.

The student wrote:

Gödel's argument is a formal version of the ontological argument, and it turns on the notion of a positive property. A property is positive when it is a perfection: roughly, when it is a good-making feature, so that a being which has it is morally better than one which lacks it. Gödel does not define positivity; he assumes it and lays down axioms for it. No property and its negation are both positive. If a property is positive, then any property it entails is positive. And a positive property is necessarily positive, so positivity is not a contingent matter. He then defines a being as Godlike if it has every positive property, assumes that Godlikeness is itself positive, and assumes that necessary existence, that is, existence in every possible world, is positive as well.

The derivation then runs as follows. Since Godlikeness is positive, a Godlike being is possible. Since a Godlike being has all positive properties, and necessary existence is positive, a Godlike being has necessary existence; it therefore exists in every possible world. Gödel works in the modal system S5, whose characteristic principle is that whatever is possible is actual, and it is this principle that licenses the passage from the possibility of a Godlike being to its existence. The conclusion is that God exists, and exists necessarily, and this follows from the axioms alone, without appeal to any particular religious tradition. The argument does not tell us which properties are positive, and so it does not by itself deliver the traditional list of divine attributes, nor does it show that God acts in history.

The most serious objection is the classical one due to Kant: the argument treats existence as a predicate, so that a being which exists is greater than one which does not, whereas existence is not a predicate, and no amount of modal machinery can convert a concept into an existing thing. This objection is fatal, since it shows that the assumption that necessary existence is positive is not a substantive claim about reality but a definition in disguise. A lesser objection, sometimes called the modal collapse, observes that Gödel's axioms have the consequence that whatever is true is necessarily true; but this is a technical curiosity about the axioms rather than a defect in the main line of the argument.
