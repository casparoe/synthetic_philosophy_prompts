I am putting together a prospectus for a one-semester, single-author research project in political philosophy, and I need you to draft the proposal. My half-formed idea is that the theory of sortition has been running two distinct ideas together under the single heading of a "fair lottery."

The first idea is what I will call lottery integrity: the selection is verifiably unmanipulated, in the sense that no one can self-select into or be screened out of the body on the basis of their views or traits, and the randomness is auditable after the fact. The second is distributional fairness: the composition of the selected body matches the population along the dimensions we care about. These can come apart. A lottery stratified by demographic quotas can score high on distributional fairness while someone has had to choose the strata and the quotas in advance, which is a point at which views can re-enter; an unstratified lottery can score high on integrity while producing, by chance, a body that is badly unrepresentative. I suspect that arguments for lottocratic legitimacy tend to lean tacitly on one property or the other without noticing the gap, and that the gap matters most for the design choices people actually face — whether to stratify, when to fix the strata relative to the draw, and how to make the draw auditable. A related idea I want to test is that a third property, the impossibility of candidacy (no one can run for the seat), may be doing more of the legitimating work than either of the first two, in which case the whole framing shifts.

Please turn this into the proposal document itself: a precise statement of the question, stated so that it has a determinate answer; why the existing positions on lottocratic legitimacy leave it open; the approach — what I would actually do, month by month over a single semester — and why a conceptual rather than empirical method is the right one here; what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether the project is too large or too small for one semester and where I should cut. I would also like the proposal to say what would be lost if the distinction between integrity and fairness turned out to collapse, so that a reader can see why the distinction is worth the effort. The final paper will be roughly 6,000 to 8,000 words, so scope the deliverable to that. Refer to positions and arguments as you need to, but do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I will supply those myself. Write in American English.
