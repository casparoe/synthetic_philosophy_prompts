Okay so this is a weird one but bear with me. I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy (ethics, mostly metaethics and value theory), and my oldest friend Priya is getting married next month to Sam. But because I'm apparently "the philosophy friend," a solid chunk of my cohort is coming to the wedding — like eight or nine grad students and two of my old advisors — and then the rest of the room is Priya's big Punjabi family on one side and Sam's very earnest Minnesota family on the other, plus a bunch of nurses and architects. Priya asked me to give the toast and her only instruction was "please, no 'two halves making a whole,' no 'you complete each other,' I will actually die." Which honestly is why I thought of G. E. Moore.

Here's my idea and I want you to just commit to it rather than hedging: in Principia Ethica Moore has this claim about what he calls organic wholes — that for certain wholes the value of the whole is not the sum of the values of its parts, and can be wildly greater or lesser than that sum, and that you can't get the value of the whole by adding up the parts, you have to intuit it as a whole. He also thinks the two greatest things we can even imagine are basically the pleasures of human affection and the enjoyment of beautiful things. So I want the toast to do real structural work with the anti-additive, organic-whole idea — the marriage as a whole whose value isn't the two of them added up — while staying totally clear of the "you complete each other" cliche Priya banned, because I think that cliche is actually the additive picture in disguise (two incomplete halves summing to a whole), and Moore's point is weirder and better than that. It has to definately land for the non-philosophers (her nani, the nurses) as genuine affection and not a seminar, but I don't want to water it down into nothing for the grad students either.

Please write the toast, roughly four minutes read aloud. Take a definite position on how Moore's organic-whole idea applies here and build the entire thing on it — I'd rather you be wrong in a specific, committed way than hedge and hand me three possible readings to choose from. No quotes or pretend-citations please (I can't look anything up at the reception anyway). At the very end, after the toast, give me a short private note — three or four sentences, clearly not part of the speech — telling me where you took a liberty with Moore or leaned on a contested reading of him, so I know what I'm defending if one of my advisors corners me at the bar.
