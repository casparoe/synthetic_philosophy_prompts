Early in my career as a physiologist I was taught, and for forty years taught in turn, that the body is a machine, that disease is a malfunction, and that health is the machine running within its specifications. Now retired and reading in the philosophy of medicine, I find that same picture carrying a far heavier load: it is what makes it seem obvious that health and disease can be characterized without appeal to values, as departures from species-typical functioning, with the specification quietly doing the work that a norm would otherwise have to do.

Please audit the analogy. Start with a table of correspondences — what plays the part of the machine, the specification, the designer, the manual, the malfunction. Then say where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the naturalist conclusion from those that are merely picturesque. I would especially like to know whether the biology I know best tells against the picture: phenotypes that vary with environment, so that "species-typical" names a reaction norm rather than a value, and whose reference class — which population, which age, laboratory or wild — is a choice rather than a discovery; fever, inflammation, and cough as regulated responses rather than breakdowns, the closer image being a machine whose thermostat has been deliberately reset; the health/disease boundary as a threshold on a continuum, so that a binary picture misdescribes it; and function in biology as a history of having worked well enough to be reproduced rather than a designer's specification — the difference between a selected-effect and a causal-role reading of function. The last point seems to me the crux: if the analogy draws its force from there being a specification, and specifications require someone who wanted something, then the analogy may be doing the naturalist's work for it rather than supporting it.

Tell me whether the naturalist conclusion survives the disanalogies, and whether a better analogy is available. State what would change your verdict and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, and do not restate the question.
