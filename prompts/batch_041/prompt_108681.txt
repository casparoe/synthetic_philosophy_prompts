Next month I am presenting to a mathematical-statistics seminar my thesis that a reasoner estimating whether an unknown computation will output 1 should adopt a presumption of independence: it should treat two quantities as independent unless it can derive a dependence between them from facts it already holds, where "derive" means a proof short enough to check. I claim this yields a precise, computable default that does not collapse into the arbitrariness of a maximum-entropy prior, and that it is the hidden principle behind why heuristic reasoning about opaque programs usually works. The room is mostly Bayesian statisticians who treat independence as a strong substantive modeling assumption rather than a default, plus two computability theorists who will find the phrase "short enough to check" suspicious, plus one philosopher of probability who will smell the principle of indifference. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, phrased the way each of these three would put it, ordered by how much damage each does, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest; tell me what to concede up front and what to hold, and name the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Write in American English.
