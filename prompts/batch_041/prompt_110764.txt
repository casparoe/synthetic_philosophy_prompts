I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Hume's essay Of Money, and we ended up disagreeing about something more basic than we realized: not about what Hume says, but about what his argument is even trying to establish. I would like help working through the passage itself.

The stretch of the essay we kept returning to runs roughly as follows. Hume begins by denying that money is wealth; it is only the instrument of commerce, its value resting on convention. He then claims that if a nation's stock of money were multiplied overnight, prices would simply rise in proportion and nothing real would change. But he immediately qualifies this in a way that seems to cut against it. The increase in money is not felt uniformly or at once. It enters through particular hands — he imagines gold and silver arriving through foreign trade and being spent by merchants and manufacturers — and while prices are still catching up, the people who receive the new money first find themselves with more purchasing power than before, and this, he says, quickens industry. Only once the money has circulated and prices have risen does the advantage disappear. He appears to conclude that an influx of money is favourable to industry precisely during the interval between its acquisition and the rise of prices, and not afterwards.

My friend says this is incoherent. If money is a veil, the interval cannot be a period of genuine real improvement; the early recipients gain at the expense of those who have not yet raised their prices, so the stimulus is a redistribution and nothing more, and Hume's talk of industry is either a confusion or a relapse into mercantilism. My own view is that Hume knows what he is doing and that the interval is doing real work, but I could not state the reconciliation cleanly, and the longer we argued the less sure I became.

What I want from you is an interpretation that makes the passage hang together, not a survey of commentary. Specifically: first, does the interval claim commit Hume to a genuine short-run increase in output or employment, or only to a redistribution among persons and classes, with the appearance of stimulus explained by the fact that the gainers spend more readily than the losers? Second, if it is only redistribution, why should it quicken industry at all — what must Hume be assuming about who receives the new money and about how different sorts of people spend it? Third, the essay seems to move between an analytic claim about money and a practical claim about what a statesman should wish for; are these separable, and does the interval argument survive when they are kept apart? Fourth, my friend proposed a further reading, that what Hume is really describing is a change in the composition of demand rather than its volume — that new money in the hands of merchants and manufacturers draws labour toward manufactures and away from subsistence production, so that industry is redirected rather than enlarged. Is that a defensible reading of the passage, or does it import a distinction Hume does not have?

I am also unsure how much the argument depends on the fact that the new money is spent by a particular kind of person, and whether the benefit is meant to accrue to those early recipients or to the nation in some sense that does not reduce to their gain. If you can tell me which of these questions the passage actually answers, and which ones I am reading into it, that would be more useful to me than a verdict on whether Hume was right.
