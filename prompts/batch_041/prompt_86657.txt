I teach a graduate seminar on idealized models of deliberation, and I want an exercise that makes students feel the distance between the HCH construction and anything one could actually run. Please write the "Subagent Handbook" of a research organization that has reorganized itself along those lines: a human at the root who decomposes each question into subquestions, delegates them to copies of the same organization, and recombines the answers. Aim for roughly 700 words of numbered clauses in the deadpan register of corporate policy.

The comedy has to come from the idealization rather than from generic office satire, so keep the machinery faithful. Include: the rule that no subagent may see another subagent's output, presented as an anti-collusion measure; the requirement that each subagent answer only the literal question it was asked, drawing no inference about the larger task it serves, together with what the handbook instructs when a decomposition is bad and no one in a position to notice can see that it is; the obligation to report honestly whether a task falls within one's competence, plus the audit meant to catch subagents that underreport; the dispute-resolution board, which is itself an HCH instance; the policy governing a subagent whose recursion never bottoms out; and the standing assurance that the human at the root would answer better than any of the copies if only the factoring were perfect.

Let the handbook's bureaucratic confidence do the work: it should never concede that it is describing something uninstantiable, even though every clause presupposes it. Include one provision whose logic is circular in a way that a reader who knows the construction will recognize at once, and one that a reader who has never heard of it will still find funny. Invent the organization; do not name or quote living researchers.
