I teach a film-and-philosophy unit and want to build a session around Peter Weir's Dead Poets Society (1989) and Hume's essay on the standard of taste. I have the plot and the major scenes in mind and don't need them recounted; what I need is a reading that holds the film and the essay together without flattening either into an illustration of the other.

The scene I keep returning to is the early classroom one in which Keating has the boys read aloud, and then tear out, the introduction to their poetry anthology — the piece that offers a pseudo-mathematical measure of a poem's greatness by plotting a poem's perfection against its importance and treating the enclosed area as the verdict. Keating calls the whole enterprise rubbish and has the page removed. My students always cheer, and I want to know whether the cheer is Humean.

So here is what I'd like from you.

First, state Hume's actual position as precisely as you can, and distinguish it from three things it is routinely confused with: thoroughgoing subjectivism or relativism; aesthetic realism, on which beauty is a mind-independent property of the object; and Kant's later claim that judgments of taste are subjective yet nonetheless claim universal assent. In doing this, set out the conditions Hume imposes on the qualified judge — delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and the good sense he ultimately demands — and explain the work done in the essay by his story of the two tasters of the hogshead of wine, who each detect a different defect and are both vindicated when a leathern thong and an iron key are found at the bottom.

Second, tell me whether Keating is a Humean. Does tearing out the introduction express a delicate sentiment, or does it express a prejudice — and if Hume's qualified judge must be free from prejudice and practiced in comparison, is Keating disqualified on Hume's own terms? Is the film's Keating in fact closer to a Romantic expressivist than to Hume, and does that matter for how the scene should be read?

Third, tell me what the film's own standard of taste actually is, and whether the film can state it without contradicting itself. The film plainly wants feeling to be authoritative — Todd's improvised poem, the boys' readings in the cave — yet it also wants to condemn certain tastes (the headmaster's, Neil's father's, the anthology's) as bad rather than merely different. That looks like the very tension Hume is charged with: a sentimentalism that quietly smuggles in a normative standard. Does the film resolve the tension, evade it, or reproduce it? (It is worth knowing that the film's handling of literary study drew criticism when it was released, which is itself a verdict about its taste.)

Fourth, tell me where the film resists the Humean reading and the honest critic has to concede as much. Two places I would press: the canon the Dead Poets Society presupposes, since the film leans on a set of poets it treats as unquestionably great, which is itself a claim about a standard of taste and about which works have stood the test of time; and Neil's suicide, which suggests that aesthetic education and the exercise of taste can be bound up with harm in a way Hume's disinterested, contemplative spectator seems not to contemplate. Does Hume's framework have the resources to say anything about that, or does the film expose a limit in it?

End by saying what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not — in particular what it dramatizes about the social embedding of taste, given that the prep school, the fathers, and the boys' class position make freedom from prejudice look like a social achievement rather than a private virtue — and what it gets wrong.

One further thing, since I will be teaching this. Separate what is genuinely settled among people who know Hume's essay from what is contested. I want to know which parts of my summary above specialists would agree on, and which are live disagreements — for instance whether Hume's standard is really normative or merely a report of what the best critics happen to approve, and whether his appeal to good sense makes him a covert rationalist about taste. Please don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
