I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy working on formal semantics, and I'm putting together the framing handout for the first meeting of a reading group on paradoxes. Before we start arguing for anyone's favored machinery, I want the specification on the table: what would an adequate theory of truth actually have to deliver? Please write that specification for me.

One thing before you begin. The fragment I care about is wider than the usual one, and I'd like you to treat it as the target rather than assimilating it to a textbook liar. Assume a language that contains, alongside the ordinary resources for talking about sentences, both a truth predicate and an operator expressing what a sentence ought to be believed. The two vocabularies can be combined freely. So the language contains at least a sentence that says of itself only that it is not true; a sentence that says of itself only that it ought not to be believed; and a pair of sentences each of which says of the other only that it is not true. It also contains mixed cases, such as a sentence asserting that the sentence about what ought to be believed is true. A theory has to have something to say about all of these, and about whether the second sort of sentence is even the kind of thing that can be true or false in the same sense as the first. I built these cases myself, so there is nothing to look up; the work is in saying what any theory must do about them.

Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it — a desideratum with no case behind it is just somebody's taste. Include the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the limitative results it cannot pretend away. Mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are the house rules of a particular camp. Then tell me whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which requirement has to give; if the list itself forces a choice, that is the result I most want from you. Finish by saying which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise popular theories, and name the family it excludes. Skip the scoring of individual views against the list — I'll run that exercise in the group — and you needn't attach author names to positions; describe them.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email. No headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables. Length is not a concern; I would rather have the reasoning than the compression.
