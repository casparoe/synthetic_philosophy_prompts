Two people I have dinner with regularly — one a human rights lawyer who spent several years taking statements for a truth commission, the other a historian of twentieth-century Latin America — got into an argument last month that I have been turning over ever since, and I would like you to referee it move by move rather than simply declare a winner. The lawyer opened by saying that the South African amnesty was a moral price that was never actually paid: the state got the truth it needed to legitimate the transition, perpetrators got immunity, and the reparations meant to balance the ledger were never delivered at the promised scale, so the bargain as implemented amounted to amnesty in exchange for nothing. The historian replied that this is an argument against the implementation rather than the design, because the amnesty was conditional — full disclosure, a political objective, proportionality between act and aim — and applicants who failed any condition were refused; under a thousand grants out of some seven thousand applications is not a blanket amnesty. The lawyer answered that conditionality is exactly what makes it worse, since it converts atrocity into a paperwork problem in which the state trades impunity for a narrative it can administer, while leaving the structural beneficiaries of the old order untouched. The historian said the relevant comparison is not the ideal trial but the actual alternative: a negotiated settlement in which the security forces could plausibly have demanded blanket immunity, or no settlement at all. Argentina, he added, shows that trials can be restarted once the political balance shifts — the early prosecutions of the junta leaders were narrowed by the Full Stop and Due Obedience laws under military pressure, and prosecutions reopened decades later after those laws were struck down and the pardons undone. The lawyer replied that "it can be revisited later" is not a feature of a design but a piece of luck, and that in the Argentine case the reopening depended on a generation of judges and activists outlasting the men who had made the amnesties; a theory of justice that relies on that is not a theory of justice at all. At that point the historian brought up Rwanda, saying that the gacaca courts — lay judges, confession-based sentence reductions, tens of thousands of cases moved through in a few years — are the clearest case where the alternative to an imperfect mechanism was an unmanageable backlog, and that the lawyer's standard would have required either releasing the accused or imprisoning them indefinitely without trial. The lawyer said that is a false dilemma and that gacaca also entrenched a one-sided account in which only one side's crimes were prosecutable, which is not an imperfect approximation of justice but a different thing wearing its name.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: rate each of the four or five distinct moves as clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Then say what each of them is missing, and whether one of them has the better overall position or whether the disagreement dissolves once the underlying questions are separated — for instance, whether the lawyer is making a claim about what a transition may permissibly do and the historian a claim about what a transition can realistically sustain, in which case a single verdict may be the wrong deliverable. I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I want the reasoning and the verdict. I would much rather you push back than be agreeable: if I have framed this so that the two of them are talking past each other in a way that makes a unified verdict impossible, or if one of the premises I have attributed to them is empirically shaky, say so plainly and explain what would have to be settled first, rather than adjudicating as posed. Please also be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your answer and identify the points where your verdict depends on contested empirical claims about stability and deterrence rather than on the conceptual issues.
