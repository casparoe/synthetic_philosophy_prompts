A long-running quarrel in the epistemology of disagreement has two camps: conciliationists, who hold that learning that an equally informed and equally competent thinker disagrees with you gives you reason to move your credence toward theirs, and steadfasters, who hold that in at least some such cases you may or should hold your ground. Each camp can recite the other's moves. I want the document the two camps could sign together, written as if they had run a proper adversarial collaboration and were publishing the results jointly.

Write that joint statement. Address it to the rest of us, not to each other. Open with the document itself: no preface, no note about your method, no restating of this brief. Its first sentence should be the joint bottom line — the single claim both camps would sign, stated flatly — and the numbered claims that follow should carry the argument for it. Do not hedge into a synthesis in which both sides turn out to be right in their own way; if the camps cannot agree, say so and say exactly where the joint document stops.

Include, in this order:

1. The agreed claims, numbered. Cover what the dispute is and is not about; the cases both accept, including at least one where both agree a disagreement gives no reason to move and one where both agree it does; what each side concedes about the other's strongest case; and the two or three claims actually doing work in the standoff rather than shared platitudes. Aim for formulations a partisan of either camp would read without flinching.

2. The residual disagreement, as a table with one row per point of disagreement. Columns: the question in neutral wording; the conciliationist answer, in wording a conciliationist would accept as theirs; the steadfast answer, same condition; and whether the row is a genuine disagreement or a merely verbal one. Say explicitly where the camps are only using "rational," "evidence," or "peer" in different senses, and what the dispute looks like once the senses are fixed.

3. The cruxes. For each, why settling it would move at least one camp; the case, argument, or result both camps agree bears on it; and what each camp predicts about it, written down in advance, such that each side could say that if it came out that way it would have to give something up. At least these three belong on the table:

- The relay. A and B are peers who disagree, 0.7 against 0.3. Before they ever speak, B has already moved to 0.45 by conciliating with a third peer, C. Now A and B exchange credences. What should A's new credence be, on each view? Does the answer depend on the order in which the conversations occurred, or on who spoke to whom first? If the resulting credence is path-dependent, is that an artifact of a correct account of evidence, or a reductio? Each side must state its prediction about what A should do, and its verdict on whether path-dependence is a problem for the other side or for its own.

- Self-application. This very disagreement is a peer disagreement. Does conciliationism, applied to itself, require conciliating toward steadfastness — and if so, is it self-undermining or merely modest? Does steadfastness, applied to itself, license each camp to ignore the other — and if so, is it a theory of rationality or a permission to stop thinking? Each side must say what its own view implies here, not what the other side's implies.

- Peerhood. Whether calling someone a peer is a descriptive fact about competence and evidence or a normative verdict about how much weight their opinion deserves. If it is normative, does the dispute become circular, since one settles whether to conciliate by settling whether the other is a peer, and settles whether the other is a peer by settling how much to weight them? Both camps should agree on a case that would settle whether peerhood can be fixed independently, and predict how it comes out.

4. The shared test. One empirical or formal result both camps would accept as evidence bearing on a crux, with each camp's prediction recorded. Then say what result would count against each camp's own view — not against the other camp's view, against its own.

5. A closing paragraph on whether any part of the standoff is merely verbal, and whether there is anything both camps agree would actually move the debate.

No citations, no reading list, and do not attribute either position to named authors: I want the positions themselves, in the two camps' own voices. Keep the whole document under about 1,400 words.
