I'm in a reading group that's working through a trade book on the epistemology of disagreement—the kind of popular survey that walks a general reader through a live debate—and I've reached the chapter on conciliationism and steadfastness, a dispute I've never studied formally. The author's summary of the whole thing runs roughly like this. Suppose two people who are equally well informed and equally rational—epistemic peers, in the jargon—disagree about some question. Conciliationism, the author says, holds that in such a case both parties must abandon their belief and suspend judgment, on the grounds that the disagreement itself proves one of them has made a mistake and, by hypothesis, neither can tell who. The steadfast view, by contrast, holds that the bare fact that someone disagrees with you is not evidence of anything at all, so you are entitled to keep the belief you already reasoned your way to. The real question, then, is whether we ought to be spineless in the face of disagreement, and the decisive objection is that conciliationism refutes itself: conciliationists themselves disagree about how to handle disagreement, so by their own lights they should give up conciliationism. In any case, the author concludes, the whole puzzle is a philosopher's fantasy, since actual people are never exactly equal in evidence and intelligence, and so the debate has nothing to say about the disagreements that genuinely divide us—politics, religion, and the rest.

Something about the chapter bothers me, though I can't quite locate it. My instinct is that the author is being unfair to conciliationism, but I also don't want to simply assume the position I find more flattering, and I certainly don't want to walk away thinking I understand a debate that I've only met through one partisan gloss. Could you audit the summary for me? Go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful conciliationist would accept as fair, which parts a careful defender of steadfastness would accept, and where the author has quietly done something else—conflated two claims the debate keeps apart, overstated a modest thesis, or presented a contested objection as a settled refutation. I'm especially curious whether the closing move holds up: does "real disagreements are never between exact peers" actually show that the debate is idle, or does it only show something about the simplified case the debate starts from?
