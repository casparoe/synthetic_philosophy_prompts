I teach a 90-minute session on decision theory to a mixed group of about eighteen graduate students in philosophy and economics, and this year I want to run it as a game rather than a lecture. The target is the argument family in which an innocuous-looking rule — take the option with the highest expected value — is turned against itself by a gamble with a vanishingly small chance of an astronomically large payoff. I want the students themselves to walk into the trap: they should spend the first stretch making a run of ordinary, sensible-looking choices, discover that the principle they used to justify those choices also commits them to a gamble they find absurd, and only at the very end hear the name for what they have done. Please design the activity for me. I need the rules, the materials (I have a projector and a printer; no real money, and the students will not have laptops), a payoff or scoring scheme that generates genuine stakes without currency, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes. I would like the mechanics to force a commitment of some kind — my instinct is that each student should have to write down, in one sentence, a policy they would be willing to apply to every lottery they are offered for the rest of the session, and that the game should then confront them with the lottery that their own policy, applied literally, tells them to take.

The debrief matters more to me than the game. Once the pinch has landed, I want to map what happened onto the moving parts of the puzzle: the claim that any fixed finite payoff can eventually swamp any probability, however small; the difference between refusing the absurd gamble because its probability is, for practical purposes, zero and refusing it because utility is bounded or because no finite good should outweigh a certainty; and the option of a rule that ranks certain outcomes in a separate, higher class altogether, so that no amount of the lower kind can trade against them. I would also like you to seed the final lottery with a prize that resists being assigned a number — something described in words rather than units — so that the group runs into the question of whether the whole puzzle depends on the payoff being commensurable in the first place.

Tell me, too, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: I expect at least one student to object that the offerer is simply lying and the probability is therefore zero, and I want to know in advance how to keep that objection from collapsing the activity before the interesting part arrives. Finally, be honest with me about which feature of the underlying argument the game cannot reproduce, so that I know what I will have to supply in my own voice.
