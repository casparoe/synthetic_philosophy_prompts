I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and I'm working on a peice about von Neumann's little book on the brain and the computer — the one he was writing at the end of his life and never finished, published posthumously in 1958. Everyone knows the crude version: neurons are like vacuum tubes, the brain is digital in one place and analog in another, moving on. But the half of the book that actually got me is the part where he's basically betting against precision. His brain is built out of slow, noisy, unreliable parts, so he reasons that reliability has to come out of sheer numbers and statistics — redundancy, averaging, coding schemes we might not be able to read even in principle — and he keeps circling the question of whether the brain has a memory organ at all the way a computer has memory, or whether storage and processing are smeared together in the same tissue. And, as he'd have been the first to note, the whole thing runs on about 20 watts.

What I want to put side by side in the piece is his brain and the brain as it shows up implicitly in the current AI scaling literature, where you've got a deterministic digital machine doing exact arithmetic, alot less redundancy than he assumed, a clean separation of storage from computation, and all the statistics pushed into training rather than into the hardware. Compare those two pictures for me. Which of his specific bets did the field end up taking, which did it quietly drop, and which of them is still live? In particular: he seems to think a system made of noisy low-precision components just can't be doing precise symbol manipulation, so it must be doing something statistical instead — yet modern models are the reverse, statistical in training and exact in execution, and they work fine. Does that refute his inference, or is he just describing the other end of a tradeoff that both kinds of machine sit somewhere on? And if you'd handed him backprop and a GPU cluster, would his statistical-reliability argument survive contact with a machine that got reliable by being exact and big instead of by being redundant and sloppy?

Then tell me who I should say was closer to right about what actually matters, and what's the one question his book asks that the scaling people still can't answer. I'd rather have a real argument here than a both-were-right-in-their-own-way thing.
