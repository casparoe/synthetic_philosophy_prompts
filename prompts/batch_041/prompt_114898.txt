I'm in a fortnightly reading group on explanation in the social sciences, and me and Priya — she's a philosopher of economics, very sharp, very stubborn — have been going back and fourth for about a year about whether sentences like 'the panic spread through the regional banks' or 'the bank was solvent but illiquid' do any real explanatory work, or whether they're just shorthand for a very long list of things that individual people did. Last week she turned up with an argument she says settles it, and I think it proves far too much. Here it is, in her numbering. (1) Only individual human beings exist and act; 'the market', 'the banking system' and 'confidence' name nothing over and above individuals and what they do. (2) A statement explains an event only if it cites a cause of that event, and the causes of events are the doings of individuals together with their upshots. (3) So any social explanation that appears to cite something supra-individual — the bank's insolvency, the spread of the panic, the collapse of confidence — is at best an abbreviation for a list of individual doings, and at worst a disguised appeal to a ghost. (4) An abbreviation that can't in principle be cashed out as individual doings isn't an explanation at all, it's a placeholder. (5) Try it on our case: Bank B was solvent at nine in the morning and finished by four, and what finished it was that every depositor knew every other depositor was pulling their money out. Spell that out and you either never finish the list, or you smuggle the macro-facts back in as you go. (6) Therefore the only explanation of the collapse is the list of individual withdrawals, and 'it was a run, and any bank with that balance sheet and no insurance would have gone the same way' adds nothing to it. (7) Therefore social science has no explanations of its own: it has summaries of psychology, and where it can't produce those, it has nothing.

I want to keep (1) and (2) — I really do think there are no group agents and no spooky collective minds, and I'm not willing to let 'the market decided' back in as a literal causal agent. But (6) and (7) I can't stomach. Two towns with identical depositors, one with deposit insurance and one without, don't fail in the same way, and the insurance doesn't work by changing anybody's psychology — it works by changing what it's rational for them to do, which looks like a structural fact doing causal work. Same with the run itself: the reason it's worth saying that it was a run rather than a case of mass irrationality is that it tells you what would have happened to any other bank in that position, and no list of what these particular people happened to do will tell you that. So I think something in the middle is doing the damage, and I half-suspect a slide between two senses of 'nothing over and above' — or maybe (2) is just false as stated, or maybe (5) is quietly an empirical claim about my patience rather than about explanation — but I can't tell which, and I dont know what I'd be buying if it turned out to be (2) that had to go.
