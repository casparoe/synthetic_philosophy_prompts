I have no background in philosophy, but I have been trying to think through a puzzle that I keep running into whenever I read popular science about consciousness, and I was recently arguing about it with a friend who studies neuroscience until we both got stuck. The puzzle is this: suppose conscious experiences are real but do no causal work — they are just a byproduct of what the brain is doing, like the whistle of a train rather than part of the engine. Then how is it that we can talk about them, report them, and argue about them? My reporting “I see a reddish patch” is itself a physical event, and if experiences are causally inert, then the experience cannot be what causes the report. So it seems like either the report is caused by the brain state instead of the experience, in which case we are not really reporting the experience, or the experience does cause the report, in which case it is not epiphenomenal. I later learned this is sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but I do not want a survey of the literature; I want to see the puzzle worked out as an actual conversation.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent people. One is an epiphenomenalist: she thinks conscious experiences are genuine features of the world, but they are causally inert; the brain state does all the work, and the experience is a kind of accompaniment. The other is a physicalist who thinks that experiences are not extra byproducts but just are certain brain states, and therefore they do cause the reports we make. Let them argue back and forth, responding to each other’s actual points rather than talking past each other. To make it concrete, have them discuss a case in which a neurosurgeon stimulates a patient’s brain and the patient sincerely says she feels a sharp pain in a foot that has been amputated, and a contrasting case in which a patient has a painful phantom limb but, because of a stroke, insists she feels nothing. The epiphenomenalist should try to explain what is happening in each case without letting the experience cause the report; the physicalist should press on whether that leaves the epiphenomenalist able to account for her own knowledge of her own experiences. Neither side should be a strawman, and neither should simply win by definition.

The dialogue should be self-contained: no citations, no references to actual books or papers, and no reading list. End with a final line beginning “Verdict:” that states, in one sentence, which of the two positions you think the dialogue shows to be better supported and why.
