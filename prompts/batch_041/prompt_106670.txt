OK so a bit of background: I spent about 35 years doing fisheries and lake ecology before I retired, and now that I've got the time I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars — political philosophy mostly, some decision theory. So I'm the guy who's watched a lot of real commons get wrecked and then read a lot of Ostrom late in life. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want a consistency audit before I say any of it out loud in seminar.

(1) Left to open access, a common-pool resource — a fishery, an aquifer, a pasture — will get overused and depleted, and the only dependable fixes are to privatize it or have some outside authority impose and enforce limits. (2) But I find Ostrom's fieldwork completely convincing: communities really do sometimes run their own fisheries and forests sustainably for generations, no privatization, no top-down state, just locally made rules, monitoring, and escalating sanctions. (3) I think the cleanest way to see this whole family of problems is as a failure of excludability, so the fix is to restore it — fence it, privatize it, or hand out tradable quotas (individual transferable quotas on fish, cap-and-trade on carbon). (4) Genuine public goods are different though — basic science, defense, lighthouses: markets will always underprovide them and the state just has to pay, because you can't exclude non-payers and my use doesn't use anything up. (5) Underneath all of it I'm a rational-choice guy: people maximize their own expected payoff, and good institutions work by changing payoffs, not by changing hearts. (6) And I'm allergic to the idea that the answer is a new environmental ethic — telling people to care more never seemed to do anything in the fisheries I worked on.
