I spent the first half of my life in the natural sciences and have only lately turned to philosophy, so I hope you will indulge a question that is, at bottom, a question about models. In what I have been reading on moral testimony, one comparison keeps surfacing: that moral testimony is to moral knowledge roughly as aesthetic testimony is to aesthetic judgement. Just as one cannot be told what is funny, or brought to see that something is beautiful by another person's say-so, so — the suggestion runs — one cannot genuinely come to know what is right on someone else's word; the thing must be judged for oneself, and a borrowed verdict is no substitute for a first-hand one. This comparison is not idle. It is made to carry an argument: that there is something defective or diminished about forming a moral belief on another's authority, that the person who defers lacks an understanding or a standing that the person who judges for herself enjoys, and hence that moral deference is at best a second-best expedient to be tolerated rather than trusted. Against this stands the more prosaic view that moral testimony transmits knowledge much as any other testimony does, and that the alleged defect is a piece of philosophical mystification.

I would like you to audit the analogy the way one audits a model in the physical sciences — not by asking whether it is elegant, but by asking what maps onto what, which relations it preserves and which it quietly discards, and whether the conclusion it was introduced to support still stands once the disanalogies are on the table. Please write the answer under the following sections, in this order:

1. **The Analogy and the Work It Is Asked to Do** — roughly 150 words, stating the comparison and the conclusion it is meant to license.

2. **Correspondence Table** — a table mapping each element of the aesthetic case (the object judged, the would-be testifier, the recipient, the act of judgement, the kind of warrant involved, the value of doing it oneself, and any others you think belong) onto its supposed counterpart in the moral case, with a column marking where an element has no real counterpart.

3. **Where the Mapping Holds** — roughly 250 words.

4. **Where It Breaks** — roughly 350 words, and here I ask you to do what a good modeller does: separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. In particular, I am unsure whether the aesthetic case turns on the content of the judgement, on the nature of the warrant, or merely on the fact that aesthetic verdicts are not action-guiding — and I would like you to tell me which of these, if any, the moral case genuinely shares.

5. **Whether the Conclusion Survives** — roughly 250 words, distinguishing the strong claim (deference is defective) from the weak one (deference is second-best), since the analogy may support one and not the other.

6. **An Alternative I Have Been Toying With** — roughly 250 words. It occurs to me that a closer analogue from my old trade might be this: relying on moral testimony is like using a measuring instrument that someone else calibrated, or extrapolating a curve fitted in one regime into a region where neither party has data. Assess this against the aesthetic analogy: does it preserve more of the relations that do the work, and where does it in turn break down?

7. **Verdict** — roughly 150 words, saying plainly whether the analogy can bear the weight put on it, and if not, what a better one would be.
