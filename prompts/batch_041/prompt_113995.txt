Assume the role of a philosopher of action and social ontology. Think carefully before answering; begin with a brief brainstorming inventory of the relevant agents, decisions, and levels of description, then give a disciplined analysis.

Analyze the 1986 Challenger launch decision as a case of collective intentionality and joint action. On the eve of the launch, engineers at Thiokol warned that the O-rings could fail in the cold; under pressure from NASA, Thiokol management reversed the recommendation, and the shuttle broke up shortly after liftoff. The basic facts are not in dispute; I want the conceptual mapping.

Who, if anyone, intended to launch? Was there a Bratman-style shared intention, a Searle-style collective intention, a Gilbert-style joint commitment, or a Tuomela-style we-intention? Did the engineers and managers form a joint action, or was the launch an aggregate outcome of individual decisions under organizational pressure? What does the case show about the difference between coordinated behavior and genuine joint intention? Where do the theories fail to capture the case, especially regarding responsibility when no single person intended the failure? Does the episode support or undermine the idea that organizations can be collective agents? End by saying what the case adds to the philosophical debate that the standard examples of two people walking together do not.
