Okay so this started as an argument with a friend of mine who does type design, and its been bugging me ever since. You know how every 'a' is an 'a'? Not in the trivial sense, in the sense that a double-storey a in a serif face, the single-storey a most people actually write by hand, the blackletter a, the Cyrillic а that is the same shape as a Latin a but is a different letter, the Greek α that we transliterate as an a and that isn't one, the Turkish dotless ı, the ɑ of the IPA — we call all of them the same letter, we do it without hesitating, and yet I cannot name a single feature they all share. Meanwhile a shape can be an 'a' in one font and, moved a few pixels, look like a 'c' or a 'd' in another; letters have split and merged under our feet (i and j, u and v, the long s that looked like an f, ß, æ which is a letter in some languages and a ligature in others, w which is two u's in a coat); and the handwriting people talk about allographs as if that settles something, when it seems to me it just renames the thing I'm asking about. My friend's view is that the letter just is the stroke — whatever a consistent motion of the pen puts down — and I can't tell whether that's obviously true or circular. And Hofstadter's group did that Letter Spirit thing back in the 90s where the program is handed a few letters of a brand-new alphabet in a consistent style and has to draw the rest of the alphabet in that same style, which seems to require it to already have both a sense of which letter it's looking at and a sense of the style, and I've never understood how those two come apart, or how a program gets the first one without begging the question. I also read Goodman years ago and retained that a notation is a scheme whose characters you can tell apart at a glance and that can't overlap, which is why a score counts as a notation and a sketch doesn't — on that picture my own scrawled 'a' should be as un-notational as a sketch, and yet we read it fine and would call it an 'a' even when it's ugly.

Here's what I actually want, and no reading list please, no cites, I want the spec. Write me the desiderata for a theory of letter identity: what any adequate account of what makes every a an a has to deliver. For each requirement, give me the case that motivates it — the actual letter, script, typeface, or historical episode that would be a counterexample if the account got it wrong — and I want the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse: letter vs. style, letter vs. glyph, letter vs. shape, being a letter of a script vs. being a token of one, and the gap between "this is an a" and "this is an a in this style" or "this is a bad a". Then the constraints that come from outside philosophy: from what type designers and printers actually do, from how children learn to write, from what OCR and image classifiers actually key on (I've read the vision models lean on texture over shape, which if true seems like it should matter here), from the plain fact that the same shape can be two different letters in two scripts.

Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's prejudice — I'd bet "there is a determinate fact of the matter for every shape" is one of the latter — and flag any subset that can't be jointly satisfied, because that's the part I most want; if the list can only be met by a prototype/radial-category story, or only by biting a bullet about vagueness, say so. Fix the standard however you like by the purpose: I want a spec good enough to tell me whether a machine could learn a font from a handful of examples — not copy it, learn the letter-sense and the style seperate. And end with one short paragraph scoring the stroke-trajectory view, the letters-are-just-arbitrary-social-conventions view, and the letters-are-abstract-objects-with-a-defining-skeleton view against the list, since I'm definately going to lose this argument otherwise. Keep the weight on the requirements though, I don't need a history of typography.
