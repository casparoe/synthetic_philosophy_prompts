So over the summer I read Franklin's autobiography and got wierdly into the part where he decides to go after moral perfection by drawing up a list of thirteen virtues, putting them in a little book with a grid, and giving each week to one virtue while he marks down his failures. I loved it, so I did the 2020s version: a habit tracker on my phone with a separate streak for each thing I want to fix about myself (don't snap at my girlfriend, don't procrastinate, actually call my mom, stop buying junk I don't need). I tick the boxes every night before bed.

Six weeks in and its a mess, but not in the way I expected. The thing is, the whole project runs on an analogy I've basically swallowed without noticing: becoming a better person is like keeping accounts. You have line items, you track debits and credits, faults are debts you clear, good days are credits, consistency makes the balance trend up, and if you just stay disciplined you grind your way down to zero, i.e. perfection. I think Franklin has this picture, and honestly every productivity app on earth sells it too.

Can you audit that analogy for me? I want the correspondences spelled out first — what plays the role of the ledger, the line items, the balance, the interest, the audit — and then where it breaks. Some breaks I think I'm already feeling: some debts don't clear when you pay them (I can log thirty good days after one bad night and my girlfriend still remembers the bad night), the virtues don't look like independent line items (being honest and being kind pull against each other, so it's not a clean spreadsheet), and I've caught myself optimizing for the checkmark instead of the thing — picking the easiest version of a task so I can tick the box. The funniest part of Franklin's own story is that the one virtue he could never keep was order, i.e. the meta-virtue of keeping the system itself running, which feels like it ought to tell us something.

So: which of these breaks actually matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support (that steady tracking equals steady moral improvement, and that you can in principle grind your way to something like perfection), and which are just noise? What does the accounting picture quietly smuggle in — say, the assumption that goodness is made of countable, independent units that sum up? And if the ledger picture fails, whats a better analogy for what's actually going on when someone tries to improve? Be concrete, don't just tell me it's a process.
