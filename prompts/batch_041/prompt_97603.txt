For the past year I have been running a small grant program on the theoretical foundations of mind and computation — twelve three-year fellowships, a budget that will not stretch to a thirteenth — and I have come to think that the most interesting cluster of problems I fund is the one nobody in the program has a name for. I would like your help deciding where the money should go.

The cluster is this. Natural selection reliably produces organisms whose parts look as though they were designed, without anyone having designed them and without the process itself having any inkling of what it is doing. A machine can execute a procedure and return the right answer while nothing in the machine represents the reasoning that the answer's form suggests. And systems trained by brute optimization now display capacities — translation, proof-sketching, code repair — that we would ordinarily take as evidence of understanding in a person, while it remains unclear whether anything in the system plays the role that understanding plays in us. In each case the product of reasoning appears where the process of reasoning seems absent, and in each case it is tempting to say that the reasoning has merely been relocated — into the environment, the training signal, the interpreter, the selection pressure — rather than eliminated.

I have a provisional distinction that I would like you to test rather than accept. Call the first kind of case a production inversion: the competence really is generated by a process that is itself non-comprehending, and the explanatory work is done by showing how. Call the second kind an attribution inversion: the competence is real, but its apparent intelligence lives in the descriptions we give of it, and the explanatory work is done by showing what the description adds. Almost everything I read runs the two together, and I suspect that some long-running disputes are really disputes about which kind of case is at issue.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey. Suppose the fellowships should be divided among the three or four questions in this area most worth three years of a good theorist's life. Which are they, and in what order? For each, tell me why it is upstream of the others — what further questions would be partly settled if this one were — how far it can be pursued by someone reasoning rather than running experiments, and how neglected it currently is relative to how much it matters. Where two questions trade off against each other — a tractable but shallow one against a deep but currently unanswerable one — say how you would break the tie, and say what would change your ranking: a result, a distinction, a failure of some program I have not anticipated.

I am especially interested in whether the regress has a bottom. If comprehension is itself to be explained by some non-comprehending process, and that process by another, is there a principled stopping point, or is the whole picture a stack of inversions all the way down — and if the latter, does anything follow for how we should treat the systems, the organisms, or the people whose understanding we are trying to explain?

Please defend the ordering on the merits. I will have to justify it to a board that includes two biologists and one lawyer, so tell me plainly which of these questions a non-specialist should be able to see the point of.
