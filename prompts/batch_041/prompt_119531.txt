I host a small, slightly over-serious philosophy of language podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending a revisionary reading of Grice. Her line, roughly stated, is that we have had the Cooperative Principle the wrong way round: it is not a norm that speakers tacitly obey and hearers tacitly assume, but a set of repair heuristics that hearers reach for only after a literal reading has failed, so that 'what is said' is an output of interpretation rather than a fixed input to it. She also wants to retire conventional implicature as a category, holding that words like 'but', 'even', and 'therefore' do ordinary at-issue work alongside presuppositional work, and she rejects the reflexive-intention analysis of speaker meaning as over-intellectualised, proposing instead that a speaker means whatever she can legitimately be held to have committed herself to. I find the last move genuinely interesting and probably wrong, but my real worry is procedural: I have read just enough pragmatics to be dangerous, and I am afraid that at every point where the going gets hard she will simply restate the heuristics line and wait for me to move on.

Please act as an expert in the Gricean tradition and the post-Gricean pragmatics literature, and take your time before writing: work out where this package of views is actually vulnerable, and which of her commitments force her into awkward places, rather than offering the objections anyone would reach for first. Then draft exactly eight interview questions, numbered, each one a sentence or two, and each immediately followed by a single-sentence follow-up I can deploy if she gives the answer I expect. Between them the questions must cover: Grice's own diagnostics for implicature, in particular cancellability, detachability, and calculability, and which of them survive her re-description; the charge that the account is circular, since a failed literal reading presupposes a settled literal reading; the question of why these maxims and not others, and what would count as evidence that the inventory is parochial rather than rational; the regress objection to reflexive intentions and what her commitment-based substitute does about the case of insincere or misleading speech, where a speaker seems to commit herself to more than she intended; and the minimalist/contextualist dispute over how much of 'what is said' is settled before implicature even gets going. Please make two of the questions turn on concrete cases of my own devising, since I want to see whether the view earns its keep outside the seminar room: first, my late aunt's will, which left 'the good china' to one niece and 'the china in the cabinet' to another, and which the family litigated on the strength of what she must have meant; and second, a chat assistant that replies to a draft with 'that's one way to do it', where I want to know whose commitment, if anyone's, is in play when the utterer is not a person. Give me the numbered list only, with no introduction, no framing remarks, and no closing summary.
