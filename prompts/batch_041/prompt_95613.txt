I am preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on realism and anti-realism, and I want to begin with the specification rather than the menu. Before we consider any treatment of Fitch's paradox of knowability — the result that a principle licensing the knowability of every truth would entail that every truth is in fact known — I want the desiderata: the full list of what any adequate treatment of that result must deliver, with the consideration behind each requirement. I want no citations and no attributions; requirements, not a reading list.

Divide the list in two. First, the constraints the derivation imposes simply by being a derivation: set out explicitly which premises the argument requires and which modal assumptions it makes, so that every requirement can be traced to a step one would have to surrender in order to avoid the conclusion. Second, the dialectical constraints, imposed not by validity but by the anti-realist's own purposes — whatever blocks the argument must leave a principle that is still substantive rather than trivially true, still motivated rather than ad hoc, and still fit for the work the anti-realist wanted it to do. For each requirement, give the consideration that generates it and mark it either as non-negotiable or as the preference of one camp. Where two requirements cannot be satisfied together, say so and say which camp must give way; that is the part I most want to see. Then identify the single requirement that eliminates the most otherwise popular treatments, and explain why it has that power.

Four further demands, since these are where I expect the genuinely contested work to lie:

1. Say whether the distribution of knowledge over conjunction — that whoever knows a conjunction knows each conjunct — is a logical truth about knowledge or an idealisation of it, and whether the reason so few treatments deny it is that it is true or that the price of denying it is high.
2. Say whether an adequate account must be able to certify some particular contingent, non-logical truth as unknowable, and whether that certification counts as a virtue or a cost.
3. Fix the reading of "knowable" — knowledge available to some possible being at some possible world, or knowledge available to inquirers like us — and show that under that reading the knowability principle remains substantive rather than vacuous.
4. Say what the account does with the self-referential cousin of the puzzle, the sentence that says of itself that no one knows it: whether it is a further test the account must pass or a separate problem it may legitimately set aside, and on what grounds.

A numbered list will do, with a short incompatibility note at the end.
