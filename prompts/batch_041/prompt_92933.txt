I am in a graduate seminar on self-knowledge and first-person authority, and for the past two weeks a classmate and I have been circling an argument he built, which I can neither refute nor accept. To keep the discussion honest I am setting aside the idiomatic use of "I don't believe it," as when someone says "I don't believe it's raining" to mean "I'm astonished that it's raining"; he intends "believe" in the strict doxastic sense, and so do I. His target is the kind of sentence Moore made famous, and for a concrete instance he uses M = "the door is unlocked, and I don't believe the door is unlocked." His argument runs as follows. He begins with three principles governing belief: (A) if you believe p and you believe q, then you believe p-and-q; (B) if you believe p-and-q, then you believe p and you believe q; and (C) if you believe p, then you believe that you believe p, since believing is transparent to the believer -- you cannot hold a belief without holding the belief that you hold it. Then: (1) suppose someone believes M. (2) By (B), she believes "the door is unlocked" and she believes "I don't believe the door is unlocked." (3) By (C), applied to her belief that the door is unlocked, she believes "I believe the door is unlocked." (4) By (A), she believes "I believe the door is unlocked and I don't believe the door is unlocked," whose content is a contradiction. (5) No rational agent can believe a contradiction. (6) Hence no rational agent believes M. (7) Since (A) through (C) are meant to be constitutive of belief rather than mere ideals, no agent whatever believes M: the Moorean sentence is not merely unassertable but unthinkable. (8) So the "paradox" dissolves, since there is nothing here specific to assertion; you simply cannot be in the state the sentence describes, any more than you can believe an outright contradiction. He adds that (C) is no convenience: the very absurdity we set out to explain is itself evidence for it, because if belief were not transparent there would be nothing wrong with announcing a Moorean sentence.

My difficulty is that (8) seems to prove far too much. If (C) holds as he states it, then no one can ever be mistaken about what she believes -- self-knowledge comes out complete and infallible -- and iterating (C) saddles every believer with an infinite hierarchy of beliefs about her own beliefs, which no finite mind could contain. And if M is genuinely unthinkable, then no one can sincerely say it; every apparently sincere utterance of a Moorean sentence would have to be a lie or a slip of the tongue, which strikes me as plainly false, since I can readily imagine someone confused, or only just waking up, saying such a thing in perfect earnest. I would like to keep (A) and (B), which look close to analytic for explicitly believed conjunctions, and I would like to keep the datum that Moorean sentences are absurd to assert. So: which step should I reject, why did it look so plausible in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost me -- in particular, can I still explain why the Moorean sentence is absurd to assert once I have given that step up?
