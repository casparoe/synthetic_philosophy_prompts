I spent most of last weekend in an argument with a friend who works in behavioral ecology, and we never resolved the point that started it. She was describing a ground-nesting shorebird that lures a predator away from its nest by dragging a wing as though it were broken, and she said, quite unselfconsciously, that the bird "was trying to draw the fox off the nest." When I asked whether the bird had any such thing in mind, she said of course not — that what she meant was that the display is *for* misdirecting predators, that selection shaped it to do exactly that, and that no one, bird or designer, ever had to entertain the goal for the description to be true. She then pushed further and claimed the same style of explanation works for an immune system "recognizing" a pathogen, a market "pricing in" news, and an algorithm "seeking" a minimum — none of which, on her view, requires a reasoner. A colleague of hers later told me philosophers have a name for this, something like *reasons without reasoners*, and that it is either profound or a category mistake, which is precisely the question I could not answer.

What I want from you is not a verdict on my friend but a reconstruction of what the explanatory practice she is using presupposes — the practice, pervasive in biology and now spreading through economics and computer science, of attributing purposes, functions, strategies, and even reasons to systems that on the speaker's own account contain no one who reasons. Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Does the practice presuppose that a reason can exist without a reasoner, and if so, what is a "reason" doing in that role — a disposition to produce an effect, a history of having been selected for that effect, a pattern a theorist can read off a system from the outside? Which rival accounts does it quietly rule out: that purposes must be someone's, that only conscious agents can have reasons, that all such talk is metaphor or useful fiction? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say whether it is internally consistent — my friend insists that selection is blind and mindless while talking as though the bird were solving a problem, and I want to know whether both can be held at once or whether the second quietly smuggles back the first's denial.

Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced whatever it presupposes with one of the alternatives. A strictly mechanistic biology would refuse all teleological language and speak only of causes and effects; an account at the opposite pole would take the intentional idiom at face value and treat the bird as genuinely wanting things. What would each give up, would either explain the phenomena better, or is the middle position the practice actually occupies the only one that preserves the explanations we care about?
