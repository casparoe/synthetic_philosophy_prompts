Two friends of mine spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about a sentence a third friend had left on a whiteboard, and I have not been able to referee it since. The sentence read, in full: "Nothing written on this whiteboard today is true." It was the only sentence written on the board that day, so it appears to be saying something about itself. I was present the whole time, said almost nothing, and would like a considered verdict rather than a draw.

Here is the exchange as I can best reconstruct it. Ines is finishing a degree in logic, Rafi works on the philosophy of language, and I have kept the turns in the order they came.

(1) Ines: If the sentence is true, then things are as it says, so nothing on the board is true, so it is not true. If it is not true, then what it says holds, so it is true. So it is true just in case it is not true. That is not a trick of wording to be explained away; it is a genuine contradiction, and the honest response is a logic — a paraconsistent one — that permits some contradictions to be true and keeps reasoning from collapsing in their presence, rather than a theory that refuses to look.

(2) Rafi: You skipped the option. The sentence is neither true nor false. It is not a claim that fails; it is a string that never made a claim, so there is nothing for the world to agree or disagree with. Both 'both' and 'neither' stop the contradiction, and 'neither' is cheaper, because it does not oblige us to rewrite logic.

(3) Ines: The 'neither' reply refutes itself, and this is the part I would press hardest. If the sentence is neither true nor false, then it is not true. But 'it is not true' is precisely what it says of itself, and a sentence that says what is the case is true. So your view is obliged to call it true after all.

(4) Rafi: You are smuggling bivalence in one level down. I do not grant that 'not true' is true of a sentence that has no truth value; if the sentence is valueless, so is its negation, so it never manages to say anything true. The bill comes due elsewhere — I lose excluded middle for these sentences, and I would rather pay there than where you pay. Your view has a bill of its own: from a contradiction, with the ordinary rules, anything whatever follows, so if contradictions can be true you must surrender one of the ordinary rules and tell me which everyday piece of reasoning is now broken. And there is a further cost: a true contradiction would have to be the world's being both one way and not, which is not a discovery about truth but a rejection of the thought that truth answers to how things are.

(5) Ines: Every view on offer has to give something up, so the mere fact that mine does is not an argument against it; the question is which loss is bearable. I claim that confining the damage to inferences that pass through a contradiction is more bearable than giving up the rule that any claim is either right or wrong — a rule we are using in this very conversation. As for the world: in these paradoxes the subject matter is the language itself, so 'how things are' is not the innocent independent court you want it to be.

(6) Rafi: Then let me pay neither bill. 'True' is always true of a language; no language contains a predicate that applies to its own sentences; so the whiteboard sentence is not a well-formed claim about itself at all. No contradiction, no gap, no glut — only a sentence that was never in the business of being true or false.

(7) Ines: That works only if you can state the hierarchy from outside it. 'No language contains its own truth predicate' is a claim about all languages, made in a language that would have to contain a truth predicate for all of them; and when we say of the whiteboard sentence that it fails to be well-formed, we are saying something true about it in English — which is what we were just told we could not do.

I would like this adjudicated move by move rather than summed up. For each of the seven, say what it establishes, where it overreaches, and which unstated premise it depends on. Then identify the moves the dispute actually turns on — the places where, if you conceded the point, the rest would follow — and say what both of them are missing, including anything that would make the whole disagreement look like a false choice. Finally, say which of the two is closer to right overall and what the other would have to establish to come out ahead. Argue it rather than reporting that philosophers are divided; no citations or reading lists are needed.
