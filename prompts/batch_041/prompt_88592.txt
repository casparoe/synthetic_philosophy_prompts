I am a retired Earth scientist — forty-one years in stratigraphy and basin analysis, most of it spent on the subsidence history of sedimentary basins — who began reading philosophy of history about three years ago, largely through a continuing-education reading group at the local university. I have drafted the essay below for that group and would like your comments before I circulate it. My own discipline reconstructs singular past events for a living, so I have firm instincts about what it is to explain one. I suspect some of those instincts are provincial, and I would rather find that out now than in front of the group.

My draft is titled Explanation in History: A Geologist's Suspicions.

1. What I did for a living. My colleagues and I explain events that occurred once, at a particular place, tens or hundreds of millions of years ago, and that no one observed. We do it by invoking processes we can watch operating today — subsidence, compaction, isotopic fractionation, the response of sediments to stress — and by asking which combination of such processes, at what rates, would have produced the record in front of us. We do not derive the event from laws. We cannot: the initial conditions are unknown in detail and the history is underdetermined by the surviving record. Yet we take ourselves to be explaining, and not merely describing. That is the datum I want to put alongside the philosophers' debate.

2. The claim I find hard to resist. Hempel's, as I read him: to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected, given antecedent conditions and general regularities. As I understand the requirement, this means the event must be deducible from universal laws; a statistical regularity will not do, since it makes the event probable at best, and probability is not explanation. The first half of this I find compelling. When I say why a basin subsided, I mean that anyone who knew the relevant mechanics and the state of the crust would have expected it to subside. The second half I find harder, because much of what I actually rely on is statistical: recurrence intervals of earthquakes, distributions of permeability, the probability that a given assemblage of microfossils implies a given temperature.

3. What historians write. Here is my puzzle. No historian I have read states laws. Gibbon does not; nor do the moderns. Either the regularities are so banal that stating them would be an insult — armies need supply, taxes provoke resentment, elites defect when defeat looks likely — or there are no regularities and history is storytelling with footnotes. I cannot see a third possibility. I take the covering-law model, put plainly, to be the thesis that good history must read like physics: laws stated, deductions displayed. If that is right, then almost no history is good, which strikes me as a reductio. I am aware that some writers hold that narrative is itself a form of explanation, but I confess I cannot see how. A sequence of true sentences, however well ordered, seems to me to be a sequence of small explanations or of none; the connective tissue between them looks like mere chronology.

4. A proposal. In my field we work under a maxim: the present is the key to the past. Let me state the constraint it imposes, which I will call the actualism constraint. An explanation of a singular past event is acceptable only if every causal kind it invokes is independently attested as operating somewhere in the accessible record, and if the inference to those causes is the best available among the alternatives. Notice what this does not require: no laws need be stated, no deduction need be exhibited, no expectation need be computed. It constrains the kinds of causes admissible, not the logical form of the argument. I claim it is weaker than the covering-law requirement, and I claim it is sufficient — sufficient to license explanation by narration, in which each step is actualistically licensed and the sequence as a whole does the explanatory work.

5. Two things that trouble me. First, an asymmetry. In my field the inference and the explanation run in opposite directions. We infer the past from the present; we say the past explains the present. A drought is inferred from a sediment core, but we say the drought explains the abandonment of the cities and not the reverse, even though the evidential relation runs the other way. If explanation were a matter of deducibility, the two directions ought to be interchangeable, and plainly they are not.

Second, a point about descriptions. Many sentences historians write could not have been written at the time of the events they describe: that a skirmish was the beginning of a war, that a policy was a regime's last chance. The explanandum, then, is not a neutral datum waiting to be deduced; it is an event under a description that only hindsight supplies. I do not know what the covering-law model is supposed to do with that.

6. Where I think I may have gone wrong. My suspicion is that the actualism constraint is either Hempel's requirement in geological costume — that a maxim about admissible causes is simply a law about causes, so that I have smuggled a law back in — or else that it is too weak, telling me which causes are admissible but not which of two admissible causes is doing the explaining.

What I would like from you, in whatever order you find natural:

(1) An audit of sections 2 and 3. Tell me where my rendering of the covering-law position is accurate and where I have misstated it, and whether the dilemma I pose in section 3 is genuine or false.

(2) A verdict on section 4. Is the actualism constraint a real alternative, a relabeling of the position I began with, or a methodological rule that leaves the question of explanation untouched? If it is a relabeling, identify the step at which the law is smuggled in.

(3) Whether the asymmetry in section 5 has a principled basis, or whether I am confusing an asymmetry in our evidence with an asymmetry in explanation; and whether the point about retrospective descriptions undermines the covering-law model or merely complicates the identification of the explanandum. On the narrativist claim I dismiss in section 3, I would like the strongest version of it, not the weakest.

(4) The strongest objection to the essay as a whole, stated as its ablest opponent would state it.

(5) A candid judgement on whether any of this is a position that already exists under a name I do not know, and if so what it is called and what its standard difficulties are.

Please be concrete. Where you say I have misstated something, quote the sentence of mine you mean and give the correction, and tell me whether the essay's conclusion — that history can be explanatory without laws in the text — survives your corrections or collapses. I would rather have the sharp version than the polite one. I am not asking for a reading list; I am asking whether the reasoning holds.
