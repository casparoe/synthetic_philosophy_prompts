I am a backend engineer with no background in philosophy or economics; what I know about the following comes from a Slack thread and from a data-science course I took years ago. I would like a careful verdict, and I do not need pointers to any literature — I want the arguments themselves assessed.

Background. Last month the leadership of the company I work for (about 200 people; we make a consumer app) announced that, starting next quarter, major product decisions will be made by prediction markets instead of the usual committee process. The mechanics are these. For any proposal on the table, two markets open. The first pays out only if the proposal ships; the second pays out only if it does not ship. Both are settled against the same figure three months later — weekly active users — so the price in each market reflects what traders expect that figure to be in that market's branch. If a market's branch never occurs, the bets in it are void and the money is returned. Whichever market prices higher wins, and the proposal ships or does not accordingly. Trading is voluntary, and the traders are us, the employees. Dev, one of the two people below, has been reading about this idea for years and calls it futarchy.

Maya: Our roadmap is currently set by whoever is most senior or most persuasive in the room. The market replaces that with money. If you believe a change will raise the number, you buy, and the price moves; being confidently wrong now costs something. And the market is not voting on what we want. It is betting on what will happen, which is an empirical question, and empirical questions are what markets are good at.

Dev: The two markets do not constrain each other. Each pays only in its own branch, so nobody ever has to hold both at once, and nothing forces the pair to be consistent — both can be priced high at the same time, since a trader who thinks shipping is bad buys the second market and a trader who thinks shipping is good buys the first. Comparing the two prices tells you which branch traders favor. It does not tell you whether either branch beats what we are already doing, or by how much.

Maya: A comparison is all we need. We are choosing between two options, not estimating an effect size. If the ships market is priced above the does-not-ship market, the traders are telling us the number will be higher with the change than without it. That is the decision.

Dev: "If it ships" is not the same question as "if we ship it." The price of the ships market moves whenever traders learn that the change is about to ship, and that can happen for reasons that have nothing to do with the change causing anything: if the team ships only when it is confident, or if the quarter is going to be good anyway, then shipping correlates with a good number without causing one. What we want is the number in the world where we intervene. A price conditioned on the event that it shipped does not give us that.

Maya: The rule severs the two. The change ships if and only if the market prices it higher, so there is no independent fact about shipping that could be correlated with a good quarter. If traders know the quarter will be good regardless, they buy both markets and both prices rise; the comparison does not move.

Dev: The market can only answer questions about the number we chose, and choosing the number is the largest decision we make. Whoever sets the metric sets the winner: with weekly active users as the metric, the market will cheerfully recommend whatever spikes engagement, including things we regret two years later. And the metric is not fixed from outside — moving from engagement to retention is itself a decision, made by whom?

Maya: Price the metric too. Open a market on whether adopting engagement as our metric leaves the company worth more in three years than adopting retention would. At every level the market decides; we only have to fix the topmost thing we are maximizing, and that is a value question, which is what voting is for.

Dev: Then the market never decides the whole question, and the part it cannot decide is where our real disagreements live. There is a second problem: the traders are us. The people with the best information about whether a change will move the number are the team building it, and their reviews and bonuses are tied to that number. A market whose volume comes from people paid on the metric is not a neutral aggregator. And since a market whose branch does not occur returns the money, the cost of betting is only paid in the world where your branch happens — the people who would have been wrong about the counterfactual never pay.

Maya: If they are wrong they lose money, and being right pays. We can also invite people outside the company to trade.

What I would like, point by point:

1. For each exchange above, in order, say who has the better of it and why, rating each move from clearly mistaken through arguable to clearly right.
2. Say what each of them is missing that neither has said, especially anything about the difference between using a market as a forecast and using it as the decision procedure itself.
3. Separate the points that turn on how real markets actually behave, which I could go and look up, from the points that turn on the logic of the proposal.
4. Finally, say who is more right overall, and whether some version of the proposal survives the objections.
