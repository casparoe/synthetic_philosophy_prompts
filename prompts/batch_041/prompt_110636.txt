I have no formal training in philosophy — I write software for a living — but I have been reading about modality on my own, and I keep hitting a distinction that I suspect may be thinner than it looks. One view has it that an individual exists in many possible worlds and that identity across worlds is just strict identity; the other treats individuals as world-bound and analyzes claims about what something could have been using a counterpart relation that stands in for identity. In my day job I would call the first a primary key shared across tables and the second a similarity join, and what bothers me is that a similarity join is not symmetric, not transitive, and can return several rows, whereas a key is an equivalence relation. That looks like a real difference in expressive power, but I cannot tell whether it ever cashes out as a disagreement over a fixed set of facts, or whether the two views are just two query languages over the same database.

So please build me one case that settles it. I want a single thought experiment in which the two views are forced to return different verdicts on one specific claim about what some thing could have been, and I want the disagreement to run in the direction I find harder to arrange: the counterpart view should deny a possibility that the transworld-identity view grants. Keep persons out of it — make the subject an artifact or an institution (a firm, a band, a codebase, a ship, a language), since I do not want the case riding on the literature about personal identity. Constraints: at most two possible worlds besides the actual one; the worlds described purely qualitatively, with both sides agreeing on every qualitative fact about them (what exists, what happened, what properties everything has); the disagreement must not come from any difference between the worlds, nor from you stipulating the verdicts; and it must trace to exactly one structural property of the two relations, which you name. Give me the scenario in no more than 200 words.

Then do the work. Walk through the verdict each view gives on your target claim and why; give at least one further claim about the same case on which the two views must agree, so I can see that the disagreement is isolated; tell me which structural property is doing the work and what becomes of the case when that property is removed (say, by imposing on the counterpart relation whatever constraint it lacks); tell me which of the two verdicts is right on the merits, setting both theories aside; and tell me what a defender of whichever view comes out worse would say to defuse the case. If your case only works under some particular formulation of the counterpart view — for instance, whether a thing may have more than one counterpart in a single world — say so outright rather than quietly assuming it. No citations or reading lists, please; I care about the reasoning, not the provenance. And since I will be testing this against my own intuitions, tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
