Hey, I've got a philosophy-of-law question thats been bugging me for weeks and I can't let it go. I'm not a lawyer, so forgive any sloppy terminology.

Here's the practice I keep staring at. In the US, and increasingly elsewhere, the vast majority of criminal convictions never come from a trial — they come from guilty pleas. The prosecutor offers a deal: plead guilty and I'll recommend five years; go to trial and if you're convicted I'll push for fifteen. Most defendants take it, including plenty who tell their lawyer they didn't do it. Meanwhile the same legal system says a confession is involuntary and inadmissible if the police got it by threatening a much longer sentence in the event the suspect refused to confess. And in most US courts a judge can accept a guilty plea from a defendant who stands there maintaining he's innocent, as long as the evidence against him strongly indicates guilt — the guy is basically saying he didn't do it but conceding the state can probably prove he did, and that still counts as a valid plea.

What I want from you: act as a philosopher of law who works on consent, coercion and the criminal process, and think carefully before you commit to anything — start by brainstorming the different ways you could read this practice, then settle on one. Tell me what theory of valid consent the whole practice presupposes.

A few things I'm especially stuck on. First, coercion: it looks like the deal only counts as coercive if the prosecutor threatens something he has no right to do, and otherwise it's just a hard but lawful bargain. Unpack that view and tell me which rival views of coercion it quietly rules out. Second, deception and understanding: the plea only has to be made with the defendant knowing the charges and the maximum penalty, not understanding the law or the strength of the case against him, and the plea stays valid even if he badly misjudges his odds — so what does that presuppose about what consent requires? Third, competence: we accept pleas from people who plainly don't grasp what's being done to them, so long as they clear a fairly low bar. And then the tension I can't get past: if threatening to seek the maximum sentence is just a lawful hard bargain, why is a confession obtained by that exact same threat coerced? Does the practice need a distinction between confessions and pleas that its own theory of coercion can't actually supply? And what does the plead-guilty-while-insisting-your-innocent thing presuppose — does this practice treat consent as a waiver of your rights, or as an endorsement of what's being done to you?

Also please be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it's underdetermined — where two or three different theories of consent would all license the same practice.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question.
