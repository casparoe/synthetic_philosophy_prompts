I write the philosophy and science desk at a general-interest ideas magazine, and for a special issue on causation I'm assembling an interactive flowchart for readers — a which-theory-of-dispositions-do-you-actually-hold piece, in the spirit of the personality quizzes people take online, except that it has to sort them by what they say about cases rather than by which label they find flattering. I want the tree built from the logical space rather than from a roll call of famous names, and I want the diagnostic nodes to be concrete cases rather than questions about one's favourite theory. Five cases should do the separating, and I'd like you to use them: a wire of ordinary conducting metal fitted with a cut-off device that senses any conductor approaching and kills the current a fraction of a second before contact, so that touching it with a conductor yields no current; an ordinary fragile glass swaddled in foam, struck but not broken; a glass made of the usual fragile stuff but fitted with a gadget that senses impacts and instantly reinforces it, so that it never breaks when struck; a bar that is not fragile at all but contains a charge that detonates on impact, so that it shatters whenever struck; and an ordinary fragile glass with such a charge inside it, struck, so that it shatters — but shatters because of the charge rather than because of its brittleness. Readers should be able to answer each node without knowing where it leads, so no node of the form do you accept that dispositions reduce to conditionals; and no node should simply ask whether they believe in dispositions.

The deliverable is a decision tree that starts from a single root question and ends in somewhere between nine and twelve named leaves, each carrying the name of the position as it is usually called, or unnamed if nobody actually holds it, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Please build it in two passes: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space. The leaves must include at least the simple counterfactual analysis; the amended analysis that adds a clause about the disposition's intrinsic basis being retained and about interfering conditions standing aside, together with a verdict on whether that clause is informative or merely a promissory note; the view that the disposition just is its intrinsic causal basis; the view that dispositions are irreducible primitives; the view that a disposition belongs to a situation or a world rather than to an object; the response-dependent or projectivist view; the view that only categorical properties exist and dispositional talk is a façon de parler; and the view that disposition names a multi-track family of notions with no single analysis. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say why the cases fail to separate them; note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and tell me which leaves turn on the difference between the finkish and the masked cases — that is, which leaves would change their verdict if I swapped one case for the other — since a reader who thinks those are one problem rather than two will be led down a different branch. I don't want citations, a reading list, or a survey of who said what; the piece stands on its own, and I'd rather the tree be candid about its own limits than exhaustive.
