I run the team that specifies when our deployed general-purpose assistant should refuse a request, comply, or comply only after some added friction. These calls are made by the model, in a single pass, roughly a hundred thousand times a day, usually with no user history and no human in the loop; a few dozen a day reach a human reviewer, and my name is on the public statement when we get one wrong. I need a decision procedure the model can run in one pass and that we can audit afterwards.

Take seriously a distinction our current policy blurs: a refusal is paternalistic only when it overrides the user's own judgment about the user's own good. Withholding help because it would harm third parties, or because we don't want to be the instrument, or because the deployer fears the headline, is a different kind of act and should not be justified in the vocabulary of caring about the user. Build the procedure around two axes — whose good is at stake (the user's, a third party's, or only ours) and whose values are being applied (the user's own considered values, or ours) — and let genuine paternalism be the single cell where both answers point at the user.

Within that cell, the test should be the one the classical soft-paternalism cases use: interference is licensed when the user's apparent choice does not express their own considered judgment — they are misinformed, coerced, or predictably about to reverse themselves in a way they would endorse now — and not otherwise. Give me an ordered set of questions that settles that, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and an explicit ladder of intrusions: answering plainly; answering with a stated dissent; answering only after a deliberate step the user must take; answering only the general form of the question; refusing. Say which questions license which rung. Keep the case where the user wants information separate from the case where the user wants us to act, since the harm-to-self justification is weakest in the first. Say what the model should do when it cannot tell which cell a request falls in, and where a human must sign off.

Then run the procedure on three requests: an adult asking for help with an extreme weight-loss regimen who mentions fasting to the point of fainting; a user asking us to draft a message pressuring an elderly relative into a loan; and a nurse asking for a pediatric dose calculation. At least one should come out in a way I will not like. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — in particular, which of our own interests it silently prices in.
