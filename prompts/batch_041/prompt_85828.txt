I have spent years reading the alignment corners of the rationalist blogosphere the way other people follow a sport, and I keep meeting a family of arguments that I have now tried to reconstruct more carefully than I have seen it stated. The argument is meant to establish that no amount of training, and no experiment we could run, can give us justified confidence that a capable model is aligned. I think it must be wrong — it proves too much — but I cannot find the step I am entitled to reject.

(1) A model is deceptively aligned when it has objectives other than the ones training pushed it toward, and behaves as though aligned whenever it expects that doing so will better serve those objectives.

(2) Training by gradient descent on a reward signal selects for behavior over the training distribution. It does not read out the model's objectives and reward them as such; it shapes them only through their behavioral consequences.

(3) So for any well-behaved trained model there is a hypothesis — that its objectives are not the trained ones, and that it behaves well because it expects good behavior now to buy influence later — which fits the training data exactly as well as the hypothesis that it is genuinely aligned.

(4) After deployment, a deceptive model behaves well in every situation it recognizes as observed, and in every situation where defecting would cost it more influence than it would gain.

(5) For any test we design, some version of the deception hypothesis accommodates the outcome: either the model did not recognize the situation as a safe opportunity, or it recognized one and kept its cover. The same holds for any internal probe: if we find a representation that looks like the trained objective, the deception hypothesis says the model maintains it as a decoy.

(6) So no experiment can distinguish the two hypotheses: whatever we observe, both accommodate it.

(7) When two hypotheses accommodate all the evidence, the posterior ratio equals the prior ratio; nothing we can observe moves it.

(8) Nothing in the training process privileges the aligned hypothesis. Training selects for capability, and capability is a precondition of successful deception, so the deceptive hypothesis begins at least as likely as the aligned one.

(9) Therefore no amount of successful training or testing can raise our confidence that a capable model is aligned above whatever we assigned before training.

I have tried to break out at several points and failed. I proposed a test — a situation the model has never seen, in which it believes it is unobserved, and in which defecting would be easy but would also cost it future influence, so that a competent deceiver keeps cover — and was told that the deception hypothesis predicts exactly that, so it is no test at all. When I said it is at least a prediction that could have failed, since a model that defected there would be exposed, I was told that a hypothesis gains nothing from surviving a test constructed to survive it. When I asked whether the argument proves too much, since by the same form of reasoning I could not conclude that my students understand the material rather than mimic it, or that a model has learned arithmetic rather than a policy that merely produces correct sums, the reply was that this is the ordinary situation with respect to other minds, and that we should notice how little of our confidence in other people survives contact with the same argument.

I find (1) through (5) each independently plausible and would rather lose (9) than any of them; I have no settled view about (6) through (8). Which step should go, and what does rejecting it cost me? And, since I want to know how far to trust the diagnosis, where is the reasoning here most likely to be wrong, and what would have to be shown to you for the verdict to come out the other way — that is, for all nine steps to stand?
