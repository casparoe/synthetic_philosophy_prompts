Ok, slightly weird ask, bear with me. I'm a novelist. The little theater in my town — 90 seats, converted butter market, nearly went under last year until a bunch of poeple bailed it out — is doing a stage adaptation of my novel, and they've asked me to give the opening-night speech. Five minutes, thanks to the donors, thanks to the actors, please come back, that kind of thing. I said yes before I thought it through.

Heres the problem. For about a year now I've been half-taken in by Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert, the one where he tells Geneva it shouldn't have a theater. The bits that stuck: that a play lets you feel like a good person without doing anything — you cry, you go home, nothing changes; that Molière's Misanthrope teaches you to be agreeable like Philinte rather than honest like Alceste, so the comedy ends up on the side of the people who laugh at the truth-teller; that a theater needs a big rich idle city to feed it, and that in a small poor one it eats whatever holds the place together; and then, at the end, his alternative — the open-air fête where the audience stops being an audience and becomes the show. (I know the man also wrote a hit opera. I've made my peace with that.)

So write me the speech. It has to actually work as an opening night — warm, funny, makes people want to come back — without me saying anything I don't believe, and I don't want it to just name-drop Rousseau as a bit of decoration. The room is half season-ticket regulars, half donors and neighbors who've never read a word of him, and it absolutely cannot turn into a lecture. And I'd like it to end by genuinely trying to turn the evening into the fête thing — get everyone out of the seats and into the square, or whatever it turns out to be — in a way that could plausibly work and could also plausibly fall flat on its face. Five minutes, meant to be said out loud.
