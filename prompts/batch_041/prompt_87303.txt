I am an applied microeconometrician. My working vocabulary is the potential-outcomes framework — units, treatments, well-defined interventions, potential outcomes indexed by treatment, identification assumptions, exogenous variation — together with a routine facility for variance decompositions, R², sums of squares, and variance-components models. I have a standing habit of treating any claim to the effect that X accounts for p percent of the variation in Y as a descriptive statement until someone writes down the intervention and the identifying assumptions. What I lack is any real familiarity with behavior genetics from the inside; my sense of the heritability debate is secondhand and made largely of slogans.

Restate the main positions in the debate over what heritability does and does not mean in my vocabulary, one at a time and as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. The positions I have in mind are these, and you should tell me if I have carved the debate wrongly or if two of them collapse into one:

1. Heritability estimates — from twin and family designs, and more recently from molecular data — measure the causal contribution of genetic differences to differences in a trait within a population, and are therefore informative about that trait's causal architecture.
2. Heritability is a purely descriptive variance ratio. It says nothing about any individual, nothing about the causes of a trait in a given person, and nothing about malleability.
3. There is no population-independent fact for the coefficient to report, since it shifts with the distribution of environments; the same trait can have a heritability near zero or near one depending on how uniform the environment is. What the statistic reports on is therefore the environment, or the environment's homogeneity, rather than the trait.
4. Talk of genes causing a trait is difference-making talk: it concerns contrasts between actual and counterfactual genomes at a fixed environment, so heritability speaks to differences rather than to the developmental process that produces the trait.

For each position, give me the best translation I can get — what it looks like when written out with units, treatments, potential outcomes, and identification assumptions — and be honest about where you had to fudge rather than translate.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want:

- Which parts of each position translate cleanly, which survive only as distortions, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all?
- Here is a wall I keep hitting. Potential-outcomes reasoning requires a treatment that could in principle be assigned to a unit, and a unit that remains the same unit under either assignment. The heritability claim seems to need a contrast between me and me-with-a-different-genome. Either that counterfactual has no well-defined treatment, or it changes the unit into someone else. Is this a defect of my framework, or is the heritability claim doing something other than stating a causal fact? If the latter, say precisely what.
- In these models, the environment appears to mean whatever is left over once genetic variance is removed, which is not how I would use the word in an identification argument. What is lost when I translate the environment as the residual, and is the loss a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the original notion was never well defined?
- Directly, and without hedging: if everything that survives translation is that heritability is a variance ratio indexed to a population, is that because the behavior geneticists are confused, or because they are tracking a genuine fact that my framework cannot state?
