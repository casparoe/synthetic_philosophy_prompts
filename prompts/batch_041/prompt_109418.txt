For thirty-five years I worked as an experimental physical chemist, and since I retired I have been sitting in on graduate seminars in philosophy; I have found that the habits of the bench — change one factor at a time, keep a control, distrust any story that explains everything at once — serve me tolerably well here, except when the subject matter refuses to hold still. Lately I have been thinking about how one ought to choose when one can save some people but not all, and I would like to lay my own verdicts on a set of cases before someone who can tell me what, if anything, they add up to. Please take the role of a philosopher working on normative ethics and the allocation of scarce rescue, and reason deliberately rather than briskly: before you answer, lay out the principal families of candidate principles — straightforwardly aggregative ones on which saving more lives is simply better; ones on which each person's claim is equally strong but such claims do not add up across persons; partiality- and relationship-based ones; and pluralist ones on which several considerations, including expected life-years and special obligations, each carry some weight — and then test each family against the cases below, rather than choosing a favorite and defending it.

Here are the cases and what I find myself thinking. (1) A flooded valley, one boat, fuel for a single trip: a lone stranger clings to a roof on one side, five strangers to a roof on the other, distances and risks to me identical, no one connected to me. I take the boat to the five, without hesitation. (2) A field clinic, one vial of an antidote that will fully cure exactly one patient: five patients in the ward will die without it, and a sixth patient, in the next bed, with the same prognosis, same age, and no weaker or stronger claim, will also die without it. Here I find that I would not hand it to one of the five merely because five exceeds one; I would draw lots among all six, and I would not think it wrong if the single patient received it. Cases (1) and (2) are the ones I cannot square, and they are the reason I am writing. (3) Same clinic, but the sixth patient is my granddaughter and the other five are strangers: she gets the vial, and I feel no real doubt, though I cannot derive this from anything above. (4) Same clinic, one stranger against four strangers, where the one is twenty-five and the four are all past ninety and gravely ill: the twenty-five-year-old gets it, again with little doubt — which seems to make age and expected life-years relevant in a way the boat case left out. (5) Same clinic, one stranger against two, where the one is the only person who can operate the clinic's generator and keep it running through the coming month: the one gets it.

The principle I tentatively hold is this: each person's claim to be saved is equally strong and does not simply sum with the claims of others, so that when only one can be saved the choice is at bottom a matter of fairness, best settled by a lottery; but where I can save more without failing anyone I could have saved, I should save more; and a special relation to one of them — a bond, a promise — can override both. I can already see that this principle may not deliver my verdict on the boat, since saving the five does fail the one. Please tell me whether any single principle can capture verdicts (1) through (5), or whether one of them must be abandoned; state the principle or the small set of rival principles precisely, clause by clause, naming for each clause the case that forces it; then say what the principle predicts for two cases I have not listed — first, the clinic again, one vial, one stranger against three strangers, where the one is a neighbor of thirty years; second, the boat again, one swimmer against five, where the five are elderly and ill and the one is young and healthy. Finish by naming, without softening it, which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and what I would be paying if I kept the principle and dropped the verdict. Think this through carefully; a tidy answer that reconciles everything by adding epicycles would be less useful to me than a clean principle that candidly overrules one of my intuitions.
