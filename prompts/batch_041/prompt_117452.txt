I'm in a reading group thats been going in circles for months on whether a person can legitimately bind their future self, and the same fight keeps reappearing one level up whenever we talk about constitutions that are deliberately hard to amend. On one side are the people I'll call the binders: they think a Ulysses contract, a psychiatric advance directive, or an entrenched bill of rights is just autonomy exercised on a longer timescale -- the sober self speaking for the drunk one, the founding generation speaking for the ones that come later. On the other are the unbinders: they think a self that can't change its mind isn't more autonomous but less, that the "authentic self" is a fiction invented after the fact to dress up whatever the earlier self happened to want, and that entrenchment is just one generation's dead hand clamped on the next. What makes it maddening is that both sides keep pointing at the same handful of cases -- the guy who has himself tied to the mast, the woman who signs away her future consent to treatment, a constitution with an eternity clause -- and reading each of them in opposite directions.

I don't want another transcript of the shouting match; I want the document they should have written instead. Something a card-carrying binder and a card-carrying unbinder could both put their names to. We also keep snagging on a distinction a friend of mine floated that I can't adjudicate: she says there's a real difference between precommitments that work by removing an option outright (you burn the boats) and ones that work by making the option expensive (you raise the toll), and that only the first deserves the name, the second being a mere tax on weakness -- and both camps seem to agree with her for opposite reasons, which I find suspicious. So can you draft it: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, covering the formal structure of precommitment, what "autonomy" can't be doing in these debates, and what the regress problem actually shows -- if the earlier self can bind the later one, why can't the later one simply bind itself back?; (2) the precise points where they still part ways, each one written the way its own side would insist on phrasing it, not a strawman; (3) the cruxes, which I suspect include whether there's any principled line between the "authentic" and the "passing" self, and whether ex post regret counts against a binding that the ex ante self wholeheartedly endorsed; and (4) for each crux, some case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. And if part of this fight is just verbal -- people sliding between different senses of "freedom" or of "the self" -- say which part and why.
