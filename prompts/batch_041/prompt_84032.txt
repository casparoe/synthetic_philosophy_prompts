My reading group has spend the last four weeks on Carrolls tortoise — the one where Achilles writes the conditional down as another premise, then has to write down another conditional to connect that premise to the conclusion, and so on, while the tortoise keeps saying it's quite willing to grant each new premise but still isn't granting the conclusion — and we've split into two camps that have basically stopped listening to each other. Camp one thinks the tortoise has found something real: that no propositional justification can make an inference binding on you, so the justification of deduction has to bottom out somewhere else — in a practice, a policy, a disposition, a form of life, a willingness to reason under the rule rather than about it — and that the fact this bottom is circular or non-propositional isn't a scandal, it's just what a bottom looks like. Camp two thinks the tortoise's demand is a category mistake wearing the costume of a demand: if you've really accepted the premises and the conditional, you've already drawn the conclusion; the regress only gets going because we let the conditional be treated as one more piece of cargo in the premise list when it's actually the track the reasoning runs on, not something the reasoning carries. We've been calling these the ticket model and the track model, and we're dimly aware that our names for them may be doing work the argument should be doing.

What I want out of you is the joint statement these two camps should have written instead of arguing past each other. So: a numbered set of claims both can sign, including about what the dialogue does and doesn't establish, about whether the regress is vicious or merely benign, and about what a justification of deduction would have to be if there could be one; then the remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view; then the cruxes — I'd guess one is whether accepting a conditional is itself just the disposition to infer or something over and above that disposition, another is whether calling the tortoise irrational quietly presupposes the very rule we're trying to justify, and a third is whether a justification of a rule that has to use that rule counts as a justification at all — and for each crux, the test, argument, or case both sides agree would actually bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. To keep that last part from being decorative, use these two: (a) an agent that is behaviorally identical to a competent reasoner on every inference it will ever be asked to perform, but which, if you ask it about modus ponens, says it's just a convention it was trained into and it can't see why it should care — does either camp predict this agent is missing something, and if so, what, exactly? and (b) a system that can recite modus ponens, checks proofs step by step, and gets every output right, but whose outputs are produced by a lookup over rules it never reasons under — is the track model committed to saying this thing has the same standing as you and me, and is the ticket model committed to saying it doesn't? I also want to know, and honestly this matters to me more than the rest of it, whether alot of our fight is verbal — whether camp one means something different by justification than camp two does, in which case I'd rather be told so in plain words than keep litigating it.

And please don't hand me a survey of positions. Take sides. Say which camp you'd actually sign with on the crux you think is decisive, commit to a view about whether the regress is vicious, and give your reasons, including what you'd have to be shown to change your mind. If you think the whole dispute is verbal, say that too, and say what you think the two camps are really disagreeing about underneath it.
