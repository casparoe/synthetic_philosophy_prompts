ok so weird request but hear me out. My partner and I finally bought a place — small, on the edge of town, technically inside the "village core" zoning district. That matters because the rules there allow one dwelling unit per lot, and we wanted to convert the detached garage out back into a little place for my mum. The city planner told us the garage is definately not a dwelling, no permanent foundation. Three weeks later a different planner told us of course it's a dwelling, it has a kitchen and a bed and someone could sleep there. Same sentence of the code, same garage. Our neighbour has a near-identical setup and got approved. I've spend way too many evenings reading about vagueness now and I want to turn it into fiction instead of another angry email to the council.

So: write me a short story, maybe 1500-2500 words, set in a city that tried to fix this problem institutionally instead of case by case. Here's the setup I want you to use. The city has an office whose whole job is to maintain, for every vague term in the zoning code (dwelling, structure, family, yard, habitable), a published list of admissible sharpenings — the reasonable ways you could draw the line. A permit is granted if it would be granted under every sharpening on the list; denied if it would be denied under every one; and if the list disagrees with itself, the case goes to a lottery, where a sharpening is drawn and that's your answer. Nobody involved thinks the lottery is a good idea, it's just what they settled on.

There's also a senior planner in the story who thinks the whole office is a category error. Her line: the garage either is a dwelling or it isn't, right now, before anyone writes a list, and the fact that we can't find out which doesn't make it a matter of policy. She goes along with the lottery because the alternative is worse, but she signs her decisions knowing that someone is being wronged and no one will ever be able to tell who.

Two things I want the story to actually do, not just talk about:

First, there should be one concrete case where the two ways of seeing it come apart — where the list says the outcome is indeterminate (so, lottery) but the senior planner thinks there's a determinate answer that we're just not in a position to know. Make the stakes of that gap real for somebody. A shed, a fence height, a garage, whatever, but it should be the kind of thing that ruins a year of someone's life.

Second, I want at least a hint of a nastier wrinkle: the list of admissible sharpenings is itself vague. Which sharpenings count as reasonable is not something you can settle with a list, so there's a second-order version of the same problem, and the office quietly handles it by not thinking about it. A character can notice this. Don't let anyone solve it.

Also, one resident somewhere should try the nuclear option: argue that because the term is vague the whole provision is unenforceable, so everyone in the district gets their permit. Whether that works should be left open.

Don't name real philosophers or cite anything, and please no scene where a character explains the positions in a neat paragraph — I want the disagreement to show up as people fighting about a shed. No tidy ending that reveals who was right.
