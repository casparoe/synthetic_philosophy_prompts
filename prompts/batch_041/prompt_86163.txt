I am a high school student working on an independent project about self-reference in biology and in formal systems, and I would like help composing a poem for it. Please act as a poet who also knows molecular biology and the philosophy of mind well enough to get the details right, and as a careful philosopher of science who will not let a pretty metaphor stand in for a real structural parallel. Think the problem through before writing, and begin by brainstorming at least three candidate formal devices for the poem, sketching each in a sentence or two; then choose the one that does the most structural work and write the poem. If my description below garbles any of the biology or the logic, correct me in your note at the end.

Here is the material I want the poem to render. In the toy biochemistry called typogenetics, which I read about in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979), a strand is a sequence over a small alphabet. An enzyme is a short protein-like molecule that recognizes one particular short sequence on a strand, binds there, and then performs a typographical operation on the strand: cutting it, inserting a segment, deleting a segment, moving a segment, or copying a segment. Crucially, each enzyme is itself encoded by a strand, so the same alphabet that gets manipulated also specifies the things that do the manipulating. The puzzle Hofstadter sets is whether a strand can be found that encodes exactly the enzymes needed to build a copy of itself. The philosophical payoff is that such a strand is at once a message and a machine: as a bare sequence of symbols it is inert data to be read and copied, while as a specification of enzymes it is a program that acts on sequences. That double role is the heart of the analogy with Gödel's construction, where the formulas of a formal system become, at a higher level, statements about formulas — meaningless marks at one level, meaning at another.

Now the poem. Write sixteen lines, with the following constraints.

Two refrains, word-for-word identical, but punctuated so that the first occurrence reads as a description (a statement about what a strand is) and the second reads as an instruction (a command issued to an enzyme, or to the reader). The difference between the two should be the poem's central event, not a decoration.

The middle of the poem should enact the enzyme operations on the poem's own language: at least one cut, one insertion, one reversal, and one copying. These must be visible in the structure — a line broken across a caesura, a word carried in from an earlier line, a line returned in reverse order, a line repeated with one element changed — not merely named in the text.

The last line must deliver the reader back to the first line so that the poem closes into a loop. A reader should feel the loop happen rather than be told about it.

The poem has to survive being read aloud. If you use a device that vanishes in speech, give the poem a second reading that does not.

The poem must be accurate. Someone who knows the typogenetics puzzle should not wince at the biology, and someone who knows the incompleteness theorem should not wince at the analogy. Do not name Gödel, Hofstadter, DNA, or enzymes unless the structure has already earned the name; a name-drop in place of the work is a failure.

Write every word yourself. Do not quote or paraphrase any passage from an existing text, and do not include citations or a reading list.

After the poem, add a prose note of roughly 150 to 200 words addressed to my class. In it, identify which formal feature of the poem corresponds to enzyme recognition, which to the cut-and-insert operations, and which to the strand's double role as data and program. State plainly one respect in which the analogy breaks down — one way a typogenetic strand and a poem are not alike. Finally, say whether the poem is a genuine self-replicating structure or only a picture of one, and why. If any constraint forced you to compromise the poem, say where.

The poem will be read aloud at the end of a class session on self-reference. My classmates know some molecular biology and no formal logic, so the poem should work for them while still being right for someone who knows the puzzle.
