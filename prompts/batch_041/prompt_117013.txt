I'm a second-year PhD student in STS — my project is on technical standards and infrastructure, so I end up in a lot of philosophy-of-technology arguments without actually being one — and I keep hitting the same rhetorical wall in reading groups. Last week we were arguing about those benches with the armrest dividers down the middle, the ones built so nobody can lie down on them; a friend said the bench 'encodes' a judgement about who deserves to rest in public space, and another friend — a propper analytic philosopher, the sort who makes you define every word before you're allowed to speak — rolled his eyes and said, well, benches don't exclude people, people exclude people. It's the same move as guns don't kill people, people kill people, which I've also heard from a tech-policy person in my department, who uses it to mean roughly: design is downstream of politics, so stop pretending the object is doing anything on its own. I think there's a genuine disagreement buried in there, but every time I try to get the two of them to state it they both insist the slogan is just obviously true and we go round in circles for an hour.

What I want from you is a menu rather than a verdict on who's right: the distinct precise claims that guns don't kill people, people kill people could be making when it's deployed in an arguement of this shape about artefacts and politics. I'd like the readings separated along the axes that actually do work here — what kind of cause the slogan is denying or asserting (sufficient, necessary, difference-making, or none of the above), whose intentions it treats as the real locus of politics, whether it's a claim about how things are designed or about how they get used, whether it's doing causal explanation or blame allocation, and whether 'politics' is meant to be the designer's intent, the artefact's effects, or the distribution of who bears the costs. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and — this is the part I care about most — whether that reading actually gives my friend what he wants, i.e. whether it touches the bench claim at all, plus what the bench claim would have to amount to for the slogan to be a real objection to it. Also tell me which reading the people who actually repeat this line in policy arguments probably mean, since I suspect they oscillate between two of them without noticing. Don't send me a reading list, I've read plenty of the literature already; I want the disambiguation itself. British spellings please, my supervisor keeps marking me down for writing 'analyze'.

Format it as a numbered menu of readings, then a short table with columns reading / precise claim / verdict (platitude, contentious, false) / does it hit the bench claim (yes, no, or only if ...), and finish with a single last line in the form Verdict: <short label for the reading my friend most likely means>.
