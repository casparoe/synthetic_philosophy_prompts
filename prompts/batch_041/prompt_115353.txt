A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences, and we got nowhere, because we kept sliding between two different claims: that a preference cycle is a defect in the shape of a person's preferences, and that it is a defect in how much of the future that person can see. I would like you to build me a case that forces the issue instead of trading intuitions.

The case I want: a single realistic scenario in which one agent has a strict preference cycle over three ordinary goods — A over B, B over C, C over A — with the preferences complete, stable over time, and fully known to the agent, and in which the agent is nevertheless not vulnerable to the standard pump, the sequence of trades that walks an agent around its cycle, charging a small fee at each step, and returns it to where it started with less money. The immunity has to be earned. It must come from something about the agent or the situation that a careful defender of the pump would have to concede is principled, not from the agent being indifferent, ignorant of its own preferences, unable to compute, unable to trade, or refusing all offers out of stubbornness. The goods should be things an ordinary person might actually have a cycle over, the fees should be amounts that person would notice, the trader should be an ordinary person rather than a supernatural bookie with knowledge of the future, and the scenario itself should fit in a single paragraph. A case that stipulates the immunity in the setup rather than generating it from the features you describe fails the brief.

Then do the work of showing the case does what I asked:

1. State the agent's preferences and the exact sequence of trades the pump would attempt, and identify the step at which it fails, spelling out the reasoning at that step.
2. Name the feature doing the work and classify it. Is it a fact about the agent's preference structure, about its foresight or information, about a commitment technology available to it, or about the trading environment?
3. Remove that feature and say whether the case collapses back into an ordinary pump.
4. Give the strongest reply available to a defender of the pump. In particular, suppose the trader may make the offers one at a time, spaced out, with no pattern the agent can detect, and suppose the agent's preferences do not change across the sequence. If your only defence is that the agent cannot see the sequence coming, say so plainly, and then say what survives. If the case can be built so that the agent ends up better off by its own lights while the trader is out of pocket, so much the better.
5. Finally, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on decision theory: whether the immunity you have built corresponds to a recognised position or is your own construction, which steps in the standard presentation of the pump are themselves disputed, and where the argument has to lean on a claim about rationality that is not common ground. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
