I want an honest map of the defeaters for a view I have been defending, which I call the part-relative design-norm account of disease. Its core claim is that 'disease' applies to parts and processes rather than to organisms as wholes: a token part is diseased when it fails to perform the function that its selection history assigned to it, and this failure is a purely biological fact, so attributions of disease need not track any disvalue, suffering or social judgement. On this account an organism can be riddled with diseased parts yet remain a healthy whole, and a part can be diseased without anyone being the worse off for it.

Tell me what would count against this — not the familiar complaint that naturalism ignores the patient's experience, but the specific cases, arguments and results that ought to move me, ranked by how decisive they would be. I should particularly like to know how the account handles parts whose selection history is indeterminate (spandrels, drift, traits under no selection, and recently engineered traits with no history at all); whether part-relative disease is even coherent once one accepts that function ascriptions are holistic, so that no part has a function in isolation; and whether the view can accommodate the possibility that one and the same malfunction is health-preserving in one organism and devastating in another without quietly importing the norm it swore off.

Finish by saying which of these the account's own defenders should concede, and whether, if the view survives them all, it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that matters in a case of this kind.
