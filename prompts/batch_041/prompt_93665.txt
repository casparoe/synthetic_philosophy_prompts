A retired actuary on our residents' association keeps ending the argument about the council's flood-defence plan with one line: 'a pound today is worth more than a pound in a hundred years'. He takes it to show that we should spend the money on things that help people now and let our successors build the sea wall when they actually need it. I am fairly sure the line is being made to do more work than it can bear, but I cannot say precisely what it is claiming. Please separate out the distinct theses it might be expressing — about what money can be exchanged for, about pure time preference, about the assumption that future people will be richer, about the probability that there will be anyone there to benefit, and about the thought that any coherent ranking of futures must discount somehow — and for each say whether it is a tautology, a substantive empirical claim, a moral claim or simply false; whether it survives the translation from pounds to lives; and whether any of the readings actually delivers his conclusion about the wall. Give me the menu, with a verdict on each item rather than on him.
