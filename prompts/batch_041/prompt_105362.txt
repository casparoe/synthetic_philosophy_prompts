I'm a freelance writer who covers science and ideas for a few magazines, and I'm trying to get my head around something for an essay I'm doing on ritual as a kind of pedagogical stopgap. I did a biology degree and haven't read any medieval philosophy since one survey course in college, so I'm well out of my depth, and I've been going back and forth about this for weeks with a friend whose actually read the stuff.

Here's my problem. Maimonides, in the Guide of the Perplexed, seems to explain the Torah's laws about animal sacrifice as a concession -- roughly: a people who had grown up surounded by sacrificial worship couldn't have been asked to drop it overnight, so the Law takes the practice over and redirects it to God alone, instead of commanding it because the offerings do something for God. But the same Maimonides is adamant, everywhere, that God is incorporeal and unaffected, that nothing we do adds to or subtracts from God, and yet the Torah itself describes the offerings in what sounds like frankly culinary terms -- something God smells and finds pleasing, food offered to God. What I want from you is the conceptual issue, not the history: what is the 'concession' a concession to, what work is it doing, and is the reading actually stable? Specifically, what's left of the value of the sacrifice if the rite does nothing for God and was only ever a response to what people were already used to -- is it anything more than historical packaging, and does he have the resources to say otherwise? And how does he get away with reusing the Torah's own sacrificial imagery, nose and appetite and all, while denying that God has anything of the kind?

Please don't hand me the standard summary of his position -- I can find that. Reason it out, and be explicit about which bits are in the text and which are your own reconstruction. And if my framing is off from the start, say so; I have a nagging feeling I've smuggled in a Christian-sounding idea of accommodation that isn't really his, and if that's right I'd rather be told than humoured. Same if the question can't honestly be answered without the actual text in front of us.
