I'm teaching a short unit on the three liberties to a senior seminar, and I want to run it through a novel instead of just assigning the usual Berlin essay. The setup: negative liberty as non-interference, positive liberty as self-mastery, and then the republican idea of non-domination that Pettit and Skinner push, where liberty isn't just being left alone but not having a master who could interfere with you at will even if he never does. My hunch is that Stevens in The Remains of the Day is close to a perfect test case for the republican view. Nobody ever forces him to do anything. Lord Darlington isn't a tyrant, he's a gentleman who mostly leaves Stevens alone, and Stevens plainly takes pride in his work and his station. And yet the whole novel reads to me as a study in unfreedom: a man who can't form an end of his own, can't say no, has no standing to object, and has absorbed the master's purposes so completely that he can't even name his own feelings about Miss Kenton. Read it that way and tell me if I'm right, and tell me where I'm flattening the book.

Where does it actually dramatize non-domination vs. mere non-interference, and which scenes should I put in front of the class? Where is it really about positive liberty instead, or about Berlin's worry that the 'real self' turns out to just be whoever gets to define you? And here's the part I most want your view on: where does the novel push back on all three framings? Stevens's dignity is presented as a kind of self-mastery, maybe even as an achievement, and it seems possible that the tragedy is that he gave his devotion to a bad cause, Darlington's appeasement, rather than that devotion itself is servile. If that's right then the republican remedy of having no masters may be exactly the wrong medicine, and the novel may be showing something the theory can't say. I also have a nagging worry about whether the republican frame even gets a grip on him, since domination usually presupposes a self with its own ends that a master could ride roughshod over, and Stevens's ends are constituted by the role itself, so there may be nothing underneath to dominate. Is that a real problem for the view or just me overreading? And say something about the ending, the banter with Farraday and the pier, is that a liberation or the same dependence simply moved to a new employer?

I dont need plot summary and don't quote anything at me, I've read it twice, what I want is the reading, plus an honest account of where the novel refuses the frame rather than illustrating it.
