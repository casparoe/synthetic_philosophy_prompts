Write me an essay on the dogmatism paradox, and I want an actual verdict at the end, not a tour of the literature. The setup, in case you want to frame it your own way: if I know that P, then any future evidence that looks like it tells against P has to be misleading, because P is true, so it starts to look like knowing P entitles me to ignore later counter-evidence, or even to refuse to engage with it at all. That conclusion is absurd, so either the reasoning is broken somewhere or knowledge really does come with a license that sounds worse than it is. Say which, and defend it.

Then take it somewhere I haven't seen anyone go: if the license is real, does it also cover preventing the misleading evidence from ever existing, rather than just ignoring it? Concretish case: a lab director knows hypothesis H is true and is good at predicting how a badly designed trial will come out, and she's confident this trial will produce a result that looks like it refutes H. By the dogmatist's lights, can she just not run it, or bin the data before anyone sees it? My gut says the answer flips here, but I can't say what does the flipping, so tell me whether it flips and what's doing the work.

And don't oversell yourself: say where your argument is most likely to fall apart, and what would have to turn out to be true for you to land on the opposite verdict.
