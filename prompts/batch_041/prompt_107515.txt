"Good's theorem says that a free piece of information can never lower the expected utility of a decision. More carefully: suppose you face a decision problem — a set of acts, a set of states, a utility function — and someone offers, at no cost, to tell you the value of some random variable X before you choose. Let U* be the expected utility of choosing the best act now, without the information. Let U** be the expected utility, averaged over the possible values of X, of the strategy: see X, then choose the act that maximises expected utility given X. The theorem says U** ≥ U*.

The proof is a domination argument. One policy available to you after seeing X is simply to ignore X and pick the act you would have picked anyway. That policy has expected utility exactly U*, so the best post-observation policy cannot do worse than U*, and averaging over the possible values of X preserves the inequality. The one substantive assumption is that the information is free; if it costs something the theorem has nothing to say, and paying more than the value of information is a bad deal.

The upshot is that a rational agent is never made worse off by looking. Having seen the data, you are at least as well off as you would have been flying blind — the theorem rules out the possibility that the new evidence leads you somewhere worse than where you started. The same domination argument, run against a less informative experiment, gives the general Blackwell result that a more informative experiment is always at least as good as a less informative one, with Good's theorem being the special case where the comparison experiment is completely uninformative.

It follows that a rational agent should always gather all the available evidence before deciding. Since information can't hurt and only ever helps, there is no reason to decide early, and the principle of total evidence — base your choice on everything you know — is vindicated by the theorem."
