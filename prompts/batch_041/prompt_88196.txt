I've been running a small reading group on formal epistemology for a few years — it grew out of the rationalist blogosphere and never entirely left it — and this term we worked through the paradoxes of rational belief. Last week I set a written exercise on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I have one participant's submission to grade. I'd like you to grade it: the mark, and more importantly the marginal comments, part by part.

The exercise (20 points, weights given in the parts) was this.

**Question.** Two agents are considering the same list of 1,000 claims, C1 through C1000, drawn from the same source. Each agent assigns credence 0.999 to each Ci considered on its own.

Agent X has been told, by a source she trusts completely, that exactly one of the 1,000 claims is false. She assigns credence 1 to "exactly one of C1 … C1000 is false."

Agent Y has been told nothing about this particular list. But she has a long track record with lists of this kind from this source, and on that basis she assigns credence 0.999 to "at least one of C1 … C1000 is false."

(a) [8 points] Set out the lottery paradox and the preface paradox in their usual forms, and say which of the two agents, if either, is in the position of each.

(b) [6 points] X is certain of the "some error" proposition; Y is merely highly confident of it. Does that difference matter — either to whether the paradox is genuine in each case, or to how it should be resolved? Defend your answer.

(c) [6 points] Evaluate the following proposed resolution.

R: An agent may rationally hold each of a set of beliefs whose conjunction is false, provided she does not also believe the conjunction. Since neither X nor Y believes the conjunction of the 1,000 claims, neither holds a proposition and its negation; the paradox dissolves once we drop the assumption that belief is closed under conjunction.

Does R work? Does it work equally for X and Y?

---

Here is what the participant wrote.

---

(a) The lottery paradox. A fair lottery is held with n tickets and exactly one winner. However high we set the threshold for rational acceptance — short of 1 — we can choose n large enough that the probability of any given ticket's losing exceeds it. So an agent who accepts every proposition whose probability passes the threshold will accept, of each ticket, that it loses; and if acceptance is closed under conjunction, she will also accept that every ticket loses. But she knows that one ticket wins. So a rule for acceptance that is both threshold-based and closed under conjunction issues in a contradiction.

The preface paradox is the same shape with the certainty removed. The author of a long book believes each of her claims, since each is well supported; she also believes, on the basis of her own track record, that at least one claim in the book is false. So she believes each conjunct and disbelieves their conjunction.

X is in the lottery case: she is certain that exactly one claim is false, and each claim is 0.999 likely, so a threshold-and-closure agent accepts each claim and their conjunction, contradicting the certainty that one is false. Y is in the preface case: she is 0.999 confident of each claim and 0.999 confident that at least one is false.

(b) Yes, it matters, and it makes Y's case the worse of the two. X's credences are coherent: 1,000 claims at 0.999 each, with exactly one false, is just the distribution you get by picking the false claim uniformly at random. Y's are not coherent. If the 1,000 claims are independent, her credence that they are all true is 0.999^1000, which is about 0.37; so her credence that at least one is false should be about 0.63, not 0.999. But she has to be 0.999 confident that at least one is false, since that is the whole point of the preface case. So her credences cannot all be what the story says they are. The preface paradox, unlike the lottery paradox, is therefore a genuine inconsistency in the agent's credences, not merely a collision between a threshold rule for acceptance and a principle of conjunction.

(c) R is the familiar move of giving up agglomeration. For X it works: X does not believe the conjunction of the 1,000 claims, so there is no proposition in her belief set that is both believed and disbelieved, and the contradiction disappears. For Y it does not work, because Y's problem is not a failure of closure but a violation of the probability calculus, as I argued in (b); no amount of tinkering with what she believes about conjunctions will repair credences that cannot be assigned to the claims simultaneously.
