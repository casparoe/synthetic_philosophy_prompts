For most of my working life I was an experimental physiologist; since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I have reached a question I cannot settle on my own. In the autobiographical chapters of Ecce Homo, and again in scattered remarks elsewhere in the late work, Nietzsche treats nutrition, climate, place, and recreation as the decisive questions of a philosopher's existence, ridicules alcohol and heavy German cooking, and argues that the body's regimen fixes the limits of what a thinker is able to think; as I read him, he also turns his own early regimen into a case study in how a scholar destroys himself, and he treats morality, religion, and the ascetic ideal as conditions to be diagnosed physiologically rather than arguments to be weighed.

The controversy I want adjudicated is whether this material is philosophy or symptom. On the deflationary reading it exposes the doctrines as by-products of a sick man's private regimen. On the sympathetic reading Nietzsche is doing naturalistic philosophy of mind, making the body a condition of thought rather than a distraction from it. I have been trying to construct a criterion for deciding between them, and I want the criterion tested rather than merely applied: a physiological account of a doctrine is deflationary only if the doctrine's content would be unchanged under a different physiology; where the content would change, the physiology belongs to the doctrine's subject matter and the deflationary charge misses its target.

Write an essay that reaches a definite verdict on which parts, if any, of Nietzsche's diet-and-health material survive as philosophy, whether my criterion survives contact with the texts, and what a working scientist should make of the specific claims about food, drink, and climate. Address the self-referential problem head-on: if all thinking is an involuntary confession of the body, what is the standing of that claim itself? Take a position and defend it, with reasons; do not end by listing the options. No more than 900 words.
