Next month I'm running a session for a mixed reading group on the paradox of fiction — two lit grad students, one psychologist, a philosopher, and a lawyer friend who mostly turns up for the biscuits. Forty-five minutes, and I want the puzzle to actually land rather than everyone settling into the comfortable view that of course it's fine to cry at a film. So dont explain the paradox to me; plan how I teach it.

What order do the pieces have to go in, and which piece has to be in place before the puzzle even looks like a puzzle? I assume it's the thought that being moved by something requires believing it's really there, but tell me if that's wrong and what it costs me if I get the order backwards.

Which single case carries the whole thing, and what does it need to have and lack? My candidate is the staged documentary: you sob at a death, then learn the death was faked, and now you don't know what to do with the feeling you've already had. Either commit to that case or tell me why it can't do the work, and give me the replacement with reasons.

Then the misconceptions, and exactly where in the sequence I head each one off — the lawyer will say we know it isn't real, the lit student will say suspension of disbelief, the psychologist will want to make the whole thing an empirical question about weather the emotion was genuine. Finally, the one question I can put at the end that tells me they've got the puzzle rather than just enjoyed the chat, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud. Pick one case and one closing question — don't hand me a menu of options.
