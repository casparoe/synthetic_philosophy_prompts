I'm a junior at a high school in Ohio, and I run our school's philosophy club, which meets on Thursdays in the library. We just spent three weeks on the Liar paradox, and it turned out to be far harder than anyone expected, so I want to write up something substantial for the members who missed those meetings. Specifically, I want a dialogue between two characters who each defend a different diagnosis of what the Liar shows about truth, and I need them to be evenly matched: neither one a strawman, and each answering the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. Character A holds that the Liar sentence, the one that says of itself that it is not true, is neither true nor false, and concludes that we should give up the assumption that every declarative sentence is either true or false; on this view there are gaps in truth value, while the rule that no sentence is ever both true and false stays intact. Character B holds that the Liar sentence is both true and false, and concludes that we should give up the rule that no sentence is both, while keeping the assumption that every sentence is either true or false; on this view truth values come in gluts rather than gaps. Both of them insist on the ordinary thought that a sentence is true exactly when things are the way it says they are, so neither is allowed to dismiss the Liar as meaningless, and neither is allowed to solve the problem by simply forbidding sentences to refer to themselves.

I'd like the dialogue to do real work rather than just sketch the two positions. First, each character should aim a revenge sentence at the other's theory, a new sentence that appears to break the theory even after it has handled the original Liar, and then defend against the one aimed at them; I've read that both sides have this problem, but I've never understood exactly what the sentence is, so please construct them rather than gesture at them. Second, I want them to wrestle with a Curry-style sentence, one that says of itself that if it is true then some obviously false claim is the case; the part that fascinates me is that such a sentence seems to let you prove anything at all without ever using negation, so I'd like each character to say honestly what their theory has to restrict in order to block it, and whether that restriction costs them something they were counting on. Third, our club treasurer, who is very practical and thinks the whole topic is a waste of time, wants to know whether ordinary people could still say everything they need to say if either theory were right, especially when we use true to endorse a long list of claims all at once or to agree with someone whose exact words we've forgotten; please give her a real answer. End the dialogue with each character saying what the other's view gets wrong about how the word true actually behaves, and which of them, if either, is closer to being right. Don't force a tidy resolution if the disagreement genuinely remains open.
