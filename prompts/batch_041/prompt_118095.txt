For the past several years, since retiring from a career in experimental geophysics, I have been trying to educate myself in philosophy, and this term I have joined a continuing-education seminar on the history of social theory. We have reached the French material on power, and I have just finished a trade book on Foucault that a colleague recommended. Before the next meeting I should like to know how much of it I can trust, because I cannot tell whether my discomfort is a sign that Foucault is mistaken or merely that I have been given a poor account of him.

The book's account, as best I can render it in my own words, runs as follows. Foucault's central discovery is that knowledge is power: what a society counts as true at a given moment is simply whatever serves the interests of those who dominate it, so there is no truth of the matter to be had, only the power to enforce one version of events. His histories of the prison, the clinic, and the human sciences are meant to show that these institutions are instruments of social control, and that the modern shift from the sovereign's power to punish to the training of bodies in schools, barracks, and factories is really a change from crude repression to a more efficient repression of the same kind. The panopticon is his master image: because the inmate may be watched at any moment, he becomes his own guard, and Foucault accordingly predicts that video cameras and mass data collection are the natural culmination of the story he tells. Since power is everywhere and resistance is futile, the book concludes, the only honest response is to abandon the pretense of objectivity altogether; and the author concedes that this is why most philosophers now treat Foucault as a literary figure rather than an argumentative one, his position being the familiar sort that refutes itself by denying truth while claiming to tell it.

I have no wish to be unfair to a thinker I have barely read, but I should confess my priors: as a scientist I am disposed to think that any view making truth a function of power has sawed off the branch it sits on, and this summary fits that suspicion so neatly that I have grown suspicious of the summary in turn. Could you go through the account claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a careful and sympathetic reader of Foucault would accept it, wince at it, or reject it outright? I should particularly like to know which of the errors are the ordinary simplifications one meets in popularizations and which are flat denials of things Foucault actually maintained — for instance, about whether he takes himself to be telling the truth, and about what he means by resistance and by the productivity of power. Finally, once the account has been corrected, I should like to know whether the self-refutation objection, stated in its strongest form rather than in the book's version, still has force; and if so, whether it tells against Foucault in particular or against any view of his general shape. Please do not send me to the literature — I am after the shape of the position, not a bibliography.
