Hey, so I'm deep in my diss chapter on epiphenomenalism and I keep banging my head against the phenomenal judgment problem. Quick version of the setup so we're on the same page: epiphenomenalism says qualia are caused by physical brain states but don't themselves cause anything. Fine. But we obviously make judgments about our own experience — "this red is more saturated than that one," "this pain is throbbing rather than sharp," that sort of thing. If qualia are causally inert, then the physical states have to be doing all the work of producing those judgments. And then it looks like a huge coinsidence that the judgments happen to be about the qualia, or that they're accurate at all. That's the paradox, roughly.

I want an essay where you defend a position on it. And I want you to engage with one specific move, because I think it's the most interesting escape route and I'm not sure it works. The move is this: the paradox only bites if you assume a judgment about a quale has to be *caused by* that quale. Instead, suppose the phenomenal state and the judgment are both effects of a common physical cause, so they reliably co-vary the way two effects of a shared cause do — like two clocks set by the same broadcast signal, agreeing without either one driving the other — and the judgment's "aboutness" is grounded in that structural correspondence rather than in a causal link. Question is whether that actually dissolves the paradox or just relocates it, since now you owe a seperate account of why the correspondence is reliable and not just a brute cosmic coincidence. (Maybe you can give that account, maybe you can't — that's the interesting part.)

Don't give me a survey. Take a side: does the epiphenomenalist have a viable story about phenomenal judgment, or is the paradox fatal? Give me your best argument for the view you reject too, and say why it still doesn't beat yours. I'd much rather read a confidently wrong verdict than a wishy-washy both-sides thing.
