I am a philosopher of mind preparing a position paper on the nature of the self, and before I commit to it in print I want a rigorous consistency audit of the theses I intend to defend. Please act as an expert in the philosophy of mind and in theories of self-reference and self-modelling — someone thoroughly familiar with Hofstadter's work on self-referential loops but not disposed to defend it by default. Think carefully and proceed in stages: before you assess anything, spend a short paragraph reconstructing the most natural reading of each thesis below and the motivation someone would have for holding it; only then begin the audit.

Here is my position set.

(1) The self is real, but it is a self-referential pattern — a loop that refers to itself through its own representations — rather than a substance, a thing, or a special kind of stuff. Talk of the "I" is talk at a particular level of description, not talk about an additional entity.

(2) I am a physicalist: the loop is realized in physical machinery, and there is no non-physical residue left over once the physical description is complete.

(3) What makes me me is the abstract pattern, not the particular atoms or the medium that carries it. The same loop realized in silicon would be the same self.

(4) A system that produces fluent, human-like outputs by exhaustive lookup — one that has the answers pre-stored rather than generated — has no inner life and is not a self, however well it mimics one. Nothing is actually referring to anything in such a system.

(5) Selfhood and consciousness come in degrees, keyed to the depth and richness of the self-model: a dog has less of a self than I do, and a thermostat has none.

(6) On the standard thought experiments: I would survive a gradual, part-by-part replacement of my neurons with functionally equivalent silicon, but I would not survive being copied, even perfectly and without error. A perfect copy is a new person, and I have no more reason to care about it than about any other person.

I suspect these hang together, but I do not trust my own eye here. Please do the following, in order. (a) Identify every pair or small subset of the six that is in genuine tension, and say precisely what the conflict is. (b) Separate the genuine tensions from the ones that only look like tensions and dissolve once a distinction is drawn — be explicit about which apparent conflicts are merely verbal or rest on an equivocation. (c) Rank the genuine tensions by severity. (d) For each, state the cheapest revision that would remove it and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. (e) Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most at the least cost, and whether the resulting package would still be recognizably the position I started with. Do not hedge by declaring everything in tension — I need you to discriminate. Where a thesis is ambiguous in a way that matters (for instance, in what "pattern" or "realized" is being taken to mean), say so and tell me which disambiguation I should commit to.
