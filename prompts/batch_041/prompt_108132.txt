Heres something thats bugged me since college and I've never gotten a straight answer. In basically every math class we prove that things exist without ever producing them. There's a real number that isn't algebraic -- the algebraics are countable and the reals aren't, so done, no example needed, nobody in the room blinks. Some polynomial has a root between 0 and 1 because it's negative at one end and positive at the other, and we move on. The professor doesn't apologize. That's just math.

But then the axiom of choice comes up and everyone changes their tune. Textbooks put an asterisk next to a theorem, or a little footnote saying this proof uses the axiom of choice, in a tone that suggests you've been warned about something. Banach-Tarski gets trotted out as a horror story about spheres. The idea that you can cut a ball into finitely many pieces and reassemble them into two balls the same size as the original is treated as a reason to distrust the axiom, or at least to feel weird about it. Same with the non-measurable sets, and the fact that you can't write down a formula that picks one out.

Meanwhile the exact same people use choice-dependent results all day without noticing. Every vector space has a basis, so the reals have a basis over the rationals -- a basis nobody can ever exhibit -- and that's in the first week of a linear algebra course with no asterisk anywhere. Hahn-Banach, Tychonoff, the existence of ultrafilters, all of it gets used like ordinary plumbing. The scandal is that Banach-Tarski is a consequence of the same axiom doing all that quiet work.

So I want to know what philosophical committments this whole practice presupposes. What view of mathematical existence is baked into the nonconstructive existence proof -- that there exists is a genuine, witness-free claim about a mind-independent domain, or something weaker? What does the double standard say about the status of the axiom of choice: that it's a real axiom, an optional convenience you could take or leave, or a rule that's true in some mathematical universes and false in others? And can you actually hold the calm attitude toward the intermediate value theorem and the nervous attitude toward Banach-Tarski at once without contradicting yourself, or does the queasiness about choice apply just as well to the excluded middle you already used in the first proof? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out -- constructivism, finitism, predicativism -- and which is it really compatible with, full-blooded platonism, formalism, the set-theoretic multiverse, whatever? Be careful to seperate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely happens to be comfortable with, and tell me where it's underdetermined, so that several different philosophies of math would license exactly the same classroom behavior and the same textbooks.

Then take it one step further. If we actually took the constructivist objection seriously and meant it, what would have to change? What would a real analysis course look like, which of these theorems survive, and which of the quiet workhorse results turn out to be luxuries we were only affording ourselves because nobody was checking? Please write it in American English, and don't bother with a reading list or who-said-what -- I want the argument, not the bibliography.
