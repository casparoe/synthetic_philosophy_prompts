I'm a PhD student working on the metaphysics of language, and I've spent the past year defending a view about propositional unity that I'm no longer sure is either trivially true or quietly incoherent. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of language and metaphysics who knows the Bradley's-regress literature and the contemporary debate over the unity of the proposition inside out, and to give me an honest map of my view's exposure. Here is the view, stated as carefully as I can manage. A proposition is not a set, a mereological sum, or a bare plurality of its constituents, and its unity is not an additional constituent or an additional relation laid on top of them. Instead, the constituents themselves, when they occur in a proposition, occur in combination: being combined is a mode or way of being of the constituents, not a further item that unifies them. On this view, the proposition that a is F is not a, F, and some unifying relation R; it is a and F occurring jointly, where the joint occurrence is immanent in the constituents rather than transcending them as one more entity. I like the view because it appears to stop Bradley's regress at the first step without positing a brute unifier — the regress being what you get when, for any further item supposed to unify the constituents, you ask in turn what unifies it with them — but I no longer trust that I've earned that "without." The objection that frightens me most is the direction or order problem: if the combination is merely a mode of the constituents, what distinguishes a's being F from F's being a, or aRb from bRa, when the constituents and the mode are the same? I suspect any answer either reintroduces structure (positions, slots, a relation) or leaves the difference brute.

What I want from you is not a survey but an adversarial audit: which observations, arguments, intuitions, and formal results would count against the immanence view, sorted by how decisive they are, together with an honest verdict on whether I have stated the view so as to insulate it from criticism. Please think carefully before writing — brainstorm first about the jobs a proposition's unity is supposed to do (truth-aptness, being the object of an attitude, explaining logical structure and entailment, distinguishing a proposition from a mere list of its parts) and only then sort the objections. Where an objection is weak, say so rather than hedging; where the view can answer an objection only by adding an unprincipled epicycle, say that too. I don't want a reading list or claims about who said what; give me the arguments themselves.

Structure the answer with these headings, in this order: (1) "The view, restated" — at most 150 words, restating it in your own terms and flagging any ambiguity or hidden commitment in my formulation; (2) "The case against, ranked" — roughly 800 words, a ranked list from most to least damaging, where each entry names the objection, gives its strongest form, states what the view must say to answer it, and assesses whether that answer is available or ad hoc. Make sure to cover at least the explanatory rather than merely entity version of the regress; the direction/order problem; whether the view can distinguish a proposition from a mere plurality at all; whether it collapses into a rival (propositions as sui generis entities, a trope-theoretic account, a Fregean unsaturated-constituent account), in which case it is a notational variant rather than an alternative; and whether it can explain what propositions are for; (3) "What a defender would concede" — roughly 200 words on which objections a sympathetic defender should grant as genuine costs and which they may legitimately dismiss as question-begging; (4) "Is it insulated?" — roughly 150 words on whether the view has been stated so as to be unfalsifiable, and if so whether that is a fatal defect or an acceptable feature of a primitivist account of unity; (5) "Bottom line" — three to five sentences telling me what to worry about first.
