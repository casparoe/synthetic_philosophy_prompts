I chair the Committee on Curricular Requirements at a mid-sized public university. At our next meeting we must dispose of a petition from a registered student organization, the Society for Creationist Inquiry, seeking exemption for its members from the mandatory first-year module on scientific reasoning. The module requires students to treat certain propositions as settled: that the same causes produce the same effects across time and space; that memory, perception, and testimony are generally reliable; that the external world does not radically change when unobserved; that the findings of mainstream science are not the product of a vast, coordinated conspiracy; and that a proposition cannot be both true and false. The petitioners argue that these are not neutral methodological rules but substantive metaphysical commitments, and that requiring assent to them burdens their religious exercise. The module’s defenders reply that without such commitments no inquiry at all is possible, and that the university may require them as conditions of a common intellectual practice. A third group on the committee wants a compromise: exempt students from affirming the propositions but require them to study how the propositions function in scientific practice.

Our committee’s working paper, which I summarize here, draws a distinction I would like you to use. It distinguishes Constitutive Hinges from Denominational Hinges. Constitutive Hinges are propositions that are not themselves the object of ongoing inquiry but that make a practice possible; to doubt them globally is to cease to participate in the practice, not to raise a live hypothesis within it. Denominational Hinges are propositions that a particular community treats as certain and that structure its identity, but that are not required for the practice in question and could be denied without making the practice incoherent. The working paper also proposes an asymmetry test: a hinge is constitutive if denying it makes the practice’s internal standards of success and failure unintelligible; it is denominational if denying it merely changes the community’s self-description. The paper is Wittgensteinian in inspiration, drawing on the hinge epistemology of On Certainty, but it does not purport to interpret Wittgenstein. It also distinguishes between exempting a student from affirming a hinge and exempting a student from being able to recognize how that hinge operates in the practice.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete disposition of the petition—approve, deny, or approve with specified conditions—and give the committee reasons it can act on. It should engage seriously with the petitioners’ strongest point, namely that what the module calls neutral method may smuggle in a contested picture of the world, and with the strongest point on the other side, namely that the university cannot teach any discipline if students may opt out of the conditions that make the discipline possible. It should say explicitly which of the listed propositions, if any, the committee should treat as Constitutive Hinges and which as Denominational Hinges, and whether the distinction survives the petitioners’ objection. It should also address whether the proposed compromise—exemption from affirmation but not from recognition—is stable, or whether it collapses into either full exemption or full requirement. The memo should be usable in a fifteen-minute discussion: no more than 900 words, with a clear bottom line in the first paragraph and a short section headed Risks of the Recommended Course.
