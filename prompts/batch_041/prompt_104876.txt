I'm a novelist — third book, set in Geneva in the late 1750s — and I've gotten myself tangled up in Rousseau's reply to d'Alembert about whether Geneva should have a theatre. My narrator is a young Genevese who's just read d'Alembert's article on his city in the Encyclopédie and is half convinced by it. The argument there is that Geneva could keep both plays and morals if the actors were regulated and made respectable, that a theatre would sharpen the citizens' taste and give them a delicacy of feeling that's otherwise very hard to come by, and that Geneva would then unite, as d'Alembert puts it, "la sagesse de Lacédémone à la politesse d’Athenes" — with the added bonus of shaming the rest of Europe out of its contempt for actors. It's charming writing and my narrator swallows it whole.

What I cant get straight is Rousseau's answer, and I need to before I write the scene where the kid is disabused. He doesn't really argue that plays are dirty, and as far as I can tell he doesn't deny that the stage refines taste and sentiment — he seems to grant it. He also doesn't think you can fix things by making actors respectable or licensing only decent plays. So where is the harm supposed to live? I keep bumping into a move where he says something like: the theatre can't change our feelings, it can only flatter and follow them, because the playwright has to please the audience as it already is — and the tears we shed for virtue on stage are somehow payment for the virtue we don't have to practice once we're back in the street. Then there's the business about small republics needing a different sort of amusement than big monarchies, something about how in a small city the point is that people see each other as themselves rather than as spectators of an illusion, which is why he keeps going on about the circles and about public festivals where everyones both audience and performer.

Help me understand the actual argument. If the plays are decent and the actors are respectable, what's the mechanism of corruption supposed to be? Is his claim empirical — this will happen in Geneva, given these people and this town — or conceptual, i.e. theatre as such can't do the moral work d'Alembert wants it to do, no matter how you regulate it? And how is the small-republic-versus-large-state contrast doing any real work, or is it just Genevese localism dressed up? One more thing I'd love: where does Rousseau think d'Alembert's picture gets its seductive force from? He clearly finds the argument dangerously attractive rather than merely wrong, and I want to know what he thinks is doing the seducing.
