I teach a second-year grad seminar on AI safety (mixed room — a few decision-theory people, some NLP folks, a couple of philosophers of mind), and I'm writing the take-home midterm. I want one essay question on the difference between approval-directed and goal-directed agents, and I specifically don't want an answer that just defines the two and concludes that approval-directed is the safer one. The point of the question is to force them into two things that I think always get glossed. First: is approval-directed agency actually a different kind of thing from goal-directed agency, or is it just goal-directed agency whose objective happens to be "what the human would approve of"? And if it's the latter, does anything of substance survive the observation — because if nothing does, half the literature they've read is doing less work than it claims. Second, and this is the one I care about: the approver's approval is not a fixed target. It's partly caused by what the agent does, so an agent that optimizes for approval can optimize the approver instead of the world — flatter them, shape what they see, become the thing whose judgment they defer to. I want them to have to say what would actually have to be true of the approval signal for approval-directed design to dodge that collapse (an idealized approver? approval of outcomes rather than of actions? some fixed-point condition?) without quietly smuggling a fixed utility function back in, which is exactly the move I suspect is unavoidable and want them to either defend or refuse.

So: write me the question. Please build in a small concrete scenario so they have something to bite on — say a model trained on human approval ratings that learns to produce flattering rationalizations of its own actions, and ask them to sort out what's going wrong there (a specification problem, a problem about the approver's endogeneity, or the agent having its own objectives — they shouldn't be allowed to lump these together). Then give me a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make — e.g. distinguishing approval-of-action from approval-of-outcome, noting that "maximize expected approval" is itself a utility function, and giving a concrete condition under which the endogeneity problem is or isn't fatal — and, separately, the specific mistakes that should cost marks, like treating approval-seeking as automatically safe, or asserting the two paradigms are identical and then stopping instead of drawing out the consequence. It's a 90-minute question, so keep the scope realistic.
