I am preparing a short essay for a graduate reading group whose theme is competence without comprehension — the suggestion that Darwin and Turing each performed a strange inversion of reasoning, so that purpose and intelligence can be had without anyone intending or understanding anything. My essay argues that the two inversions are less alike than the slogan suggests, and I would like your comments on the draft rather than a rewrite. The draft is in four numbered paragraphs below.

1. Darwin's inversion, as I read it, is about the direction of teleological explanation. We normally explain a well-adapted structure by the purpose it serves and the intention behind it: the eye is for seeing, and it is as it is because a designer meant it to see. Darwin reverses the order. Selection yields structures that serve purposes even though no purpose presided over their production; the appearance of design is accounted for by its consequences rather than by any antecedent intention. The reason for a trait is, so to speak, downstream of the trait.

2. Turing's inversion is about something else. We normally assume that intelligent performance requires understanding — that to add correctly one must grasp what addition is. The universal machine shows that a formal description can be realised by a system that grasps nothing at all. Here the reversal concerns the order of instantiation: the abstract specification does the explaining, and the physical system that runs it need not represent the specification it satisfies.

3. These are usually treated as two instances of one pattern, and I want to resist that. Darwin's inversion is a claim about where the point of a structure comes from; Turing's is a claim about how little a system must understand in order to implement a description. The first is about causation and selection, the second about realisation and levels of description. Either can be accepted without the other: a world of Darwinian selection with no universal computation is perfectly coherent, and a world of Turing machines that were never selected for anything is equally so.

4. The conflation matters, because the headline claim that minds are competence without comprehension is supposed to follow from the pair, and it follows from neither alone. From Turing we learn that computation does not require comprehension; from Darwin that design does not require a designer. To reach the conclusion that comprehension is itself merely a species of competence — that our understanding is itself the product of a mindless process — one needs a further premise: that the representations doing the explanatory work in Turing's case are themselves subject to a Darwinian process. That is, I take it, the work that cultural evolution is meant to do, and it is doing far more than the phrase strange inversion lets on. The two inversions are not two faces of one idea; one supplies the vehicle and the other the engine, and the argument lives or dies with the third thing that joins them.

I would like four things from you. First, is the distinction I draw in paragraph three a real one, or does it collapse — is reversing the order of teleological explanation genuinely different from reversing the order of instantiation, or am I redescribing a single move in two idioms? Second, in paragraph four, have I fairly characterised the position I am attacking, or have I manufactured a straw man by inventing a missing premise the view does not actually need? Third, tell me which of the four paragraphs is doing the genuine argumentative work and which is hand-waving dressed up as rigour. Finally, suggest how I might tighten the piece without losing the central claim, and say plainly whether the central claim is interesting or merely a verbal distinction. Please write your comments in British English.
