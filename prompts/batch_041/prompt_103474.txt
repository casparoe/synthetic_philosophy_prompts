I have agreed to run a single fifty-minute session for a reading group of non-specialists — intelligent adults who are comfortable with the arithmetic of expected value but have no background in decision theory or the philosophy of probability — and I want to build the session around the puzzle commonly called Pascal's mugging. I am not asking you to explain the puzzle to me. I am asking you to design the explanation: the sequence in which the ideas must be introduced, and why each has to wait for the one before it; the single example that should carry the whole session; the misconceptions the group will arrive with or develop, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be defused; the one question I can put to them at the end that would show whether the puzzle has actually landed rather than merely been heard; and what I should deliberately refuse to cover while telling them that I am refusing.

There is a constraint on the carrying example, and it is the reason I am writing to you rather than to a textbook. I want to stay away from the theological version that everyone has already met, and I have a candidate of my own that I would like you to assess and, if it fails, replace. My candidate is a mugger who demands a trivial sum and who threatens no fixed harm: he offers to inflict whatever quantity of suffering I care to name, scaling the threatened disvalue to any number that would make my expected-value calculation come out against me. In a mirror-image variant, the same figure offers to produce any quantity of good on the same open-ended terms. The example has to be one in which the group feels the pull of the calculation rather than merely being told about it, and I suspect that whether I use the threat, the reward, or the matched pair changes what they end up believing. Tell me which version should carry the session, what the others should be held back for, if anything, and what the chosen example must contain and must conspicuously lack if it is to do this work rather than distract from it.

Tell me what has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them. I take it that something about the difference between a probability being small and a probability being ignorable, and something about the difference between refusing to let a consideration move me and denying that it is a consideration at all, must come first — but I would rather you specify the dependencies than have me guess at them, including any that I have not anticipated.

Then the misconception map. The one I am most confident about is this: they will try to dissolve the puzzle by insisting that a mugger's threat deserves no credence whatsoever, and they will feel that this settles the matter. I want to know where in the sequence that move has to be met, and why meeting it too early or too late spoils everything downstream. Beyond that, give me the remaining misconceptions in the order they will actually surface, the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, and, for each, whether it is a misconception about the puzzle itself or a misconception about decision theory that the puzzle merely exposes. I expect at least one of them to arrive already carrying the slogan that one should simply maximize expected value, and at least one to arrive convinced that the puzzle is a refutation of utilitarianism rather than of anything more general; sort those into the map as you see fit.

Finally, tell me what to cut. I want an explicit list of the material that is genuinely part of the surrounding discussion but that I should omit at this level, with a sentence I can say to the group about why it is being omitted, so that the omission reads as a decision rather than as an oversight.
