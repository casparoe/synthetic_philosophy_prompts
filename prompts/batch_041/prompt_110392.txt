I am preparing a dialogue for a graduate seminar on inductive inference, and I want it to be genuinely adversarial rather than a survey of the standard positions. The two speakers are both philosophers of science who work on learning theory. One defends Solomonoff induction: the claim that the universal prior over computable hypotheses, defined via Kolmogorov complexity, is the correct formalization of Occam's razor and solves the problem of induction in the limit. The other is a critic from computational learning theory and Bayesian nonparametrics who holds that Solomonoff induction is a suggestive idealization rather than a foundation: the universal prior is only lower semicomputable, the universal mixture is a semimeasure rather than a proper measure, the invariance theorem fixes the prior only up to an additive constant that can be arbitrarily large, and the dominance results are asymptotic, so they say little about the finite-sample regime in which inference actually happens.

Write the dialogue. The critic should not merely repeat the uncomputability point -- that is well known, and the defender has a reply. Instead, have the critic press the following line: the no-free-lunch theorems show that no learner dominates another when performance is averaged over all possible target functions, so any apparent superiority of Solomonoff induction must come from a substantive prior commitment -- the assumption that the world is generated by a short program -- rather than from a neutral principle of parsimony. The defender should respond that no-free-lunch averaging is over a uniform measure on all functions, which is itself a prior that assigns overwhelming weight to high-complexity hypotheses, so the theorem is not a neutrality condition but a reductio of the demand for prior-free learning. The critic should then shift ground: even granting that, the choice of description length as the measure of simplicity is underdetermined; why not weight hypotheses by computational time, as the speed prior does? The defender should have to say what makes description length the right notion of simplicity rather than one useful notion among others. The critic should also press the point that the invariance theorem is asymptotic and that the additive constant is the Kolmogorov complexity of the reference machine, which no computable bound controls, so the theorem does not rescue the finite case. The defender should concede what is true in that objection and then argue that the asymptotic result is the right idealization for a foundational claim.

The exchange should be a real back-and-forth: each speaker should answer the other's actual point, concede where the point has force, and then show where it does not. Neither should be a strawman. Set the dialogue in a single continuous conversation, with no narration beyond what is needed to mark who is speaking. Let the dialogue end with a definite verdict: one of the two has the better of the exchange, and you should say which and why. Do not end by saying that both have a point and leaving it there. I want the verdict argued, not asserted, and I want the losing side's strongest surviving objection acknowledged in the verdict.
