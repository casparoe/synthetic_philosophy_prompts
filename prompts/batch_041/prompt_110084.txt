I chair the investment committee at a midsized foundation. Five of us, we meet monthly, and each meeting we commit somewhere between half a million and five million dollars on the strength of forecasts from two staff analysts. Every other meeting or so the two of them flatly disagree about something we have to decide that same afternoon -- is a recession coming, will this company make its covenants, is this fund's track record real -- and we end up deciding on vibes, or on whoever talks last, and its making me nuts.

Here's what I half-remember from somewhere. There's a result in Bayesian decision theory -- Aumann's agreement theorem, I think -- that says two people who start from the same prior, both know what the other one thinks, and are both rational literally cannot keep disagreeing about a probability. So if my two analysts are still fighting, something has to give: either their priors aren't actually the same, or the disagreement isn't really common knowledge between them, or one of them isn't being as rational as he looks. And there's a companion point I've heard about betting: if we truly disagreed about the odds, one of us should be happy to take the other side of a bet at those odds, so if neither of us will put money on it, the disagreement probably isn't about probability at all. If I've mangled any of that, say so up front and then fix it -- don't just go along with my version.

What I want is a procedure I can run in twenty minutes of committee time. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and hands me a number or a decision, and where it has to stop and hand the thing to a person because what's left is values or risk appetite rather than belief. It has to be runnable by people who dont know the theory and don't speak probability fluently (we say 'probably' and mean it).

Then run it on three of our recurring situations. (1) Both analysts are numerate, both saw the same deck, and they're at 20% and 60% on the same event. (2) They agree about the expected return but one of them won't touch the thing because of the downside -- I suspect that one isn't a disagreement about the world at all. (3) One of them has seen something the other can't see and can't really convey -- he walked the plant, he knows the family.

I also want the case where the procedure rules against me: tell me where it says the chair just has to own a judgment call and stop pretending the analysis settled it. And tell me where it will misfire, which steps are judgment wearing a lab coat, and what it flat out can't do -- including what happens when it's five of us instead of two, and when one of us is only publicly agreeing. Skip the literature, I'm not going to read it.
