I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for going on fifteen years now, and the thing that still nags at me is how the whole corner of the internet treats "akrasia" and "hyperbolic discounting" as if they name the same phenomenon — you procrastinate, therefore your discount curve is hyperbolic, mystery closed, go buy a timer. Those are two different claims, though, and I want a case that actually pulls them apart rather than a hand-wave about them being different.

Definitions first so we don't talk past each other. Hyperbolic discounting: a fact about your preferences over time — the weight you give the sooner of two rewards rises as it gets closer, so your ranking of the two can flip. Akrasia: at the moment you act, you do the thing you judge, all things considered, that you shouldn't do, and you're still holding that judgment while you do it.

Build me a single setup with one knob that yields both of these:

1. Hyperbolic discounting with no akrasia — preferences flip as time passes, but at every moment the person is doing exactly what they think they should be doing. No gap between judgment and action.
2. Akrasia with no hyperbolic discounting — the person's preferences are stable and time-consistent (exponential, if you like), and yet they act against thier own live, all-things-considered judgment.

Constraints: realistic — an ordinary person, ordinary life, no addiction, no brain damage, no alien desires grafted on. No homunculus or "second self" doing the work. The person isn't confused about the facts or about what they themselves want. And you can't stipulate the verdicts: the gap in (2) has to fall out of the structure of the case, not out of you writing "and then they acted against their better judgment."

For each case give me what they do at each time, what their judgment is at that time, whether there's regret or a sense of having been defeated, and what changes if they're sophisticated about their own future behavior instead of naive. Then tell me what the single knob is that separates (1) from (2), and what happens when I turn it: make the discounting exponential in (1) and see whether it turns into akrasia, and see whether (2) survives at all.

Two last things. If you think (2) is impossible — that akrasia without time-inconsistency is a contradiction in terms — say so and prove it, but you have to show the impossibility isn't just an artifact of defining akrasia as preference-violation. And tell me whether (2) still looks like akrasia if we take seriously the idea that the person's later selves are literally separate agents; the blogs can never decide if that's a metaphor or a thesis, and I suspect it changes which of the two cases is the interesting one. My money's on (2) either way, cause I want to know if that's just my bias.
