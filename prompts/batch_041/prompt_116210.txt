My firm replaced its quarterly forecasting meeting with an internal prediction market. Anyone with a login can buy and sell contracts that pay a dollar if a stated event occurs by a stated date, and the operations handbook instructs us to treat the closing price of a contract as the firm's probability for that event: a contract trading at forty cents is, for planning purposes, a forty percent chance. The next paragraph explains why the market is supposed to be accurate, and the answer is disagreement. Traders who think the crowd is wrong are expected to trade against it, and it is precisely that disagreement, the handbook says, that pulls the price toward the truth. Separately, each analyst is scored every quarter on her own forecasts, and the scoring rule is deliberately chosen so that she does best by reporting exactly what she believes rather than what she expects the market to say.

Two things now bother me. The first is what the price is supposed to be a probability of. The handbook talks as though there is one probability sitting out there to be discovered and the price is our best reading of it. But if we are all competent, have read the same materials, and update on the same news, then in what sense is the price a probability rather than a running tally of who has placed which bets? The second is why we trade at all. If we really did start from the same place and reason correctly, we would already agree, and there would be nothing to bet about, yet the handbook treats active trading as the engine of accuracy. The scoring rule seems to tell me something different again: that my probability is mine and yours is yours, that each of us is graded on her own honest credence, and that there is no single number I am getting right or wrong.

What view of probability, belief, and disagreement does this practice presuppose? Does reading the price as the firm's probability require the assumption that we all begin from some shared starting point, so that our differences are only differences in information? Which parts of what we do strictly require that assumption, and which would survive without it? Which rival pictures of belief does the practice quietly rule out? And if we dropped the assumption and admitted that traders simply have irreducibly different starting beliefs, what would have to change in how we read the price, in what we tell analysts to report, and in whether we should expect any trading at all?
