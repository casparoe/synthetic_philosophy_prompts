I'm a clinical ethics consultant at a mid-sized hospital, and I just had a long, slightly wine-fueled arguement with my oldest friend about Hume's essay on suicide. She thinks it's just a period piece — an 18th-century guy being shocking — and that nothing in it could possibly help me when I'm on call. I think there's actually a usable proceedure buried in there, but I got tangled up trying to state it. So: act as a philosopher who also knows the practical constraints of a hospital ethics consult. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the different arguments Hume actually makes in that essay and what each would imply for a bedside decision. Then turn the defensible ones into a decision procedure I could actually run on a consult.

I do maybe two or three of these consults a month, usually with about twenty minutes before I have to give the team an answer, and the attending has to sign off. The procedure should be a checklist or ordered set of questions for a patient who is asking about assisted dying where it's legal, or who is refusing life-sustaining treatment in a way that will certainly end their life. It needs to say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a clear no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. I don't want a bibliography, reading list, or citations — I know the essay, and I can't use them at the bedside anyway. I want the operational core.

The hard cases I need it to handle: (1) a patient with a terminal illness and intractable pain who says they're ready to go; (2) a patient who says they want to die because they don't want to be a burden on their family; (3) a patient who says God forbids suicide but they want to die anyway; (4) a patient whose request seems to be driven by untreated depression, but who insists it isn't. For each, show me how the procedure runs and where it either gives an answer or gets stuck. I especially want you to include one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — maybe where it says we should honor a request I'd instinctively resist, or refuse one I'd instinctively honor.

Also, be honest about the seams. Hume's argument about our duty to God is the one I can't see how to operationalize in a secular hospital. Is that a problem for the procedure, or can it be bracketed? And say how confident you are in the procedure you've given me, and single out the one point you're least sure about. Don't just tell me it's all context-dependent — give me the actual steps, even if some of them are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
