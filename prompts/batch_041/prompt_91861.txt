I work on deployed predictive models — the kind that get retrained on data that the previous version of the model helped generate. I'm fluent in the usual machinery: distribution shift, performative prediction, strategic classification, feedback loops and retraining schedules, Goodhart-style arguments about proxies, causal graphs in which the model's output is itself a node rather than a fixed input. What I have never read is the philosophy. I'm told that Ian Hacking spent a good part of his career on what he called looping effects in the classification of human kinds, and that he held that classifying people differs in kind from classifying minerals: the people classified take up the category, live in it, and are changed by it, which changes what is true of the category, so the targets of the classification move. More strongly, he seems to have thought such classifications can bring new kinds of people into existence rather than merely describing kinds that were already there.

Restate that position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use — label, feature, target, base rate, decision threshold, deployment, feedback loop, retraining, equilibrium, exogenous versus endogenous data-generating process, the difference between a shift in the marginal distribution and a change in the mechanism that produces it. Do it in two passes: first a mapping of the loop he describes onto the mechanics of a retraining pipeline, worked through on a concrete example of my choosing and yours (a risk score that gates access to something and whose label is partly constituted by the gate's own past decisions; a diagnostic label that becomes an administrative category people organise around), and then a pass in which you say, for each element of his account, whether it survives translation intact, survives only in a weakened or distorted form, or has no counterpart at all.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. I want to know what in his account cannot be said in my vocabulary. Candidates I suspect but cannot assess on my own: that the loop runs through the classified person's self-interpretation rather than through a best response to incentives; that the category can be wrong at the moment it is applied and then be made right by the loop, which is not the same thing as a distribution moving under a fixed decision rule; that his contrast is between human kinds and natural kinds, which sounds like a distinction my vocabulary can register only as endogenous versus exogenous, and I want to know whether that is a translation or a replacement; and that there is a normative question about who is authorised to classify and what it costs to be classified, which I see no way to express as a term in an objective function. For each, tell me whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary — something it ought to have and lacks — or evidence that his claim was doing less work than it appeared to. Where my vocabulary has an advantage he lacked, say what it is. Don't be diplomatic about the last part: if the translation shows the original was vague, say so, and if it quietly imported my assumptions and called that fidelity, say that instead.
