I'm drafting something on Carroll's tortoise dialogue and I want you to tear it apart before it goes to my supervisor.

Here's my line. The usual reading is that the dialogue shows inference isn't the acceptance of a conditional: a rule of inference isn't one more item in the notebook, it's what connects the items, and the tortoise's demand to have the rule written down as a premise is a category mistake, with the regress as the symptom. I think that's right as far as it goes, but that people stop one step too early, and that the stopping is doing all the work.

The bit I want to push: notice what the tortoise actually asks for at the start — not "show me that Z follows", but that Achilles force him, logically, to accept Z as true. And Achilles' answer at the end is 'Then Logic would take you by the throat, and force you to do it!' — he thinks there's a fact about validity that does the forcing. But the tortoise's trick is that he'll always grant the truth of the hypothetical and never grant the inference, so nothing about the propositions forces anything. Force isn't a relation between propositions. If that's right, the dialogue isn't a cute point about premises versus rules, it's a proof that validity can't be the source of the norm to infer — a much bigger claim, and I suspect it's why people keep the standard reading, since it lets you agree the tortoise is silly without giving up the idea that logic obliges us.

Now the objection I can't shake. Fine, no proposition forces him. But I've only shown he isn't compelled, not that hes wrong, and I can't get "wrong" out of anything in the dialogue, because the tortoise never contradicts himself — he just refuses to close. Two ways people try to close it for him: (a) accepting a rule just is being disposed to infer in accordance with it, so someone who really accepted A and B would already have concluded; (b) reading C suppositionally, so that accepting "if A and B then Z" just is being disposed to accept Z on supposing A and B, which makes the tortoise incoherent rather than merely unreasonable. Both look to me like they buy the conclusion by redefining acceptance. Against (a): a disposition can be had and not exercised, so the tortoise can have the habit and still sit there writing — that's a psychological observation, not a normative one. Against (b): it makes the tortoise not a bad reasoner but an impossible one, which is a different reply, and it doesn't give Achilles a reason, it takes away the tortoise's standing to ask. Also notice the ambiguity the dialogue runs on: "if A and B are true, Z must be true" can be a claim about propositions (in which case it's validity restated and adds no premise) or a claim about what one ought to accept (in which case it's the norm again, and writing it down as a premise is exactly what generated the regress). The tortoise exploits that equivocation and I don't think Achilles ever spots it.

So my conclusion: the regress is harmless against "we infer" and fatal against "validity obliges", and the real question the dialogue raises is not about the justification of deduction but about what accepting a rule consists in — and the honest answer may be a practice with no further justification, which is either the end of the discussion or the start of a much worse one. Weakest point, I think: I assert that force isn't a relation between propositions and move on, and I never say what I think accepting a rule is, only that two attempts fail.
