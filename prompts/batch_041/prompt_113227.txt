A colleague in evolutionary biology, who is not a game theorist, offered me this summary of the literature on the evolution of cooperation and asked whether it was fair. I'd like your assessment before I respond.

"The prisoner's dilemma proves that rational agents always defect, so cooperation is impossible in a single interaction; it can only emerge when the same two players expect to meet again. Even then it needs an open-ended horizon: if both players know the game will end after a fixed number of rounds, backward induction unravels cooperation from the last round backwards, so a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma contains no cooperation at all. What Axelrod's tournaments showed is that tit-for-tat -- start by cooperating, then copy the opponent's previous move -- is the best strategy, and because it is evolutionarily stable it cannot be invaded once it has taken hold of a population. The general result, the folk theorem, is that repetition guarantees cooperation: once the shadow of the future is long enough, mutual cooperation becomes the rational outcome."

Please audit this claim by claim. Which statements would a game theorist accept as written, which are defensible shorthand, and which are wrong or misleading -- and in the last case, what is the correction? I am particularly interested in whether the thesis the summary is driving at, that cooperation depends on interaction being open-ended, survives once the errors are repaired, or whether it is an artifact of how the summary has framed the problem.
