In November 2009, Swiss voters approved a federal popular initiative that amended the constitution to prohibit the construction of new minarets. The vote was about 57.5 per cent in favour, against the recommendation of the federal government, and the four minarets already standing were left untouched. The initiative had been launched by a committee of politicians from the Swiss People's Party and a smaller Christian-conservative party, after a long local dispute over a six-metre minaret on a Turkish community centre; because the measure took the form of a constitutional amendment, it was not subject to judicial review. The public case made for it was not itself religious: its proponents argued that the minaret is not required by Islam at all but is a symbol of a claim to religious-political power, and they cited a speech they attributed to Recep Tayyip Erdoğan from 1997 to make the point. Opponents argued from religious freedom, equality before the law, the standing of a religious minority, and the reputational cost to the country; the government's own stated objection was that the amendment conflicted with basic constitutional principles.

Analyse this episode through the theory of public reason, and let the analysis run in both directions. Use the theory's own machinery rather than summarising it: the criterion of reciprocity as distinct from the mere accessibility or non-religiosity of a reason; the duty of civility and the difference between what it asks of officials and what it asks of citizens; the proviso under which a comprehensive doctrine may be invoked provided public reasons are supplied in due course; the thought that public reason applies with special force to constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice; and Habermas's translation proviso, together with his insistence that the liberal state cannot demand that religious citizens alone split their identity in two. Be explicit about who is bound by which constraint at each point — the initiative committee, the voters, the parliament, the courts — and about what each party treated as the relevant considerations.

Then tell me what the theory explains and what it does not. In particular, address three things. First, the case is the mirror image of the familiar objection that public reason unfairly burdens religious citizens: here a majority restricted a religious minority, but the winning arguments were secular and publicly accessible, and the arguments of the burdened minority were accessible too. Does the theory's constraint track the distinction that matters here, or does it mistake accessibility for reciprocity? Second, the paradox of the forum: public reason is supposed to bind most strictly on constitutional essentials, yet this constitutional essential was settled by the one procedure in which the addressees of the law are also its authors and no intermediary performs the filtering the theory assumes — while the bodies actually bound by the duty of civility were on the losing side and had no judicial recourse. Third, the grandfathering: the ban leaves prayer, assembly and dress untouched and forbids only a future symbol. What, if anything, can the theory say about that kind of expressive or status harm, and if it cannot, does the case expose a gap in the theory or show that the objection was never really about coercion?

Finish by saying what the episode shows about the theory — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated — and where the theory is genuinely silent, rather than forcing it to speak. I do not want a literature review or a survey of who said what; I want a disciplined mapping onto this case and an honest account of its limits.
