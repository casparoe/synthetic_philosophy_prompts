After forty years in experimental physiology I have taken up moral philosophy in retirement, and I am out of my depth in the Second Essay of Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals (I am reading Horace B. Samuel's translation). The sentence I cannot get past is this: 'Forgetfulness is no mere vis inertiæ, as the superficial believe, rather is it a power of obstruction, active and, in the strictest sense of the word, positive—a power responsible for the fact that what we have lived, experienced, taken into ourselves, no more enters into consciousness during the process of digestion (it might be called psychic absorption) than all the whole manifold process by which our physical nutrition, the so-called "incorporation," is carried on.' Later in the same section he describes the promise-keeper as having 'an active refusal to get rid of it, a continuing and a wish to continue what has once been willed, an actual memory of the will'; he insists that man had first to become 'calculable, disciplined, necessitated even for himself and his own conception of himself'; and he closes by calling the sovereign individual 'the autonomous "super-moral" individual', remarking that 'autonomous' and 'moral' are mutually exclusive terms.

My own guess, which I suspect a philosopher would reject, is that the 'active forgetfulness' is best understood as a homeostatic process — something that must continually spend energy to keep material out of consciousness — so that the 'memory of the will' is an engineered exception to it rather than a second faculty. Please tell me what this reading gets right and what it misses. I would like, in particular, to understand what 'active' and 'positive' can mean for a process of exclusion, how the 'memory of the will' is supposed to function, what work the digestive comparison is doing, whether the 'morality of custom' is offered as a piece of history or as something else, and how an individual can be the product of moral discipline and yet be called 'super-moral'.

Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
