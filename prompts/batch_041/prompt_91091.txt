In my research group an analogy has quietly become load-bearing, and I would like it audited rather than either waved through or rejected on sight. The analogy is this: an autonomous system deployed in the world is like a corporation. On this picture the developers and deployers occupy the roles of shareholders and board; the objective function, training corpus, and reward signal play the part of the corporate charter and standing policy; the individual modules, planners, and learned sub-policies are the employees; and the system's actions in the world are corporate acts, attributable to the organization even though no single member of it willed them. The work the analogy is being asked to do is deflationary. Because we already hold corporations to account for deeds that no individual member intended, and because we possess a settled apparatus of legal personality, vicarious liability, and internal compliance for precisely that purpose, the much-discussed responsibility gap for autonomous systems is said to be not a new metaphysical predicament but an old institutional one, to be closed with the institutional tools we already have.

I want to know how far this actually holds. Set out the correspondence explicitly -- a table mapping source onto target is welcome -- and then tell me where it breaks. In particular I want the features the corporate picture silently imports that the target may lack: a procedure for aggregating the reasons of members into a single decision; an internal locus of deliberation that the members' intentions can underwrite; a mechanism by which sanctions reach someone capable of changing future behavior; and the existence of persons who are the ultimate bearers of the organization's obligations. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the deflationary conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. Then say whether the conclusion -- that the responsibility gap is familiar rather than novel, and closable by familiar means -- survives once those disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along.

One member of the group wants to push the comparison further. She observes that environmental and food-and-drug enforcement sometimes holds a responsible officer liable for a violation she neither personally knew of nor directed, precisely so that the organization's ignorance cannot shield everyone, and proposes that we treat the designers and deployers of an autonomous system the same way: liable by role rather than by knowledge. Assess whether this extension repairs the analogy or merely exposes where it was already broken. Finally, if the corporate analogy cannot bear the weight, say what analogy would, or whether the comparison should be set aside in favor of stating the problem directly.
