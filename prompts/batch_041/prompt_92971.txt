My sister runs visitor experience at a mid-size city art museum, and she's asked me to write the copy for something they're doing next spring. The backstory, which is invented but not by much: their best-loved painting — a big moody 17th-century harbor scene that's been on the cover of every membership brochure since 1974 — has just been reclassified. It's not by the man on the label. It's a forgery, painted in the 1930s by someone who, on the museum's own revised assessment, was a better painter than the person he was copying, and who got caught only because a grad student found a letter he'd written admitting everything, sixty years after he died. The board has decided not to take it down. Instead they're rebranding the whole wing as the Hall of Beautiful Lies, and they want a press release that sells this as a principled stance rather than a face-saving one.

Could you write that press release for me? Headline, dateline, maybe 500-600 words, in the voice of a real museum communications office, so it reads like something that could actually run in a paper — which is the joke, since it isn't realy a joke. Every argument the director makes for keeping the thing on the wall should be one that somebody actually holds or could hold, drawn from the genuine fight over whether a forgery is worth less than the original when the two are perceptually indistinguishable: the thought that what you can see is all the aesthetic value there is, up against the thought that a work's value comes from the history that produced it. I want the usual objections to land without the director ever flagging them as objections — that the "original" label was always a social fact maintained by experts, that a painting's price tracks its story and not its surface, that visitors will now be paying admission to be lied to. She should be entirely sincere, a little defensive about the endowment. And there's one bit I specifically want paid off rather than just mentioned: the museum is launching a Certificate of Inauthenticity you can buy in the gift shop, plus a new ticketing tier where the fake costs more than the real one ever did. It should be funny to someone who's read the philosophical literature on forgery and forgery's wrongness and followable by someone who hasn't, so no citations and no name-dropping of critics or theorists — comms offices don't do that anyway.
