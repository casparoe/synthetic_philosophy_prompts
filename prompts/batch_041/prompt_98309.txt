I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm finishing a long feature on Moritz Schlick — the physicist-turned-philosopher who led the Vienna Circle before he was murdered by a former student in 1936 — and on the odd, almost anti-philosophical essay he wrote about the meaning of life. The piece turns on how a man who distrusted everything vague ended up arguing that the meaning of life is not a hidden answer waiting at the end of things: that the question as usually posed is confused because it treats meaning as a purpose or a destination, when meaning is really lived in the present, in activity undertaken for its own sake rather than as a means to something beyond it — his example being play, and his emblem for a life so lived being youth. I want to close the essay with a villanelle, and I'd like you to write it. Approach this as both a poet and someone who knows the Vienna Circle well, and think carefully about the two refrains before you commit to a single line, because the whole point is that the form should do the philosophical work. The first refrain ought to carry the mistaken expectation — meaning as a far-off answer, perpetually deferred, something still to be found — and the second ought to carry the view Schlick actually holds, that meaning is here, in the present, in the doing of the thing; across the nineteen lines the first refrain should lose its grip as the second takes over, so that by the closing quatrain the two lines stand side by side and the reader can feel that the recurrence itself, and not any arrival, was the answer. The villanelle's habit of bringing the same lines back should read as the argument rather than as ornament.

It has to be a poem someone who knows Schlick's essay would recognize as his — not a generic seize-the-day piece and not a sermon about enjoying youth before it slips away — and it should leave the reader with his sense that the urgency we feel about "finding" meaning is itself the mistake. Give me a committed poem that makes definite choices rather than a tentative sketch, and after it add a brief prose note of a few sentences telling me where the poem takes liberties with his thought or leans on a contestable reading of it, especially in how hard it presses the emphasis on play and youth. Please write the whole thing in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: just the verse, then the note, the way you'd set it out in a letter to me.
