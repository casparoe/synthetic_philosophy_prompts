I'm a first-year PhD student in a PPE-ish program and I've somehow ended up as the resident defender of the "violence has been declining" story in my cohort, mostly because I got annoyed at how smug everyone was about it. But I'm tired of defending a slogan, so I want to nail down the actual version I hold and then have somebody try to break it. Take the role of a hostile-but-fair historical sociologist / philosopher of the social sciences who has spent years on measurement problems in long-run history and who is not impressed by TED-talk versions of this thesis. Think carefully, and please brainstorm for a bit before you start ranking anything — I'd rather have your messy first pass at the space of objections than a tidy list of the three famous ones.

Here's my actual position, in the form I'd defend it. (i) If you pick the right unit — deaths from intentional violence per person per year, roughly — the long-run trend across most of the world is down, not up, and the twentieth century's absolute body count is consistent with that because the denominator got huge. (ii) That decline is not mainly an artifact of better record-keeping or softer definitions; the medieval-to-modern homicide gap is too big to be explained by reporting. (iii) The mechanism is roughly Elias-plus-Tilly: state monopolization of force, commerce, and the fact that people who can't afford to lose each other stop killing each other. (iv) There is a fact of the matter about whether it declined — it isn't just a story we tell. I'll flag that I'm least confident about (iv) and most emotionally attached to (ii).

Two things I've been chewing on that I haven't seen anyone put quite the way I'd put them, and I want you to tell me whether they're the same worry wearing different hats. First, denominator arbitrage: any decline claim has to pick a baseline and a denominator, and the choice is usually made after the fact — the per-capita number gets cited when it's flattering, the absolute number when it's damning, and the baseline is almost always the worst available century. Second, and this is the half-baked one: I've been wondering whether we should be counting something like the share of human interactions that are coercive rather than the share that are lethal. Call it a coercion share. If you measure that instead — domestic stuff, prisons, wage relations under duress, colonial administration, the ordinary unfreedom of most people in most centuries — I genuinely don't know which way the trend goes, and I suspect deaths-per-capita is partly a metric that was chosen because it moves in the right direction. Tell me if that's a serious objection or a category error, and what a serious version of it would look like.

So: what would count against my position? I want the observations, arguements, and conceptual problems that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be, with a clear line between what a defender like me should concede counts against me by my own lights and what I can reasonably wave off. Include the ones that would be fatal and the ones that are merely awkward. Tell me what my position predicts that its rivals don't, whether (i)–(iv) can be qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that's definately a problem for a view of this kind, and — if I had to stake the whole thing on one possible finding — what the single most decisive piece of evidence would be. And tell me honestly if you think the right response to all of this is that there's no fact of the matter after all. No reading lists, please; I want the argument, not the bibliography.
