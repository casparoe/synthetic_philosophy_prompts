I'm a graduate student preparing a session for a methods seminar on thought experiments, and I've hit a distinction that I can state but cannot explain. We are routinely told that some thought experiments are probative — that their verdicts are genuine evidence about the concept or the phenomenon at issue — while others merely manufacture a verdict through the way the case is described. I can feel the difference (Gettier-style cases on one side, a lurid trolley variant on the other), but I cannot give an account of it, and every criterion I try turns out to presuppose the very verdict it is supposed to license: calling a description "loaded" seems to require already knowing which answer the description is rigged to produce, which is exactly what the thought experiment was supposed to tell us. I want you to explain the conceptual issue underneath this, and in particular to say what, if anything, separates a case that isolates a concept from a case that merely dramatizes a conclusion.

Two candidate criteria have occurred to me, and I want a verdict on each rather than a survey of the literature. The first is an isolation criterion: a case is probative when the target concept is fully determined by the features the case explicitly specifies, and when none of those features is itself the thing in dispute — so Gettier-style cases qualify, because the specified features fix whether the belief is justified, true, and luckily arrived at, whereas most trolley cases do not, because the line between killing and letting die is precisely the contested notion. The second is a robustness criterion: a verdict is probative only if it survives de-pumping — that is, only if it is unchanged when you strip the case to its logically relevant features and re-present it in the flattest vocabulary available, with the narrative trappings removed. My worry is that the second criterion proves too much. Consider a case I have been using in the seminar: an orchardist sprays a pesticide on the strength of the best studies available to him and, twenty years later, learns that it gave his neighbors cancer. Pumped, the verdict is that he is to blame; de-pumped, as a question about whether a person should be blamed for a consequence he could not have foreseen, the verdict is that he is not. If the robustness criterion declares the first verdict an artifact, it would equally license dismissing the outcome-sensitivity of blame that many people take to be a real feature of our moral practices.

So: tell me which criterion, or which combination of them, is actually defensible; whether the orchardist case shows that robustness is too strong or instead shows that our pumped verdict about him is indeed an artifact; and what the correct diagnosis is of the circularity I described — whether it can be broken, and if so by what. Please commit to a position rather than laying out the options for me to choose among, and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then argue for it: no preamble and no restating of my question.
