Some context. A friend and I spent most of last week arguing about whether disjunctivism is a genuine solution to the argument from illusion or just a refusal to accept its conclusion, and the argument left me unsure how much of the standard dialectic I actually have straight. I'm marking this problem set tomorrow and I'd rather not grade it on general impression, so I want an assessment I can defend line by line.

Question (40 marks): Set out the argument from illusion and assess whether disjunctivism about perceptual experience offers a satisfactory response to it. Be explicit about what the argument is meant to establish and about the commitments the disjunctivist thereby takes on.

Candidate's answer: "The argument from illusion begins from the fact that the senses sometimes deceive us. A straight stick half-submerged in water looks bent; a white wall in shadow looks grey; a square tower seen from a distance looks round. In each case the experience presents us with something that is not the case. The argument then appeals to the fact that such deceptive experiences may be subjectively indistinguishable from veridical ones: the stick in water looks exactly as a genuinely bent stick would look. If two experiences cannot be told apart from the inside, the argument says, they must be experiences of the same fundamental kind. Since the deceptive experience cannot be an awareness of a mind-independent object — there is no bent stick there — and since it is of the same kind as the veridical experience, the veridical experience cannot be an awareness of a mind-independent object either. Both must be awareness of something mental, a sense-datum or an appearance, and the conclusion drawn is that perceptual experience never puts us in direct contact with the material world.

Disjunctivism denies the move from indistinguishability to sameness of kind. The disjunctivist insists that the veridical experience is not a case of the same kind as the illusion: in the good case the subject is perceptually related to the mind-independent object itself, whereas in the illusory case no such relation obtains. There is no common core shared by the two cases; the resemblance is at most a matter of the subject's inability to tell which case she is in. Austin's Sense and Sensibilia is the classic statement of this line of thought, and it is largely devoted to showing that the argument's talk of 'appearances' is a piece of philosophical fiction rather than a description of anything we are ever aware of. The disjunctivist therefore rejects the common kind assumption, and with it the conclusion that perception is only ever of the mental.

I take this response to be successful. If the veridical and the illusory experience are not of the same kind, then the fact that I might be having an illusion is no evidence at all about the nature of my veridical experience; the sceptical possibility is not one that the good case leaves open. Moreover, since the good case just is a case of being related to the object, the perceiver who is in the good case is in a position to know that she is: nothing in her experience falls short of the object, so there is nothing for the sense-datum theorist to point to. The argument from illusion therefore fails to generalise from the deceptive cases to the veridical ones, and the disjunctivist is entitled to claim that in the good case we really do see the mind-independent world."
