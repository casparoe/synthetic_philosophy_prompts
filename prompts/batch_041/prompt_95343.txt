I'm a high school student, and I've been trying to think carefully about the view that the mind isn't sealed inside the skull — that a notebook, a phone, or a synced cloud folder can count as part of someone's memory when it is reliably available, easily searched, and endorsed as authoritative. What I want to examine is a practice I know from the inside: how schools handle exam accommodations, and I'd like to know what theory of mind that practice presupposes.

On an ordinary exam day, notes or a phone in the room is cheating; the exam is supposed to measure what is in the student's head. Yet the same school, under the same rules, will hand one student a calculator for a diagnosed arithmetic disability, another a text-to-speech device or a scribe for dyslexia, another extra time, another a spell-checking laptop. These are not presented as favors but as removing a barrier, so that the exam measures the student's knowledge rather than the disability. So the institution is willing to attach cognitive equipment to a student and still count the resulting performance as that student's own — but only when a professional certifies a deficit and the equipment moves the student back toward a normal baseline. A teacher once told me, half-seriously, that if the accommodations office hands out calculators it should let everyone bring one index card of notes, and was told this is obviously a different thing. I cannot say why it is.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the practice presuppose that cognition is bounded by the skull, with tools admitted only as compensatory exceptions? Or is it compatible with — or even better explained by — a picture on which a reliably available, endorsed external resource is simply part of the cognitive system, in which case the ordinary ban on notes looks arbitrary? Which rival views does the regime quietly rule out, and can it consistently give one student a calculator while confiscating another's? I'd also like you to distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: does it need any claim about a boundary at all, or would a purely fairness-based rationale — equalize the inputs, then measure the output — license exactly the same rules without any commitment about where the mind ends? Finally, if a school did take the extended picture seriously as a matter of policy, what would have to change: would accommodations stop being tied to diagnosis, would ordinary students get their notes back, and would the purpose of the exam have to be redescribed? If the practice turns out to be underdetermined, so that several theories of mind would license it, say so, and say which parts of it are doing no real work.
