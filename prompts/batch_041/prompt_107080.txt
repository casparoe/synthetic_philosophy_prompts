To give those arguments something concrete to grip, I set a short written question each week and ask the members to answer it in a page or so, and then I read them over and try to give useful feedback. This week the topic was the "scale argument" for taking insect sentience seriously, and I would like your help assessing the answer one member submitted. Please act as an expert in animal ethics and philosophy of mind who is also a patient and demanding teacher, and think carefully before you commit to anything: begin by working out for yourself what a genuinely strong answer to my question would have to establish, and only then judge the submitted answer against that standard, rather than reacting to how it sounds.

Here is the question I set:

"What is the 'scale argument' for treating the possibility of insect sentience as a serious moral matter, and how much should our uncertainty about whether insects are sentient count against that argument?"

Here is the member's answer:

"Most of us assume that whether an insect can suffer does not matter much, because an insect is so small and its life so brief. The scale argument says this is a mistake, because the real question is not how much any single insect matters but how much all of them matter together. There are something like a billion billion insects alive at any given moment, and if even a small fraction of them can feel pain, then the total quantity of suffering in the insect world could easily dwarf the total suffering of humans and other mammals. On a utilitarian view, what matters is the sum, so we are not entitled to ignore insect suffering merely because each instance is tiny.

The argument does not require us to be certain that insects are sentient. It requires only that we admit there is some real chance that they are, and then notice that the sheer number of them multiplies that chance into a large expected quantity. This is the same logic we use when we take precautions against a small risk of a catastrophic outcome; in fact the scale argument is essentially Pascal's wager applied to animals. So uncertainty is not a reason to do nothing — it is already built into the argument.

That said, the argument only works if we are willing to assign a non-trivial probability to insect sentience. If the probability were very small — say one in a million — then even a billion billion insects would give us a comparatively small expected total, and we would be justified in setting the whole question aside. This is why the argument really turns on the evidence about insect nervous systems. Insects have brains and respond to injury, but it is disputed whether they have the kind of centralized nervous system that supports subjective experience. If they do not, the scale argument has no grip at all, since multiplying zero by any number is still zero.

I think the strongest version of the argument therefore has two parts: first, that we should not demand certainty before taking a possible source of suffering seriously, and second, that the numbers are large enough to matter even if the probability is modest. The main weakness is that we may never have enough evidence to settle whether insects are sentient, and in that case the argument is more of a reason to investigate than a reason to act."
