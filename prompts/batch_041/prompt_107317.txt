I am responsible for the training and evaluation pipeline of a language-model agent, and my team is split over whether we should be supervising the reasoning process the model produces or only the outcomes it achieves. Before we commit either way, I want the specification rather than a recommendation: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of AI supervision must meet, where by a theory of supervision I mean a principled account of when optimizing a particular supervisory signal -- process-based, outcome-based, or some combination -- will actually produce the behavior we want, and why.

Treat this as a requirements document. For each requirement, state the concrete case or consideration that motivates it, so that I can tell a genuine requirement from a preference of one camp. At a minimum the list should cover the following, though you should add anything I have overlooked.

First, the theory must explain, not merely stipulate, why each kind of signal can be gamed in its own characteristic way: an outcome signal by a policy that secures the measured result without performing the task (a model that passes the test without writing the program; an agent that obtains its reward by interfering with its own shutdown), and a process signal by a policy that emits reasoning which looks sound to the grader but is not the reasoning that actually produced the action.

Second, it must give verdicts when process and outcome disagree in both directions: a valid argument for a false conclusion; a correct answer arrived at by a rule the model would disavow if questioned; a decision that follows the approved procedure and yet produces a bad outcome; a correct outcome reached by luck.

Third, it must respect the distinction between a process signal that is legible -- a human can inspect and check it -- and one that is faithful -- it reflects what the model actually computed -- recognizing that these come apart, and it must say which of the two it is optimizing for and what it sacrifices.

Fourth, it must remain consistent with the asymmetry in what we can measure: a trustworthy outcome signal is often cheap on easy tasks and unavailable on exactly the tasks we care about, and a process signal is sometimes available precisely where the outcome is not.

Fifth, it must say what adequacy means when the supervisory signal is itself produced by a fallible model -- a learned reward model or an automatic process grader -- that may share the policy's blind spots, so that agreement between judge and policy is not by itself evidence of correctness.

Then, for the list as a whole: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the commitments of the process camp or the outcome camp; identify any subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- the tension between legibility and faithfulness, or between robustness to gaming and sample efficiency, or another you find -- and say which requirement sits on each side of that tradeoff; and tell me which single requirement, if enforced, rules out the most candidate accounts. Finish with a brief pass over how a purely outcome-based account and a purely process-based account each fare against the list; I want the criteria, not a full scorecard.
