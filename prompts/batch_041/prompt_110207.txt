I chair the publications committee of a philosophical association that puts out two journals and a book series. We currently have inclusive-language guidance that is advisory only, and a proposal before us would make it mandatory and add three provisions: authors should not use a generic masculine pronoun for an unspecified person; authors should not build illustrative examples around a protagonist of one gender when the gender is doing no work in the example; and authors should not use role or occupational terms that presuppose a gender unless the presupposition is itself under discussion. The second provision is the contested one. It was drafted after a complaint about an article in which every hypothetical agent, across a dozen examples, was male, and the author's reply was that the examples were arbitrary and the complaint was a category mistake about style.

One committee member has circulated Douglas Hofstadter's satire on language reform, the piece usually called the Person Paper, as an argument against the whole proposal. His reading is that the satire demonstrates the reform's logic running to absurdity, and that whatever force it has comes from a racial transposition that proves far too much. Other members read the same piece as showing precisely how invisible a default assumption can be, and take the racial analogue to be the point rather than a reductio. I need the committee's written decision before the vote.

Draft that memo. It should reach a concrete recommendation: make the guidance mandatory as proposed, keep it advisory, or adopt it with specified conditions and a review date. It should include a scope rule covering satire, fiction, and quoted historical material, since we publish all three and one member argues that a mandatory rule would chill exactly the kind of writing the association exists to protect. It should give an honest account of the strongest argument against whatever you recommend, in a form its holder could recognize as fair.

In dealing with the satire, do not quote it and do not summarize it at length; the committee has read it. Work instead at the level of its argumentative structure. Identify what it treats as the unmarked default, what the transposition from one axis of description to another is supposed to accomplish, and whether the resulting argument is best reconstructed as a consistency argument, a reductio, or a tu quoque, and whether it succeeds in that form. Then separate two claims that the current debate keeps running together: the claim that certain usages encode a default assumption about who counts as the standard case, and the claim that a scholarly association should compel authors to avoid them. Say what additional premises are needed to move from the first to the second, and whether this committee can honestly claim them.

Finally, address what might be called the laundering problem. The piece circulates in excerpted form, is cited both by people pressing for reform and by people resisting it, and will predictably be invoked by authors who read any mandatory rule as an attack on satire. Recommend how the committee should explain the rule to authors who invoke it in good faith, and say whether the committee owes anything different to authors who invoke it in bad faith. Close with the motion you recommend and the conditions, if any, under which the committee should revisit it.
