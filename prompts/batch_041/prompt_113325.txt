I work in a small lab that builds little chemical blobs, and I need help deciding something by Monday, so let me just describe them plainly. Each one is a droplet with a skin it makes itself. It keeps its inside different from the outside (saltier, more acidic), it pulls simple molecules out of the broth around it and uses them to rebuild its own skin and innards, and when it starves it doesn't rot -- it just goes quiet, and if you feed it a week later it starts up again. No DNA, no proteins, none of the usual stuff; it's a totally different chemistry.

Here's the thing. It doesn't reproduce. New droplets only show up when we stir the broth hard enough to tear one in two, and both halves carry on. So there are lineages, but nothing like a parent making a copy of itself. And it doesn't really evolve either -- the halves are near-perfect copies, and we control the broth, so there's nothing for them to be selected against except our own stirring. Every now and then a droplet ends up with a slightly different recipe and passes it down for a few rounds, but it always drifts back. A virus is the usual comparison people reach for, but a virus at least has a genome to pass on.

My PI wants to tell our funder we made "the first living thing from scratch." Our safety officer read that draft and now wants it handled like an organism, which means new paperwork, different shipping, the works. A collaborator says it's a fancy chemical reaction and we're anthropomorphizing. I have to pick a lane, because it changes what we're allowed to do.

So: is there a principled way to settle whether this counts as alive, or is "alive" just a word we can assign however we like? And whatever you tell me, can you spell out what it would actually mean for the three decisions in front of me -- the claim in the paper, the shipping rules, the safety paperwork -- because I can't go back to a funder with "it depends."
