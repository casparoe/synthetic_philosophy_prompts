I am a doctoral candidate in the history and philosophy of science, and I have been trying to use Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes as a framework for appraising a long-running methodological dispute in the empirical literature I work on. I have read the central text, but I keep second-guessing whether I have the machinery right, and a colleague whose judgement I generally trust gave me the following thumbnail sketch when I asked for a refresher:

"Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes is essentially Popper's falsificationism with the rough edges sanded off. A research programme is built around a hard core of assumptions that are unfalsifiable by stipulation, together with a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that take the empirical hits. A programme is scientific, on Lakatos's account, precisely insofar as it is progressive -- it keeps generating novel predictions that are subsequently confirmed -- and pseudo-scientific once it turns degenerating, that is, once its defenders merely make ad hoc adjustments to accommodate the anomalies they failed to foresee. Because the whole point of the exercise is to give scientists a decision procedure, Lakatos holds that a rational scientist abandons a research programme once it enters a degenerating phase, and the history of science can then be written as a rational competition in which progressive programmes drive out degenerating ones. The hard core is simply the part of a programme that its adherents refuse to question, and the positive heuristic is just the stock of auxiliary hypotheses they draw on to explain anomalies away."

What I want is a sentence-by-sentence audit rather than a general overview. For each claim in that paragraph, tell me what a philosopher of science who works on Lakatos would accept as accurate, what they would tolerate as a fair simplification, and what they would reject outright -- and in the last case, what the correction is and why it matters for anyone actually trying to use the framework. I am especially interested in whether the sketch uses Lakatos's own vocabulary (hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristic, progressive and degenerating problemshifts, novel fact, ad hoc) in the senses he gave those terms, or whether it quietly slides into their everyday senses, since I suspect that is where most of the damage would be done.

My own suspicion, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that the sketch is broadly defensible up to the sentence about progress and goes wrong after it -- specifically, that the alleged decision procedure and the tidy "rational competition" picture are doing work Lakatos would not endorse, and that the account of how a programme is abandoned misstates something structural about his view of when a judgement of degeneracy can be made at all. Please tell me whether that suspicion is well founded, and if so, exactly which sentences it convicts.

Finally, I would like you to say what a defender of Lakatos ought to concede to the sketch's critic. In particular: if the corrected reading of the methodology really does withhold any present-tense rule for when to abandon a programme, does that leave the framework vulnerable to the charge that it cannot be used to criticize anything -- that it merely rationalizes whatever survives? I want your own assessment of whether that charge lands, or whether the defender has a principled reply available.
