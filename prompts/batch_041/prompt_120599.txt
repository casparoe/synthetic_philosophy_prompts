I am in my final year of secondary school and have spent the past term on an independent project about whether large language models understand the language they produce. I have arrived at a position of my own, and I am writing to ask you to attack it rather than to explain it. I have already read the standard moves on both sides — the Chinese room and the systems reply, the parrot objection, and the usual counterexamples drawn from games and translation — so I would rather you did not restate them. What I want to know is what would count against the particular thing I believe.

Here is the position. A system such as a contemporary language model can have, at most, inferential competence without referential content. By this I mean that it can become extraordinarily good at moving between sentences — at registering which claims sit together and which clash, what follows from what, what a passage commits its author to, which continuations are coherent — while none of its words ever comes to stand for anything outside the corpus. On my view its terms are related to one another by compatibility and consequence, and to nothing else. I take this to be a difference in kind rather than in degree, because it seems to me that genuine aboutness is not something a system can possess a little of: either a term stands for something or it does not.

What would have to be true for this to be false? I would like the case against me made as strongly as it can be, and organised as follows. First, what does my position predict that its rivals do not — predictions that could have come out the other way, rather than the vague expectation that a model will sometimes fail? Second, what would count decisively against me, and what would count merely as awkward? Rank these, from fatal to inconvenient. Third, what would I myself have to concede without giving up the view — the strongest admission an opponent could extract from me? Fourth, is my position at risk of unfalsifiability? If no test could distinguish a system that has referential content from one that merely behaves as though it did, tell me whether that makes my position empty, and whether that would be a serious objection or a tolerable one.

Two further matters I cannot resolve alone. The first is an objection I have met and cannot answer. If the meaning of a term depends on the causal and social history of its use rather than on anything inside the speaker, then the words a model emits might inherit their reference from the people who trained and prompted it, and my claim that it has no referential content would be false for reasons invisible from the inside. Is this a genuine defeater, or a confusion? If it is genuine, what is left of my position?

The second is a set of findings I have read about and do not know how to weigh: that one can train a probe on a model's internal activity and recover a representation of the state of the world described in a text it was trained on; that models' stated reasons often fail to match the factors that actually drove their answers; that models sometimes generalise to combinations of material they never encountered together; and that they can report uncertainty about their own answers. For each, tell me whether it bears on my position at all, and if it does, whether it counts for or against it — and what a defender of my position would say in order to explain it away.

What would move me most is a case in which the best explanation of a model's success is that it grasped something about the world, rather than that it exploited a regularity in its training data. But I cannot tell whether that is a reasonable demand or simply the demand that the model not be a model, so please say if the bar itself is the problem.
