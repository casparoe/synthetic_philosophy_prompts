I sit on the three-person jury of a small biennial prize for poetry in translation, and we vote on Thursday. The shortlist is down to two English versions of the same short sixteenth-century French poem, and I must circulate a written opinion beforehand: a recommendation on which version should take the prize, with reasons.

The poem runs sixteen short lines about a woman watching a river and thinking of a man who has gone. It never once uses the letter e, the commonest letter in French, and that is its entire reputation: it survives in a single manuscript whose copyist underlined the trick, and it is anthologised for the feat rather than for anything it says. The sense is slight; the images are plain.

Version A keeps the constraint — not one e in its sixteen lines. To manage it, it turns two concrete images into abstractions, drops the flowers the original names, and twists word order until a reader has to work out what each line is doing. Version B lets the constraint go: accurate, metrical, and reading as if written in English, so that no reader of B would guess anything had been forbidden. A third possibility, which nobody has yet moved, is to award B and let a translator's note explain what the original did.

Our charter says the prize goes to the version that best gives an English reader with no French the experience the original gave its first readers. One colleague reads that as a claim about what the poem means; another reads it as a claim about what it cost; I suspect the sentence is doing more work than its drafters realised. Please draft the opinion I will circulate: a clear recommendation, the reasoning for it, an honest statement of the strongest case against it, and — if the charter needs amending before the next cycle — the amendment you would propose, including what it should say about a version that requires a note to be understood.
