The exercise, in the instructor's wording:

Exercise 4. The norm of assertion is the standard an assertion must meet in order to be proper. Three candidates are standardly distinguished: the knowledge account (one may assert only what one knows), the truth account (one may assert only what is true), and the belief account (one may assert only what one believes). Answer all three parts.

(a) Say what each account predicts about the propriety of the lottery assertion — a speaker who has not checked the draw asserting that her ticket has lost — and say which account, if any, best explains the intuition that the assertion is improper. [about 200 words]

(b) Explain the difference between a norm that is constitutive of assertion and one that is merely regulative of it, and say what follows from that difference for the question whether a speaker who violates the norm is open to criticism. [about 200 words]

(c) A company's spokesperson asserts at a press conference that the company's new product is safe. The safety conclusion is supported only by the joint upshot of findings held across four separate departments: each department holds part of the evidence, and the four parts together entail that the product is safe, but no one has assembled them, so no individual employee, and not the spokesperson, knows the conclusion. Assess whether the spokesperson's assertion is proper, and say which account your verdict rests on. [about 250 words]

The script I was assigned:

The three accounts of the norm of assertion differ in how demanding they are. On the truth account an assertion is proper when its content is true; on the belief account it is proper when the speaker believes it; on the knowledge account it is proper when the speaker knows it. Because knowledge entails belief and belief does not entail truth, the knowledge account is the most demanding of the three.

(a) In the lottery case the speaker has not checked the draw, so although her ticket will in fact have lost, she does not know that it has; she believes it on the strength of a probability that almost every ticket-holder shares. The truth account would license the assertion, since the content is true, and this seems wrong: we retract the assertion, and think it should not have been made, even after the draw confirms that the ticket lost. The belief account would license it too, since she believes it, and this also seems wrong: confident belief is not by itself a licence to assert. Only the knowledge account explains why the assertion is improper, which is the strongest argument for it. The same account explains why it sounds bad to assert that one's ticket has lost and then add that one does not know that it has.

(b) A norm is constitutive when it is part of what makes the behaviour the kind of act it is, rather than a rule laid on a practice that would exist without it. On the constitutive reading, an utterance that violates the norm is not an assertion at all, so the knowledge account cannot be refuted by any example of an improper assertion: any example we produced would be disqualified by the very norm under test. This means the account is not answerable to the lottery case in the way I suggested above. It also means that the criticism we direct at a speaker who asserts without knowing is not the criticism of someone who has broken a rule — since she has not asserted — but only the observation that she has not performed the act.

(c) The spokesperson asserts that the product is safe. She does not know that it is safe: the evidence is distributed across four departments, she has not assembled it, and the fact that the organisation's evidence entails the conclusion does not put the knowledge into her head. Her assertion therefore violates the knowledge norm. Because it violates the norm she has asserted something she had no right to assert, and she is dishonest in doing so and deserves the criticism she gets. The belief account would reach a different verdict only if she herself believed the conclusion, which the case does not tell us.

3. Part (c): the application to the spokesperson case.
4. Clarity and argumentative discipline across the whole script.
