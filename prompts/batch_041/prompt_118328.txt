I host a podcast on decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice, and my next guest is a philosopher of economics who defends an unusually strong line on satisficing: she holds that satisficing is not a second-best procedure for agents too limited to optimize, but the primary form of rational choice, with utility maximization as a degenerate special case that applies only when the option set is given, finite, and fully ordered. Her central distinction is between satisficing as a stopping rule — a way of ending search once an option clears a threshold — and satisficing as a constitutive claim, on which the adequacy of an option is settled before and independently of any comparison among options; she argues that most of the literature collapses these two, and that the collapse is what makes bounded rationality look like a confession of failure. She also insists that the familiar objection that satisficers can be exploited or leave money on the table equivocates between ex ante and ex post rationality. Treat her as defending exactly this package; there is no need to tie her to any particular published author.
