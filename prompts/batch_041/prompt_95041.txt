I am assembling the criteria for a theory of AI takeoff speed before evaluating any particular fast-takeoff or slow-takeoff account, and I want the specification in hand first. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of how fast AI capabilities could improve has to meet: the phenomena it must account for, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the findings from outside the debate with which it must be consistent. Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which requirements are genuine obligations on any theory and which are merely the commitments of one camp dressed up as conditions of adequacy. I need the theory to be adequate for a practical purpose — good enough to tell a regulator whether an intervention in the next few years, a pause or a compute threshold, would reduce risk or merely displace it — so where a requirement is defensible only relative to some other purpose, say so.

Anchor the list to cases rather than truisms. At a minimum it should say what a theory owes about each of the following: a narrow self-improving system — a theorem prover, or a compiler that improves its own heuristics or its own code — which has existed for decades without anything like an explosion; the human-in-the-loop version of the same loop, in which researchers use models to do machine-learning research faster, which is already the ordinary case; a system with no new data, compute, or experimental feedback available to it; and a system whose further improvement is bottlenecked by processes that run on the timescale of wet labs, fabs, and power grids. It should separate the speed of capability gain from the speed of deployment and both from the speed of real-world consequence, and say what becomes of the theory if intelligence is not one scalar but a bundle whose components have different returns. It should be able to represent a large stock of already-built but idle compute being switched on without thereby asserting anything about how fast the underlying capability would improve, and it should distinguish a system that can do a task from one that can do it fast enough, cheaply enough, and reliably enough to displace the humans currently in the loop. Two facts from outside the AI literature should constrain it: that measured productivity growth in the economies that adopted information technology most aggressively has been roughly flat to declining since the mid-2000s despite very large investment, and that in several fields, semiconductors among them, sustaining a constant rate of progress has required a rising number of researchers.

Then tell me which subsets of the requirements cannot be satisfied together — I expect there to be at least one such pair, and it is the part I care most about — and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate theories. If the list rules out the standard families of view, note in a sentence or two which camp each requirement cuts against.

Close by saying what would change your mind: which consideration, if it turned out to be true, would make you drop or demote a requirement, and where your reasoning in constructing the list is most likely to fail. No citations or reading lists, please — I want the criteria, not the literature.
