Ok so I'm in a grad seminar on realism vs anti-realism and I've ended up somewhere I didn't expect to land: I think Fitch's result actually does refute the knowability thesis, and by extension the Dummett-ish anti-realism that needs it. Not in a "fun puzzle, still working it out" way — I mean I think the thesis is dead and everyone who keeps it is either fudging or hasn't noticed. Here's the version of the proof in my head, tell me if I've even got it right: take the claim that every truth is knowable — for any p, if p is true then it's possible that someone knows p. Now plug in the proposition "p, and nobody knows p." If that were knowable, there'd be a possible knower who knows both halves of it; knowledge is factive and distributes over conjunction, so they'd know p and know that nobody knows p, hence know p and not know p. Contradiction, so that proposition isn't knowable, so by the thesis it isn't true, so there are no unknown truths. And since the thesis was supposed to be about non-omniscient agents, we've been forced into "all truths are known," which is exactly what the anti-realist was trying to avoid. What really bothers me is that the anti-realist's ususal escape hatch — blame classical logic — is mostly closed, because the derivation up to "there are no unknown truths" goes through intuitionistically fine; it's only the last hop to "all truths are known" that leans on a classical equivalence about negated conditionals. And it generalizes to almost any factive-ish operator you plug in for the knowledge operator, which makes me think the problem is structural rather than an accident of the epistemic vocabulary. (Also, weirdly, it started life as a throwaway theorem in a 1963 paper about value concepts, not as an attack on verificationism at all.)

So here's the ask. I hold the position that Fitch's proof is a genuine refutation. Tell me what would count against it. I don't want a reading list or names to go look up — I want the actual live escape routes with the arguments for and against each, ranked by how much they'd really move someone who already knows the proof. Roughly: the restriction moves (limiting the schema so it doesn't cover propositions that themselves mention what is or isn't known, or only covering propositions whose being known isn't ruled out a priori); denying that knowledge really distributes over conjunction, or that it's factive in the sense the proof needs; rejecting the modal logic, i.e. arguing that the possibility operator here isn't a normal modality at all and the proof's use of it is question-begging; the move that says the anti-realist never needed this thesis in the first place, because their real commitment is about grasp of meaning and verification conditions rather than about truth as such; and the bullet-biting move that says fine, all truths are known — at the limit of inquiry, by whoever gets there. For each of these I want to know whether it's a principled repair or a fudge, whether the defender would themselves concede that it costs them something, and which ones should actually make me drop my claim.

Then the meta question, which is the one I actually care about. Has my position quietly turned unfalsifiable? If every anti-realist response gets read by me as either "ad hoc restriction of the schema" or "closet omniscience," then what would it even take to show me I'm wrong — and if nothing would, is that a bug in my view or just evidence that the proof is that decisive? I'd also like an honest read on which parts of the proof's packaging are doing logical work and which are doing rhetorical work. The "p and nobody knows p" trick especially feels like it's sneaking something past me and I can't articulate what, so if it turns out the trick is load-bearing in a way that's contestable, say so plainly rather than reassuring me.
