Two economists I've been interviewing for a piece on how governments do cost-benefit analysis have gotten into an arguement I can't referee, and I want you to stage it properly. The first says Harsanyi's aggregation theorem basically settles the ethics for you: grant that each person's preferences are coherent in the expected-utility sense, grant that an impartial chooser behind a veil of ignorance gives everyone's interests equal weight — equal odds of being anybody — and you are forced to maximize the unweighted sum of individual expected utilities. So the utilitarian spreadsheet isn't a value choice, it's the only consistent one. The second says fine, but that theorem is a machine that takes expected-utility preferences as input and prints out utilitarianism, which means all the controversy is hiding in the input — including one thing that's false in half the disputes I cover, namely that everyone shares the same probabilities. When two experts disagree about the chance a dam fails, or the rate of a rare vaccine side effect, whose numbers does the sum use? And there are choices where the expected-utility sums come out identical whether you hand the scarce thing to one person outright or run a lottery, and yet almost everyone says the lottery is fairer.

Write a dialogue between these two, both of them sharp and neither one a strawman, where they actually take up each other's points instead of talking past each other — the second pressing on whose probabilities get used and whether "expected utility" is even defined for a group, the first replying that without one shared probability distribution there's no impartial standpoint left to occupy, and the fairness case getting fought over properly. Begin with the dialogue itself, no preface or commentary.
