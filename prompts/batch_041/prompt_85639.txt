ok so I'm in a formal epistemology course this term and we've just done van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the idea that your credence in something right now should already equal what you expect your credence in it to be later (or: if you're certain that by tomorrow you'll be at 90% on P, you should be at 90% on P now). Honestly I've basically swallowed it. It seems like the only sane way to treat your future self as an authority rather than as a stranger whose opinions happen to be located nearby in time. And the stock objection — you know you're about to be drugged, so tomorrow you'll be dead certain you can fly — feels like a cheap shot, since that future credence obviously isn't really yours.

So I want you to play devil's advocate properly. What would actually count against the principle? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one tell me whether a committed defender would concede it counts against them or just write it off. Some things I keep chewing on:

- Does the isn't-really-your-credence move make the whole thing unfalsifyable? Like, what would a genuine counterexample even look like, if any inconvenient future credence can be dismissed as corruption?
- Why the asymmetry with the past? Nobody thinks my credence now has to match what I believed five years ago. If Reflection is really about evidence rather than about the direction of time, that needs an answer.
- Cases where I know I'll simply forget something I know now — does it really tell me to lower my confidence today to match my dumber future self?
- Precommitment: decision theory lets people bind their future selves (Ulysses, the mast, the sirens). If we're fine with that for preferences, why is it supposed to be incoherent to bind my future credences? And does any of this rest on my future self being the same person as me in the psychological-continuity sense — if continuity gets broken, is there even a self there to defer to?
- Does Sleeping Beauty bear on this at all, and if so how? I can never tell whether it's a counterexample to Reflection or just to the halfer's version of it.

The one that actually nags me is the past/future asymmetry, but tell me if I'm wrong to be more bothered by that than by the drugged cases. And if it turns out the principle has just been qualified into something that cant lose, say so, and say whether that's fatal or fine. No need for citations or who-said-what, just argue it out.
