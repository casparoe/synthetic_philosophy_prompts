Consider two rival criteria for calling a society's history a collapse. On the connectivity criterion, a collapse is the fragmentation of the network through which goods, information, and obligations circulate: the largest component of that network shatters into parts that no longer reach one another, regardless of what happens to total population or output. On the legibility criterion, a collapse is the failure of shared norms to make members' behavior mutually predictable, so that the society ceases to be a single thing anyone can plan around—and this can occur with no decline in population or material throughput at all. Compare the two. Does either entail the other? Can there be fragmentation without a loss of legibility, or legibility without fragmentation? Then test both criteria against three cases: the end of the Bronze Age in the eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya, and Rapa Nui. For each, state what the connectivity criterion would count as collapse, what the legibility criterion would count as collapse, and whether the two verdicts diverge. Finally, tell me whether one criterion is doing the real explanatory work while the other merely redescribes it in different words, or whether they carve the phenomenon at genuinely different joints; if the latter, which should anchor the concept of collapse, and what would a historian give up by choosing it? Please answer in American English.
