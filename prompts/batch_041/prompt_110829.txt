I have been reading the rationality blogs for close to twenty years, and I now run a small upper-level seminar on the foundations of quantum mechanics for students who mostly arrived by the same route. I am the instructor of record and I am grading the midterms. Below is one exam question and one student's answer.

Question 3 (20 points). Bell's theorem is commonly summarized as showing that no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce the quantum-mechanical correlations. A classmate proposes the following: the shared state of the particle pair and the two analyser settings are all fixed by conditions laid down in the common past of the experiment, so that the settings and the outcomes are correlated from the outset; each analyser setting is generated in the lab by a process that is deterministic but, for all practical purposes, unpredictable; and each outcome is then determined by the local state and the local setting, with no influence passing between the wings. (a) Does this proposal evade the theorem, and if so, at which step of the derivation? (b) Does it restore locality? (c) What does the availability of such a proposal tell us about what the theorem does and does not establish?

One student's answer, reproduced as written:

(a) Yes. The derivation assumes that the hidden state λ is statistically independent of the settings a and b, that is, that P(λ | a, b) = P(λ); in other words, it assumes the settings are genuinely random. The proposal denies exactly this assumption, since the state and the settings share a common cause. Bell's result is a conditional: local causality together with λ-independence together with the quantum predictions is inconsistent. Remove one conjunct and the inconsistency has no force, and the model reproduces the singlet correlations. The theorem is evaded at the step usually called measurement independence.

(b) Yes, and this is the whole point. Nothing is transmitted between the wings, so nothing here conflicts with relativity; no measurement on one side can be used to send information to the other, and the marginal statistics on each side are unaffected by what is done on the other. Locality is therefore restored, because the only thing that ever looked nonlocal was the correlation, and correlations are not causal connections.

(c) It shows that Bell's theorem is not a proof that nature is nonlocal. What the experiments exclude is a certain combination of local causality, measurement independence, and the quantum predictions. Since λ-independence cannot be tested by experiment, the theorem leaves the choice open, and the naturalistic conclusion is that the conspiracy is real: a common cause in the deep past is a smaller price than action at a distance, which would mean giving up relativity. The theorem is best read as a constraint on what a physical theory may say rather than as a discovery about the world.

I would like a grade out of 20 and, more than that, a point-by-point assessment: which claims earn credit, which do not, and what a full-credit answer would have had to say. I want the specific comments, not an overall impression.
