I coordinate research for a mid-sized urban school district, and since a contentious board hearing last month — where one speaker cited twin studies to argue that our achievement gap is largely genetic, another cited the same literature to argue the opposite, and I was asked by parents at two schools whether their child's score means something fixed about them — I have become the person who vets every heritability claim that reaches the board or the superintendent. I need a decision procedure I can run myself, in about twenty minutes per claim, without a geneticist in the room.

Give me the questions in the order I should ask them: what a study has to report before I can even tell what quantity it estimated (which population, which range of environments, which design — twin, adoption, or DNA-based — and what each design quietly assumes); how to tell whether a figure is being used to explain variation within a group or a difference between groups, and why only one of those uses is even in principle supported by the usual estimate; how to handle the fact that a heritability coefficient is a ratio that moves when the environmental variance moves, so that a rise or fall in it is not evidence that genes matter more or less in any absolute sense; how to treat gene-environment correlation and interaction, and the gap between twin-based and DNA-based estimates of the same trait; and finally how to translate whatever survives into a claim about what we may expect from an intervention. I want to know what such an estimate can license, what it cannot, and where the number is simply silent and the question becomes one of values rather than facts.

Then run the procedure on three claims actually in front of me: (1) a regional study reporting that the heritability of math achievement in our district rose from roughly 0.4 to roughly 0.7 over two decades, which an op-ed has taken as proof that schooling cannot close gaps; (2) an argument that because reading disability is strongly heritable, we should shift screening money toward family-history-based identification and away from classroom instruction; and (3) our own draft brief, which says the gap is environmental on the grounds that within-group heritability estimates say nothing about group differences. I expect the procedure to be unkind to the first two; I need to know whether it is equally unkind to the third, because if our own brief fails the checklist I have to rewrite it before Monday.

Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a formula, which steps I can run with no numbers at all, and what the procedure cannot settle. I would rather have a procedure that flags its own limits than one that pretends to be mechanical.
