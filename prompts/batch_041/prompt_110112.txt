I'm a third-year philosophy student writing a final paper for a course on Wittgenstein and the philosophy of mind, and I've decided to write on Robert Zemeckis's Cast Away. My working premise is that the film is a dramatization of the private language argument, but I suspect that is too quick, and I want a reading that is honest about where it fails.

The material: Chuck Noland, a FedEx systems engineer, survives a crash over the Pacific and spends years alone on an island, salvaging cargo that includes a volleyball. He gives it a face and a name, Wilson, talks with it, deliberates with it, and grieves when it is lost at sea during his escape. Please read the film against the argument in Wittgenstein's later work, and be precise about which of the theses people habitually run together is actually at stake: that a private language is impossible; that it could never be known to have been used; or that privacy and language are from the outset an incoherent combination.

Two things I want pressed especially. First, Chuck's speech is not a private language in the strict sense — he is speaking English, learned in a community, so years of solitude do not test the hypothesis at all; what they test is whether meaning survives without an interlocutor, and the film seems to answer that it does not, which is why he manufactures one. Second, Wilson is the hard case: the only criteria available to Chuck for the ball's states are his own behaviour toward it. Does that show criteria can be self-supplied, which would pressure the appeal to public criteria, or that his behaviour is parasitic on the community that formed him, which would support it? Say which way the film's evidence cuts and how confident you are.

Close by saying what the film adds that the argument alone does not — my hunch is that it relocates the issue from the solitary diarist's sensation vocabulary to the need to address a second person, and I want you to say whether that is a genuine addition or a change of subject. Where the film resists the reading, say so plainly: it is a film about time, purpose and love, and its emotional effectiveness may beg the question rather than supply evidence. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which scene, or which way of reading the later Wittgenstein, would overturn your verdict — and where your reading is likeliest to fail.
