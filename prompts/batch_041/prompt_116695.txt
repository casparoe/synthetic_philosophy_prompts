I have been trying to work out, on my own, which tradition gets the relation between the person and the community right — the East Asian one or the Western one — and I keep running into trouble. I had been assuming that "individualism" and "collectivism" name the two available answers to a single question: whether the individual or the group is the more basic and more valuable thing. On that assumption, anyone who emphasizes relationships, duties, and harmony is a collectivist, and anyone who emphasizes rights and self-reliance is an individualist, so Confucianism comes out collectivist, the modern West comes out individualist, and the only thing left to decide is which of the two is correct. What unsettles me is that the Confucian tradition seems to care intensely about the moral formation of the single person, while Western liberalism constantly demands sacrifice for the common good. Have I misdescribed the disagreement — does the individualism/collectivism axis mash together several separate questions, about what persons are made of, about what has value, and about how to explain what people do, which the two traditions answer in different combinations? If a view can say that persons are constituted by their relationships without saying that the group outranks the individual, then maybe neither label fits. What, if anything, is the real point of contrast, and does either side actually get it right?
