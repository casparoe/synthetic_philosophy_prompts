Something about museum repatriation buggs me and I can't put my finger on the philosophical shape of it. When a museum won't give an object back, the argument is usually that it belongs to all of humanity and is safer in a big encyclopedic collection; when the same museum does give something back, the argument flips and the object belongs to a particular people, whose claim beats everyone else's. Maybe those are just different cases, but here's the part I can't get past. The things being returned were almost always taken from a polity that doesn't exist anymore -- the bronzes were looted in 1897 from the Kingdom of Benin, and they go back to the modern Nigerian state and its institutions, not to the kingdom or the lineage that actually held them. So the practice seems to quietly assume that today's nation-state simply inherits the cultural claims of whatever kingdom or chiefdom an object came out of, even though the borders, the government, and the state's relationship to the original owners have all been completely transformed since. What view of who owns culture does that presuppose -- is culture owned by a state, by a people, by whoever holds power now, or is it better understood as something else entirely, like a trust held for the future? And do the keep-it argument and the give-it-back argument actually contradict each other, or could a single consistent theory of what cultural objects are for license both? Tell me what the practice really commits you to, what it merely fits with, and where it's just underdetermined.
