I am writing a dissertation chapter on the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and before I commit to a framing I need to anticipate how my advisor will react. She has spent years giving verdicts, in seminar and in comments on drafts, on whether particular pairs of theories are genuinely underdetermined by the evidence. Her record so far:

- Classical mechanics written with forces and accelerations, versus the same mechanics written in Lagrangian or Hamiltonian form: not a genuine case. Her reason was that nothing is underdetermined where nothing is disagreed about.
- Newtonian mechanics with absolute space, on which there is a fact of the matter about which bodies are truly at rest, versus a formulation that postulates only absolute acceleration, so that rest is always rest relative to something: genuine.
- Standard quantum mechanics versus Bohmian mechanics, which she regards as agreeing on every prediction while disagreeing about determinism, about whether the wave function is complete, and about whether particles have definite positions at all times: genuine.
- Two rival accounting schemes for the evolution of cooperation, one assigning the fitness effects of a trait to the actor and its relatives, the other assigning them to the group: not a genuine case; she says the two can be translated into each other line by line.
- A proposed rival to our best particle theory positing a new charged particle light enough that experiments already run would have produced it: not a genuine case, since the evidence does bear on the proposal, and she will not allow that rescuing it with a further auxiliary hypothesis converts it into a serious rival.

Two cases are now on my desk.

First: a rival to our best fundamental theory, constructed by adding a new kind of entity that is, by stipulation, causally inert and undetectable, so that it makes no difference to any observation we could ever make; it was introduced not to explain any anomaly but simply to generate a rival.

Second: a pair of rival extensions of our best fundamental theory, each defended seriously by working physicists, each fitting every datum we have, differing only over what happens at energies far beyond anything we can build or reasonably expect to build, so that no existing or planned experiment distinguishes them.

Predict her verdict on each of these. Then set out the rules that fit her record — I believe more than one does — and say what each rule implies for the two new cases, where those implications come apart, which rule you think she is in fact running and on what grounds, and which single further case would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, together with what each possible verdict on that case would establish. I want your reasoning rather than references. Finally, tell me how much confidence the prediction deserves and what would change your mind: which further case, or which fact about her other philosophical commitments, would push you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is likeliest to break down.
