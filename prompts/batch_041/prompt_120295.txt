ok, quick one. I'm in a political philosophy course and we just did distributive justice, and I can't stop thinking about how pro sports leagues are set up. In the NBA and the NFL the league caps how much any single team can pay its players, the teams that finish worst get the first pick in the draft, and the rich teams have to hand money over to the poor ones through revenue sharing. So the best player in the league can't be paid like the best player in the league, and the worst team gets handed the best new talent. Meanwhile the exact same league hands out MVPs, retires jerseys, inducts people into a Hall of Fame and sells them as legends. What does that package presuppose about desert and fairness? Is it the view that reward should track contribution, with the cap as a straight contradiction of it, or is it the view that competitive balance is the good actually being produced, so what a player deserves is whatever keeps the games close? Does the draft, which literally rewards being bad, rule out any desert-based reading all by itself? My prof keeps bringing up Jantelagen and tall poppy syndrome like its some Scandinavian or Australian hangup, but this looks like the same instinct built into one of the most American institutions there is, so I'm suspicious of that framing. Pick one reading and defend it as the best fit for the whole package, say what it does with the MVP awards and the Hall of Fame, tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined and would fit several theories just as well, and what would have to go if you swapped that reading out for a straight desert view. Don't hedge, I want your verdict, not a menu.
