I retired last spring after 38 years in a chemistry lab (electrochemistry, mostly), and Ive fallen in with a philosophy reading group at the library — retired engineers, a couple of high-school teachers, one fellow who did math as an undergrad. They've asked me to run next month's session on Russell's paradox and why it finished off the naive idea of a set. I want to plan the session before I plan the slides.

Don't explain the paradox to me. Tell me how to teach it.

The order. What has to be in place before the paradox can land as a problem rather than a curiosity? My instinct is that they have to genuinely believe 'any property you can state determines a collection' — believe it enough to have signed off on it — before I show them it can't be true. So how much set machinery do I need first, and what's the minimum? Do they need functions, ordered pairs, cardinality, any of that? And a related thing I'm unsure about: does the contradiction need the set of all sets to exist, or does a single instance of the principle do all the work? I think it's the latter and that this matters for how I sequence things, but I'd like you to tell me.

The example that carries it. I won't use the barber, and I won't use the catalogue of all catalogues that don't list themselves — both make it look like a riddle about words rather than a failure of a principle they'd have endorsed a minute earlier. Is there something better? Here's what I've been playing with: drop sets entirely and use adjectives. Call an adjective self-describing if it has the property it names — 'short' is short, 'English' is English — and non-self-describing otherwise — 'long' isn't long, 'French' isn't French. Now ask about 'non-self-describing': if it's self-describing then it has the property it names, so it isn't; if it isn't, then it fails to have the property it names, so it is. Same squeeze, nothing but a dictionary. Is that a good carrying example, or have I just swapped one riddle for another? If it's good, where does it sit in the sequence, and what do I have to say immediately after it so that nobody walks away with 'so language is broken'?

The misconceptions, in the order they'll arise, and where to head each one off. My guesses: (a) it's wordplay, a cousin of 'this sentence is false', so it's the philosophers' problem and not the mathematicians'; (b) self-reference is the culprit, so the cure is simply not letting things refer to themselves; (c) whatever the repair is, it's an arbitrary rule bolted on to save the theory; (d) since the repair works, naive sets were never doing real work anyway, so nothing was lost; (e) sets aren't real, or mathematics is just a convention. Which of these will they actually form, which are wrong or half-right, and where in the sequence — before the paradox, during it, after the repair — does each have to be defused? Related: I want an 'aha' at the end, namely that once you restrict the principle the paradox doesn't vanish, it becomes a theorem (there's no set of everything, and no set of all sets that aren't members of themselves), and that this is a feature rather than a wound. Can a 60-minute sequence actually deliver that, or does it just sound like a dodge?

What to leave out, and how to say I'm leaving it out. I know there's a zoo of further cures — types, classes, paraconsistent logic — and I don't want to open those doors, and I definitely don't want to wander into Gödel. But is there one sentence I should say precisely so they don't conclude the repair I do show them is the only possible one?

As a lab person my instinct is to frame the whole thing as an instrument that reads correctly everywhere except one dial setting. Useful, or does that mislead?

The check. One question to ask at the end that shows they understood why the unrestricted principle had to go, as opposed to just being able to recite the paradox back at me.

60 minutes, whiteboard, no handouts. These people are sharp but they haven't done formal logic; they'll take symbols if I don't drown them.
