I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I would like the analysis built up from first principles rather than drawn from a literature I have not read. I will supply both the episode and the framework myself and ask you to bring them into contact.

The episode is the British Employment and Support Allowance regime and the Work Capability Assessment that decides eligibility for it. Treat the following as stipulated, so that nothing turns on your recalling the details: this is a benefit for adults whose illness or disability makes ordinary work difficult; eligibility is decided by a formal assessment that sorts claimants into a group expected to prepare for work and a group excused from it; the assessment is a scored test assembled from a fixed list of functional descriptors, with a threshold score; for most of its life it was administered by a private contractor; unsuccessful claimants can appeal to a tribunal, and a large share of those appeals succeeded; and the scheme drew sustained public controversy, including reported cases of claimants who died soon after being assessed as fit for work. The exact figures do not matter for what I am asking, so if you are unsure of a number, say so and proceed without it.

I want to run that episode through a way of sorting the justifications for attaching conditions to benefits, which I suspect are usually conflated. Treat conditionality as potentially serving three distinct functions. First, reciprocity: those who can contribute should, and the condition enforces fair terms of cooperation between claimants and everyone else, the wrong it addresses being unfairness to those who do work. Second, desert or character sorting: the condition separates the genuinely needy from the undeserving, on the view that assistance should track willingness or character rather than need alone. Third, rationing: the condition is chiefly an administrative device for holding down the numbers and the cost, whatever moral story is offered on its behalf. My suspicion, which I want tested rather than assumed, is that these come apart — that a scheme can be justified in the language of the first while operating as the third, and that the place this shows up is in what the scheme does with people who plainly cannot reciprocate at all, whether it routes them around the machinery or pushes them through it.

Please do three things. First, map the episode onto the three functions: for each, state what it predicts about how the assessment would be designed, who the relevant actors are and what each of them faces, and what would count as the scheme succeeding or failing on that reading. Second, adjudicate which function the design and its observed behaviour actually serve, and give the evidence — the structure of the test, the incentives of the assessors and the contractor, the handling of the manifestly incapable, the pattern of appeals and how they were resolved — saying where two functions pull against each other and which one the design in fact favoured. Third, run the analysis the other way: what does the episode show about the framework itself? Does it confirm the three-way split, or collapse two of the functions into one, or expose a fourth the framework never anticipated — for instance, a function that is really about the state managing its own exposure rather than about the claimants at all?

I think of this the way I think about a specification that has drifted from what a system actually optimises for, so be disciplined about the mapping and explicit about where the framework runs out. If the episode turns out to be a case the framework was never built to handle, say so plainly and say what a better one would have to contain. Do not force the theory to speak where it has nothing to say.
