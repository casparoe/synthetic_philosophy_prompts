I am a high school student taking a philosophy of religion elective, and our unit this term is on karma. We have been reading the Milindapañha, or Questions of King Milinda, an ancient Buddhist dialogue between the monk Nāgasena and King Milinda, in a nineteenth-century translation whose English is frequently harder for me than the doctrine. One exchange has stopped me cold, and what I want is help working through it rather than a summary of Buddhist teaching.

Here is the passage:

"‘Is there any being, Nāgasena, who transmigrates from this body to another?’
‘No, there is not.’
‘But if so, would it not get free from its evil deeds.’
‘Yes, if it were not reborn; but if it were, no.’
‘Give me an illustration.’
‘Suppose, O king, a man were to steal another man’s mangoes, would the thief deserve punishment?’
‘Yes.’
‘But he would not have stolen the mangoes the other set in the ground. Why would he deserve punishment?’
‘Because those he stole were the result of those that were planted.’"

Nāgasena then applies the image directly: when one name-and-form commits deeds, pure or impure, it is by that karma that another name-and-form is reborn, and so the doer is not set free from its evil deeds. Our teacher glosses name-and-form as the mind-and-body complex that is reborn, and says the point of the illustration is that karma is like a debt that follows the fruit rather than the planter, but that explanation left me more confused than the text did.

What I cannot see is how the illustration answers the king's question. I read the king as objecting that if nothing transmigrates, then the one who acted has ceased, so the deserts of the act have no one to land on and the act drops out of the picture altogether. The mango case, as I read it, shows only that where one thing comes to be as a result of another, the later one can be answerable for the earlier one — but that the later person is causally descended from the earlier was never in dispute; it is the claim, not the argument. I have two further worries. First, the thief in the story is punished for his own act of taking, and the planting serves only to settle whose fruit it is; the karma case seems to require the punishment to fall on someone for an act he did not do, which the story does not illustrate. Second, the thief is liable because the planter is still there, still the owner of the fruit that grew from what he set in the ground, and a continuing owner looks like exactly what the no-self teaching denies — so the illustration may be assuming the thing it is meant to prove.

My teacher also compared the position to the live argument about whether people today can owe anything for wrongs committed by their ancestors, and I could not tell whether the Buddhist answer to the king carries over to that question or whether it works only if some continuing subject is admitted, in which case the comparison would tell against Nāgasena.

I would be grateful for a reconstruction of the argument in this passage: what precisely the king is asking, what work the mango comparison is doing at each step, and whether it succeeds on its own terms. If it does not, I would like to know what a better defence of karma without a self would have to say, and whether the same defence would settle the question about inherited debts.
