I'm a third-year student in the second half of a course on classical Chinese philosophy, and for the past few weeks we've been reading the Zhuangzi, mostly the passages that circle around death — the friends who go on singing when one of them is dying, the skull that tells Zhuangzi how pleasant it is to be dead, and the scene everyone remembers, where Zhuangzi's wife has died and Hui Shi comes to offer condolences only to find him squatting on the ground, pounding on a tub and singing. I find the whole outlook beautiful and I would like it to be right. But a classmate in the seminar who refuses to be charmed keeps pressing an argument on us that I cannot answer, and I have finally written it out in premises:

(1) Dying is a transformation of the very stuff that composes a living person: material that was briefly gathered into a body is dispersed and later regathered, exactly as it is in falling asleep, in growing old, and in the turning of the seasons. (2) In the transformations we can actually observe — sleep, ageing, the passage from one season to the next — nothing bad happens to the thing transformed simply in virtue of its being transformed. (3) So nothing bad happens to a person simply in virtue of their dying; death is not a harm to the one who dies. (4) An emotion is fitting exactly when its object really has the feature that the emotion presents it as having, and grief presents its object as something bad that has happened. (5) So grief at a death is never fitting. (6) The reasoning is indifferent to whose death it is: it applies to a stranger, to a friend, and to one's own child. (7) But a parent's grief at the death of a child is not a mistake, and I will not accept the verdict in (6). (8) Therefore one of (1) through (5) is false.

What unsettles me is that if the argument works it does not merely license Zhuangzi's drumming; it would also leave me without any reason grounded in the drowning child's own good to pull her out of the river, since dying would be no harm to her. I can't tell whether one of the steps is doing something illicit, or whether the argument is sound and one of the things I want to keep simply has to go. If I had to choose I would rather keep (1) and (2), the picture of dying as one more seasonal reworking of the same stuff, than keep (4); but I want to know what that choice actually costs me. Which step should I give up, why did it look so plausible in the first place, and what do the live alternatives each require me to surrender? I am not asking for a survey of what the commentators say; I want the argument itself assessed.
