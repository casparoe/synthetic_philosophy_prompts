I spent thirty-five years as a health physicist — mostly keeping radiation doses at a nuclear site and two hospital departments inside the rules — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy seriously, decision theory and ethics mainly, which is a strange hobby for a 68-year-old but here we are. What keeps nagging at me is something that never bothered me while I was actually working. The rulebook we operated under had two halves that I'm no longer sure were speaking the same language. On one side there was a hard limit: nobody was permitted to exceed a certain annual dose, period, and if someone did it was an incident, an investigation, a report to the regulator, the whole business. That dose was a line you didn't cross, and it made no difference whether crossing it would have saved the plant a fortune or spared us a nasty shutdown. On the other side, well below the limit, we were under a standing obligation to keep doses as low as reasonably achievable, and "reasonably" was cashed out in an explicit cost-benifit calculation — you multiply the collective dose by a monetary value per unit of dose, compare that against the cost of the extra shielding or the extra staffing, and if the shielding is cheaper than the dose avoided, you build the shielding. So the same physical event, a millisievert landing in somebody's body, was sometimes an absolute wrong that no amount of money could justify and sometimes a commodity with a price tag, tradeable against concrete costs, depending only on whether we happened to be above or below an arbitrary number on a chart. I'm told this is a familiar shape of problem in philosophy, but I'd like to see it worked through properly rather than gestured at.

What I want from you is this. Tell me what theory of risk and value that whole apparatus presupposes — and be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit. Is it just expected-utility maximization with a deontological side-constraint bolted on, where the ceiling has nothing to do with risk attitudes and everything to do with a rule against treating people as means? Or does the structure actually need something else, namely the thought that risk itself is what we're averse to, so that a small chance of a bad outcome deserves more weight than its probability alone would give it? A colleague in the economics department once tried to explain a view to me — I believe it was called risk-weighted expected utility — in which you don't simply multiply each outcome's probability by its value; you first bend the probabilities, so that the worse outcomes get overweighted relative to the better ones, and then you maximize. If our limits-and-optimization setup is really a clumsy institutional way of doing that bending, say so and show me how. If instead it's plain expected utility plus a side-constraint that has nothing to do with risk weighting, say that, and tell me what the constraint is doing that the arithmetic couldn't. I'd also like to know where the practice is underdetermined: my guess is that several quite different theories would each license everything we did, and I want to know which features of the practice actually pin a theory down and which are just regulatory sediment.

Then push it a step further. What would genuinely change if we swapped the presupposed view for straight expected-value reasoning — would we ever let a worker go over the ceiling when the expected benefit were large enough, and what would the ALARA calculation look like if we stopped pretending the ceiling was absolute and instead put a very large but finite price on a bad outcome? And going the other way, if the risk-weighted picture is right, is there any principled reason for the line to sit where it sits, or does the whole thing collapse into one continuous tradeoff with no line in it anywhere? I'm not after a reading list or citations — I can't follow them up anyway, and I'd rather have the argument — so just lay it out as you would for someone who spent his life measuring things and is only now learning the vocabulary.
