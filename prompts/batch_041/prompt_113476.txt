My freind and I got into a stupidly long arguement last night about a puzzle she heard in a grad seminar. The setup: you're dropped somewhere in a territory that's split into Red and Blue, and each of those is split into sub-regions (one of them has a headquarters area and a second-company area, or something like that). You have a map with the sizes, but you don't know where you are. Then you get a radio message, and the message isn't a plain fact like "you're in Red" — it's conditional: "if you're in Red, you're probably in the headquarters part." And the question is how you're supposed to change your probabilities after hearing that. My friend says there's a standard answer; I say there isn't, or at least not an obvious one, because the thing you learned isn't a proposition you can just conditionalize on. We couldn't even agree on what a good answer would look like.

So before I read anything else on this, tell me what a theory of updating on conditional information would actually have to get right. Not a list of named theories — the requirements. Which cases it has to handle (the radio message case, ordinary conditionalization as a special case, cases where the conditional has probability 1, cases where it doesn't, cases where you learn several conditionals in a row and the order shouldn't matter, etc.), which principles it can't violate, what it has to say about how much your probabilities are allowed to move, and for each requirement, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Also tell me which of these are non-negotiable versus just preferences of one camp, flag any requirements that might be jointly unsatisfiable, and say which single requirement does the most to rule out candidate rules.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
