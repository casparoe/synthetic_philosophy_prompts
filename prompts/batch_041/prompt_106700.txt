For the better part of thirty years I studied social insects. I marked foragers, counted trophallactic exchanges, and wrote up colony-level statistics in the passive voice, as though the colony itself were the agent doing the foraging and choosing the nest — a grammatical habit I never examined. Only after I retired did I begin reading philosophy in earnest, and I found that the question I had been dodging for decades has a name and a literature. Hofstadter, in the book where he braids Gödel and Escher and Bach together, imagines an ant colony he calls Aunt Hillary that can hold up its end of a conversation, and asks whether a colony might have a mind when no single ant has anything remotely like one. I cannot decide, and I have stopped believing the indecision is a defect in me.

I would like you to write me a short story that puts this question back in the field rather than in the seminar room. Aim for roughly 2,500 words, a single point of view, and no framing lecture: the ideas have to live in the events and the voices, not in an essay smuggled in through a character's mouth.

Here is the situation I have in mind, though you may depart from it where the story demands. My protagonist is a field biologist near the end of a long career, who keeps two records of the same colony. One is a low-level log — every marked ant, every trail, every exchange, nothing attributed to anyone but the individual insect. The other is a high-level narrative in which the colony is written as a single character with moods, hesitations, and what can only be called preferences. Both records are scrupulously accurate. Neither can be translated into the other without loss, and the biologist cannot say which one is the real description.

Three things I want the story to stage. First, an episode in which the high-level narrative anticipates something the low-level log does not — an evacuation, a choice between two food sources, a swarm decision — and then the suspicion, which the reader must also feel, that this anticipation is a retrodiction dressed up as a prediction, that the narrative only looked prescient because it was written after the fact. Second, the intuition behind the fugue in Hofstadter's title: the individual ants as voices, the colony as the piece, and the question of whether the piece exists anywhere in the voices at all. Use music concretely rather than as a tidy allegory — the biologist should have a reason to be thinking about counterpoint that has nothing to do with the argument. Third, an ending that refuses a verdict. No character should win. But by the last page the reader should see why neither "it is only ants" nor "of course it is a mind" will do, and should feel the specific discomfort of not being able to say which description the colony answers to.

Invent the field station, the colony, and the younger colleague who thinks the whole high-level record is sentimentality; keep them fictional and do not lean on real researchers or real papers, and please give me no citations — I am not after a reading list, and a model working without a library would only be guessing at titles. Where the science of ant colonies appears, keep it plausible; where it is invented, I would rather you invent frankly than dress a guess as fact.

After the story, add a short author's note of no more than two paragraphs. Tell me where the piece takes liberties with the ideas it dramatizes, and which reading of the colony-mind question it leans on where that question is genuinely contested. Then say how confident you are that the story is faithful to the philosophical positions it stages, and single out the one point in it you are least sure of.
