For a graduate seminar I'm taking on the philosophy of computation, I've been asked to stage a dialogue between two people who disagree about how far the results of computational complexity theory actually reach beyond the theory itself. I'd like you to write it. The scene is a reading group that has just finished a session on worst-case versus average-case hardness, and the two interlocutors are a philosopher of science and a complexity theorist. Both should be genuinely capable and neither should be a strawman; I want the philosopher to have real teeth and the theorist to have real teeth.

The philosopher's opening move is that results such as the NP-hardness of finding the shortest tour through a set of cities, or the intractability of general automated planning, license a conclusion about real agents rather than only about algorithms: no resource-bounded system — a brain, a market, a bureaucracy — should be expected to solve such problems exactly, and so the ubiquity of heuristics, approximation, and institutional workarounds is not a contingent fact about how we happen to be built but a necessity imposed by the mathematics. The theorist's opening reply is that worst-case asymptotic hardness is a statement about a sequence of adversarially constructed instances as their size grows, and that it says almost nothing about the structured, average-case instances real agents actually encounter; on this view the philosopher has mistaken a fact about a mathematical limit for a fact about the world. I'd like the dialogue to develop from there: the philosopher should have a comeback that doesn't merely restate the opening — for instance, pressing on whether the instances a given organism or institution faces really are drawn from a benign distribution, or on what "the instance" even is when the agent is the thing being modeled — and the theorist should be forced to say what, if anything, complexity theory could tell us about real limits if not this. Let each side make at least one concession, and let them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than trading monologues. Please don't rest any part of the dialogue on particular published papers or names; the arguments should stand on their own.
