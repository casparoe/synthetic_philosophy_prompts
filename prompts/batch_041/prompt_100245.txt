I have spent the last several months trying to decide whether to have a child, and the question has defeated every attempt I have made to reason about it in the ordinary way. When I described my difficulty to a friend, she offered what she took to be a decisive analogy. Deciding to have a child, she said, is like emigrating to a country you have never visited: you may read the guidebooks, study the language, and listen to those who already live there, but you cannot know what it is like to live there until you are living there; and by the time you have arrived, you are no longer quite the person who set out. From this she drew a practical conclusion — that since the requisite knowledge is simply unavailable in advance, there is nothing to deliberate about, and one should go.

I am not persuaded, but I do not want a verdict; I want the analogy audited. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the guidebooks, the language, the departure, the arrival, the destination, and the person who changes — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in three disanalogies that I suspect are doing real work.

First, the traveller can in principle return home, whereas the choice before me cannot be undone. I want to know whether the analogy's persuasive force depends on a reversibility that it quietly imports from the travel case and that my case lacks.

Second, the country the emigrant reaches is a determinate place that existed before the decision and that is known, at least in principle, to its inhabitants. In the case before me it is not clear that anyone occupies the vantage point of the inhabitants — that there is a settled fact about the life on the other side which someone already knows. Tell me whether the analogy presupposes such a vantage point, and what becomes of the argument if there is none.

Third, the analogy treats the chooser and the choice as separable, so that one and the same person can be imagined weighing the life left behind against the life arrived at. But if the transformation changes the chooser's preferences, then neither the person before nor the person after may be in a position to judge whether the change was for the better. Does the analogy have any room for this, or does it silently assume that the standards of evaluation survive the crossing?

Finally, tell me whether my friend's conclusion — that deliberation is pointless and one should simply go — survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If it cannot bear the weight, say what a better analogy would be, and what it would and would not license.
