I'm a science and ideas journalist at work on a long feature about pleasure, and I've run into a philosophical problem I'd like to get straight before I commit to a framing. My reporting keeps circling the distinction between the brain systems that make us want things and the systems that make us enjoy them, and around a body of work suggesting that what we call pleasure is less a single process than a motley — the animal contentment of a hot bath, the sharp relief of bad news turning out to be false, the slow satisfaction of a proof finally clicking, the pleasure some people take in a rival's humiliation. A researcher I interviewed said that pleasure is a gerrymandered category, closer to weed than to water. A philosopher I spoke with drew what he took to be the inevitable conclusion: hedonism — the thesis that pleasure alone is good in itself and pain alone bad in itself — has been refuted not by any counterexample but by its own central term. I find myself persuaded, and I'd like to state the point sharply in the piece: if there is no single feeling common to all pleasures, then there is no single good for hedonism to name, so hedonism is not merely false but ill-formed, and the serious options left are a pluralist objective list and desire-satisfaction.

Two things nag at me, though, and I'd like your help working out which of us is right. First, a colleague argues that the heterogeneity of pleasure shows only that pleasure cannot be defined by one sensation, not that it fails to be one value, and that the view can be repaired by saying pleasures are unified by something other than how they feel — by being experiences one takes a certain pro-attitude toward, say, or experiences that seem to their subject to be good. That strikes me as either circular or a costume change: if pleasure is just what we are for, hedonism says the good is what we are for, which is desire-satisfaction wearing hedonism's name. Second, I don't see how hedonism can so much as be stated without a common currency: if two pleasures have nothing in common, on what scale is one greater than another, and does not the very injunction to maximize pleasure presuppose the single quality that the science is supposed to have discredited?

So — is the researcher right that heterogeneity kills hedonism, is my colleague right that it kills only one formulation of it, and if the latter, what exactly does the surviving view say pleasure is? If that view does survive, tell me what work the heterogeneity problem was really doing all along, since I'd rather not write the piece around a conclusion I can't defend.
