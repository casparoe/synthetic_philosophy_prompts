I have been reading LessWrong and its diaspora for close to two decades now, and the episode I keep circling back to is the 2010 post in which a user named Roko proposed that a future benevolent superintelligence might precommit to punishing anyone who learned of its possible existence and nonetheless failed to work toward bringing it about. What interests me is not the punishment but the trigger: the argument treats mere knowledge as sufficient to place you in the threatened class, and the community's response — treating the idea itself as an information hazard and banning discussion of it — tacitly conceded that the thought experiment's grip comes from that premise. I would like to see that premise tested against a work of fiction instead of another decision-theory post.

Read Ursula K. Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the structure of the basilisk argument. I want the correspondence drawn explicitly. The child in the cellar plays something like the role of the future agent whose suffering or power is the price of the city's flourishing; the citizens who are told and stay play the contributors; the ones who walk away play the refusers; and the narrator's refusal to say where they go plays the question of whether there is any outside to the threat. Then give me the disanalogies that matter. Omelas's bargain is causal and present — the child's misery actually sustains the festival — whereas the basilisk's leverage is entirely acausal and its punishment is threatened rather than carried out. Le Guin's citizens can verify that the child exists; the basilisk's existence is only a probability estimate over a decision procedure we cannot inspect. And the story never says the ones who leave are punished, which is precisely what the basilisk argument denies. Tell me which of these differences are load-bearing and which are decoration.

I also want the honest critic's verdict on where the story resists the reading. Le Guin wrote the piece as a utilitarianism thought experiment and said as much, so importing an acausal-decision-theory frame risks putting words in her mouth. But does the story nonetheless dramatize something real about the basilisk — that knowledge creates the obligation, and that there is no clean way to unknow a thing once you have understood it? Or does the basilisk argument need something the story suggests is optional, namely the conviction that walking away is itself a move within the game rather than an exit from it?

Close by saying what the story adds that the forum arguments do not: whether fiction makes the acausal threat clearer or merely makes it feel more vivid, and whether the ones who walk away constitute a genuine counterexample to the basilisk's logic or only an evasion of it. I am not interested in the usual dismissals — that it is Pascal's wager in new clothes, or that a friendly AI would never follow through — since those answer the conclusion without touching the structure that generates it.
