At a recent interdisciplinary workshop on causation that I attended as a reporter, two philosophers got into a dispute over a hospital case, and I have been unable to decide who had the better of it. I am not a philosopher, and I am reconstructing the exchange as faithfully as I can, without taking sides.

The case: a patient with a documented penicillin allergy is admitted with pneumonia. The electronic order set defaults to piperacillin-tazobactam. The resident accepts the default without opening the allergy tab. The patient develops anaphylaxis and dies. Everyone agrees that had the resident checked the allergy tab, she would have changed the antibiotic and the patient would very likely have survived.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

1. The first philosopher argued that the resident’s failure to check caused the death. The test is difference-making: had she checked, the death would not have occurred; that is the core of what it is for something to be a cause.

2. The second philosopher objected that this is not a cause at all. Causes are events or states that bring things about; the antibiotic brought about the anaphylaxis. The non-check is an absence, a gap in a process, not a part of it. We can blame the resident, but blame is not causation.

3. The first replied that we do not treat the penicillin allergy as the cause in the same way, even though it was also necessary. We single out the omission because it was avoidable and abnormal, and because law and medicine routinely treat failures to act as causes.

4. The second said that this concession proves the point. The singling-out depends on a norm. If the hospital protocol had said “do not check allergies,” the counterfactual would still be true—had she checked, the death would not have occurred—but we would no longer call her omission a cause. So the causal claim is doing normative work, not metaphysical work.

5. The first answered that difference-making accounts can restrict themselves to relevant possibilities, ones in which the agent has the relevant information and capacity. That is not covert normativity; it is part of the truth conditions for causal claims. And even if norms shape which difference-makers we call causes, that is causal selection, not causation. The omission is still a difference-maker in the world.

6. The second replied that this makes causation depend on how we draw the model. Change the model—treat the allergy as fixed, treat the resident’s choice as fixed—and the omission disappears as a cause. A relation that can be made to disappear by redescription is not a worldly relation.

7. The first said that all causal claims are model-relative in that way. We do not say the Big Bang caused the death, even though without it the patient would not exist. We select. That does not make causation unreal; it makes it perspectival. The resident’s omission is a cause in the model that matters for assigning responsibility.

8. The second responded that if it is only a cause in a model that matters for responsibility, then it is not describing the world; it is describing our practices. And without a norm, the view proves too much: if I fail to donate to famine relief, and someone dies who would have lived had I donated, did I cause that death? The first said no, only role-based or norm-governed omissions count. The second said that is exactly the point: you need the norm, and the norm is not in the counterfactual.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then identify what each party is missing, and give an overall verdict on whether omissions can be causes—and if so, in what sense. Do not simply summarize familiar positions; engage the specific arguments as actually made.
