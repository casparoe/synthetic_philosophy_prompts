I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one of the arguments I keep returning to is the old dialogue in which a tortoise grants a pair of premises but refuses the conditional that would license the conclusion, so that every attempt to supply the missing conditional simply generates a further conditional. I would like a poem that makes this regress audible rather than merely describing it. Please write a pantoum—four-line stanzas in which lines two and four of each stanza recur as lines one and three of the next, and the first and third lines of the opening stanza return in the closing stanza—in the voice of the tortoise, addressed to an increasingly impatient reasoner. The poem should move through at least three exchanges: the initial grant of the premises, the reasoner's addition of the conditional, and the tortoise's cheerful acceptance of that addition followed by the same refusal one level up. As the repeated lines migrate, they should shift from propositions the tortoise accepts to rules he is asked to apply, and finally to the demand that a rule be turned into another proposition. The form should do the philosophical work: the interlocking repetitions must make it impossible for the poem to reach a stable conclusion, so that the final return of the opening lines sounds like the regress starting again rather than a resolution. The tortoise should not be a mere pedant; his refusal should feel like careful bookkeeping, and the reasoner should never be able to name the exact moment the tortoise becomes unreasonable without already applying the rule at issue. Do not mention the tortoise or the reasoner by name, and do not use the words logic or deduction in the poem. The distinction between accepting a proposition and applying a rule of inference should be legible to someone who knows the problem, and the poem should remain readable to someone who does not.
