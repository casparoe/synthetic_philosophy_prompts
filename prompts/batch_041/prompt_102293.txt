I'm a backend engineer by trade with no formal training in philosophy, and over the past year I've fallen in with a small reading group on the philosophy of time that meets in a seminar room at the local university. Next month I'm the one presenting, and I want to defend a line I've worked out myself about the old puzzle of the sea battle: the argument that if it is already true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then tomorrow's battle is already settled and nothing anyone does can change it. My line, roughly, is that the argument only goes through if you accept an extra step that nobody ever states out loud, the step from the proposition being true to the outcome being necessary, and that I can hold on to bivalence for future contingent statements — I don't want to give up the idea that such a statement is determinately true or false right now — while denying that this kind of truth pushes backward into necessity. I picture the future as a branching tree in which exactly one branch is the actual one: tomorrow's battle has a definite answer relative to that branch, but the branches not taken are not thereby impossible, and deliberation is not pointless. The whole puzzle, I suspect, lives in the gap between what is true and what is settled, and I want to make that gap do real work rather than just name it.

The group is small and I already know roughly who will be there, which is why I want to prepare rather than improvise. Two of them do analytic metaphysics and treat bivalence as non-negotiable; one is a logician who has been pushing three-valued and supervaluational treatments of the future and will think my notion of a determinate truth value for future contingents is either naive or quietly incoherent; one works on free will and divine foreknowledge and will worry that I have closed the future without noticing; and a couple of sharp, sympathetic graduate students will keep asking what "settled" is even supposed to mean if it isn't just truth under another name. Take the role of a professional philosopher and logician who has refereed exactly this kind of paper a hundred times, and think carefully before you commit to anything; begin by brainstorming this audience's likely commitments and the fault lines in my position, then write the deliverable.

What I need is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this specific room, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, each paired with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is soft or where I would be bluffing. Tell me which objections I should simply concede at the outset, which I should hold firm on, and which single reply has to land for my view to survive the session. Finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Please do not hand me reading lists, citations, or suggestions to go consult the literature on something — I have three weeks and no library access, so everything has to stand or fall on the argument as I have stated it here.
