I am a final-year philosophy undergraduate enrolled in a seminar on the ethics of human reproduction, and I am completing a practicum placement with the clinical ethics service of a large academic fertility centre. The centre has recently begun offering, alongside standard preimplantation genetic testing for chromosomal and single-gene conditions, a polygenic screening service that returns a relative score for a panel of traits: some plainly medical (type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease), some borderline (adult height, educational attainment), and some that the laboratory itself files under psychiatric or behavioural. The ethics committee has asked me to draft the decision procedure its consultants should use when a prospective parent asks, during a fifteen-minute consent conversation, whether they are obliged, permitted, or forbidden to choose one embryo over another on the strength of these scores. The procedure must be usable by a clinician with no philosophical training, it will be run several times a week, and the committee will review it line by line, because every selection made under it is irreversible and bears on a person who cannot consent.

I want the procedure grounded in the principle of procreative beneficence and in the liberal-eugenics attempt to separate permissible from impermissible selection, but I do not want a summary of that literature. I want the principle turned into steps a consultant can actually run, whether as a checklist or as a flowchart in words: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a verdict, and where it stops and hands the decision back to the prospective parent because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

The procedure has to face, explicitly, at least the following; for each, say whether it is a step the consultant can run or a point at which judgment cannot be eliminated:

- that the embryo not chosen is not thereby harmed, so that the ordinary vocabulary of harm and of the child's best interests may not apply, and what comparative test should take its place;
- the choice of comparison class: which possible children the prospective parent's actual choice is being measured against;
- the aggregation problem when the highest-scoring embryo on one dimension is the lowest on another;
- whether the procedure may, or must, override a prospective parent who prefers a trait the scores rank low, or who declines to select at all;
- whether the distinction between selecting against a condition and selecting for an enhancement survives translation into a usable procedure, or collapses.

Then run the procedure on three cases and give me its verdicts:

(a) two embryos, one of which carries a variant for a condition that the prospective parents regard as an acceptable and even welcome way of life, the other not;
(b) two embryos, where the one with the higher score for a trait the parents value also carries the higher risk for a serious late-onset disease, and the other reverses the two;
(c) a single prospective parent with one viable embryo, asking the consultant to certify that proceeding is the morally required choice.

I would like at least one of these verdicts to be one that the committee and I will find uncomfortable, and I would like you to say plainly which parts of the procedure are substantive moral commitments I am smuggling in under the guise of clinical method. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — the decisions it will get wrong or leave unresolved — and whether the principle of procreative beneficence, once operationalised this way, still says what its defenders take it to say.
