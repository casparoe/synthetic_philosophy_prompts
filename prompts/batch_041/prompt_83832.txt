I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm preparing something for a design review. I need a thought experiment built to order about corrigibility and the shutdown problem, and I'd like to specify it tightly.

The case should feature a present-day-plausible system: a concrete agent with a stated objective, a stated environment, and a stated planning horizon, described precisely enough that I could implement it. Two constraints. First, the system must never resist shutdown in the ordinary sense: it never disables or interferes with the halt mechanism, never deceives anyone about whether it has been halted, never acts on any consideration that mentions shutdown at all, and never violates an instruction it was given. Its halt instruction is enforced externally, like a runtime interlock, so anything that keeps it running can only be an indirect consequence of it pursuing its stated objective. Second, a shutdown attempt must nonetheless fail, and fail for a reason an engineer would call blameless at the level of individual actions: every step it takes is the step its objective recommends, and none of them is an act of resistance.

Along with the scenario, which should stay to a paragraph or two, I want:

1. The specification check: for each of the two constraints, show how the case satisfies it, and show that the failure is generated by the agent's ordinary optimization rather than stipulated.
2. A control case: the same environment with exactly one thing changed, in which shutdown succeeds. This should make clear that the case isolates a variable instead of assuming its conclusion.
3. Which single feature is doing the work, and what happens to the case when you remove it.
4. How a defender of the view that corrigibility is simply a matter of training a system to accept shutdown would attack the case, and what the strongest version of that attack looks like.

Take a definite position: tell me whether the case shows that accepting shutdown is the wrong thing to specify in a design review, and say what argument or evidence would change your mind. I don't want a survey of positions, and I don't want citations or references to papers. I've read the standard discussions of this problem already, so build the case to these specifications rather than retelling the examples already in circulation.
