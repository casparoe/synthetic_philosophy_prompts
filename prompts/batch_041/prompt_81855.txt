I'm in a consciousness seminar and I've somehow ended up volunteering to defend epiphenomenalism in a paper, which I'm already regretting. The version I want to defend is the boring one: experiences are real, brain states cause them, and they don't cause anything themselves — no downward causation, no qualia nudging neurons around. My problem is I can't tell what I'm actually signing up for. So before I write a single word, I want the desiderata: what does an epiphenomenalist account of the mind have to deliver to count as adequate? Give me the whole spec — the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't let collapse, the findings from other areas it can't contradict — and for each one the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than just something a rival camp happens to want. And say which of them are non-negotiable and which are really house preferences of physicalists or of dualists in disguise.

The one I keep circling is the judgment business. We say things like "the ache is dull" or "this red looks more orange than that one," and those reports are reliable, they track the experiences. But if the experience is causally inert, whats doing the tracking? A friend told me this is the paradox of phenomenal judgment and that it's fatal; another said it's a nuisance that's already been dealt with. I don't want the verdict yet — I want to know whether "explains the reliability of phenomenal judgment" belongs on the list as a hard requirement, and if it does, what it actually demands. Does the experience itself have to cause the judgment, or is it enough that a common brain state causes both? And if I take the common-cause route, can I still say the judgment is about the experience and not just about the brain state? Then there's a pile of stuff I suspect belongs on the list and don't know where to file: causal closure of physics, the way our introspective vocabulary looks designed, the gap between having an experience and judging that you have one, the fact that people sometimes sincerely report experiences they aren't having.

Sort it out for me, tell me if any two of the requirements can't both be satisfied, and tell me which single requirement does the most damage to epiphenomenalism. A table would help if you can lay it out that way.
