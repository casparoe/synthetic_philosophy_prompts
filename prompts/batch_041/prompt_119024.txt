In 1996 the British government announced that a new variant of Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease was probably caused by bovine spongiform encephalopathy, reversing a decade of official assurance that British beef was safe; the European Union promptly banned exports of British beef, and in the years that followed the Union elevated the precautionary principle into a general rule for regulating under scientific uncertainty, first in environmental policy and then in food safety. I would like that episode -- the BSE crisis and the doctrine it produced -- analyzed through the Ellsberg paradox and the literature on ambiguity aversion, and I would like the analysis to run in both directions, the theory illuminating the episode and the episode testing the theory.

Take the Ellsberg setup as the theory: two urns of a hundred red and black balls, the first with a known fifty-fifty composition, the second with an unknown composition, and four bets -- red from the first urn, black from the first, red from the second, black from the second. Most choosers prefer the known urn in both colors, a pattern that cannot be represented by any subjective probabilities over the ambiguous urn and that violates the sure-thing principle. Now do the mapping explicitly, and do not let it be loose. In the BSE case, what is the known-composition urn and what is the ambiguous one? Who is the chooser -- the ministry, the cabinet, the Commission, the member states, the consumer? What are the acts, and what are the stakes? The pre-1996 official line rested on an apparently well-behaved base rate: no established case of transmission across the species barrier to humans. The dissent rested on a gap that could not be quantified: the incubation period, the infectious dose, the species barrier itself. Say whether the eventual regulatory reversal is best read as ambiguity aversion, as ambiguity seeking, or as the collapse of an ambiguous prospect into a known one by administrative fiat, and whether there is a genuine Ellsberg-style reversal in the record -- a preference for the known urn in one pair of choices and for the ambiguous urn in the complementary pair.

Then weigh the competing accounts of why ambiguity is aversive: that it is comparative ignorance, aversive chiefly when a better-understood alternative is salient beside it; that it is a matter of source or competence, people avoiding unfamiliar sources and embracing familiar ones; that it should be modeled either by non-additive probabilities or by a second-order probability with an aversion to the spread of the second-order distribution; and that it is simply a mistake, since the composition of the urn is a fact and the aversion violates expected-utility axioms. Add to the menu two findings I find underrated: that ambiguity aversion is stronger when the chooser expects to be held to account for the outcome, and that ambiguity can attach to what the outcomes are or how bad they are, not merely to the probabilities. The BSE case is full of both. Which account does it support, which does it embarrass, and does the fact that official aversion was addressed as much to blame and public outrage as to physical uncertainty fit any of them?

Finally, run the analysis backwards. The precautionary principle looks like ambiguity aversion written into law. Can the theory underwrite that conversion? Press the objection that Ellsberg's problem is a within-person choice among bets with a fixed and exhaustive outcome set, whereas the BSE decision had a contested outcome set (deaths, an industry, public trust, sovereignty over food policy), a shifting decision-maker, and no clean act of choosing between two urns. Does the theory have anything to say where the ambiguity concerns values rather than probabilities -- where the disagreement is not about the composition of the urn but about what is being won or lost? And does the case suggest that ambiguity aversion, whatever its standing as a betting preference, is the wrong thing for a regulator to institutionalize, given that the aversion can be manufactured and that a precautionary rule cannot be applied symmetrically to the risks of acting and of not acting?

Close by saying what the episode shows about the theory: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated, and what a better frame would be if the theory cannot carry it.
