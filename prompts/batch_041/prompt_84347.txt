DRAFT

The Argument as a Subsidized Offer: No-Trade Reasoning and the Persistence of Philosophical Disagreement

1. The result I am importing.

Consider a market in an asset whose value is the same for every trader (a common value) and is determined by an unknown state of the world. The traders share a prior distribution over that state; it is common knowledge that everyone is rational, that the information structure — who learns what, and when — is itself common knowledge, and that no one trades for any reason other than private information. Assume, that is, that nobody has a need to hedge, no liquidity shocks, no noise traders. The result, due to Milgrom and Stokey, is that no trade occurs, even though traders do hold private information. The intuition is that a bare offer is itself an information event. If you bid to buy from me at a price p, I ask why you would; since the asset is worth the same to both of us, your willingness to buy at p suggests that your information makes the asset worth more than p — and if it is worth more than p to you, it is worth more than p to me, so I decline. The offer destroys the gains it was meant to capture. A sibling result, due to Aumann, makes the same point about belief rather than exchange: two Bayesians with a shared prior cannot have common knowledge of divergent posteriors. If their disagreement is on the table, it cannot be stable; if it is stable, it is not fully on the table.

The literature standardly lists the escape routes, and my essay leans on the shape of that list. Trade can occur if the priors are not in fact shared; if values are private rather than common, so that the asset is worth different amounts to different holders for non-informational reasons such as consumption value or risk tolerance; if rationality is not common knowledge, so that I may think you are the one making the error and therefore that trading with you is profitable; or if there are non-informational motives to trade, such as hedging and liquidity.

2. The transplant.

Treat a philosophical thesis as the asset, a credence as the price, and an argument as an offer. On its face the analogy is terrible, and I want to state the disanalogy before defending anything. Market offers are bare: a bid says nothing about why. That bareness is exactly what makes the offer informative in the perverse way the theorem describes. Arguments are the opposite. An argument is an offer with its reasons attached. So the mechanism that makes market trade impossible — the offer leaking private information — is voluntarily supplied by philosophers, for free, as a matter of professional practice. The interesting question, then, is not why philosophers disagree, but why, given that they hand each other their information without being asked, they still do not converge.

3. An audit rather than a prediction.

If we grant common priors and common knowledge of rationality, persistent disagreement among competent inquirers forces us to locate the failure in one of four places: (i) the priors are not really shared, so the disagreement rests on an input that is not itself argued for; (ii) values are private, so the theoretical virtues are weighted differently and the same thesis is worth different amounts to different people, which makes the dispute not fully truth-directed; (iii) rationality is not common knowledge, so someone is making a mistake, but the result is silent about who; (iv) non-truth-directed motives are in play, and positions are held for other reasons. The framework is therefore a diagnostic, not a decision procedure: it tells you what kind of dispute you are in without telling you who is right.

4. Why reasons fail to transfer.

Because philosophical offers are reason-giving, disagreement should be stable only where reasons fail to transfer. And reasons transfer poorly in exactly the places where the escapes bite. If your standards of theory choice are constituted by your training or your community, then handing me your reasons does not hand me your standards; the information you leak is inert in my hands, and the exchange rate between us is not fixed by anything we can argue about. I conclude that persistent philosophical disagreement is evidence about the convertibility of inquirers' standards, not evidence about the propositions in dispute. That is a deflationary conclusion, and I am not sure whether it is a modest one or a devastating one.

5. The asymmetry.

In markets the escape routes are features rather than bugs. Hedging is risk-sharing; private values are the basis of ordinary exchange; liquidity provision is a service; and trading with the irrational is both profitable and, arguably, price-improving. In inquiry, every one of the four escapes is an embarrassment. A view held partly as insurance against the failure of your community's standards, a dispute whose resolution depends on idiosyncratic weights, an error that no one can locate, a position held for professional reasons — these are all ways of not being in the truth business. So the isomorphism, if it holds, is a mirror that only shows defects. That asymmetry, I think, is the real payoff: it explains why the transplant feels like an accusation rather than a description.

6. Conclusion.

The no-trade framework does not predict that philosophers will agree. It identifies the conditions under which agreement is forced, and thereby converts the observation of persistent disagreement into a short menu of diagnoses, each with a different moral.

COMMENTS REQUESTED

1. Is the transplant legitimate? In particular, can a credence play the role of a price, given that a credence is not a quantity that different traders hold in different amounts and exchange with one another? If not, is there a better candidate for the asset role — a bet on the thesis, a research programme, a method, a reputation — and what would change if I substituted it?

2. Is the point in section 4 a genuine disanalogy that rescues the analogy, or does it show that the analogy fails? Once the offer is reason-giving, does the mechanism by which an offer reveals private information still operate, and if so, in which direction?

3. Are the four escape routes exhaustive? If there is a fifth, state it and say what it would imply for the audit. In particular, consider whether the theorem's assumption that the information structure is common knowledge has an analogue in inquiry at all, given that inquirers typically do not know what evidence the other party has or how it bears on the thesis.

4. Is the asymmetry in section 5 real, or an artifact of my moralizing about markets? Give me at least one case in which an escape route is a virtue in inquiry, and say what it does to the argument.

5. State the single most damaging objection to the essay and say whether you consider it fatal or survivable, and why.

6. What should be cut, and what is the one addition that would most strengthen the paper?

Be concrete throughout. Where a section strikes you as a metaphor dressed up as an argument, name the section and say what an argument would have to look like there.
