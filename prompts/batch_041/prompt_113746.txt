For the past term I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy, and we have just reached the material on how we may treat non-human animals — the position that we have no duties to animals strictly speaking, only duties regarding them, which are in fact owed to ourselves or to humanity, and which rest partly on the way cruelty to animals coarsens the agent's own sympathies and partly on the analogy between animal nature and human nature. I had always read this as an odd but harmless appendix to the system. Then in September my partner and I adopted a retired racing greyhound: a gentle, entirely unphilosophical animal whose good plainly consists in things like warm floors and not being frightened, and the passage has become difficult for me in a way it never was before. What has made it worse is that the two sharpest people in the room — a doctoral student who defends Kant's considered view, and a visiting student who thinks that view is a scandal a Kantian ought to be able to repair — have now spent two sessions circling one another without ever quite joining issue. I would like to see the argument done properly, in writing, before I have to say anything about it myself.

Please write the dialogue I wish I had heard. Both speakers should be philosophers who know Kant's texts closely and also know the contemporary Kantian literature on animals; neither is a fool, neither is a caricature, and each must state the strongest version of the other's position before attacking it. Give them at least four substantive exchanges apiece, and make every reply answer the actual point just made rather than a convenient version of it — where one of them concedes something, the concession should cost them something. Neither may win by rhetoric or by appealing to obvious moral sentiment, and they should not melt into vague agreement at the end; a partial convergence, if it comes, should be earned. They may name positions and authors, but they need not cite anything, and I would rather have the argument than a bibliography.

A few pieces of material I would like to see them work through, though they should feel free to add others. First, the premise that seems to do the real work in Kant's position: that to be owed something one must be capable of being obligated, since a duty is a relation between beings who can bind one another. Second, the objection that on the indirect view the wrongness of cruelty turns out to be contingent on its effects on the agent's character, and the counterexample of someone cruel to animals in complete isolation who never meets another human being — along with the best reply available to the orthodox side, namely that such cruelty violates a duty to oneself or to humanity in a way that is not merely a prediction about downstream callousness. Third, the difference between the ground of a duty and its object: whether the animal's suffering can figure as the content of what is required even if it cannot be the reason the requirement exists. Fourth, the parallel with human beings who lack rational agency, such as infants and the severely cognitively disabled, and whether that parallel helps the extensionist or backfires. Fifth, one recent line of argument within Kantian ethics, associated with Christine Korsgaard's Fellow Creatures (2018), which distinguishes being an end in itself in the active sense — being able to legislate ends that others must respect — from being one in the passive sense, namely being such that what is good or bad for one is good or bad absolutely, and which holds that a creature for whom things can go well or badly is a source of value without needing to be a lawgiver; the orthodox speaker should be allowed to press hard on whether this is a development of Kant or a departure from him. Let the greyhound come up once or twice as a concrete case, but do not let the dialogue turn into sentimentality about her. End not with a verdict but with each speaker naming the single premise on which they take the dispute to turn and saying what would move them. Roughly 1500 to 2000 words.
