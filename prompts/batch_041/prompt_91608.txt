For my school's philosophy club I am writing a short paper on whether it can ever be wrong to bring a person into existence, and I am using a friend of mine as a case study, because her views are firm and, as far as I can tell, consistent. Her verdicts so far are these. A couple who would conceive a child certain to live in constant, unrelievable pain, with a life plainly worse than nothing, would be doing something wrong. A couple who would conceive an ordinary child with a good life ahead of them would not be doing anything wrong, though she insists they are under no obligation to do it and that the child is in no way better off for having been born. And a couple who would conceive a child whose life will be hard in the ordinary ways — few pleasures, many disappointments, no great suffering — but which she agrees is worth living would likewise not be doing anything wrong, though she says that if they decided against it, nothing of value would be lost.

The case I would like you to consider is this: a couple could conceive a child who, because of a painful but treatable condition, would spend the first several years of life in severe and persistent pain, after which the condition would resolve completely and the child would go on to a long and happy life, one clearly worth living on any ordinary accounting. What would my friend say about this case? Please lay out the rule she appears to be running and at least one rival rule that fits all of her earlier verdicts just as well, say what each predicts here, give your best guess at her verdict along with your reasons for preferring one rule over the other, and name one further case that would most cleanly tell the candidate rules apart.
