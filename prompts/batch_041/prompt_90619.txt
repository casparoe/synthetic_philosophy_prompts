A colleague of mine defends a view she calls the division of epistemic labor: the norm of assertion is knowledge for the speaker, while the norm of testimonial acceptance is justified belief for the hearer. She believes this resolves the problem of testimonial knowledge, since the speaker must know and the hearer need only be justified in believing. I am puzzled, however, by a case in which a hearer is justified in believing a speaker's testimony that p, although the speaker does not know that p, and the hearer subsequently asserts p to a third party. On the division view, the hearer's assertion violates the knowledge norm, since she does not know p. Yet she seems epistemically blameless: she did everything her epistemic situation required. Does the knowledge norm of assertion render her assertion improper even though she is blameless? If so, does that not show that the knowledge norm is too demanding for testimonial exchanges, since it holds hearers responsible for a condition beyond their epistemic reach?
