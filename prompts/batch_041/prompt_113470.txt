For the past three years I have convened a fortnightly discussion group of retired scientists and engineers — the membership runs to about a dozen, comfortable with equations but innocent of set theory — and next month the session falls to me, on the continuum hypothesis. My own formation was in physics, and I have spent the last several years reading my way into the philosophy of mathematics; the consequence is that I understand the hypothesis well enough when I meet it on the page and quite badly when I have to stand up and build it in front of other people. What I need from you, therefore, is not an account of the continuum hypothesis — I can supply that myself — but a plan for teaching it in a single ninety-minute meeting, with nothing but paper, pencils, and a blackboard.

Tell me the order in which the constituent ideas must be laid down, and why each depends on the one before it; I am persuaded that the whole edifice topples if one states the hypothesis before the ground beneath it is firm, but I cannot say with confidence what that ground is, and I keep catching myself wanting to open with something like "the real numbers form a set larger than the natural numbers," which I suspect already smuggles in half of what ought to be established. Name the single construction or example that should carry the session — the object we return to again and again, until the group feels it rather than merely follows it — and say what it must accomplish and what it must not presuppose; I have been assuming the diagonal argument, but I am no longer sure whether that is the load-bearing example or merely a preliminary. Then set out the misconceptions you would expect this audience to bring or to acquire midway, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be met — I already foresee that they will translate "independent of the standard axioms" into "neither true nor false, hence a matter of convention," and I do not know whether to head that off in the first ten minutes or to let them arrive at it and then complicate it; nor whether I should, at the close, let them glimpse that some regard the question as one without a single determinate answer, or whether that would only deepen the very confusion I am trying to prevent. Finally, tell me what I should deliberately omit at this level and announce that I am omitting, and give me one closing question that would disclose whether the group has understood the question itself rather than merely retained its answer.
