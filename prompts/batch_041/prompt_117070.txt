I have held, for about a year now, the following thesis, and I would like you to try to break it. For any question with a determinate answer, if two agents are made to argue opposite sides of it before a judge far weaker than either of them, the agent defending the true answer can force a win — provided the exchange runs long enough and the judge follows it. Call the agents debaters and call the claim soundness. I take the interactive-proof analogy seriously: just as a short proof can convince a verifier who could never have found the theorem, a short exchange should convince a weak judge who could never have adjudicated the claim directly.

What I want is a map of the thesis's exposure. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results count against it, ranked from decisive to merely awkward? What does the thesis predict that its rivals do not? And what would its defenders themselves concede, as opposed to what critics say on their behalf?

Three matters I have not been able to settle, and on which I would most like your judgement.

First, obfuscation. I suspect two distinct phenomena travel under that word and that at most one is a genuine threat: (a) the dishonest debater makes the case so long or so tangled that the judge cannot check the rebuttal, and (b) both cases are simple, but the burden of proof is unclear, so the judge cannot tell which side has failed to discharge it. Is either a defeater, or do both merely restate the premise that the judge is weak?

Second, existence against findability. My thesis asserts that a winning strategy exists for the honest side; it does not assert that a computationally bounded honest debater would find it. Should I be worried that only the second claim is interesting, and what would show whether the gap between the two is exploitable?

Third, shared defects. If both debaters are instances of the same model, any bias they share is invisible to the debate and is inherited by the judge. Does that tell against the thesis, or does it lie outside its scope?

Finally, I want an honest verdict on whether the thesis risks anything at all. I have caught myself adding clauses — long enough, follows it — each of which seemed reasonable when I added it. Which of my qualifications are doing real work, and which are quietly making the claim unfalsifiable? If it has become unfalsifiable, say so plainly, and say whether that is a defect of the thesis or an artifact of the question being asked of it.
