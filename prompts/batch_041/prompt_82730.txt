I sit on the appraisal panel at a government delivery body (won't say which) where we sign off business cases for big public projects — new rail lines, hospital IT systems, that sort of thing. The recurring problem is that the cost and schedule estimates come from the project's own team, built up from their own plans, and they're wrong in the same direction every time: over budget, late. I've read enough Kahneman to know this is the planning fallacy and that the supposed fix is the 'outside view' — ignore the plan, find a reference class of similar projects, and take the distribution of their outcomes. In practice that's where it falls apart for us. What counts as 'similar'? Take the reference class of 'all government IT projects' and we get one number; take 'all health-sector IT projects above a certain size' and we get another; and sometimes the team argues, with what sounds like good reason, that their project really is different — new technology, no precedent, the old ones failed for reasons we've since fixed. And when we do apply the outside view it can feel like it's just saying 'assume you'll fail like everyone else', which is demoralising and maybe not even true for the cases that matter most.

Write me a decision procedure the panel can actually run on a business case: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, when the outside view should be overridden by inside-view reasoning and what evidence that override requires, and where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to the panel as a judgement call. Then run it on three cases — (a) a new metro line in a city that already has one, (b) a first-of-its-kind national health data platform, (c) a project whose sponsor has a documented track record of delivering on time — and be honest about the case where the procedure will tell us something we dont want to hear. Finally, say what the procedure can't do and where it's really just dressing up a hunch. No citations, I'll fill those in myself. Keep it to something we can run in a single panel meeting, we haven't got a week per case.
