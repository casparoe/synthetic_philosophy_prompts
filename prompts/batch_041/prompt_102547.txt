I spent thirty-five years doing renal physiology, most of it on tubular transport, and since retiring I have been trying to educate myself in the philosophy of medicine. I have gotten as far as the naturalism–normativism dispute about health and disease and have stalled there, and what I want from you is not a defense of either side but a diagnosis of the dispute itself.

Here is the dispute as I have reconstructed it. One camp, descended from Boorse's biostatistical account, holds that a disease is the impairment of a part's functioning below the efficiency typical for the species, that this is a plain biological fact about the organism, and that the badness we attach to the word is a further, separate matter contributed by us. The other camp holds that physiology can never deliver a verdict on disease by itself, because the concept is ineliminably evaluative: calling something a disease already commits one to its being bad for the person, or to its obstructing something the person has reason to value, and biological facts enter only once that evaluative frame is in place. Both sides seem to agree on the physiology; the argument is about what the physiology is being asked to do.

I can see why I find this frustrating, and I have a hypothesis I would like you to test rather than accept. My own field runs on reference intervals — ranges derived from what is common in a chosen population — and I was trained to treat those as neutral facts, though I lived through the episode in which the reference equations for my own organ were revised because a coefficient for race had been doing normative work under a statistical label. Meanwhile the physiology I actually studied was about regulation toward set points, and the set points themselves are plastic, context-dependent, and reset by load and history. So there seem to be two different things called a norm in play: a sampling norm, which says what is common, and a criterion norm, which says what a part is for. A seventy-eight-year-old's kidney filtration rate is normal by the first and arguably impaired by the second, and I cannot tell whether the two camps are disagreeing about that case or talking past each other about which norm is at issue. My suspicion is that the naturalist keeps appealing to the criterion norm while the normativist keeps pointing at cases where medicine actually runs on the sampling norm plus a judgment about whether to treat — and that the real dispute is concealed because both sides assume a single concept must serve several jobs at once: classifying conditions, warranting treatment, grounding claims about responsibility and entitlement, and organizing research and reimbursement.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it substantive, about whether a biological fact can bear normative weight? Is it verbal, about the word disease? Are the two sides answering different questions under one name — one about the extension of a descriptive category, one about the conditions under which we should act? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, namely whether physiology can supply criterion norms that are determinate enough to settle contested cases (aging, adaptive responses such as fever or insulin resistance, traits fixed by environment)? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed and should be replaced?

Engage the positions concretely rather than surveying them, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a decision to split the concept, a formal result, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. I have no way to look anything up, so please argue from the positions themselves; I am not asking for references, and I will find the literature myself.
