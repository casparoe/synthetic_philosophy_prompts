I'm a science and ideas reporter, and I keep tripping over the same one-liner in constitutional arguements: 'no Parliament can bind its successors'. I heard it twice last week — once from a barrister on a panel about entrenching long-range climate targets, who used it to say the whole exercise was pointless, since the next lot just repeal whatever you pass, and once from a campaigner who plainly meant something bigger, about how no generation gets to boss the next one around. Then a colleague of mine who's read too much Elster said it can't be right as stated, because binding your future self is exactly what a Ulysses contract is, and a constitution is supposed to be the political version of one. Which leaves me wondering how any entrenched constitution works at all, if the maxim holds.

So give me the menu: the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the trivial to the radical — claims about what judges would in fact do, claims about what sovereignty means by definition, claims about the dead hand of the past, claims about what future Parliaments will actually do, and the purely rhetorical uses. For each I want a definate verdict — true, false, or not really a claim at all — and who exactly is committed to it. Then say which reading does the work the barrister needed it to do, and whether any reading survives my colleague's objection.

Don't hand me a survey with the options left open — pick, and defend the pick. British English please, and no reading lists; I'm not going to chase down law review articles.
