A friend of mine is really into metaontology and she's got this two-part picture of verbal disputes that she keeps trying to sell me on. Her line is that when people argue about whether something "really" counts as X — a disease, a planet, a sandwich, a person — alot of the disagreement is verbal in the boring sense: swap the disputed word for two sharper words and the fight dissolves, because they were just talking past each other. Thats the shallow kind. The deep kind is the one she actually cares about: you do the disambiguating, both sides now agree about all the facts, and they still disagree about which of the sharper words the original word should be made to mean from now on. She thinks the deep ones aren't disagreements about the world at all, they're fights over which vocabulary we should adopt, and they get settled (if they get settled) by appeal to what we want, not by discovering anything new. She also admits you usually can't tell which kind you're in until you've already done the disambiguating, which can take years.

Suppose she's basically right. What are the most important implications of this picture for a committee that has to actually draw a line and publish it — say the people who decide what goes in the DSM, or the ones who demoted Pluto? Not the obvious stuff like "define your terms." I mean what does it change about how they should argue, what evidence is even relevant to their decision, and how they should treat the people who lose the vote?
