You are helping me grade a short-answer exam in a second-year metaphysics course. The question on the paper was:

Explain the difference between the transworld-identity account of de re modality and Lewis's counterpart theory. Then state the Humphrey objection to counterpart theory and assess how damaging it is.

One student wrote the following:

"On the transworld-identity view, associated with Kripke, an individual such as Humphrey is not confined to a single world: the very same Humphrey exists in many possible worlds, and a de re modal claim attributes a property to that individual. To say that Humphrey could have won the election is to say that the same person who in fact lost is a winner in some other world, so the modal predicate expresses a genuine property of Humphrey himself.

Lewis rejects this. On counterpart theory, every individual is world-bound — nothing exists in more than one world. A claim that Humphrey could have won is therefore not about Humphrey winning anywhere, but about a counterpart of Humphrey: a distinct individual in another world who resembles Humphrey sufficiently closely. The counterpart relation is a relation of similarity rather than identity, and unlike identity it need not be transitive or symmetric. Lewis introduces it partly to avoid the problem of accidental intrinsics, since an individual present in two worlds would have to possess, in the one case, an intrinsic property that it lacks in the other.

Because counterpart theory is the semantics that accompanies Lewis's modal realism — the view that possible worlds are concrete universes as real as our own — anyone who rejects concrete possible worlds must also reject counterpart theory, and so must fall back on transworld identity.

The Humphrey objection is Kripke's. It holds that counterpart theory changes the subject: when I say that Humphrey could have won, counterpart theory tells me I am really talking about a different individual who merely resembles Humphrey, whereas intuitively the modal claim is about Humphrey himself — it is Humphrey, not a stand-in, who could have won.

Lewis took this objection to be decisive and gave up counterpart theory in his later work, which leaves transworld identity as the correct account of de re modality."
