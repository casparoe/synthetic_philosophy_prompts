I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've somehow ended up running the session at our neighborhood ideas night: about sixteen people in a rented room above a bike shop, ninety minutes, a mixed crowd — a few engineers, two designers, a high-school history teacher, a retired archivist, and a handful of people who come for the argument. I've read the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, the 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and what struck me is the part I can't get across by talking: that monumental, antiquarian, and critical ways of relating to the past aren't three opinions about the past but three practical postures that change what a group is able to do in the present, and that each one buys its usefulness at a cost. I want the ninety minutes to make the group feel that rather than hear it, and I'd rather nothing be named until the debrief.

Here's the shape I have in mind, and I'd like you to design it properly, filling in everything I've left vague. Everyone works on one case: a fictional twenty-two-year-old neighborhood bicycle co-op that has to decide, tonight, whether to sell its building and go mobile or keep the building and shrink its programs. I'll hand out a dossier of raw material — a timeline with gaps, excerpted minutes, a founder's obituary, an email thread about a botched expansion, a financial irregularity that was quietly settled, a membership list with some names scratched out — and split the room into four groups. Three groups each write a short history of the co-op in a prescribed voice: one that reads as a call to action, all heroes and high points and a founding deed; one that catalogs and preserves everything without judgment; one that prosecutes the past and treats the co-op's history as something to be broken with. The fourth group is the board: they receive all three histories, a hard clock, and a rule against asking for more information, and must commit to a recommendation and wager tokens on it. I want real small stakes rather than money — a token scheme where the motivational history earns points for getting the board to act, the catalog earns points for completeness but is scored against the clock, the prosecution earns points for surfacing the buried irregularity but loses points for each member who says they'd quit, and the board is scored on whether its decision survives the later release of the facts the histories suppressed. Then the second half: swap histories between groups and have each defend the same course of action in the voice it didn't write, and only then release the suppressed facts. Please include the timeline, the rules, the materials, the payoff arithmetic, the second-half beats, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the three modes and onto the idea that the past is judged by what it does for the living — without the debrief turning into a lecture on the essay.

Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. I expect the catalog group to feel punished for doing the assignment well, someone to object that the whole thing is just spin, the board to ignore the histories and decide on vibes, and the prosecuting group to discover that writing a damning history of anything is alarmingly easy and to get uncomfortable about it. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I'll have to say in the debrief instead — in particular, I suspect the exercise makes the three postures look like freely chosen styles, and I don't know whether that's a distortion. And separate clearly what is settled among people who know this material from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether the three modes are a taxonomy or a developmental sequence, how far Nietzsche is attacking professional historiography as opposed to a broader cultural condition, and whether his talk of forgetting is meant as a normative ideal — rather than handing me a contested reading as if it were the consensus, since I'll be repeating it to a room as though I knew what I was talking about.
