So I'm a novelist — second book comes out next spring, it's partly about a woman who walks out of a corporate job in Osaka after the guy who trained her drops dead at his desk, and somehow that got me invited to host one of those conversation series at a small literary festival, theme is "work and time." They've paired me with a political philosopher I'd never read, and a friend sent me one of her papers so I wouldn't show up cold. Her thing, as far as I can tell, is a case for a right to leisure that doesn't go through distributive justice at all: what's wrong with overwork isn't mainly that the good stuff gets shared unfairly, it's that depending on an employer hands the employer arbitrary power over your whole life, and leisure matters because it's what gives you real exit options and enough room to be a citizen instead of just a worker. On top of that she thinks the 35-hour week is kind of a trap — it pins down the number of hours while leaving the dependence itself untouched — and she'd rather have what she calls a "right to reduce," portable benefits plus a job guarantee so you can cut your hours without losing your health cover or your pension. She also has this line about karoshi I don't fully follow: she says the scandal isn't the death, it's that the workplace was set up so the person couldn't say no, and the death is just the evidence. And she's got a bigger historical claim that what she calls the "productivity ethic" is the secular heir of the old Protestant work ethic — same moralizing of time, different god.

I want to actually interview her well and not just nod along for forty minutes. Can you draft me 9 or 10 questions, ordered so the thing builds? I specifically want one that puts pressure on why leisure needs a non-distributive grounding at all (isn't fairness about shares enough?), one on whether her exit-power story survives her own job guarantee — isn't dependence on the state a dependance too? — and one on the karoshi point, where what I'm really asking is whether she means something about Japan specifically or about wage labor as such. For each of those three, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not sitting there flatfooted when she says the obvious thing back. I'd also like one that makes her say what would actually count as evidence against the domination-based picture, and one about whether the productivity ethic is genuinely a different thesis from the Protestant one or just the same claim with the theology filed off. Keep them concrete and answerable without a library — she's sharp and she'll punish anything soft, but I don't want it to be me showing off that I read the one paper.
