Bit of an odd request, this. I've somehow ended up as the speaker at our local history society's monthly meeting next month — I'm not an academic, I just got obsessed with this stuff during lockdown and one thing led to another — and the title they've given me is the one I pitched, which is basically: when does a legend turn into history? I'm using the three cases everyone argues about, the Trojan War, King Arthur and the Exodus. My actual line isn't the boring one, that they all really happened or none of them did. It's this: a story turns into a historical claim at the moment somebody pins a date and a place on it. Troy is the clean case — there's a mound, there are city levels in it, and once you say this mound and this war you've said something a spade can test. Arthur is the opposite, the name floats; half the counties in Britain have a hill or a stone or a well with his name on it, and the moment one place claims him the claim has nowhere to land. The Exodus sits in the middle but sort of backwards: it comes with a fixed itinerary and no address you can dig, and the people who carry it have reasons for it to stay unfalsifiable. So what I'm really claiming is that historicity is a matter of form before it's ever a matter of evidence — the date and the place are what make a story the kind of thing that can be true or false, and whether it is true is a separate question that comes later.

The trouble is I know exactly who's going to be in the room. The chair is a retired school history teacher, very dry, who is going to tell me I'm confusing the past with the past as told. There's a bloke who's spent fifteen years trying to get our local hillfort recognised as Camelot, and he will hear the entire talk as a personal attack, and he always says that absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence, twice. There's a retired vicar who's a lovely man and doesn't read Genesis like a newspaper but will not have me reducing the Exodus to a bit of social engineering. There's a young archaeology postgrad who comes to these evenings mainly to be superior and thinks the whole question is beneath contempt because whether something happened is a question you ask about a trench and not about a story — he'll say my framework isn't even testable, which honestly stings a bit. And there's a Homer obsessive who'll want to talk about the Iliad as literature and will get tetchy if I keep treating it as testimony. What I want from you is a prep sheet: the eight or nine things these people will actually throw at me, in roughly the order they'll come and phrased the way each of them would phrase it (the teacher's version of you're begging the question and the archaeologist's version are not the same sentence), each with a short answer I can give in a couple of minutes, and a note on where that answer is thin. I'd rather be told which two objections I can't survive than be told I've got a lovely talk. And at the end, tell me the one question I'm privately praying nobody asks.
