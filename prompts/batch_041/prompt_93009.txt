I have no training in philosophy — I work in freight logistics — but I spent five weeks making medical decisions for my father after a stroke took away his ability to speak or understand, and I have been turning over what the hospital asked of me ever since.

He had left no written directive. So the team told me I was to decide as he would have decided, not as I thought best for him, and they were insistent that the difference was not a formality. The paperwork had a name for it: substituted judgment. I was, in effect, asked to be a place where his wishes were stored and could be read out loud. I noticed the edges of this only later. When a patient has signed a directive, the document beats the family's sense of what the patient would have wanted. When nothing is signed and no family is present, the same team may decide on the patient's behalf according to what they judge to be the patient's interests, which is a different question altogether. So sometimes the patient's own stated will governs, sometimes my reconstruction of a will he never stated governs, and sometimes neither does.

What I want to know is what this practice assumes about what a person is. When they asked me what he would have wanted, they spoke as though he were still somehow in the room, and as though the part of him that mattered had been copied into me. A friend pointed me to Douglas Hofstadter, who argues in his 2007 book I Am a Strange Loop that the self is a pattern rather than a piece of tissue, that people who live closely together each hold a large part of the other's pattern in their own brains, and that when one of them dies the other still carries a reduced version of them that goes on thinking. I cannot tell whether that is a metaphor or a literal claim about where a person is located, and I cannot tell whether the hospital was committed to it or to something much weaker.

Please work out what the practice presupposes. Does it require the view that a self can be spread across more than one brain, or only the much thinner thought that a person's values are stable and legible to those close to them? Which rival accounts of what a person is — that a person is a single living brain, that a person is the story told about them, that a person is just whatever subject is having experiences at the moment — would make the practice incoherent, and which would merely make it look different? Are the three rules I described consistent with one another, or do they pull toward different pictures of the person? And if we replaced the picture with one of the rivals, what would the conversation at the bedside have to become?
