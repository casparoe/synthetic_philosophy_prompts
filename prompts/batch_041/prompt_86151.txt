So here's my situation. I write the copy for a small conservation nonprofit — three staff, we do wetland restoration, and every December I have to produce the year-end appeal letter, which gets harder every year because for the last two I've been reading obsessively about wild animal suffering and now I can't look at a heron the same way. A friend runs a little comedy-and-philosophy zine and asked me for something, so I want to do a parody of exactly that document: a deadpan year-end fundraising letter from a fictional environmental charity that has quietly, completely reoriented itself around reducing wild animal suffering, and is now trying to sell that to its old donor base without ever quite saying so. Invent the org (something like "Hollow Creek Wildlife Trust," a wetland outfit in the upper Midwest), and give it all the real furniture of these letters: a subject line, a soft-focus story about one particular animal, an "impact report" section, an FAQ that begins "you might be wondering…", a reply slip with giving tiers, and a P.S. The voice should be sincere, slightly defensive, donor-facing, the way real appeal letters are — the irony has to sit entirely in the content, never in a wink or a nudge. No real charities or real people, living or dead; if the letter needs a villain, make it a board member or a skeptical donor.

The jokes need to come from getting the arguments right, so a specialist should read it and think "yes, that's the actual bind," not "ha, hippies." Some seeds, though please don't treat this as a checklist that turns the letter into an essay with a bow on it — the satire has to be carried by the institutional voice. The r-strategy arithmetic: a fish lays thousands of eggs, nearly all the fry starve or get eaten, often while still moving. The numbers problem: whatever the charity saves, the welfare of animals nobody would put on a tote bag dominates the ledger, and the letter should report its impact in an invented unit — say "suffering-adjusted wild years" — with just slightly too much confidence in the conversion rate. The predator problem: the org has a "transition plan" for phasing out predation, and a leaked internal memo quoted in the FAQ or the P.S. should let slip that most of the prey it rescues from being eaten alive will starve instead, which the letter treats as a rounding error. The charisma bias: the photos are all otters and fawns, the suffering is not. And the ecosystem-collapse objection, which the letter should handle the way real institutions handle inconvenient science — a footnote and a brisk change of subject. Maybe work in a donor who writes asking whether it wouldn't be kinder to just let the wetland be, and let the letter answer him without ever answering him. I'm genuinely torn on all this myself, so it shouldn't read as a hit job on the charity; the letter should be able to make its case, and the reader should close it feeling the real question — whether we owe anything to wild nature, and whether we could ever know if we'd made it better — without anyone in the piece stating it. Around 800 words, and start with the letter itself, subject line and all, with no preface or commentary from you.
