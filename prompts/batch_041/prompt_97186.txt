I sit in on a reading group on the ethics and governance of autonomous weapons, and we have been working through the idea of a responsibility gap — the thought that a system can act in a way that leaves no one properly blameworthy, liable, or even answerable for what it did. Last week one member of the group, an engineer seconded from a defence contractor, tried to close the whole discussion with a proverb: guns don't kill people, people kill people. His point, as far as I could tell, was that the gap is illusory, because somewhere upstream of any autonomous system there is always a human whose decision it was — the designer, the commander, the operator, or the person who chose to deploy the thing. I am not sure he is simply wrong, but I suspect the sentence is doing several jobs at once and that only some of them actually deliver the conclusion he wants. What I would like from you is a menu of the precise claims that proverb could be making — causal, moral, legal, and anything else you think is in play — with a verdict on each: which readings are near-trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and, most importantly, which of them would actually support treating the responsibility gap for autonomous systems as a non-problem. Please lay the readings out as distinct options rather than choosing one and defending it.

Be explicit about what each reading assumes regarding agency, causation, and the kinds of entity that can bear responsibility, and keep the hard cases in view: a loitering munition that misclassifies a target after months of unsupervised learning; a system whose designers demonstrably could not have foreseen the failure mode; a case where a human operator had only seconds to veto an engagement. It would help if you separated responsibility as attributability, as accountability, and as liability, and said which of the proverb's readings are about which of those, since my suspicion is that my colleague's confidence comes from sliding quietly between them — and also whether the reading he most likely intends survives once we drop the assumption that the responsible party must be a single identifiable individual rather than a distributed chain of designers, commanders, maintainers, and (if you think it earns a place) the system itself. Somewhere in there I would like to know whether "meaningful human control" is a genuine answer to the gap or just a restatement of the proverb in regulatory vocabulary.
