I am a judicial assistant to one of the judges on the constitutional court of a small parliamentary democracy. The jurisdiction does not matter and I have anonymised the case names. Every case in this line turns on the same question — how far a present majority may bind a future one, the Ulysses question — and next term we have a sixth. My judge is not a theorist and cites nobody; her reasons are short, sometimes two lines, and I have to write her a bench memo that predicts her vote. What I need from you is the prediction and the structure behind it, not a survey.

Her five rulings so far.

1. The Standing Orders Case. Parliament, by simple majority, amended its own standing orders to require a two-thirds majority to suspend a new timetable regime, for the remainder of that term only. She voted to uphold. Her reason was that the device made the House pause rather than stop: the same two-thirds that could make the rule could unmake it, and it lapsed with the term in any event.

2. The Fiscal Charter Case. A constitutional amendment, carried by a cross-party two-thirds majority, required the budget to balance over the cycle, with an escape clause available on a two-thirds vote during a declared recession. She voted to uphold. She stressed the escape clause and the breadth of the majority that had carried the amendment.

3. The Lame-Duck Entrenchment Case. In its last weeks in office, a government that had just lost an election used a narrow majority to amend the constitution so as to make a set of its own policy settlements — a privatisation, an electoral formula, a tuition-fee regime — unamendable, and to remove them from the ordinary amending procedure altogether. She voted to strike the amendment down, and gave two reasons that I have never been able to reduce to one. The first was that the settlements had been placed beyond the reach of any future majority. The second was that a side that had just been beaten at the polls had used its remaining weeks to fix the terms on which the winners would have to govern.

4. The Retirement Age Case. A statute provided that the statutory retirement age could be altered only by a unanimous vote of all parliamentary groups. She voted to strike it down, saying that a settlement imposed by one side and never accepted by the other is not a commitment but a defeat with a delay built into it.

5. The Emergency Powers Case. Emergency legislation, renewable only on a supermajority vote and lapsing automatically after ninety days unless renewed. She voted to uphold it.

The sixth case. A constitutional amendment, carried by a large cross-party majority after a long public deliberation, provides that the security of judicial tenure and the requirement that judicial appointments be made on the recommendation of an independent commission may not be altered by any subsequent amendment, and it removes both from the ordinary amending procedure. No procedure exists for repealing the clause itself. The question before the court is whether the clause is valid.

What I need.

(a) A prediction: would she uphold the clause or strike it down?

(b) The rule or rules that best fit all five earlier rulings, each stated plainly enough that I can see what work it is doing. I have tried several times to write down a single rule and keep producing something that fits the first, second and fifth cases and then breaks on the third or fourth. The lines I keep returning to are: whether the constraint can be undone at all, and at what cost; who is binding whom, and whether the body that does the binding is the body that will be bound; and whether what has been entrenched is a way of deciding or a decision. Treat those as prompts rather than as a menu. If the best account is none of them, or two of them together, say so.

(c) For each candidate rule, what it predicts about the sixth case, which of the earlier rulings it handles worst, and which earlier case is the closest analogue and why it is not closer.

(d) Your best guess, with a confidence you are willing to defend, and a statement of what would move you off it. If two rules fit the record equally well and the sixth case falls differently under them, I want that stated as the answer rather than smoothed into a single verdict.

(e) The further case — one she has not decided — that would do most to separate the surviving rules, and what each rule predicts about it.

Two things I would rather have than a tidy memo. First, if I am wrong to assume she is running a rule at all, and the honest reading is that these are five decisions taken case by case with my pattern an artefact of my wanting one, say so and tell me why, instead of manufacturing a formula that fits. Second, if the sixth case is genuinely underdetermined by the record, say that plainly; I would rather carry the sentence that the precedents do not reach this into the memo than a confident prediction I cannot support.
