I'm a teaching assistant on a second-year undergraduate module on meaning, rules and scepticism, and I need a second pair of eyes on one script before marks go back. I have been reading around this material — and arguing about it in online comment threads — for years, so I am alive to how much credit a fluent answer can pick up on style alone; what I want from you is an itemised assessment rather than an impression.

The examination question was:

> Set out the sceptical paradox about rule-following which Kripke develops, and assess whether the solution he offers in response to it succeeds. Engage with at least one serious objection to that solution.

The candidate wrote:

"The paradox begins with the question of what fact about me makes it true that when I used the word 'plus' in the past I meant addition rather than quaddition. Define quus so that it agrees with addition whenever both numbers are less than 57, and returns 5 otherwise. Every sum I have ever computed is one on which the two functions agree, so every fact about my past behaviour, my training, and any mental image or feeling I can introspect is compatible with my having meant quus all along. The sceptic concludes that there is no fact of the matter about what I meant, and hence that my confident answer of 125 to 68 + 57 — the first case in which the two functions come apart — is unjustified. It matters that the difficulty is not evidential: the sceptic is not saying that we lack evidence, but that nothing in the world could play the required role, since any candidate fact is compatible with the deviant hypothesis just as much as with the standard one. This is why the paradox reaches every rule and not merely arithmetic, and why Kripke treats it as a new form of scepticism rather than a version of the problem of other minds.

He considers the dispositional response — that meaning addition consists in my being disposed to produce the sum — and rejects it on two grounds. First, our dispositions are finite: they cover only a bounded range of cases, whereas meaning addition determines an answer for numbers nobody will ever compute. Kripke treats this as a contingent limitation of our psychology, so that the objection would lapse for a being with a larger brain and more time. Second, and more importantly, dispositions are the wrong kind of thing to underwrite correctness, since the fact that I am disposed to answer in a certain way cannot make that answer right; I may simply be disposed to make mistakes.

Kripke's own response is what he calls the sceptical solution. It is sceptical because it concedes the paradox rather than refuting it: there is no fact in virtue of which I meant plus rather than quus. What it offers instead is a redescription of what we are doing when we say that somebody means addition. Kripke presents this as Wittgenstein's own position, reconstructed from the rule-following sections of the Investigations; his only caveat is that he may have misread some of the details. Such statements of meaning do not state facts; they record the conditions under which they may legitimately be asserted. On this account the assertion that Jones means addition by 'plus' expresses our confidence that Jones will go on as we do, and its authority derives from the fact that we form a community which agrees in its responses. A solitary individual could not be said to follow a rule at all, which is why Kripke treats the argument as a new and more powerful route to Wittgenstein's conclusion that a private language is impossible. Where the community agrees, the rule exists; its practice constitutes the standard of correctness, and so the sceptic's question is answered rather than merely set aside.

The most obvious objection is that the sceptical argument can be re-run against the community. If my past responses are compatible with quus, so are the past responses of everyone else; the fact that we agree does not supply the missing fact, and agreement in responses is just a further piece of finite behaviour. Kripke acknowledges the pressure, and it is not clear that the appeal to assertability conditions does more than rename the problem: if statements of meaning have no truth conditions, it is hard to see in what sense a community whose members systematically answered differently could be wrong, and equally hard to see what distinguishes our practice from a merely causal regularity. I take this to be the strongest objection, though the solution still does real work in showing that meaning-attribution does not require the kind of foundation the sceptic demands."
