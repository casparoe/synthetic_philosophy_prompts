My niece is graduating from a big state univeristy in June and she's asked me to give the commencement address. She's in the philosophy-and-econ honours stream, so maybe forty people in an audience of three hundred will know what I'm on about; the rest are parents, grandparents, siblings, and the folding-chair crowd who came to see a name read out.

I said yes. Now I'm stuck, and it's a philosophical problem, not a scheduling one. I'm a consequentialist, and for years I've been half-convinced by the cluelessness worry: even when you can see the short-run effects of an action plainly, the ripples run out through a causal web so vast and tangled, and over such a long stretch of time, that you can't have any real confidence about whether the thing made the world better or worse all told. It usually gets discussed as a problem for whether utilitarianism can give any practical guidance at all. I want to find out what it does to a podium.

Because the assignment is: tell these kids to go out and do some good. And nearly every sentence in that genre is something I'd be asserting without a shred of warrant. 'Go into medicine, you'll save lives' — sure, and maybe you'll also spend thirty years doing something you'd rather not look at directly. 'Work on the hardest problems you can find' — warrant? 'Give a chunk of your salary to the most effective charity you can find' — that's the pitch I'd have made ten years ago, and it's exactly the one I can't now make in good faith.

So write the address, for me, in my voice. Six to eight minutes out loud. Warm, funny once or twice, no jargon the grandparents can't follow, and it has to sound like an actual commencement speech and not a paper with jokes in it. But the cluelessness thing has to do the structural work: it should decide what I'm allowed to say and what I have to give up, and I want the speech to be honest about the giving-up instead of sliding past it. My hunch is that there's still something you can tell a person to do without knowing whether it will make the world better on balance, and I'd like the speech to arive at it rather than announce it. Don't let me cheat with 'it's about the journey' humanism that dissolves the moment you press on it. And the philosophy kids will be listening to see whether I face the hard version, so whatever I can't get around has to be said out loud, not gestured at.
