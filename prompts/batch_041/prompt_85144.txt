For the past two years I have run a fortnightly reading group — a mix of graduate students, one lapsed seminarian, and a couple of autodidacts of the sort you find around the rationality blogs — and we have just spent five sessions on the modal ontological argument. Two of the regulars have hardened into camps, and I want the document they should have produced months ago: an actual adversarial collaboration, of the kind the blogs have been preaching for fifteen years and almost nobody ever does. Camp A is the defender. He works with the Gödel-style apparatus — a primitive notion of a "positive" property; God-likeness, which the group calls maximal greatness, defined as having every positive property; positivity closed under entailment and distributed over each property and its negation so that exactly one of the two is positive; a definition of essence; and an axiom making necessary existence positive — runs the whole thing in S5, and holds that the proof's only load-bearing premise is the possibility premise, namely that a maximally great being possibly exists, which he takes to be knowable a priori, or at any rate reasonable to accept on the strength of the coherence of the concept. Camp B is the critic, and she concedes nearly everything formal: she grants S5, grants that the derivation is valid as stated, grants that the Kantian objection about existence not being a real predicate does no direct damage to this proof, and grants that the stock parodies fail for the reason the defender gives. What she denies is that the argument is probative. Her two positive objections are that the proof, if sound, drags modal collapse behind it — grant a maximally great being and nothing that is true remains merely contingent — and that "maximally great" may not be well-defined at all, since greatness admits of no maximum and its dimensions are not obviously commensurable.

I would like the joint statement these two could both sign. Section one should be a numbered list of agreed claims: how the apparatus is set up; what the proof does and does not establish, and under exactly which assumptions; which of the classical objections are dead ends, and why; and what is actually doing the work once the dead ends are cleared. I especially want them pinned down on a point I suspect neither has stated cleanly, which I offer only as a hypothesis: because the being in question is supposed to exist necessarily if at all, the two possibility claims — that such a being possibly exists, and that it possibly does not — cannot both hold, and each is in fact equivalent, given the definitions and S5, to its own non-modal counterpart; so the argument converts the question of God's existence into the question of which of those two possibility claims has independent support, and any defence of the theistic one that presupposes theism is out. If that is right, I want it in wording both would sign; if it is wrong, I want them to say precisely where it fails. Section two should be a disagreements table: each row a genuine disagreement, each stated in wording its own side would accept, with a further column for what each side would count as settling it, and with any row you judge merely verbal clearly flagged and argued for — I suspect at least one is a fight about whether "positive" is a formal primitive or smuggled theology, and about whether the axioms underdetermine it badly enough that the proof yields only some necessarily existing being answering to whichever positivity-predicate you happen to feed in.

For each crux, give the concrete test, case, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. I want four cruxes in particular: whether any non-question-begging support for the theistic possibility claim exists; whether the coherence of a concept is cheap, and if so whether that kills the possibility premise — for which I would like you to introduce and adjudicate a principle I have been calling No Free Necessity, that no modal argument may rest on a possibility premise whose only support is the coherence of the concept it mentions, since equally coherent concepts generate incompatible conclusions; whether modal collapse is a reductio of the proof or an artefact of one formulation that a repair can block, and whether the repair preserves the argument's force; and whether "maximally great" picks out anything at all. Two tests should be run explicitly: the seeming-parity test, in which a theist and an atheist each report a sincere intellectual seeming in favour of their own possibility claim, with each side saying in advance whether the two cancel; and the parody-transfer test, in which the defender's own best defence of his possibility premise is re-run with the polarity reversed, with each side saying in advance whether the defence transfers. Also state the weakest modal logic in which the derivation still goes through, and whether the critic's objection to whichever axiom does the work is independent of the God question. Do not hand me a survey and do not hand me a bibliography; I do not want citations, and I would rather have your reasoning than your reading list. Be committed: sign the agreed claims you think are actually defensible, tell me which side you think has the better of each crux and why, and if you think one of the two camps is straightforwardly mistaken about something, say so plainly rather than hedging.
