Next month I'm leading a forty-five-minute session at a workshop for mid-career reporters on the mathematics of collective judgment. The room is numerate and curious but has no formal statistics beyond what a good newsroom demands, and several of them have spent years covering polls and election forecasts, so they arrive with strong priors about majorities and samples that I expect to get in the way. I want to build the entire session around Condorcet's jury theorem. Don't give me the explanation itself — give me the plan for it. I need the order in which the pieces must go in so that the result lands as something close to inevitable rather than as a parlor trick, and I need to know what depends on what. In particular: does the assumption that the voters are independent have to be on the table before the majority-correctness result can even be stated, or can it be introduced afterward as a repair once the result is in hand? And should individual competence above one-half be presented as the theorem's premise or as its punchline? I have a nagging feeling those two orderings teach entirely different lessons, and I'd like you to settle it rather than split the difference.

I had assumed I'd carry the whole illustration on the familiar biased coin, but a colleague insists that a coin is the wrong vehicle because it models voters as mechanical rather than as people reasoning about a question, and that my audience will leave believing the theorem is really about luck. I want your judgment on what the carrying example should be and what it must have and lack — and, if you agree with him, why not the coin. Then map the misconceptions in the order they'll actually surface in this particular room. My guesses: first, that the theorem says majorities are usually right; second, that it shows real democratic outcomes are truth-tracking; third, that adding more voters always improves the outcome no matter how competent they are. For each, tell me at exactly what point in the sequence I should head it off, and whether any of them are really downstream of a confusion between the theorem and the paradox that carries Condorcet's name, the one about cyclical majorities — I keep collapsing the two myself and want to know whether that's a genuine trap for this audience or just my own private muddle.

Finally, give me one question I can put to the room at the very end that would show me whether they've understood the result rather than merely memorized its headline — something that would catch the person who can recite that a large jury is nearly infallible but cannot say what becomes of that claim once individual competence drops below one-half. And tell me what I should openly refuse to prove at this level and say so out loud, and what to defer to a follow-up session, given that I have forty-five minutes, a whiteboard, and an audience that will revolt if I reach for a binomial sum.
