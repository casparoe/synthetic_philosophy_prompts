I teach the first half of a two-semester world history survey at a large state university; my half runs from the earliest human societies to 1500, and my colleague's half picks up at 1500 and carries on to the present. The division is so standard that I had never thought to question it, until a colleague pointed out an oddity I now cannot unsee. In the first half I teach the Silk Road, the Indian Ocean trading network, the Mongol conquests, and the spread of the Black Death from East Asia toward Europe as one connected episode; in the second half I teach Atlantic exploration, the Columbian exchange, and the emergence of an integrated world economy as the moment the world became connected. So I seem to be telling students both that long-distance interconnection is very old and that real global history starts in 1500, and I would like to know whether that is a straightforward contradiction, two distinct claims I have been carelessly running together, or something the practice can consistently mean.

What I am after is a reconstruction of what the 1500 division, considered as an institutional and pedagogical practice rather than as a thesis anyone defends, actually presupposes. Does it commit me to a view of historical connection as a threshold that some event crosses, as a matter of degree, or as a perspective the historian adopts for particular purposes? Does it presuppose that civilizations or societies are the natural units whose relations are then traced, and if so what work does that assumption do? Does it presuppose that periods are discovered in the evidence rather than imposed by calendars, departments, and source anthologies? And does it smuggle in a ranking, on which some regions are early and others late, so that the dates encode a verdict about who is ahead rather than merely when things happened? I would like these separated from one another, and I would also like to know which parts the division strictly requires as against which it merely happens to fit — the two-semester sequence, the anthologies organized by civilization, the tidiness of 1492 as a hook — since I suspect the academic calendar is doing more of the work than any historical argument.

Finally, suppose the presuppositions turn out to be untenable. What would a genuinely non-Eurocentric world history course have to change? Could it keep a pivot at all, and if so where — a colleague proposes the thirteenth century, arguing that the Mongol conquests first linked the Eurasian networks along which the plague then traveled — or would it have to give up the single pivot for overlapping regional chronologies, and if so what would hold a survey course together? And would the same reasoning unsettle my colleague's half as much as mine, or does the second half rest on different commitments than the first? I am not sure what the relevant vocabulary is for any of this, so please tell me what I am actually asking.
