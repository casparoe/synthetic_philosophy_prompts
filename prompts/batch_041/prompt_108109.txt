I sit on a hiring/promotions committee at work and we keep running into the same annoying thing. We vote on the sub-questions seperately -- is this person good at research, good at teaching, good with students -- and then we vote on whether to promote, and the tallies come out backwards: she wins on every sub-question but loses the overall vote, or the reverse. A friend who did a PhD in econ says this is a known puzzle in social choice, something about aggregating judgments rather than preferences, and that there's a whole literature on whether a group can be rational when each of its members is. Before I wade into any of that I want the spec written out first, the way I'd spec a tool I was going to build (I might actually try to code something up for the committee, we're tired of arguing about the tallying).

So: what would a theory of collective judgment formation actually have to deliver to count as adequate? Give me the desiderata list. Which cases it has to get right -- our committee mess, a jury deciding a fact, a panel of experts who disagree, a group that lands on the right answer by luck, a group that ends up with no coherent position at all. Which distinctions it can't collapse: aggregating judgments vs aggregating preferences; the premises of an argument vs its conclusion; a procedure that gives everyone equal weight vs one that weights people by competence; a group genuinely having a view vs just reporting a tally of individual views. And what it has to stay consistent with in practice -- that we sometimes need the group to abstain or declare a question ill-posed, that two people on our committee plainly know more than the rest of us, that one guy I'm fairly sure votes strategically to get the outcome he wants, that the result shouldn't flip because somebody adds an unrelated item to the agenda, that whatever we build has to not be trivially gameable. For each requirement, say what's behind it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are really just the preference of one camp.

The part I most want is where you think two or three of these can't be had together. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so, tell me which one I'd have to give up, and what dropping it buys me. If a single requirement does most of the work of ruling out otherwise plausible procedures, say which one that is and why. Skip the scorecard of named theories, I'll do that after I have the list -- and please no reading suggestions, just the requirements themselves and the reasoning behind them.
