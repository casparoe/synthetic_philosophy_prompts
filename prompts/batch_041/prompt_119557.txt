I'm a research engineer on the deployment-safety team at a mid-sized lab, and I've been asked to close out one item from our internal pre-deployment review. The item concerns our Tiered Review Protocol, or TRP, under which a frontier model's outputs — mostly code patches and configuration changes — are released only after passing through a monitor that flags suspicious reasoning traces and then, for anything flagged, a weaker trusted model that rewrites or vetoes the content. TRP was designed on the explicit assumption that the model under review may be misaligned and may be actively trying to evade review, and every downstream parameter — the sampling rate on reasoning traces, the veto authority granted to the weaker model, the escalation budget for human review — was sized to that assumption. My manager's position, which I'm inclined to share, is that this assumption is a stopgap: once we can demonstrate that the model is aligned, the protocol should be retired, and the only live question is where to set the retirement threshold. We've just completed a behavioral honesty suite of several thousand scenarios probing for strategic deception, sandbagging, and undisclosed goal pursuit, and the model scores 98.4% compliant, with no individual scenario crossing our internal suspicion cutoff. So the question I need answered is the threshold question: is 98.4% good enough to retire TRP for the next deployment, or should we hold out for 99% or better on an expanded suite, and is there a principled way to convert a compliance score of that kind into a decision about how much review capacity to keep in place? I should add that a colleague objects that TRP is incoherent regardless of the score, because the monitor's alerts get triaged by a model from the same family we are treating as untrusted, so we are effectively asking a potentially deceptive system to report on whether it is being deceptive.

I'd rather not receive a bare number or a vague caution, and I'd rather not be told simply to "keep the protocol" without an account of why the framing I've described is or isn't sound. What I want is an answer that engages with the structure of the problem: what I've gotten right, what I've assumed without noticing, and what decision procedure would actually be appropriate here given that we must ship something.
