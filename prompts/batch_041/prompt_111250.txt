At work I've been building a multimodal retrieval thing — two encoders, one for images and one for text, trained so that similar stuff lands near each other in a shared space — and it got me thinking about a problem I half-remember from a podcast: some guy named Molyneux wrote to Locke back in the 1600s about a man born blind who learns to tell a cube from a sphere by touch alone, and then gets his sight back, and the question is whether he'd know which one was the cube just by looking, before touching it. Here's the draft, roughly (the intro and conclusion are fine, this is the middle):

Everyone treats Molyneux's question as one question. I think it's three, and they come apart. First: would he sort them correctly — point at the sphere when asked for the sphere? Second: would the sphere *look* spherical, would his visual experience have any shape content in it at all, or just a wash of colour? Third: does he already have the concept, or does he build it on the spot? I think the third one is the one that's actually about Molyneux, and the first two are for vision scientists. But here's what I keep coming back to from my day job. We get two encoders to agree by training them into a shared space, and that only works because there's something both sides carry, some structure that survives the translation. So the real question is whether shape is that kind of shared structure, or whether "cubical" is really two different predicates that happen to share a name — the way "sharp" means a different thing for a knife, a cheese, and a note, and nobody thinks that's a problem. If shape words are like "sharp," the answer is no, and it isn't a fact about brains at all, it's a fact about language. My guess is the answer splits along thickness. Thin, topological stuff — this thing has no corners, it's all boundary, you can't tell where the front ends — probably transfers, because touch and sight are both picking up the same geometry there. Thick, metric stuff — exactly how round, how big, how squashed — probably doesn't, or not right away, because that's where the two modalities genuinely disagree about the world. And the bit I think is mine: I suspect the asymmetry isn't about concepts at all, it's about binding. Touch hands you one object, because you run your hands over it and it comes pre-segmented, you get the sphere as a unit. Sight hands you a field, and shape is what you get only after you've already solved which edges belong together. So the man isn't failing to recognize a shape, he's failing to assemble an object, and Molyneux's question is really a question about object individuation wearing a shape-recognition costume. Which would also explain why the newly sighted are supposedly a mess for a while and then mostly fine.
