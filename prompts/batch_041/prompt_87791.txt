I am writing a novel set in the 1740s, much of it in the household of Émilie du Châtelet, and I have reached a scene I cannot yet hear: two of my characters must argue their disagreement through rather than gesture at it. The first is a woman of about forty — a mathematician, a gambler, a lover, and a philosopher by her own lights — who holds roughly the view of happiness that du Châtelet sets out in the essay on happiness she left unpublished at her death. For her, happiness is an art rather than an insight, and its ingredients are good health, strong passions (love, play, study) turned to the service of one's contentment, freedom from prejudice, virtue, and above all a deliberate openness to illusion: a willingness to keep the agreeable picture intact, to refuse to dwell on death and other sad ideas, and never to repent. We are the authors of our own happiness, she thinks; we choose our route and then strew it with flowers.

Her interlocutor is a visiting abbé, a Stoic of the old school and no pedant, who holds that this is mood-management dressed up as philosophy. A happiness built on passion is one that fortune can confiscate; a happiness built on illusion is one that any honest inquiry will puncture. For him the real thing is the understanding of necessity — including the necessity of one's own death — and the freedom from passion that follows from it. I do not want a strawman on either side, and I do not want two monologues in alternation: each should give the other's argument its strongest form and answer the point actually made.

Please write the dialogue. I would particularly like the abbé to press the charge that a happiness requiring illusion is self-undermining — that a person who has understood her own recipe has already begun to lose the taste for it — and I would like the marquise to have a genuine reply rather than a graceful retreat. Her best line of defence, I suspect, lies in the difference between an illusion and a false belief: we weep at a tragedy we know to be invented, and knowing how a trick is done does not always spoil it. Somewhere in the exchange, let them arrive at the uncomfortable question of whether the philosopher's lucidity is itself just one more passion with a better reputation. Keep the scene light — a summer evening, a garden, a lamp, the litter of a card game — so that the argument carries the scene and not the scenery.
