I'm a backend engineer, no formal philosophy training (one undergrad ethics class I mostly slept through), and I've ended up in a long-running argument with a friend who did a philosophy minor and never lets me forget it. Her position on the sunk cost thing is basically: it's a fallacy, full stop, the money and the hours are gone, only future costs and benefits count, and any time I defend sticking with something I've put time into I'm just rationalizing. The problem is my gut doesn't work that way and I don't think my gut is being dumb, but I also can't state a rule that gets my gut to behave consistently. So here's a pile of my actual verdicts, no hedging, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just making it up as I go. Non-refundable movie ticket, 25 minutes in, it's awful — leave, no debate. Two-hundred-dollar conference ticket already paid, morning of I'd rather sleep and catch up on work — skip it, also no debate. At work we spent six months on a feature, it's clear it won't ship, I'm the one who killed it — fine. A game I've put two years and maybe 500 hours into, I don't enjoy it and nobody would play it — quit, fine, though I felt bad for a week and I think that's just grief, not a reason. A novel, 400 pages, three years, and the only reason I open the file is the three years — stop, fine. A mountain, 12 hours up, 80% of the way, I'm beat but the weather's fine and the top is two hours off — keep going, and I'd say this one only *looks* like sunk cost reasoning, since the reason isn't the 12 hours, it's that I'm nearly there. My dad's half-restored car: we were working on it together when he died, it's expensive, I don't enjoy it, and I'm going to finish it anyway, and not for the money or the car — if I sell it half-done, the years we spent on it turn into nothing. A startup where I stayed two extra years partly because my parents had money in it — I'm less proud of that one, it wasn't even my money, but from the inside it felt identical. A relationship, 11 years, no kids, not happy, no reason to think it improves — leave, and this is the one where people say "but think of all that time" and I think they're being manipulative. A friendship, 15 years, we've drifted, hanging out is a chore — I stay in touch, and I can't tell if that's sunk cost or loyalty or what the difference even is. And the one I keep flip-flopping on: the novel stopped feeling like waste the moment I realized I could carve the best 80 pages into three short stories. Same hours sunk either way, and suddenly quitting felt like a good decision instead of an admission. That one I genuinely can't get past.

My tentative principle, and I have no idea if it's a real one or just the fallacy with extra steps: sunk costs count when giving up would retroactively turn the past into nothing, and don't count when the past stays meaningful anyway, or when I can carry it forward into something else. A friend of mine called the two things "ballast" and "dead weight," which I liked but can't defend. Test it against my cases, fix it if it's broken, tell me honestly which of my verdicts I have to give up if I keep it, and say what it predicts for a few cases I haven't listed — a company that's burned five years on a product, is 90% likely to fail, but whose team would be gutted if it shut; someone who goes to the gym purely because they've been going for years; a country a decade into a war. Please don't send me a reading list, I won't read it. Lay it out as: **The principle** (about 200 words, stated precisely enough that I could actually apply it to a new case, and define any jargon you use); **Case by case** (my cases in the order I gave them, one or two lines each, saying which verdict you keep and how); **What it can't save** (150-200 words, be straight with me, including the flip-flop case); **Cases I didn't give you** (two or three, with your verdict on each); **Bottom line** (three or four sentences).
