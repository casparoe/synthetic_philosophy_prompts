Write an essay on the nonidentity problem. I've spent enough years in the rationalist and effective-altruist blogosphere to have watched this one get hashed out many times, and the standard shape of the discussion has become predictable: an action changes who will later exist, the resulting people have lives worth living, no one is made worse off by comparison with any alternative in which they exist at all, and yet the action still seems criticizable — so the person-affecting restriction on ethics is supposed to collapse, and we are meant to accept some impersonal principle that ranks outcomes by aggregate welfare. The conservation-versus-depletion case, the repugnant conclusion as the toll exacted for consistency, the longtermist worry that future people may have no claim on us precisely because our choices alter which of them will exist: I take all of this as common ground, and I don't want it recited back to me. I want the machinery stress-tested.

The pressure point I want at the center is this. The nonidentity problem turns entirely on a fact about which possible person someone is: the reason no one can complain is that, had things gone otherwise, the very person who would complain would never have existed, and someone else would have existed in their place. That presupposes that identity across counterfactual situations is determinate and all-or-nothing — that there is always a fact of the matter as to whether the person who exists under one policy is or is not the person who would have existed under another. But Derek Parfit, who put the problem on the map, was himself a reductionist about personal identity: he held that our identity is not a deep further fact about the world, and that what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness, which comes in degrees. His reductionism was chiefly about identity across time, and it is a genuine question whether it extends to identity across possible worlds. That question is exactly what I want you to press. If trans-world identity is not always a sharp fact, does the nonidentity problem keep the grip it appears to have, or does it draw its force from a picture of identity its own author rejected? Take seriously the possibility that the problem is an artifact of an all-or-nothing metaphysics, and then say honestly whether that dissolves it, merely relocates it, or leaves it fully intact.

Second, I want the problem tested on subjects that are not human beings. Run the same structure on which of two differently-trained artificial systems comes to exist, or on a population of digital minds that can be copied and forked: no single individual exists across both branches and is worse off in one, so the person-affecting complaint seems to lose its grip, and yet something still seems to be at stake. Does the intuition survive the change of subject, or was it tracking something other than identity all along — the quality of the lives, an impersonally bad outcome, a claim about what we owe the world rather than any particular person?

Reach a verdict. Is the nonidentity problem a genuine refutation of the person-affecting restriction, a puzzle that dissolves once we get clear about the metaphysics of identity, or a real problem that the person-affecting view can survive in repaired form? Don't end on the fence, and don't simply restate the dialectic. Then tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, resolved one way rather than the other, would move you to a different verdict, and where in your own argument you would expect the weakest link to lie.
