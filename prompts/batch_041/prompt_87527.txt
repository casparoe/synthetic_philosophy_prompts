I took up philosophy after forty years as a field geologist, and one problem in decision theory has occupied me since. A stranger tosses a coin before the episode begins: if it lands one way, he asks me for a sum I can afford and keeps it; if it lands the other way, he makes me wealthy, but only on the condition that the version of me who was asked would have handed the money over. The wrinkle I want built into the story is that his forecast of what I would do is not concealed from me. He gives me the document before I decide, and tells me truthfully that it was written before I could possibly have read it, so that the man it describes is a man who did not yet know what it said.

Write a short story of about two thousand words that puts a character in precisely that position. Set it somewhere concrete and unremarkable — a county records office, a rural clinic, a ferry terminal at the end of the season — and let the stake be something other than money: a diagnosis, a residency permit, a child's place at a school. I want no narrator who explains the puzzle, no stranger who turns out to be a god or a fraud, and none of the words counterfactual, utility or precommitment in the story's own prose. Give me one character who refuses the arrangement altogether, on the ground that a demand whose reward lies in a branch that never happened is not a demand, and end the story without deciding between them.
