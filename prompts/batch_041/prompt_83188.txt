Right — I want you to write me an essay on a live controversy in the epistemology of knowledge, and I want you to actually come down somewhere at the end of it. No cop-out about both sides having their merits and the truth lying somewhere in between; if you genuinely can't decide, say which way you're leaning and what would have to be true for you to jump.

Here's the lay of the land, briefly, so we're on the same page. There's the contextualist move: knows is context-sensitive, and the context that matters is the one belonging to whoever is doing the attributing. Then there's the encroachment move: it isn't the word that shifts, it's the state — whether someone knows can itself depend on what is practically at stake for them. These two are usually run against each other using cases where the person doing the attributing and the person who has (or hasn't) got the knowledge are more or less in the same boat, or at least in the same room. I want you to attack a case where they come apart, because that's where I think the whole thing gets genuinely interesting and where I'm definately unsure what to say.

The case — I've been calling it the Dosimeter Case, and it's mine, so don't go hunting for it in the literature:

Nadia is a lab technician. She takes a dosimeter reading on a storage room, gets a number comfortably below the safety threshold, and on that basis believes the room is fine to work in. She's handing in her notice next week and moving abroad; nothing whatsoever rides, for her, on whether the room is actually contaminated. Her stakes are zero.

Ravi will be working in that room full time from next month. He's just found out he's going to be a father and is trying to work out whether it's safe enough for him to keep the job. His stakes are about as high as stakes get.

Amina is a regulator who, three years later, reviews the file after a cluster of illnesses in the building. Nothing happens to her personally either way, but if she gets this wrong the consequences are enormous. High stakes again — but impersonal ones, attaching to someone who isn't exposed to the room at all.

One body of evidence, three practical situations. Now:

(i) Does Nadia know the room is safe? Does your answer change if she never meets Ravi, or if she's the one who'll be working in there? (ii) Whose context is doing the work when I say that Nadia knows? What about when Ravi says it, or Amina, or Nadia herself — and is that a change in what we're talking about, or a change in what's true? (iii) Here's the part I most want you to chew on. Consider a principle — call it No Borrowed Stakes: a subject's belief cannot be downgraded, or upgraded, epistemically by practical facts that belong to other people and that the subject has no reason to think obtain. Is that principle true? If you accept it, it looks as though you get one theory for first-person cases and a different one for third-person attributions, which is a bit ugly. If you reject it, you owe me an account of how knowledge can be held hostage to facts the knower can't see. (iv) One more wrinkle and then I'll stop: does the direction of other people's stakes matter? Take a variant in which the person with the most at stake actively wants the room to be contaminated — a neighbour building a negligence claim, say. Should that move the standard any more or less than Ravi's dread does? There's something in the thought that wishful thinking cuts one way and fearful thinking cuts the other, but I can't get it to come out cleanly, so have a go.

No references, please — I'm not asking for a bibliography and I'd rather the arguement stood on its own feet. End with a verdict on the Dosimeter Case: does Nadia know, and does that verdict survive swapping who's doing the asking? Whichever way you go, I want to see you take the strongest version of the other side seriously before you knock it down.
