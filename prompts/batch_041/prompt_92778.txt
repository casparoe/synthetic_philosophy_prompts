Trying to spec out what a decision theory owes us in the Pascal's mugging family of cases, before I get into any particular proposal. The mugging: a stranger tells you to hand over $10 or he'll torture you forever, and puts the odds at something like 1 in 10^100. Straight expected utility says pay him. I want the criteria such a theory has to meet.

Cases it has to get right: the mugger himself; St Petersburg, where expected value is infinite so you should pay any finite fee to play, which nobody believes; a two-sided mugging where the same $10 buys a 1-in-10^100 chance of eternal bliss and a 1-in-10^100 chance of eternal torment, so you have to compare infinities rather than just weigh one against the other; a million muggers in a row, each individually worth paying, whose combined demands would ruin you; the case where the stated probability is literally zero; and the case where the threat is only issued to someone who turns down a finite offer first.

Distinctions it can't collapse: odds that come from a physical setup versus odds that come from a stranger's say-so; infinite versus merely astronomically large; and the mugger's offer being evidence about what kind of world you're in, not just a lottery ticket. Cheap results it can't contradict: dominance, and not being money-pumped by a sequence of choices that each look fine on their own.

Then tell me which of these are genuine and which are just squeamishness about where the math lands, and whether some of them can't be had together. My guess is that "dont be fanatical about tiny probabilities" and "infinite stakes are decisive whatever the odds" are in conflict, but I'd like to be shown otherwise. And it has to be something a person with a normal brain could actually apply standing on the street, so no rules that require comparing infinitesimals.
