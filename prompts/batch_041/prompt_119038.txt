I run a small reading group on rationality and self-interest, and next week is the dollar auction — a dollar goes to the highest bidder, bids climb in nickels, and the second-highest bidder also has to pay what they bid and gets nothing back. I want to open the session with a short poem, 14-18 lines, spoken the whole way through by the bidder who ends up losing, with the other bidder never given a mind: they only ever show up as a number in the poem, since the trap doesn't require you to know what the other person is thinking. The form should do the escalating. The bids have to climb line by line by a real, consistent increment and the arithmetic has to hold, so that at every point the speaker's next bid is genuinely better for them than dropping out — until the turn where the same logic that got them in is what keeps them in, because quitting now costs more than bidding again. I'd like the last stanza to repeat the first almost word for word, with different numbers, so you can see it's the same move being made, only with everything on the table this time. Dont make them stupid; the whole thing only bites if each step is defensible.

Then, after the poem, a few sentences telling me which parts of this are settled and which parts people who study it actually argue about. The mechanics, and the fact that the escalation can be locally rational while being ruinous overall, are settled. Whether this is really a model of the escalations we care about (wars, litigation, R&D races), whether the failure lies in the bidders or in the game itself, whether it's the same animal as sunk-cost thinking, and what if anything fixes it — none of that is consensus, so please flag it as contested rather than selling me one side.
