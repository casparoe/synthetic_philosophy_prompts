Our corporate strategy group periodically receives game-theoretic models — small payoff matrices for a handful of players and strategies — from the internal economics team, and has to turn each one into a recommendation on a live decision: a pricing move, an entry threat, a bid. The analyst who runs these models knows basic game theory but has no background in linear programming, has roughly a day per model, and must produce a one-page note that the head of strategy signs off on. What we lack is a procedure we can apply the same way every time.

Write that procedure. I want a decision procedure for reducing a finite game by iterated elimination of dominated strategies, including domination by mixed strategies, that a careful non-specialist can run in order, with an explicit stopping rule and explicit points at which the procedure hands the decision back to a person. Keep what the theory actually says — do not water it down into advice — and be honest about what the procedure cannot do.

It should, at a minimum:

- begin by fixing the game and, before anything else, the scale of the payoffs: whether the numbers are magnitudes that can be averaged (so that the expected payoffs of mixtures can be compared) or only a ranking of outcomes. If only a ranking, the procedure must stop short of the mixed-strategy step and explain why.
- screen for strategies strictly dominated by a pure strategy, eliminate them, and record why this step is safe (it never deletes a strategy used in a Nash equilibrium) and why the order of elimination does not change what survives.
- give an operational test for domination by a mixture, since the analyst cannot solve an optimization. State the criterion underneath it — a strategy is strictly dominated by some mixture exactly when it is never a best response to any belief about the opponents — and then give a search a person can actually carry out: which mixtures to try, how to recognize quickly that a candidate survives, and when to stop looking.
- say when to iterate and when to halt, and what the surviving set licenses. A single surviving profile is a prediction; a surviving set is not, and the procedure must say so rather than paper over it.
- forbid, or fence off sharply, the iterative use of weak dominance, stating the cost: it is order-dependent and can delete a Nash equilibrium.
- flag the point at which the answer depends on whether the opponents' choices are treated as independent or allowed to be correlated — a distinction that bites in games with three or more players — and state which assumption the procedure makes and how the note should disclose it.
- mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on two models. The first is this three-by-three game (player 1 chooses the row, player 2 the column; each cell gives player 1's payoff, then player 2's):

            L          C          R
  T       (4, 3)     (3, 0)     (1, 0)
  M       (2, 0)     (2, 3)     (2, 0)
  B       (1, 0)     (3, 0)     (4, 3)

The second is a game in which the only candidate for elimination is weakly dominated and yet belongs to a Nash equilibrium, so that the procedure must decline to eliminate it; construct a small example and run it. For at least one of the two, the procedure will reach an answer the group does not want; state plainly what that answer is and why the group should accept it anyway.

Close with a short section on the procedure's blind spots: the cases it will get wrong, the inputs it cannot repair, and the decisions that must go back to a human.
