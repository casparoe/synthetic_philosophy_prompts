I am a retired neurobiologist who, after a long career at the bench, has spent the past three years trying to give myself a philosophical education, mainly by sitting in on a weekly discussion group at my local university. I have run into a puzzle about one of the other members and would like your help with it. Please take up the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in moral responsibility and in the tradition that descends from P. F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes, and give me the kind of careful, hypothesis-driven reasoning I would expect from a good colleague rather than a general survey. Before you settle on any answer, I would like you to brainstorm: lay out every candidate principle you can think of that might be driving the member's judgments, and only then narrow down to the ones that actually survive contact with the evidence. Please write in American English, and please flag clearly where you are confident and where you are guessing.

The member, whom I will call D., is a committed Strawsonian and has explained her framework to us more than once. As I understand it, she holds that what matters for moral responsibility is not some metaphysical fact about whether an agent could have done otherwise, but whether the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, guilt, and the rest — are fitting responses to the agent. Those attitudes are fitting when we take up the participant stance toward someone, treating him as a fellow member of the moral community whose attitudes and intentions we can address; they are out of place when we take up the objective stance, treating him as a thing to be managed, treated, or cured. She insists that the objective stance is warranted in two quite different situations, which she is careful to keep apart: an agent may be excluded from the moral community altogether, as we exclude a very young child or a person in the grip of severe psychosis, or an agent may remain a full member while a particular action of his is excused because it expressed no ill will toward anyone, as when he acts under duress, or in ignorance, or by accident. She is also fond of pointing out that the general causal history of a person's character — how he came to be the way he is — is not by itself a reason to take the objective stance. The facts of a brutal upbringing, she says, are simply the facts of that person, not an exemption from the moral community.

Over the past year the group has worked through a series of cases, and D.'s verdicts have been steady and, I think, revealing. Here they are, as accurately as I can report them.

First, a man who defrauds his elderly mother of her savings to feed a gambling habit. D. holds him responsible; she says resentment is fitting.

Second, a man who, with a gun to his head, tells his captors where his friend is hiding. D. does not hold him responsible; she says the objective stance is warranted here.

Third, a toddler who, while flailing during a tantrum, knocks a valuable vase off the table. D. does not hold him responsible.

Fourth, a woman in an acute manic and delusional episode who recklessly dissipates her employer's money. D. does not hold her responsible.

Fifth, a man raised from birth in a violent militia, who is now an adult, beats and humiliates those under him, and feels no remorse for it. D. holds him responsible; she says his upbringing is not an exemption.

Sixth, a man who stands up a friend for dinner because he was rushing his wife to the hospital. D. does not hold him responsible; she says resentment would not be fitting, though disappointment is.

Next month we take up a new case, and this is where I need you. The case is drawn from my old field, and I can vouch for its clinical plausibility. A man of previously gentle and reliable character develops a slow-growing tumor in his frontal lobes. The tumor produces episodes of cold, deliberate cruelty toward his family: he says cutting things, betrays his wife's confidences, and on one occasion destroys a prized possession of his daughter's. During these episodes he is fully alert, aware of what he is doing, and able to give shallow reasons for it, though he feels no remorse at the time. Between episodes he is his old self; he is horrified and ashamed of what he has done and begs his family to have him treated. His capacity to reason and to take part in his relationships is intact except during the episodes themselves. Nobody has suggested that he is delusional or that he fails to understand what he is doing.

I would like three things from you, in this order.

First, predict D.'s verdict on the new case. Does she hold the man responsible and resent him, or does she take the objective stance? Give me your single best prediction, with the reasoning behind it.

Second, tell me what rule or rules she is actually running. I suspect that more than one principle fits all six of her earlier verdicts, and I want you to state each such principle precisely and show how it accounts for the six. I am especially interested in whether the six verdicts leave it open whether she is operating a membership condition, a quality-of-will condition, or some combination of the two, and in how the new case bears on that question.

Third, and most important to me as a scientist, tell me what further case would tell these rules apart. Design the case — an actual scenario, not a schematic — and explain what each surviving rule predicts about it and why the predictions come apart there. If it turns out that the rules do not really come apart anywhere, say so, and tell me what that would mean for D.'s position.
