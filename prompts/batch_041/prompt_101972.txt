Last night a friend and I argued ourselves into a corner about common knowledge, and I am not sure I lost on the merits. Her claim was that everything epistemic game theory loads onto common knowledge can be recovered from plain mutual knowledge plus enough cleverness, and that the infinite-regress condition is a philosopher's artefact doing no real work; I defended the regress and then found I could not say cleanly what it buys that a very high finite level of mutual knowledge does not. Now I have to run next week's session, and the argument has made me realise I do not understand the idea well enough to teach it.

The group is a mixed reading group of humanities graduate students — competent with careful argument, innocent of game theory, and allergic to anything that looks like formalism for its own sake. I have eighty minutes and a whiteboard. Plan the explanation for me rather than giving it: the order in which the components must be introduced and why, with the dependencies made explicit (what genuinely has to be in place before the next piece will land), so that by the end the distinction between mutual knowledge, common belief and common knowledge feels necessary rather than fussy, and the assumption of common knowledge of rationality in a solution concept looks like something chosen rather than something obvious.

I want to carry the whole session on one scenario of my own, not the muddy children, which half of them will have met and the other half will half-remember. My sketch: a group of friends in a chat with read receipts, deciding whether to turn up to something worth attending only if enough of them come; each read receipt adds one more layer of knowledge and no finite number of them ever closes the gap. Tell me whether that scenario can bear the weight, what it must contain to do so, and what it must not smuggle in. If it cannot carry the session, tell me what to replace it with and why.

Then map the misconceptions — the ones they will arrive with and the ones the session itself will plant. I expect at least: that common knowledge is just mutual knowledge iterated many times; that it demands an infinite chain of actually held beliefs in finite minds; that it is the same as common belief; and that common knowledge of rationality is an empirical claim about how people behave. Say where in the sequence each should be headed off, which of them I should let surface deliberately, and what I should openly decline to settle at this level.

Give me one question to put to the room at the end that would show whether the idea has landed rather than merely been followed, and tell me what a wrong answer to it would most likely reveal.

Finally, be candid about where this plan is most likely to fail: which ordering decision or reading of the audience you would abandon if I came back and said the session had gone badly, and what I could observe in the room that would count as evidence the dependency structure itself is wrong rather than that I taught it poorly. Write in British English.
