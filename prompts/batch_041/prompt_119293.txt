I teach a jurisprudence seminar that pairs the trial scene of The Merchant of Venice with the textualism–purposivism debate, and I want a reading of the play that can survive contact with the doctrine. The scene is usually taught as equity pleading against law, but Portia's winning argument is the most literalist move in the play: the bond grants Shylock flesh and says nothing about blood, so the strictest possible reading of the very words he chose defeats the end he wanted — the pound cannot be exact and bloodless at once, and any blood shed forfeits his estate under Venetian law. My suspicion is that the play is making a harder point than the usual one: that literalism and purposivism are not rival methods but one technique of strictness aimed at different texts, so that what separates the camps is where each stops reading, and the real discretion lies in that stopping point. Read the play as an argument about interpretation and test that suspicion against it, bringing in its other binding instruments — the casket test, and Portia's father's will, a text she must obey and cannot amend, whose literal sense misleads every suitor who trusts it. Say where the trial scene confirms the thesis and where it resists it; be honest that the resolution is not jurisprudential at all but ducal prerogative, a confiscation statute aimed at aliens, and a forced conversion, and say whether the ending is an argument or a display of power. No quotations needed. End with what the play knows about interpretation that the literature on the two methods assumes away.
