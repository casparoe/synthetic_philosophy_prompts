I am preparing a graduate seminar on the epistemology of testimony and want an exercise that forces the issue between reductionism and anti-reductionism without falling back on the standard examples. By reductionism, I mean the view that testimonial justification is never basic: to be justified in accepting a speaker's claim, one must have non-testimonial evidence—perceptual, inductive, or otherwise—for the speaker's reliability or sincerity on the matter at hand. By anti-reductionism, I mean the view that testimony is a basic source of justification: one is default entitled to accept what one is told, absent special reasons to doubt, roughly as one is default entitled to trust one's perceptual faculties. Assume the question concerns justification rather than knowledge, and do not appeal to any particular author or text; reason from the descriptions alone. You may note whether a case bears more on global reductionism (the demand for a general background of non-testimonial evidence) or local reductionism (the demand for positive reasons for each particular testimony), but the score should reflect the case's overall force for the reductionist side.

Below are ten testimonial cases. Rate each on a scale from -5 to +5, where +5 means the case, taken as a paradigm, strongly supports reductionism, -5 means it strongly supports anti-reductionism, and 0 means it is neutral or cuts both ways. For each case, give a short paragraph justifying the score. Then rank all ten cases from most reductionist-friendly (highest score) to most anti-reductionist-friendly (lowest score). If you think a case is ambiguous, say which reading produces which score and choose the reading you find most natural.

1. A visitor to a foreign country asks a passerby for directions and follows them, having no independent evidence that the passerby is reliable, but finding nothing suspicious.
2. A child acquires the word 'red' by accepting a parent's correction, and later uses the term confidently; the child has no independent check on the parent's color vision or honesty.
3. A large language model tells you that a certain historical treaty was signed in a particular year; you have no independent knowledge of the treaty, but you have a track record of the model being accurate on similar factual questions.
4. A witness in a criminal trial testifies against her own apparent interest, and you know from general experience that people rarely lie against their interest.
5. A colleague tells you that she is reliable as a testifier; you have no other evidence of her reliability, but you accept her claim and then treat her subsequent testimony as justified.
6. You are a member of a scientific community and accept a result because it was reported by a trusted laboratory, though you have not seen the raw data and cannot independently reproduce it.
7. A hostile political opponent tells you a damaging fact about his own party; you have strong independent evidence that he is hostile and would not help your side unless the fact were true.
8. A person reports a mystical experience that is logically possible but radically unlike anything in your experience; you accept it because she seems sincere and you have no specific reason to doubt her.
9. You accept testimony from someone you regard as an epistemic peer, but she disagrees with you on a controversial philosophical question; you have no independent way to adjudicate the disagreement.
10. A whole community's knowledge of its history is transmitted orally; each individual has only fragmentary independent evidence, but the chain of testimony is long and largely uncheckable.

End with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing the ranking as a comma-separated list of case numbers from most reductionist-friendly to most anti-reductionist-friendly, each followed by its score in parentheses, e.g., \answer{3(+5), 7(+3), 1(0), 8(-4)}. Do not include anything after that line.
