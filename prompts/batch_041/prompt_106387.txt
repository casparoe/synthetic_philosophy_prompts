I write a column on science and ideas, and I've spent the better part of a month trying to get to the bottom of Hempel's old raven paradox — the point that since the hypothesis that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens, a green apple or a white shoe ought to count as evidence for it, which nobody can quite stomach. What has struck me in the conversations I've had about it, with friends over dinner and with a couple of philosophers I cornered at a conference, is how fast everyone reaches for their preferred machinery — Bayes nets, likelihood ratios, inference to the best explanation, contrastive accounts — without first saying what the machinery is supposed to deliver. One colleague, a philosopher of science, keeps insisting that the real lesson is that confirmation is always contrastive, that evidence bears on a hypothesis only relative to a set of live alternatives, and that the raven paradox is an artifact of pretending otherwise; I can't tell whether that belongs in the piece as the moral or as one candidate among many.

So before I read another word of advocacy, I want the specification. What would a theory of confirmation actually have to get right to be adequate — not for a textbook, but for a working scientist who has to decide, in the field, whether a stray observation is evidence for the hypothesis she's chasing? I don't need a reading list, a bibliography, or citations; a model that can't look anything up would only be guessing at those, and so would I. What I want is the requirements themselves: the cases any adequate theory must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results it has to be consistent with, each one tied to the particular case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are merely the prejudices of one camp, and — the part I care about most — whether some subset of them cannot be jointly satisfied, in which case the list itself is a result. Say too which single requirement does the most to rule out the usual contenders, and which of your requirements the contrastive idea, if it's right, would revise.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Let each item state one requirement, name the case behind it, and say whether it is negotiable; if two of your five cannot both be met, say so inside the entry where the tension surfaces, and make clear which single entry carries the most excluding force. For each, I would rather have a calibrated hedge than a show of confidence — tell me how sure you are — and single out the one entry you are least sure of.
