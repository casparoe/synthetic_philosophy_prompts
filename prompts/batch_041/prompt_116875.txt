I'm a graduate student in a normative ethics seminar, and I've been asked to respond to a classmate's presentation on the so-called cluelessness objection to consequentialism. I'd like you to act as a philosopher who specializes in consequentialism and the epistemology of moral decision-making, and to give me a careful accuracy audit of her summary before I commit to any of it. Here is the gist of what she said. Consequentialism holds that an action is right just in case it produces at least as much good overall as any available alternative, so to know what you ought to do you would have to know what all the consequences of your options will be. But because causal chains are chaotic — she invoked chaos theory and the butterfly effect — even a trivial act like buying a coffee ramifies outward until it eventually touches everyone, so no one can ever know the consequences of anything. That, she said, is the cluelessness problem, which she credited to Parfit's discussion of mistakes in moral mathematics, and she took it to show that consequentialism is not merely hard to apply but actually incoherent, since it demands that we maximize a quantity we can never even estimate. The only escape, on her telling, is to abandon consequences and defer to rules, so that any consistent consequentialist turns out to be a rule-consequentialist in disguise. She added that the problem is peculiar to utilitarianism, because it depends on the impossible project of measuring pleasure and pain, and that a consequentialism built on some other good would be untouched by it.

I'm inclined to think she's broadly right — I'd always heard cluelessness described as a serious problem for the view — but several of her moves felt too quick, and I don't trust my own grasp of the literature well enough to sort out where the trouble lies. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me, for each, what a philosopher working on this topic would accept as fair, what they would regard as a defensible simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation — paying particular attention to any place where a modest thesis has been inflated into a refutation, where two distinct objections have been run together, or where a technical term is being used in its loose everyday sense. Then tell me whether her concluding claims — that consequentialism is incoherent, and that rule-consequentialism is the only refuge — actually follow from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, and what the most natural replies on behalf of the consequentialist would be. Before you begin, take a moment to lay out the structure of the objection as you understand it and to list the main points of dispute, so that your audit is organized rather than a running commentary. I don't need citations or a reading list; what I want is your reasoning about the substance.
