For roughly three years a reading group I belong to has been circling one argument, and I have come to suspect that we are not actually having one argument. The question is whether a hypocrite can have the standing to blame. The paradigm is someone who reproaches you for a fault he is himself guilty of — the man who is unfaithful to his wife and then upbraids a friend for being unfaithful, the professor who plagiarized as a student and now fails a student for plagiarizing, the friend who has not exercised in a decade and tells you that you are ruining yourself with drink. One camp holds that such blame is defective in a way the truth of the accusation cannot repair: even when what the hypocrite says is accurate, he is in no position to say it, and the person he blames may properly set the reproach aside as not his to make. The other camp holds, just as firmly, that this conflates two questions — whether a fault is blameworthy, and what sort of person is doing the blaming — and that the hypocrite's own failings, real as they are, do not touch the accuracy or the force of what he says; at most they give the blamed person something to retort with. A third voice in the room, more skeptical than either, says that 'standing' is a piece of machinery we have invented and that everything of substance in the dispute is really about what good or harm comes of voicing the reproach. The two main sides agree that the hypocrite is doing something criticizable and agree that the underlying act is wrong, and in three years neither has moved.

Two wrinkles keep tripping us up, and I suspect they are doing more work than we admit. First, 'hypocrisy' sometimes means failing to live up to one's own standards and sometimes means professing standards one does not hold — insincere preaching — and the two sides seem to slide between the two senses without flagging it. Second, we cannot agree on how the answer should behave in the awkward cases: the reformed hypocrite who has since mended his ways, the hypocrite whose fault is unknown to the person he blames, and the case in which no one has clean hands, so that if hypocrisy stripped standing, no one could blame anyone. My own hypothesis — I offer it as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that one side is asking whether the blame is warranted and the other is asking whether the blamer is authorized to voice it, that these are two genuinely different questions which both answer to the name 'standing,' and that the impasse is a collision of two questions rather than of two answers. But I cannot tell whether that is the true diagnosis or merely a polite way of saying the sides have different bedrock pictures of what blame is for: whether it is at bottom a demand issued between equals, so that a hypocrite's standing is genuinely compromised, or a registering of a fact, so that it is not.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, engaging the positions as I have stated them, and be explicit about whether the sincerity-versus-consistency wrinkle and the awkward cases change the diagnosis or merely illustrate it. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a case that forces one side to concede, an argument that 'standing' is doing no work here and should be abandoned, or a reason to think that the impasse is simply what a genuinely hard normative question looks like. I would also like you to say what your diagnosis predicts about the everyday question underneath all this — whether the person blamed may simply ignore the hypocrite's reproach, or must still answer it.
