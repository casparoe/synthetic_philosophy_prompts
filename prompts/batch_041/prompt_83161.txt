So my reading group has burned like three sessions on Moore's proof of an external world and we are going in circles, and I want an actual diagnosis, not another lap. The setup: Moore holds up a hand and says, roughly, here's one hand, here's another, so at least two external things exist, therefore the external world exists — and he insists this is a genuine proof, a perfectly good one, and that anyone who won't accept it is being unreasonable. One camp in the group thinks he's basically right: you have to start somewhere, you can't prove everything, and the demand for a proof that doesn't presuppose anything about the external world is itself the unreasonable move. The other camp thinks it's the worst kind of question-begging: a proof that only works if you already accept the conclusion doesn't give a doubter any reason to stop doubting, so it isn't a proof at all, it's just Moore stomping his foot.

Here's my hypothesis, and I want you to either back it or kill it. I dont think this is a disagreement about whether we know there's an external world. I think it's a disagreement about what 'begging the question' even is. The Mooreans seem to treat it as a property of an arguement on its own (does the premise presuppose the conclusion?), and on that reading Moore is fine, because he isn't deducing the conclusion from itself. The skeptics treat it as a property of an argument relative to an audience or an epistemic position (does it give this doubter a reason?), and on that reading Moore is hopeless. If that's the real fault line, then 'Moore begs the question' and 'the skeptic begs the question' can both be true, just relative to different things, and we've been fighting about a fallacy rather than about knowledge.

So which is it? Is that the impasse, or is there a substantive epistemic disagreement underneath — about whether perceptual knowledge requires that you be able to answer the skeptic, or about whose standards of proof govern? Are the two sides just answering different questions under one heading, whether we know versus how we could know? And be honest about progress: is there a distinction, a concession, or a case that would actually move this, or is it one of those that shouldn't move and we should stop meeting about it? Also, would the Moorean move survive against a different skeptic — say, one who doubts other minds rather than the external world — or does it only feel good against the one opponent it was built for?
