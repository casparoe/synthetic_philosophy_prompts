I spent most of last Saturday evening playing Nomic — Peter Suber's 1982 game in which the move is a proposal to change the rules, and in which even the rule requiring players to obey the rules can be amended — and the evening ended in an argument with my friend Dana that we were still having on the walk home. Dana had proposed, and got adopted by an ordinary majority, a rule-change making that very rule repealable only by a unanimous vote, with any repeal carried by a smaller margin void. We then spent an hour failing to agree on whether the rule had ever validly been in force: the vote that entrenched it was a simple majority, so by the standard the proposal itself set it should never have taken effect; but then the standard never existed, so the ordinary majority threshold governs, so it did take effect, so it did not, and round again. Dana's view is that this is a bug in the ruleset, an artifact of Suber's numbering and of our sloppy chairing; mine is that it is the whole point — the same thing constitutional theorists worry about when they ask whether an amendment can entrench itself against the amending power that created it. I want to settle the matter the way adults settle things, which is by writing a joke about it.

So please act as both a philosopher of law who knows the self-amendment literature and a game designer who has refereed a long-running Nomic league, and write me a joke I can tell out loud at our next game night: under two minutes spoken, self-contained enough that someone who has never played Nomic still follows what went wrong, but genuinely funny to the people around the table who have. The punchline has to turn on the actual structure of self-application rather than on the atmosphere — I do not want jokes about how the rules inevitably become too complicated, how much philosophers enjoy paradoxes, how nobody could ever win, or how the whole game is an excuse to argue. It should turn on something real about a rule reaching the conditions of its own adoption or repeal, on the priority puzzle when two rules each authorize amendment of the other, or on the fact that deciding what the rules say is itself an act that the rules have to authorize. It can take any form you like — an anecdote, a courtroom exchange, a proposed rule-change with a ruling on it — as long as it works when spoken.

Before you write, work out privately which of those mechanisms gives the cleanest comic turn and exactly what the joke has to state in full for the turn to land; then give me the joke and nothing else. Begin with the joke itself: no preamble, no restating this message, no explanation of the setup, and the last line should be the punchline. After it, on a new line, add at most one sentence naming the self-amendment premise the punchline turns on, so I can check that the joke is doing real work.
