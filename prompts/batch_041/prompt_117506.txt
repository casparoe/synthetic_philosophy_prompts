I retired last year after thirty-five years as a physical chemist — mostly kinetics, a bit of stat mech — and I've been spending my mornings trying to teach myself philosophy of mind. I keep bouncing off the consciousness literature, and I think it's because I don't actually know what I'm suppossed to be asking of a theory. So before I read one more book, give me the requirements list: what would a theory of how micro-experiences combine into a single macro-experience actually have to deliver to count as any good?

Some things I'd put on the list myself, from my own background, but tell me where I'm wrong or where I'm asking for the impossible:

- It has to tell me what the combining relation is, the way bonding tells me how atoms make molecules. If the answer boils down to "they're just unified," that feels like naming the problem rather than solving it.
- It has to explain why the result is qualitatively new without simply asserting emergence. In chemistry the new property — wetness, colour — is at least in principle derivable from the parts plus their arrangement. My understanding is you can't do that here, so what's the substitute?
- It has to respect that I'm one subject and not, say, a subject that includes me and my chair. So there's a boundary or individuation requirement. What fixes the boundary, and can it be arbitrary without the whole thing collapsing?
- It has to survive the fact that we've never detected some new force or field that would do the combining. Does the theory need new physics, or can it be built out of what we already have? And if it needs new physics, isn't that a serious mark against it?
- It has to say something about grain — why a small handful of micro-qualities would give rise to the enormous variety of macro-qualities I actually experience. I have a vague sense someone calls this the palette problem but I don't trust my memory on the name, so please don't lean on that.

What I want from you: the full spec, with the case behind each requirement, marked as non-negotiable versus just a taste of one camp. Flag any pair of requirements that can't be satisfied together — if the list turns out to be self-defeating, I suspect that's the real result and I'd rather hear it now. And if one requirement does most of the work of killing off the popular options, tell me which and why. Last thing: I keep wondering whether an analogy to coarse-graining or renormalization — where the micro-details wash out at larger scales — is a genuine help here or a cheat, since it seems to buy the unity without buying the experience. I'd definately like your take on that. No reading list please, I'll never get through it; just the requirements and the reasoning.
