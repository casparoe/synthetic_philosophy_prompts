I am grading the final question on a midterm in an upper-level undergraduate course on rational choice and decision theory. The question was written for this exam, so the students had not seen the puzzle before. It read as follows.

Question. A driver will pass through exactly two intersections on a highway. At each intersection she may either exit or continue. She has no memory of having passed the first intersection by the time she reaches the second, she cannot tell the two intersections apart, and she cannot leave herself notes or otherwise make the two occasions distinguishable; whatever rule she follows at one intersection she follows at the other. Exiting at the first intersection yields a payoff of 0. Exiting at the second yields a payoff of 6. If she continues at both intersections, the journey ends with a payoff of 1. (a) What probability of continuing at an intersection maximizes her expected payoff? Show the calculation. (b) Set out how a decision-maker who treats each intersection as a separate, one-off choice would reason, and explain why the result differs from your answer to (a). (c) What, if anything, does this puzzle show about the requirement that a rational agent maximize expected utility?

Student's answer. (a) Because she cannot distinguish the intersections, she must continue with the same probability, p, at each. There are then three possible outcomes: she exits at the first, which happens with probability 1 − p and yields 0; she exits at the second, which happens with probability p(1 − p) and yields 6; or she continues past both, which happens with probability p² and yields 1. Her expected payoff is therefore 6p(1 − p) + p² = 6p − 5p². Setting the derivative to zero gives 6 − 10p = 0, so p = 3/5, and the expected payoff is 6(3/5)(2/5) + 9/25 = 45/25 = 9/5. At this value she exits at the second intersection 9/25 of the time and misses both exits 6/25 of the time, so the strategy buys her a real chance at the 6 rather than settling for the 1. (b) A decision-maker who treated each intersection as a one-off choice would compare the two options at the point of decision. If she is at an intersection, she has no reason to think she is at one rather than the other, so exiting is worth (1/2)(0) + (1/2)(6) = 3, whereas continuing is worth only the 1 that the journey yields if she continues at both. So act-by-act reasoning says: exit. It says this at both intersections, which means that a driver who follows it exits at the first and collects 0 — the worst outcome in the problem. The act-level recommendation thus prescribes a policy worse than any alternative, which shows that the two choices cannot be assessed separately: what exiting at one intersection is worth depends on what she does at the other. This is the structure of Newcomb's problem. The driver's choice is correlated with a state of affairs she cannot causally affect — whether the first intersection is already behind her — and the standard act-by-act approach goes wrong because it refuses to let that correlation, and the act's evidential bearing on that state, guide the choice. An analysis that evaluates whole policies rather than isolated acts gets the right answer here. (c) The puzzle shows that expected-utility maximization is not well defined at the level of individual acts when a single rule governs more than one decision point. The rational agent must maximize over strategies: she must adopt a rule of action and evaluate it as a whole, and when she does so the answer is determinate, 3/5 in this case. So the puzzle is not a counterexample to expected-utility theory but a constraint on the level at which the theory is applied; any account that insists on assessing each choice in isolation will be self-undermining in cases like this one.
