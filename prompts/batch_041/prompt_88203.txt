My younger brother is an infantry officer who has just come back from a deployment, and over the past few months our phone calls have turned into an argument that has now become my seminar paper. I am in my second year of law school and have just finished the module on the law of armed conflict; he has a serving soldier's impatience with what he calls civilian hand-wringing. His position is that the moral equality of combatants settles the matter. As long as a soldier obeys the rules -- does not target civilians, does not mistreat prisoners -- what he does is morally the same as what the man opposite him does, because the justice of the war is a question for the politicians who started it and not for the sergeant who has to fight it; my instinct that the soldier on the unjust side is doing something worse is, he says, a category mistake, a confusion of jus ad bellum with jus in bello. I half-accept the point about the two bodies of law, and yet the more I read the less I can see how the doctrine can be a moral doctrine at all rather than a rule of prudence for the law of war. If the moral equality of combatants is true, it seems to follow that the cause a soldier fights for makes no moral difference to what he does -- that the man defending his own country and the man taking part in a war of conquest are performing the same act, morally speaking, provided both shoot straight. And if that follows, then the doctrine either licenses aggression outright or, at best, tells us what we must tolerate because we cannot convene courts-martial across a battlefield. Nor does the other horn look any better to me: if the doctrine is false, then every soldier who fought in an aggressive war is a murderer, and the conscript handed a rifle at gunpoint is as guilty as the general who planned the campaign. I am also applying for a direct commission as a reserve legal officer, which is probably why this has stopped being an abstract question.

What I want to know is whether the moral equality of combatants is a claim about the permission to kill (each side may kill the other), about liability (each may be killed), about blame (neither side's soldiers are culpable for the war), or about all three at once -- and if it is true in the permission sense, whether anything at all follows about whether the soldier on the unjust side is doing something morally wrong. My brother insists that nothing follows, and he dismisses as armchair moralists the people he keeps seeing cited as revisionists, who hold that unjust combatants are not permitted to kill and that the doctrine is simply false. What I cannot work out is whether he and they are even answering the same question, or whether the phrase is being used in two different senses by the two sides, in which case the argument I have been having all summer may be verbal rather than real. Please tell me what the doctrine actually asserts, what it does not assert, and where the genuine disagreement lies once the equivocation, if there is one, has been cleared up.
