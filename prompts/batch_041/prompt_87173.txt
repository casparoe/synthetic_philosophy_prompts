For about two years now, three friends and I have been re-litigating the same argument, and I have started to suspect we are not disagreeing about the thing we think we are disagreeing about. It began with Hofstadter's 1983 Scientific American column on superrationality, later collected in Metamagical Themas, and specifically with two of the games in it. In the Platonia Dilemma, twenty people are each told that if exactly one of them sends in a telegram bearing only their own name, that person wins a billion dollars, and otherwise nobody wins anything; contact among the participants is forbidden and the winner is barred from sharing the money. In the Luring Lottery, readers were invited to send a postcard with a number on it, the prize being one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received and awarded to a randomly chosen entry, so that entering more times improves your odds of winning while shrinking what there is to win. Hofstadter's line is that a certain kind of reasoner, one who notices the symmetry of the situation and treats the other participants as reasoners who will reach the same conclusion, should not simply play the selfish best response; and, as I understand it, the answer usually given for the Platonia Dilemma is that each participant should send a telegram with probability one in twenty. My friends and I keep reaching the same two positions and never moving past them. One of us says this is the whole point: expected-utility maximization is incomplete, and the superrational reasoner is doing something real that the standard model cannot represent, namely acting on the fact that the other players are running the same deliberation rather than on any causal influence over them. The other says that is magic: my choice does not make anyone else's telegram arrive, and dressing up a correlation as a reason for action is exactly the mistake evidential decision theory makes and causal decision theory corrects. Hofstadter's own reply, as I read him, is that the word choice is doing illicit work here, because when you are trying to work out what the answer is the question is not what you will freely choose but what the correct answer to a symmetric problem is, and that reply strikes one of us as a genuine insight and the other as a sleight of hand.

Two further things have made the argument worse rather than better. First, the randomization. If the recommended play in the Platonia Dilemma is to send with probability one in twenty, then the twenty participants are running identical strategies but performing statistically independent acts, so the very correlation the theory was built on has evaporated; and if I have the arithmetic right, an agent whose nineteen counterparts each send with probability one in twenty would strictly prefer to send for certain, so the recommended profile is not even an equilibrium of the game. Is that a reductio of extending superrationality to mixed strategies, or does it show that the sameness superrationality needs is sameness of strategy rather than sameness of act? Second, I built a variant to find out where the theory actually lives: take the Platonia Dilemma but suppose the twenty participants differ, each one's susceptibility to the symmetry argument being publicly known to be some number between zero and one, drawn from a distribution everyone sees. Nothing about the situation seems to have changed in kind, yet superrationality now appears to have no verdict at all; it looks like it is defined only for perfectly symmetric problems and falls silent the moment there is any asymmetry, which is most of the cases anyone would care about. We also keep dragging in the fact that the lottery, when it was actually run, produced entries like a googolplex, making the prize worthless; half of us think this shows something and half think it is evidence about human psychology that could not bear on a question about what is rational.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I will offer my own hypothesis to be shot down. My hypothesis is that this is two questions wearing one name: the superrationalist is answering a design question, which procedure should agents like us settle on given that others like us will settle on it too, while the critic is answering a deliberative question, what should I do now given my credences about what the world will do, and these have different truth conditions, so both can be right. The alternative I take seriously is that the case is Newcomb's problem in disguise and that the impasse is inherited, since it turns on whether evidential or causal conditioning is the right notion of rational support, which is itself unsettled. A third possibility I cannot rule out is that the question is ill-posed, because the reasoner is asked to treat its own deliberation as both open and determined by the very reasoning under way. Which of these is right, and what would count as progress given the diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result about what superrationality is as a solution concept, a counterexample, or a demonstration that the dispute should be split into two disputes that can each be settled separately? I would also like you to say what the stalled argument looks like once the randomization puzzle and the asymmetric variant are taken seriously, since those are the places where I think the two sides are least able to talk to each other.
