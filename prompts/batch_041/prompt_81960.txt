I host a small philosophy podcast that tries to take one conceptual puzzle and push a guest past the encyclopedia-entry version of it, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a version of the procreation asymmetry that she calls the conditional-value asymmetry. The basic asymmetry, as I understand it, is the thought that failing to bring a happy person into existence is not bad in the way that bringing a miserable person into existence is bad. Her version adds a specific structure: the reason not to bring into existence a person whose life would contain more suffering than happiness is unconditional and deontic, whereas any reason to bring into existence a person whose life would be worth living is at most conditional on that person's own future endorsement of having been born, and is axiological rather than deontic. She also insists that the asymmetry is a thesis about the structure of reasons rather than about the value of states of affairs, and that it avoids the non-identity problem—the fact that the person whose life is at issue would not exist at all under the alternative course of action—because the relevant contrast is not between existence and non-existence but between two courses of action available to the prospective parent under uncertainty. She is known for deflecting objections with the reply that the objection, if sound, would prove too much for any view in population ethics. My co-host thinks the view collapses into anti-natalism, while I think it might be the only way to keep the asymmetry without accepting that we have no reason at all to create happy people; we would like the interview to adjudicate that disagreement.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the asymmetry. I want at least one question each on: (i) how the view distinguishes the axiological and deontic halves of the asymmetry, and whether that distinction can be maintained without covertly relying on a comparison between existence and non-existence; (ii) what the conditional-value clause actually adds, given that the future person cannot endorse anything before she exists, and whether the condition is best read as counterfactual, dispositional, or idealised; (iii) how the view handles the non-identity problem and the repugnant conclusion—the claim that a sufficiently large population of lives barely worth living would be ranked above a smaller population of flourishing lives—including whether it can avoid the latter without abandoning the intuition that a life barely worth living is still worth living; (iv) what the view implies for the practical question of whether to procreate, adopt, or remain childless, and whether it can make sense of the thought that parenting is a genuine good rather than merely a permissible project; and (v) what would count as evidence or argument against the asymmetry, given that its central claim is about reasons rather than empirical facts. For each question, include a follow-up that is keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, especially follow-ups that make her standard deflections harder to use.

The questions should be usable in a forty-five-minute conversation with a philosophically literate but non-specialist audience, so avoid asking for literature reviews or citations, and avoid questions that merely invite her to name her opponents. End with a closing question that asks her to state the strongest objection to her own view and to say what she would have to give up to answer it.
