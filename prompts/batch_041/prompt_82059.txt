I write novels, not philosophy, so if I've got the technicalities wrong just say so and move on. Here's the belief I've ended up with, and I want you to try to knock it down. Two people in exactly the same situation, both perfectly rational, cannot do different things. Theres nothing for them to differ on: same inputs, same reasoning, same output. Which means that when I'm up against someone who thinks the way I think, I'm not choosing my own move, I'm choosing the pair of moves, and (defect, defect) is worse for me than (cooperate, cooperate) -- so in a one-shot prisoners dilemma against a perfect copy of myself, the answer is cooperate, not out of niceness but because those are the only two outcomes on the menu. I believe Hofstadter came up with this in Metamagical Themas, though dont quote me on the source. The part I can't stop chewing on isnt the dilemma itself, it's the general claim underneath: that two people who reason alike are one agent wearing two hats, and that the right move is whatever is best for the pair.

Why it matters to me: the book I'm writing has identical twins who've never met, each hired for the same job, each deciding whether to sell the other out, and I built the whole plot on the assumption that they'll independently land on the same choice. Theres also an early scene where they play a game as kids -- each secretly picks one of two doors, same door and they both lose, different doors and they both win -- and I wrote them both picking the same door, because thats what two identical kids would do, and then I noticed that's also the worst possible result. I can't tell if that's a real counterexample or just me being sloppy about what counts as the same situation.

So: what would count against the position? I already know the boring reply is "nobody's ever exactly alike," and my instinct is to answer "then it holds to the extent they're alike" -- but I suspect that's precisely where the trouble starts, so tell me honestly whether the view survives that or dissolves into a shrug, and whether it's really a claim about what to do or only about what to expect. And tell me what it predicts that its rivals dont, if anything.
