I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and the HCH construction has always struck me as the cleanest idealization of deliberation anyone has offered: a hard question gets answered by a tree of bounded subagents, each of whom may in turn consult further subagents, with the root's answer simply counting as the answer. The factored-cognition picture built on top of it is the natural companion — the hypothesis that a large cognitive task can be carved into subtasks that are each small enough for a bounded agent and, crucially, cheap for whoever called it to check. I have also spent about a decade running operations for a mid-sized firm, and I think about coordinated work in the vocabulary of organizational economics: division of labor, span of control, monitoring and verification costs, principal–agent problems, team production, and the make-or-buy decision. I would like the HCH and factored-cognition idealization restated in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can manage, followed by a residue report.

Do it in three passes. First, give the translation: map the parts of the construction onto their organizational counterparts as precisely as the material allows — decomposition onto division of labor, the caller's check on a subtask's output onto monitoring or audit, the recursive call onto delegation, the boundedness of each agent onto the bounded rationality of an employee or unit, amplification through depth onto whatever the closest organizational analogue is. Be explicit about which mappings are tight and which are loose, and resist the temptation to quietly substitute the target framework's assumptions for the original's. Second, report the residue: which features of the HCH picture have no counterpart in organizational vocabulary, and which features organizational economics insists on that HCH simply assumes away — I am especially interested in whether the assumption that a caller can cheaply verify a subtask's output corresponds to anything in the theory of the firm, and in what happens to the picture once you admit divergent objectives, private information, and costly monitoring. Third, adjudicate: for each significant piece of residue, say whether it marks a defect of the organizational vocabulary (something it cannot express) or a sign that the original idealization was less clear or less complete than it looked.

Close with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, which reading of the residue you take to be correct overall — the target vocabulary is missing something, or the HCH idealization is papering over something.
