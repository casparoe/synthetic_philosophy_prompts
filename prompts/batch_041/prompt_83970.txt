I run a monthly philosophy discussion group at a public library: a dozen or so adults, none of them trained philosophers, though most have sat through a few earlier sessions with me, so they can follow the intrinsic/instrumental distinction when it is stated cleanly and they have a rough sense of what a theory of well-being is supposed to do. Next month I want to spend fifty minutes on tranquilism — the view that the only thing intrinsically good for a person is the absence of craving and suffering, and that pleasure, achievement, satisfaction, and everything else people usually list as goods matter at most instrumentally, as means to that absence or as features that happen to keep it in place. I have a whiteboard, no handouts, and an audience that will walk in assuming this is either a repackaged Buddhist doctrine or pessimism dressed up as ethics. Don't explain tranquilism to me; plan how I should explain it.

What I need is the sequence: which piece has to land before which other piece can even be stated, and why that order rather than another. Somewhere in there you will have to tell me what to do about the fact that the view has two clauses and they may not be independent — if craving just is a species of suffering, the second half of the claim is doing no work, and if craving is bad even when it does not hurt, the view owes an account of why, and my group will be asking for that account by minute twenty. So tell me whether to present craving as part of the suffering or as a separate bad, which choice leaves the view coherent, and where in the sequence that fork should surface. Then give me the single example that carries the whole session, the case I keep returning to, and say what it has to have and has to lack for it to do that work. Then the misconceptions, in the order they will actually show up. My guesses: they will hear it as hedonism with the sign flipped, as negative utilitarianism under a new name, as a claim about what we ought to do rather than about what is good for someone, and as a doctrine that forbids them to want anything. For each, tell me the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off and roughly what I should say. Finally, one question to put at the end that would genuinely distinguish a group that has grasped the view from a group that has merely agreed with it, and a short list of things I should explicitly refuse to take up in fifty minutes and announce as out of scope.

Two things. First, if the tranquilism I have sketched is not a coherent position — if the craving clause collapses into the suffering clause and leaves me with an empty second half, or if the absence of a bad is not the same as a good and the view therefore cannot be stated the way I have stated it — say so plainly and tell me what I would have to change, rather than politely planning a session around a muddle; I would rather find that out now than in front of twelve people. Second, write in American English, and do not send me to any readings, because my group does not read.
