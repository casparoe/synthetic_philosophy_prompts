On the clinical ethics committee I sit on, we spend a lot of time trying to put a number on one person's risk — a 58-year-old smoker with diabetes who lives next to a highway — and two of us will cite perfectly good data and land on 30% and 8%, one reading off the whole cohort, the other off the subgroup that matches him on everything we happen to know. Somebody then says "just use the narrowest group you have solid data for," and everyone nods, except that rule only does any work if the groups are nested; the factors we actually care about — age, sex, income, neighborhood, occupation, genotype — crisscross, so there's no single narrowest group and the rule quietly has nothing to say. So we fall back on "it's the factors that actually cause the thing," which just smuggles in a causal story from outside the numbers, and we don't agree on the story either. Half the time the group we settle on is just the one that suits whoever has to defend the call.
