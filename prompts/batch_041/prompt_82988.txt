I help run a film and book discussion group at my local library, and for the past year I have been turning over, on my own, a puzzle I cannot resolve: when a friend whose taste I trust tells me that a film is beautiful, I seem to come away with something less than what I would have had from seeing it myself, and some writers I have run across say that what I get from her is not knowledge at all — that a verdict about beauty cannot be handed over the way a fact about the weather can. Our next guest is a philosopher who defends exactly that skeptical position. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press him on the substance rather than let him restate the thesis, and give me a follow-up for each, keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I want at least one question on whether his target is the experience of the work or the justification for the verdict — that is, whether he means that I cannot get the experience secondhand or that I cannot get grounds for the judgment. I want at least one on a distinction of my own: between his telling me that a film is beautiful and his telling me that I will find it beautiful, since the second seems like something I can perfectly well learn from him. I want at least one on whether it matters how many people the testimony comes from — one trusted friend against a hundred strangers' ratings — and why we treat a consensus of critics as informative if a single verdict is inert. I want at least one that tests whether the position proves too much, since we routinely take other people's word about which wines are good and which books are worth the effort. End with a question he cannot wave away by saying that all of this is just what deference is, and order the sequence so that the pressure builds.
