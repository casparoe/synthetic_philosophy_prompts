I am putting together a paper on how false assumptions earn their keep in scientific modelling, and I want the specification before I take sides. Set out the desiderata that any adequate account of idealisation must satisfy: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it has to be consistent with. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. I am after something that could adjudicate disputes over whether a model's assumptions disqualify it and guide choices during model-building, not a descriptive sociology of what modellers happen to do. I am most interested in requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied: if the list turns out to be inconsistent, that is itself the result, so tell me which requirement is doing the excluding. A table for the desiderata is welcome.

A few stipulations, so that you work from my framing rather than a textbook one.

1. The account must cover deliberate distortion (frictionless planes, point masses, zero transaction costs) and omission (a model that simply leaves a factor out) under one heading without collapsing them. I suspect a good deal of the apparent disagreement in this area is really a failure to keep the two apart.

2. It must not appeal to the truth of the conclusions the model is used to reach. That is what the model is supposed to help us establish, so a criterion of the form "the idealisation is legitimate when the result survives relaxation" has to be cashed out without presupposing the answer.

3. It must accommodate the fact that incompatible models, resting on different and mutually inconsistent false assumptions, can be predictively successful at the same time, and that agreement between such models is treated as evidence for their shared result.

4. It must say what to make of the thesis Friedman is known for — the one Samuelson named the F-twist — that the significance of a theory varies with the unreality of its assumptions. Say whether an account of idealisation must accommodate that thesis, whether it is a heuristic for choosing between models, or whether it is a mistake. Do not report merely that it is contested.

5. It must apply to models used for intervention and extrapolation, not only to prediction within the range of the data.

6. It must be usable as guidance by a modeller who does not yet know which idealisations will turn out to matter. I suspect this sits badly with requirement 2, but do not take that on trust.

Then, briefly, how three sorts of account fare against the list: one that treats assumptions purely as instruments with no representational content, one that treats them as fictions we make-believe, and one that ties legitimacy to the preservation of whatever causal structure is relevant to the purpose at hand. Keep the weight on the requirements; the scoring is the least interesting part and I can do it myself. No reading list or citations, please — argue from the cases. Write in British English.
