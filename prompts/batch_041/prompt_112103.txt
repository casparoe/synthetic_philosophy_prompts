I serve on the governing council of my college, and on Thursday we vote on a proposal to strip President Truman's name from our largest scholarship, take his portrait down from the senior common room, and issue a short public statement explaining why. I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about it with an old friend who teaches moral philosophy, and I came away less sure of my own position than when we began. Before the meeting I have to circulate a written opinion to the other eleven members, and I would like you to draft it.

Some context you will need. Our draft policy on historical honours provides that an honour may be withdrawn where the conduct in question was condemned as gravely wrong by a substantial body of informed opinion at the time, and not merely by later generations, and it adds that reasonable disagreement among contemporaries should weigh against withdrawal. My friend's objection is that this test is not merely too strict but backwards, and that it mistakes the point of the protest Anscombe made when Oxford proposed to confer an honorary degree on Truman. On his reading, what she refused to treat as decisive was precisely the approval of the time: the question is whether the deaths of the civilians were intended as a means to ending the war or merely foreseen as a side-effect, and whether an appeal to lives saved is a justification at all rather than a pseudo-justification that assumes what it is supposed to prove. He then proposed replacing our test with this one: an honour may be withdrawn where the conduct can be defended only by treating the killing of non-combatants as a cost worth bearing for a greater good, since on the double-effect doctrine that is what marks those deaths out as intended rather than foreseen.

I am not sure he is right, and I am fairly sure he was too cheerful about where his test leads, since applied consistently it would empty our honour roll: almost any statesman who has ever fought a war can be described in those terms. But I do not want the memo to be a defence of my own instinct, and I do not want it to become a general survey of the just war tradition either.

There is one further wrinkle. A colleague wants us to commission a historian's report before we vote: on whether the bombings actually shortened the war, what the projected casualties of an invasion were, and whether a surrender was imminent in any case. My friend says commissioning it is a category error and a way of postponing a decision we already have the materials to make. I cannot see my way through that objection, and the council will probably vote on the report before it votes on the scholarship, so the opinion needs to take a position on it.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a specific course of action, with a timetable, and where the policy's wording needs to change, the amended wording I can put to the vote. It should make my friend's argument as strong as it can honestly be made before saying whether it succeeds, and it should say plainly what we would be doing to the college's other honours if we accepted his test. Roughly nine hundred words, in British English, and self-contained: no footnotes or reading list.
