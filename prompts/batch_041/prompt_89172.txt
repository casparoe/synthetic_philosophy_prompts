I sit on the research committee of a foundation that supports work at the boundary of the natural sciences and history, and I am the only physical scientist on it. I retired four years ago from a career in tephrochronology and ice-core chemistry — most of it spent trying to establish when particular eruptions occurred and how much sulfur they put into the stratosphere — and I have since been reading philosophy on my own, mainly on causation and the philosophy of history, with no formal training. Next month the committee must make one large award, and I need the memo I will circulate before the vote.

The two finalists have put us in a bind. The first, from a geoscience consortium, would spend the money on a high-resolution multi-proxy program — polar sulfate records, tree-ring width and isotope series, and two varved lake sequences — to fix the timing, magnitude, and spatial footprint of a volcanic cooling episode in the sixth century, and then to date a set of archaeological and documentary discontinuities against it. The second, from a group of historians with one archaeologist, would spend the money in archives and in the field, reconstructing grain policy, taxation, land tenure, and relief institutions in the same region over the same decades, on the argument that the discontinuities are explained by institutional fragility rather than by the eruption. Each side's referees say, in effect, that the other cannot identify a cause at all: the geoscientists say the historians will produce a vivid description with no independent variable; the historians say the geoscientists will produce a well-dated coincidence and call it a cause. We cannot fund both at full scale, and I do not believe we can honestly fund either as written.

Please draft the memo: a recommendation the committee can act on — fund one proposal, fund a redesigned joint project with conditions, or decline both — with the reasoning spelled out, the conditions we should attach if any, and a serious statement of the strongest case for whichever option I will be voting against. I do not need a reading list; I need the argument.

There is a second thing I want the memo to settle, because it is the reason I keep hesitating. I have been trying to work out a distinction that may or may not be real. A volcanic or climatic event can enter an explanation as a trigger, as an amplifier of a vulnerability that already existed, or as an alibi — a device for moving the moral and political weight of a disaster off the people who made decisions. My suspicion is that the third is not a causal category at all but a rhetorical one; my counter-suspicion is that this is too clean, because whether amplification shades into alibi seems to depend on what the decision-makers could have known and done at the time. Please take a position on how the funded work should be required to frame causation, and say whether the alibi worry belongs in the grant conditions or only in the public summary.

Write in American English. At the end, tell me what would change your recommendation — which argument, finding, or piece of evidence would move you to a different one — and where in your own reasoning you would expect it to fail first.
