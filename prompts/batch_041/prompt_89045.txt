My dissertation supervisor died in March, and the department has asked me to write the memorial essay for this year's review — the kind of piece that is read aloud to the people who knew him and then filed with the annual reports. To work out what such a piece can honestly do, I have been rereading Plutarch's Lives alongside a shelf of saints' lives, and the exercise has turned up a cluster of convictions I appear to have been carrying around without ever checking whether they fit together.

(1) Moral understanding is carried by particulars rather than by rules: one learns how to live by dwelling on a life, and any general lesson one extracts is thin next to the case it came from.
(2) A life instructs only when it is told truthfully, flaws included — what a reader can actually use is the record of how someone handled his own weaknesses, and a smoothed-over portrait deletes precisely that.
(3) The memorial essay is a genre that idealizes by nature: one selects, one softens, one does not speak ill of the dead. I take this to be the form's job rather than its corruption, and I have been telling myself that a tribute owes sincerity where an obituary owes accuracy.
(4) We become better by imitating exemplars: admiration is not a spectator emotion but a practical one, and to admire someone just is to be disposed to become like them.
(5) All the same, the people I admire most are ones whose lives I could not possibly imitate — a vocation I do not share, a steadiness whose occasion will never arise for me.
(6) And I would never take someone's having done a thing as a reason to do it myself; that is an appeal to authority, and it is no better in ethics than anywhere else.

The practical question that set all this off is whether to write about the thing everyone in the department knows — that for a stretch of years he was savage to the students who could not keep up — and I cannot tell whether omitting it would be a lie or merely the genre doing its job.

Give me a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, and nothing else: no introduction, no closing paragraph. Each item should take up one of the joints above — a pair that pulls apart, or a single conviction the others will not let me keep — and say whether the conflict is real or only apparent, how much it costs me, and the cheapest way out. Order them by how much I would have to give up, worst first.
