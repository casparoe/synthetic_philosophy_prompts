The draft goes something like this. The usual way of telling the story is that Hobbes was a great political philosopher who had a bizarre and embarrassing hobbyhorse, squaring the circle, and that John Wallis properly humiliated him in the 1650s, and that the whole episode shows Hobbes was out of his depth in mathematics. I want to argue that this is too quick. Hobbes's geometry is not just failed mathematics; it's a worked-out, if ultimately unsuccessful, philosophy of what makes a science demonstrative. For Hobbes, geometry is demonstrable because we make the figures ourselves. The definitions are genetic or causal: we understand a circle by understanding the motion that generates it, and we understand a line by the motion of a point. This is the same model he uses for civil philosophy: we make the commonwealth, so we can know it demonstratively in a way we can't know natural bodies, which God made. So geometry and politics are the two sciences where the maker's knowledge model applies. The twist I want to develop is that Wallis is not just a better mathematician but a representative of a rival epistemology of mathematics. Wallis's algebra is symbolic and manipulative; it doesn't require you to construct the object in intuition, it requires you to follow rules for signs. Hobbes sees this as a return of scholasticism, a way of talking without knowing what you're talking about. So the quarrel is about what counts as a demonstration, not just about who can square the circle. Then I want to say that Hobbes's constructivism has a real tension. If geometrical knowledge is knowledge of what we make, then it's knowledge of our own artifacts, not of independent reality. But Hobbes is a materialist who thinks geometry is about bodies and motion. So is he saying we make real bodies when we draw a circle? Or is he equivocating between constructing a figure on paper and constructing the object in thought? And does his refusal of algebra actually follow from his constructivism, or is it just a prejudice that makes his own proofs fail? I think the failure of his squaring attempts is probly overdetermined: he refuses algebraic methods, he relies on diagrams in ways that smuggle in assumptions, and his kinematic definitions are too thin to constrain the constructions. So his epistemology of geometry doesn't save his geometry. But I want to claim that the episode is still philosophically interesting because it shows a coherent alternative to the algebraic revolution, and because the maker's knowledge model has a real problem that later constructivists also face.
