I have no training in philosophy; I have been reading on my own, and I would rather be corrected than handed the reading I am about to ask for. The work I want read is the television series The Good Place. The idea I want it read against is the dispute between moral particularism and moral generalism. As I understand it, the generalist holds that there are principles which tell us, in advance, which features of a situation count as moral reasons and how much they weigh — that the fact a promise would be broken is a reason against an act, and that this holds as a matter of general moral truth and not merely of local circumstances. The particularist denies it: the same feature can be a reason for an act in one case, a reason against it in another, and no reason at all in a third, so no finite set of principles could capture what matters. I take that to be the position associated with Jonathan Dancy, though I have not read him.

The show is built on the generalist's dream. Everyone who dies receives a numerical score computed from everything they did, and the score decides their eternity; the afterlife has an accounting department and a head accountant; one character, Doug Forcett, has spent his whole life trying to earn points; another, Mindy St. Claire, ended up in a Medium Place of her own because her life came out exactly on the line. Later the show reveals that nobody has been admitted to the Good Place for centuries, and, as I remember it, the accountants explain that the system has become unworkable because modern life is so tangled that almost any act is caught up in consequences and institutions the person could not have known about. If I have that explanation wrong, say so and work with what the show actually does.

What I want to know is this. Is the show dramatizing particularism — the claim that the moral character of an act is not fixed by general rules but shifts with the case, so that a scoring function is not merely impractical but impossible in principle? Or is it making only the weaker, epistemic point that we cannot know enough to apply the rules, which would leave generalism intact and merely add that better information is needed? These seem to me very different claims, and I cannot tell which one the show is making, or whether it notices the difference. Give me a reading that takes both the show and the dispute seriously: where the show genuinely illuminates the particularist case, where it trades on the confusion between having no principles and being unable to apply them, and what the show manages to show that an argument on its own could not.

Two smaller things I have been chewing on, which you should tell me are confused if they are. First, Chidi, the ethics professor who cannot choose anything, looks to me like the show's picture of generalism lived out — a man who believes morality is a procedure he must get right before he is allowed to act — and Eleanor looks like the opposite, someone who improvises case by case and improves. Is that fair to the two of them, or does it flatten what the show is doing? Second, if the show's answer really is that each situation must be judged on its own terms, does it have anything to say about two people who judge the same case differently and neither of whom is obviously corrupt? Particularism has always struck me as risking leaving us there, and I want to know whether the show offers a response or only a warm feeling.

If particularism and generalism are the wrong categories for what the show is up to, or if my way of drawing the distinction is muddled, say so plainly at the start rather than building the reading I asked for.

Answer in under 800 words, and begin with one sentence stating your overall verdict on what the show is claiming.
