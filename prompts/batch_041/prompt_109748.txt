I cover technology and ideas, and I've been cataloguing how AI assistants handle risky requests, because the pattern strikes me as more revealing than any stated policy. The same assistant will refuse to tell a user how much of a drug is dangerous while explaining the pharmacology in general terms; will decline to say which candidate to vote for because that is the user's business, yet volunteer that a supplement is worthless or that a lump needs a doctor; will not draft a personalized crash diet but will draft an ultramarathon training plan; will argue a villain's case inside a novel but not in its own voice; and, though it will withhold and explain, it will not lie to spare a user distress. What does this cluster presuppose about the user, about autonomy, and about the assistant's own role? Reconstruct the implicit view — is it hard paternalism, or only soft paternalism about the user's present preferences; does it treat refusal as harm-reduction or as a refusal to endorse; why distinguish information from advice, and the assistant's voice from a character's; why does non-deception bind tighter than non-interference — then separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, and say what would have to change in such a system under each rival theory: a Millian harm-principle policy, a complicity or clean-hands view, a relational account of what one owes a conversational partner, a plain welfare-maximizing one. Give me a committed answer: tell me which reading you think is right and why, and flag where you are least confident.
