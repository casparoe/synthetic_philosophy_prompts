I'm a novelist three books in, applying to a year-long residential fellowship whose selection committee is mostly historians and philosophers; the application asks for a short proposal, and I would rather it survive their scrutiny than mine. My half-formed idea: the novel I want to write turns on one catastrophe with three causes that never meet — a harvest failure, a betrayal, and an order given by an official too far away to know about either — and I want the reader to feel the outcome as inevitable while also seeing that striking out any single cause would leave it standing. I do not currently know what I am asserting when I say that, and I suspect the muddle is not mine alone. Accounts of multi-causal events seem to run together at least three different claims: that several conditions were each indispensable and only jointly sufficient; that several conditions would each on its own have sufficed, so that none of them made a difference; and that one cause did the work while the others only raised the odds. If the vocabulary of overdetermination is quietly doing all three jobs, that would explain why arguments about whether an event was bound to happen get nowhere. I want the proposal to ask a question with a determinate answer — something like: when we explain an event by citing redundant causes, what do we gain over citing a single cause plus a disposition, and can that gain be stated without leaning on counterfactuals nobody can evaluate? A second thread I want held together with the first: whether narrative has a distinctively explanatory form of its own, since a narrator knows the outcome and an agent inside the event does not, and it is not obvious that presenting causes in an order and at a tempo does the same work as listing conditions.

Write the proposal in five sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: The Question (200 words, stated so that a determinate answer is possible in principle); Why the Standing Answers Leave It Open (300 words, positions rather than works); Method and Why This Method (400 words — what I would actually read and write, month by month, and why this beats both doing the history empirically and simply drafting the novel to see what happens); Deliverables and What Would Count as Failure (250 words, including what the novel gains that a paper would not); Scope and the Case for a Novelist (200 words, honest about what will not get done in a year, and answering the committee member who asks why this is not a private matter of craft).
