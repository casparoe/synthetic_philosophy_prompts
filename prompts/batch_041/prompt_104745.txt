My old sociology professor used to boil down the whole individualism-vs-holism thing like this, and I've been repeating it for years — now a friend in a philosphy of social science seminar says I'm parroting something confused, but she wouldn't say exactly where. Here's the gist of what he'd say:

Methodological individualism is the view that social facts are really just facts about individual people — their beliefs, wants, and choices — so any real explanation in social science has to be cashed out in terms of individual psychology, and all the talk about classes, norms, and markets is just shorthand for lots of individual actions. Holism is the opposite: societies are real things over and above the individuals in them, with their own properties and even their own interests, so you explain a social fact by pointing to other social facts. Durkheim on suicide rates is the poster child — he showed suicide is a social fact with social causes, which refutes individualism — and Weber's the individualist, Durkheim the holist. And since you can't observe a society, only people, holism isn't really science.

He'd also toss in a test he called the "independent contribution test": a macro-level factor (say the unemployment rate) counts as a genuine cause in its own right only if it still predicts the outcome after you've controlled for the individual-level stuff — if it doesn't, then it's just an aggregate of individual choices and belongs on the individualist side.

Go through it point by point and tell me what a careful person in the field would sign off on, what's a standard simplification rather than an error, and what's straight up wrong — and tell me what's actually at stake in the debate, because I suspect his test isn't even aimed at the real disagreement. The suicide example especially: I'd like to know whether it does the work he thinks it does.
