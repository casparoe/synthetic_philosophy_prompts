I'm giving the farewell remarks at next month's annual meeting of our local astronomical society, where I'm stepping down after nineteen years running the volunteer telescope nights. The room will be about forty people: roughly half are longtime members who know every inch of the society's history and equipment, and half joined in the past year or two, mostly because a neighbor dragged them out to look at Saturn once and they stayed. I'm seventy-six. My knees have stopped negotiating, I can't stand in the cold past eleven anymore, and for the last two winters I've been the one who unlocks the equipment cabinet and then sits in the car. I want the speech to be honest about all of that without either pretending it's nothing or turning into a list of complaints, and I want it to say something real to the new members who will be holding the cabinet key by this time next year.

I've been rereading Cicero's dialogue on old age, in which Cato the Elder answers the usual charges against it one at a time — that it takes you out of active life, that it weakens the body, that it strips away pleasures, that it stands too near death. The trouble is that most of Cato's answers quietly assume a life shaped like his own: consulships, commands, a farm in the country, a reputation to defend. Please write the remarks for me. Let those four charges be the actual spine of the speech — I want the audience to feel its shape even if they've never heard of Cicero — but have me answer them from the position of someone whose working life was small, repetitive, and mostly spent in the dark with strangers, and let the handing over of the key be a genuine moment rather than a sentimental one. It has to land for the old members who will recognize the arguments and for the new ones who mainly want to know why they should keep showing up. One Latin phrase at most, and only if it earns its place.
