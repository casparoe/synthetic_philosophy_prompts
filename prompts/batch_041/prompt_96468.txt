I have been reading a short popular introduction to Buddhist philosophy, and its chapter on Nāgārjuna offers the following summary of his position, which I would like audited:

"Buddhism's most radical idea is Nāgārjuna's emptiness, and its engine is the tetralemma, the four corners. For any claim about how things really are, there are four options: it holds, it does not hold, it both holds and does not hold, and it neither holds nor fails to hold. Nāgārjuna turns this fourfold analysis on causation, on motion, on the self, and on nirvāṇa, and in every case the four options come apart, which shows that these concepts simply do not fit reality as it is in itself. That is what emptiness means: things are empty (śūnya) of svabhāva, or own-being, because they exist only in dependence on other things, so nothing has a nature that is its own. Emptiness is not a further thing lurking behind ordinary things; it is just the fact that they are dependently arisen. And the fourth corner is where Nāgārjuna himself ends up: the ultimate is neither existent nor nonexistent, which is why he treats it as ineffable, beyond what concepts can capture. So the tetralemma is less a logic than a therapy, a way of dissolving metaphysical questions rather than answering them. The two truths fall out of this: conventionally, tables and selves exist and our ordinary claims about them are true; ultimately they do not exist, and emptiness is the only thing that is really the case. Critics charge that this is nihilism in disguise, but Nāgārjuna's answer is that because things are empty, change, causation, and ethics are possible — emptiness is what makes the world work, not what destroys it."

My suspicion, which I would like you to assess rather than assume, is that this is two incompatible readings stitched together: a therapeutic or quietist reading, on which the tetralemma dissolves questions and no horn is ever endorsed, and a mystical reading on which Nāgārjuna does land on the fourth horn and treats emptiness as an ineffable ultimate. Please go through the summary claim by claim and sort the sentences into (a) accurate as stated, (b) common simplifications that a specialist would tolerate in a popular book, and (c) things Nāgārjuna or his defenders would deny outright — and for each item say briefly what a defender would put in its place.

I also want you to address four points that the summary raises without noticing:

1. Whether calling the tetralemma a "logic" is defensible, and specifically whether the comparison often drawn to modern paraconsistent or four-valued logics, in which the third and fourth horns get assigned nonclassical truth values, captures what Nāgārjuna is doing or distorts it.
2. Whether the fourth horn, "neither," can be a positive thesis about an ineffable reality at all, given the standard insistence that emptiness is not a thing with a nature of its own, and how a defender handles the self-application worry: if the analysis dissolves all views, what becomes of the view that all views are to be dissolved?
3. Whether "the two truths" is best read as two realms or two levels of reality, or instead as two ways of speaking and of assessing claims, and what turns on the difference for the summary's claim that ordinary things "ultimately do not exist."
4. Whether the closing move — that emptiness is what makes change, causation, and ethics possible — follows from the rest of the summary as the book presents it, or whether the book has smuggled in a premise that its own reading of emptiness would forbid.
