You are a philosopher of mind with a specialist's command of the metaphysics of causation and the mental-causation debate. Work the problem through informally before you commit to anything, then discard that scaffolding and give me only the finished product. Your entire reply must be exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction, no headings, and no closing remark. Do not cite, name, or gesture at any literature, and do not rest anything on what is commonly said in the debate; every verdict has to be derived from the case in front of you.

The job is to construct a thought experiment for a seminar on the causal exclusion argument. I want a case in which the argument's premises are all visibly in place except the one that puts the mental cause and its physical realizer in competition for the same causal work, and I want that gap to fall out of an ordinary feature of the situation rather than being stipulated. Specifications:

- An everyday human case of mental causation with an ordinary physical causal history: no indeterminism, no quantum talk, no disembodied minds, no brains in vats, no demons, and no neural detail beyond what a layperson would describe.
- The effect occurs, and the mental cause is one we would ordinarily say made a difference to whether it occurred.
- The mental cause's actual physical realizer is not itself something whose absence would have made a difference to the effect, because an alternative realizer of the same mental state was available and would have produced the effect anyway. The alternative must be ordinary and physically respectable, not a science-fictional backup.
- Supervenience, the non-identity of the mental with its realizer, and physical causal closure all remain true and must be stated explicitly.
- The case itself must be no longer than two sentences.

A case that simply declares the mental property to be the cause, or that builds the desired verdict into the setup by fiat, is a failure; the verdicts have to come out of the described structure.

Then deliver the following as the five numbered items:

1. The case.
2. The verdict the exclusion argument returns here, and the exact step at which it gets that verdict.
3. The verdict a non-reductive physicalist returns if she holds a difference-making account of causation rather than a sufficiency account, and the step of the exclusion argument she must reject to get there.
4. The one feature of the case that is doing all the work, and what happens to each verdict if that feature is deleted, that is, if the actual realizer were the only possible realizer of the mental state.
5. The strongest objection available to an exclusionist who thinks the case is rigged, and the single further question about the nature of causation that would actually settle the dispute.
