The question was: "Searle distinguishes original from derived intentionality. Explain the distinction and what work it does for him. Then say whether Dennett's two-bitser example should be read as supporting that distinction, undermining it, or as simply not addressed to it, and defend your answer." Here's what the student wrote:

Searle's distinction is between the kind of aboutness that mental states have on their own and the kind that things like words, maps, and computer states have only because someone uses them to stand for something. My thought about my friend's birthday is about her all by itself; the word "birthday" printed on a calendar is only about her because we mean it that way. Searle calls the first kind original and the second kind derived, and the point of the distinction is to block the claim that a computer could think just by running the right program: a program manipulates symbols, and symbols have at best derived intentionality, borrowed from whoever wrote or reads them. This is the moral of the Chinese Room — the man shuffling characters in the room does not thereby understand Chinese, and no amount of syntactic processing adds up to semantics. Searle's positive view is that original intentionality is a biological phenomenon: only systems with the right causal powers, i.e. brains, can produce it, which is why he thinks strong AI is impossible in principle.

Dennett's two-bitser is a vending machine built to detect US quarters, which later gets shipped to a country that uses coins of the same size and weight, so it keeps working. Dennett asks whether its internal state now means "quarter," or "local coin," or neither. His answer is that there is a fact of the matter, but it is fixed by the machine's design history and by how it is actually used — that's why the example is supposed to show that the machine's intentionality is derived, inherited from its makers and users, whereas our own mental states are original.

So the example supports Searle's distinction rather than undermining it, and the disagreement between them is really only about how wide the derived category is: Searle thinks computers can never cross over into original intentionality, while Dennett is more optimistic that a suitably organized system could, which is why he's willing to take the intentional stance toward machines.
