I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere and its various spin-off forums for the better part of a decade now, and I've noticed that my views about belief attribution to AI systems have been quietly assembled from sources that probably don't agree with each other. Before I write any of it up for the discussion group I run, I want a consistency audit. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) I take the intentional stance seriously in Dennett's sense: to attribute a belief is to adopt a predictive strategy toward a system, and the question of what it really believes, over and above what the stance lets you anticipate, is either empty or at least not the question we should care about. (2) I think it is both permissible and correct to say that a current large language model believes things — that when it answers a question in a way that reliably tracks the facts, and revises in ways that look like revision, it holds the belief its answer states, at least in the dispositional sense in which we attribute beliefs to people who aren't currently thinking about the matter. (3) I think there is nonetheless a determinate fact of the matter about what a given model believes at a given moment, the sort of fact that mechanistic interpretability could in principle get right or wrong, and that this fact does not depend on our choosing to adopt any stance. (4) I think believing is something only an agent can do: a state earns the name only if it belongs to a system with ends of its own, whose doings can be explained and assessed for coherence, not merely predicted. (5) I think the mechanistic story — what the weights are doing — is the more fundamental one, and that belief-talk is a useful overlay rather than a level at which anything further is true. (6) I'm allergic to anthropomorphizing, and I think anyone who describes a model as happy or worried, or as wanting things the way a person does, has made a straightforward mistake.

My suspicion is that I cannot have all six, and that the seams are not where I would naturally look for them. So what I want is an audit rather than a lecture: which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only appear to conflict and dissolve once you are careful about the terms, and for each genuine conflict, the cheapest revision and what it would cost me. Rank the real tensions by severity, tell me which single commitment I could drop to clean up the most, and be specific about which premise is doing the work in each case rather than gesturing at the general difficulty.

One further thing. Please keep the epistemic status of your claims visible: distinguish the parts of this that are settled among people who actually work on the intentional stance and on the cognition of language models from the parts that are live and contested, and do not hand me a contested reading as though it were the consensus. If a tension I've listed is really only a disagreement between two camps rather than an inconsistency inside my own set, say so, and if you find yourself unsure whether the literature has settled something, flag that rather than resolving it for me.
