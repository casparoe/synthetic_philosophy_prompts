I am assembling teaching materials on the ethics of deception, and I would like a piece of sustained parody written in the register of a genuine corporate document. Write the internal style guide of the communications office of an invented organisation — a mid-sized company, trade association, or public-affairs consultancy, plainly fictional and not modelled on any real firm — governing how its staff may speak to journalists, regulators, and customers. Approximately 900 to 1100 words, in numbered sections with headings, in flat institutional prose, with no narrator, no winking, and no asides to the reader. The document must believe in itself completely; the comedy should come from the gap between the absolute rule it announces and the permissions it then constructs, and from the reader's recognition that such a document could exist.

The governing fiction is that the organisation does not lie. It defines lying narrowly — a statement that is false, made with the intention to deceive — and permits everything that definition does not reach. The manual should then become, without ever admitting it, an exhaustive practical guide to deceiving people without lying.

Build it out of the real distinctions rather than vague gestures at them: what a sentence asserts as against what it merely implicates; what it presupposes; the true but uninformative answer that violates the maxim of quantity; the truthful denial aimed at a proposition the questioner never asked; the manoeuvre sometimes called paltering, in which one answers a different question truthfully; silence, omission, and the belief the audience arrives at unaided. Include at least:

(a) a definitions section, where the narrow definition of lying is set out and defended as a matter of precision rather than convenience;
(b) an approved-practices section, each entry with a worked example of an exchange between a staff member and a questioner, all examples invented;
(c) a very short prohibited section, most of whose prohibitions turn out to concern tone, timing, and unauthorised speakers rather than truth;
(d) a ladder of denials, ranked from weakest to strongest, with the conditions under which each rung may be used — the rungs should differ genuinely in what they commit the speaker to;
(e) an FAQ resolving at least these cases: whether a true statement carrying a false presupposition is a lie; whether silence can be a lie; whether one may describe an action in terms that are true of it only under an unusual description; and what to do when a questioner asks directly whether one has lied;
(f) a closing note from the organisation's legal adviser or chief executive, still in character, that lets the reader see the price of the policy.

Two constraints on the reasoning inside the document. First, it must be internally consistent: no rule may contradict another, and wherever the guide is self-serving it must be self-serving in a way its own stated principles can support. Second, its logic should be allowed to arrive at a place the reader recoils from — for instance, a stated preference for the form of deception that leaves the deceived party no recourse, justified by the organisation's exposure rather than its honesty.

Do not quote or cite any real text, philosopher, statute, or case, and do not name real people or companies; the distinctions should read as the organisation's own hard-won common sense, as though it had discovered them for itself. Do not supply references.

Someone who works on the philosophy of lying and misleading should find the distinctions handled correctly and the satire fair: the guide must not conflate the intention to deceive with falsity, must not treat every implicature as a lie, and should make visible the uncomfortable possibility that its own standard forbids the detectable deceptions while licensing the effective ones. A reader who knows nothing of the literature should still be able to follow it and laugh. By the end, a reader who began by thinking the difference between lying and misleading is a technicality should feel the force of the question whether it marks any moral difference at all — though the document itself must never concede the point.
