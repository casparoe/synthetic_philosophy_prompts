In July 2011 Anders Breivik killed 77 people in Oslo and on Utøya, and the Norwegian response has become a familiar talking point, so take the following as given: the ordinary maximum sentence in Norwegian law is 21 years; the psychiatric evidence split and he was found criminally responsible; he received 21 years of preventive detention (forvaring) with a minimum term of 10 years, extendable indefinitely in five-year increments; and in the years since he has repeatedly sued the state over the isolation of his confinement rather than over its severity. Treat this as a case study in the two standard theories of punishment, and be disciplined about the mapping rather than rehearsing the usual commentary.

Begin with the retributive reading. Who is the punisher, who actually holds the desert claim (the state, the victims' families, the offender himself, and do they agree?), and what exactly was deserved? Distinguish positive retributivism — desert as a positive reason to inflict hard treatment — from negative retributivism — desert as a floor and ceiling on what may be inflicted for other reasons — and say which, if either, the Norwegian sentence satisfies. Then run the consequentialist ledger: what the sentence bought in incapacitation, deterrence, and rehabilitation, what it cost, and which of those goods the indefinite extension is really serving.

Then say where each theory stops being able to speak, addressing three questions in particular. First, is retributivism a theory of the amount of punishment, of the fact of punishment, or also of the conditions of confinement — does anything follow, on retributive grounds, about a three-cell suite versus a supermax? Second, if part of what punishment does is express the community's condemnation, what is denunciation supposed to accomplish when the person denounced denies that the community has standing to condemn him, and may in fact have wanted the condemnation? Third, is the Norwegian settlement best described as a retributive sentence with a consequentialist tail, or as a third thing — an incapacitative scheme, or a refusal of the exchange the offender was trying to force — that neither theory was built to describe?

Close with what the episode shows about the two theories: a confirmation of one, an anomaly for the other, or a case neither anticipated.
