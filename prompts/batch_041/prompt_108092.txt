Exam question: Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties are caused by physical processes but never themselves cause anything. Many philosophers think this makes it impossible to explain how we could ever know, or justifiably judge, anything about our own experiences. Set out the problem, and assess the most promising reply available to the epiphenomenalist.

Student's answer: Epiphenomenalism says that experiences are caused by brain processes but never cause anything themselves. The standard objection runs like this. If the stabbingness of my stabbing pain does no causal work, then it cannot be what causes me to judge that the pain is stabbing; something physical does that. So my judgement is not produced by the very feature it is a judgement about, and that makes it look like a coincidence that it should be about that feature rather than another one, or about nothing at all. If the connection is coincidental then it is not reliable, and if it is not reliable it cannot amount to knowledge, so the epiphenomenalist seems forced to say that we cannot know our own experiences — which is absurd, since introspection is meant to be the one place where we are on absolutely firm ground. This is a problem for epiphenomenalism specifically, and not for its rivals, because epiphenomenalism is the only one of the standard positions that denies mental causation outright. A dualist who lets the experience push the brain around gets the causal link back, and a physicalist who identifies the experience with the brain state has no gap to bridge in the first place. So the paradox is really an argument against one view rather than against dualism as such. The best reply, I think, is to give up the demand that the experience itself do the causing and appeal to reliability instead. What matters for justification is not that the quale produced the judgement but that the judgement is reliably correlated with the quale, and that is something the epiphenomenalist can grant. The quale is reliably correlated with the brain state that causes the judgement, so whenever that state occurs I get both the experience and the judgement about it, and the correlation is as tight as anyone could want. The quale is, you might say, the brain state as it appears from the inside, and the judgement tracks the brain state and thereby tracks the quale. There is then no mystery about how the judgement comes to be about the stabbingness rather than anything else: the stabbingness is what makes the difference to which judgement I form, in the sense that if the quale had been different then the correlated brain state would have been different and I would have judged differently. So I think the epiphenomenalist comes out of this fairly well. The paradox only bites if you insist on a causal theory of content and a causal theory of justification, and neither of those is compulsory. Once reliability is allowed to do the work, the epiphenomenalist can keep the experiences, keep the judgements and keep the correlation between them, and the thought that we could never know our own minds turns out to rest on a picture of knowledge that no one has to accept.
