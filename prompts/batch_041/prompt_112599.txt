I am a science and ideas journalist, and three weeks from now I have to run a session at my newsroom's annual off-site for the general-assignment desk: a dozen reporters and two editors, all quick and well read but with no philosophy of science between them, ninety minutes, no slides, only a whiteboard and me. My brief is the question of whether the sciences carve nature at its joints, which for this room means whether the categories scientists work with — species, elements, diseases, minerals, ecosystems — answer to divisions that exist in the world or are simply the filing systems we have found most useful so far. I do not want the explanation. I want the plan for delivering it: the order in which the ideas must arrive so that each is in place before the next one depends on it, why that order rather than the one that looks natural, and what has to be established before the question can even be heard as a question rather than as an obvious yes.

The case I want to carry the whole session is crabs, and I would like the plan built around it rather than around water and its molecular formula or gold and its atomic number. The crab form has evolved independently in at least five separate groups of decapods: king crabs descend from hermit-crab ancestors, porcelain crabs are closest to squat lobsters, and the true crabs are a different lineage again. The category is therefore a shape rather than a branch — it does not pick out a single clade — and yet it is plainly not arbitrary: the form recurs for reasons we can state, and a claim such as that crabs are common in intertidal zones is a generalisation that carries. Tell me what the example has to have, and what it must not have, in order to do this work; where it will break if I lean on it too hard; and what I should do at that point in the session.

My audience will arrive with, I expect, three false starts. They will take natural kind to mean the classification scientists currently happen to use. They will take the discovery that a category is not a natural kind to mean that it is therefore arbitrary, a convention, or worse, an imposition. And they will bring a folk essentialism: that behind any real category there sits some hidden property that makes each member what it is. I want to know where in the sequence each of those has to be headed off and how, and what further misconceptions I should expect that I have not listed, including the ones my own example is likely to induce.

By the end I want the room to hold one further thought that I do not know how to get them to without it sounding like a concession: that a single organism or substance can be classified correctly in more than one way, with the schemes answering different questions rather than competing for one truth — cladistics and functional ecology, say — so that pluralism does not collapse into anything-goes. Fit that into the arc if it can be fitted.

Finally: what I should leave out at this level, and how I should say that I am leaving it out without making the room feel cheated; and one question I can put to them at the end whose answers would tell me the distinction has landed rather than merely been heard.
