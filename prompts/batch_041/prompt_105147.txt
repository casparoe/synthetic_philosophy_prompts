Consider a variant of Scanlonian contractualism on which each person's objection to a principle is indexed to the stakes for that person: a complaint counts against a principle only if the loss it registers exceeds a threshold set by the context of choice, and below that threshold complaints may be aggregated across persons. My credence that this stakes-indexed variant resolves the aggregation problem without either making the threshold arbitrary or collapsing into a disguised form of utilitarianism is about 0.4. Give me your own credence in that proposition, decomposed into the sub-questions you take it to depend on: the probability that any threshold-based complaint model can avoid arbitrariness; the probability that stakes-indexing preserves the contractualist idea of justifiability to each person rather than smuggling in interpersonal comparisons; the probability that the model handles the single-person case, where one severe complaint must outweigh many small ones; the probability that the aggregation/non-aggregation line remains stable under changes in population size; the probability that the model can guide policy without a prior resolution of interpersonal utility comparisons; and any further term you think belongs. Give a number and one line of justification for each, show how they combine, and say which term your final number is most sensitive to and what case or argument would move it most.
