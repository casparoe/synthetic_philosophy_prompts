A few of us got into a long, slightly wine-fuelled argument at the weekend about etiquette abroad, and I never got a straight answer out of it. It started because my mate Dan — just back from a month in the States — was insisting he'd been morally obliged to tip 20% everywhere, and that stiffing a waiter wouldn't just have been rude but actually wrong; when I pushed him on why, he kept saying 'when in Rome, do as the Romans do', as if that settled the matter. Then someone pointed out that on the same trip he'd jaywalked all over the place, which is illegal there, so by his own slogan he should have been standing at the lights like a lemon at 2am; and a third friend said the slogan actually cuts against him, because in plenty of countries queueing isn't really a thing and you're meant to push in, so the visiting Roman should shove her way to the front as well. We went round in circles for about an hour and nobody could actually say what the sentence means, so I'd like you to seperate it out for me.

Take 'when in Rome, do as the Romans do' and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, from the trivially true to the seriously contentious. Is it a prediction about what will happen to you (you'll get funny looks), a piece of prudence, a claim about what's polite, or a claim about what you're genuinely morally required to do? Is the force of it that local conventions themselves create obligations for visitors, or that the obligations were always there and the convention is merely evidence of what they are, or something stronger — that in these everyday matters there's nothing more to the moral question than what the locals do? Does it cover only things like tipping and queueing, or does it extend to cases where the local custom is one we'd otherwise call morally appalling? And what happens when the local convention comes apart from the local law, which jaywalking seems to be exactly: does the slogan tell you to do what people actually do, or to obey the rules? I'd like a verdict on each reading — platitude, plausible-but-substantive, or plainly false — plus your best guess at which reading Dan was actually reaching for, and whether any of them really gets him his conclusion about the tip, or whether he's smuggling in something the slogan doesn't say. Lay it out as a menu I can forward to the group chat rather than picking one reading and defending it. Please write it in British English — we're in the UK and someone will definately start a fight if you say 'obligated'.
