I've been talking myself into the Keynesian picture of probability — that a probability is a logical relation between evidence and hypothesis, a degree of partial implication or degree of rational belief given the evidence, not just somebody's degree of confidence, and that some probabilities aren't even comparable with each other: there's no fact of the matter about whether one is more likely than the other, you just have to decide (he had the umbrella-in-ambiguous-weather case). Plus the extra dimension he calls the weight of the argument — how much evidence stands behind the judgment, which is separate from the probability itself. I like it because it lets economics say "this is genuinely uncertain" without pretending everything is 60/40.

So give me the case against it, taken seriously. Which arguments, results, or cases should actually move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be? I dont just want "Ramsey and de Finetti replaced all this with subjective degree of belief" — I want to know what actually bites. Does non-comparability survive contact with decision theory, or does any usable decision rule smuggle comparability back in? If the logical relations are supposed to be objective, where do they come from — doesn't the view need something like the principle of indifference, which he himself spent a chapter attacking? And has the position been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability: if two probabilities aren't comparable but you still act, in what sense was there no fact of the matter? Tell me what a Keynesian would themselves concede counts against them versus what they'd just wave away, and give me an honest verdict — including if that verdict is "basically dead as a theory of probability, survives only as a loose theory of uncertainty."
