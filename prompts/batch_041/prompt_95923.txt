For my school's philosophy club I have been asked to run next month's session on the social construction of kinds, and our discussions keep stalling in the same place: someone says that race, gender or money is socially constructed, someone else denies it, and it turns out that nobody has said what the phrase is supposed to mean or what would count as evidence either way. What I want from you is not an explanation of social construction but a dialogue I can hand out at the meeting, between two speakers who both know the territory well enough to be dangerous and who hold genuinely opposed views. The first, call her A, holds that there is a single relation at issue: a kind is social when it exists because of collective acceptance together with some rule or authoritative act of classification, and she thinks this one story covers money, racial classification and the categories recorded on a birth certificate alike — the differences among those cases are differences in which rule is doing the work, not differences in what sort of thing construction is. The second, B, agrees that all three are constructed but argues that the phrase names a family of quite different dependence relations, and that money and race pull apart in a way A's single-relation account cannot absorb: money, on his view, is propped up by codified rules and by an authority that could change them overnight, whereas racial categories are propped up by habits, statistics, institutional arrangements and self-reinforcing feedback loops that nobody authorised and that survive the repeal of the rules that once announced them — and, he adds, a kind can be produced by such processes even if no one accepts it, believes in it, or intended to produce it. Both should be at their best; I do not want a strawman on either side, and I would rather see A win an exchange cleanly than see B handed a victory.

There is one distinction I would like the dialogue to put to work, because I think it is where the real disagreement lives and it is not something my club has met before. Distinguish the question of what grounds a social fact — what makes it the case that this slip of paper is money, or that this person is a member of that racial category — from the question of what fixes the fact that those are the facts doing the grounding, which we could call the anchoring question. A should argue that the money-versus-race difference is a difference only in anchoring, so that nothing follows from it about whether the two kinds are constructed in different ways; B should argue that a difference in anchoring simply is a difference in the mode of construction, and that A's move is exactly what makes construction talk sound as though it has explained something when it has only relabelled it. Please give them a concrete case to fight over rather than letting them trade abstractions: a local scrip that no authority ever issued and no statute recognises, which two neighbouring towns nonetheless use to settle debts and pay wages, and which the tax office refuses to treat as money — A should say it is not money until some authority recognises it, and B should say the tax office's refusal is beside the point. Then have them carry the same dispute over to a case of racial classification and to the categories recorded on a birth certificate, including what happens when a person's own account of their category conflicts with the official record.

A few practical things. Do not have them cite or name anyone; I want the arguments themselves, not a bibliography, and neither of them should appeal to authority at any point. I would like at least two moments where a speaker has to give ground or narrow a claim in response to the other's actual point, rather than repeating the opening thesis in new words, and I want each of them to press the other on a case the other's account handles badly. Let it run long enough that the case gets worked through properly — four pages double-spaced would be about right. End with each of them saying, in a sentence or two, what they take the surviving disagreement to be: whether it is a disagreement about the facts of these cases, about what the words mean, or about what is at stake in calling something constructed in the first place.
