I spent forty years in a materials lab — solubility, corrosion, fracture toughness, flammability testing, that sort of thing — and I retired a few years back and finally had the time to read philosophy, which I'd always meant to get to. I've hit something I can't get passed and I'd like your help with it.

It's about the fact that we call things soluble, brittle, flammable, and mean by that something like: put it in the right circumstances and it will do the thing. I gather philosophers have been chewing on that conditional for a long time, and I've now run into the counterexamples that made them give up on the simple version, and I can't unsee them.

First one: a sugar cube sealed inside a thin plastic shell never dissolves, however long it sits in water. Everyone says it's still soluble, and the coating is only masking the disposition. Fine, I'll take that. But now the nasty one. Take a live wire — live meaning it would light a bulb if a conductor touched it. Wire it to a gadget that kills the current the instant anything conductive comes near, and it never lights anything, ever; its behaviour is exactly that of a dead wire. Apparently it's still supposed to count as live, because the disposition was there and the gadget just finked it. Then flip it around: a dead wire with a hidden battery in the circuit, which would light the bulb the moment you touched it, so it behaves for all the world like a live wire without being one. So we have dispositions that never manifest, and manifestations that come from no disposition at all. (I've seen the words finks, masks and mimics attached to these, though I honestly couldn't tell you which label goes where.)

What I'd like from you is the most important implications of all this — implications for me, for what I did with my working life, not a tour of the puzzles. Two things I keep chewing on:

One: if every failure of a material to behave as advertised can be chalked up to a mask or a fink, and every success can be a mimic, then does "this is soluble" have any testable content left? What is my standardized solubility test actually measuring, if the property and its test can come apart in both directions? I've seen the suggestion that a disposition needs an intrinsic base to be real — but couldnt the finking gadget be built into the wire itself? An internal fuse that blows the moment a conductor arrives seems just as good, and then the base isn't doing the work either. So is the base what I was really measuring all along, with solubility just a label on it, or does the disposition survive as something over and above its base?

Two: I rather like the idea, which I've seen floated, that the laws of nature are really about the dispositions of things rather than about regularities or brute necessity. What are the implications of the fink and mask business for that picture? If every disposition claim is hostage to an open-ended list of possible interferers, does dispositionalism about laws survive, or does it quietly turn into something else? And is the escape hatch of ceteris paribus laws a real fix or just a name for the problem?

Rank the implications by how much they should actually change a working scientist's practice or metaphysics, and be honest about where they stop. If the honest answer is that this matters a lot for how you phrase conditionals and barely at all for the chemistry, say so — I'd respect that more than a list of consequences you have to inflate to fill out. And don't send me to a library; I'm after the argument, not the citations.
