I retired four years ago from a career in chemical engineering — process design, and later a good deal of constrained optimization — and I have spent the time since working through moral philosophy at roughly the pace of a diligent undergraduate, which is to say slowly and with a great deal of underlining. I am applying for a small departmental fellowship that buys one semester of independent study, and I need a proposal of about two pages. My half-formed idea concerns what the literature I have been reading calls the paradox of deontology: a constraint forbids me to do the very act that would minimize the number of times that same constraint is violated, so the constraint appears to make the world worse by its own lights, and if it is not there to make the world better, it is hard to say what it is there for. What strikes me, coming from my old trade, is that this looks a great deal like a confusion between a constraint and an objective. In design work, the requirement that a vessel not exceed a certain internal pressure is not a competitor to the objective function and does not make the design worse in any well-defined sense; it removes points from the feasible set, and costs are then measured only among the points that remain. I have a suspicion, which I cannot yet distinguish from a pun on the word "worse," that the deontological constraint is meant to work the same way — as a rule of the activity rather than a term in the score — and that once one sees this, the paradox either dissolves or becomes a different and more interesting question about what deontology is a theory *of*. I would like to know which, and I would like the proposal to be honest that these are the two horns.

Please act as a professional philosopher working in normative ethics, with genuine command of the structure of deontological theories and of the decision-theoretic apparatus that people bring to bear on them, and begin by spending some space thinking through the terrain before you write anything: what exactly the paradox is a paradox *for*; which of the standard replies — constraints as side constraints on what one may do, constraints as expressions of agent-relative value, constraints as constitutive of a certain kind of agency, constraints as grounded in a distinction between doing and allowing — already absorb the engineering analogy and where each of them quietly relocates the difficulty rather than removing it; and whether my analogy is in fact a distinction without a difference, given that deontology does seem to care how many people are harmed, which a pure feasibility constraint would not. Think carefully about that last point especially, because it is the one I expect to be wrong about.

Then write the proposal itself, addressed to a fellowship committee of philosophers: a one-sentence statement of the question, sharp enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the existing positions leave it open, described by argument rather than by citation, since I will fill in the references myself and a proposal that depends on my getting an author and a year right is a proposal I will lose sleep over; the method, broken into something like a month-by-month plan for a single semester, saying what I would actually work through, what I would write, and — this is the part I most need help with — how I would tell at the end whether I had dissolved the paradox or merely redescribed it in my own vocabulary; the deliverable, which should be something a committee can recognize as a result rather than as a survey; and a candid paragraph on scope, including what would make the project too large for one semester and which part I should carve off and save for later. Finally, separately from the proposal and clearly marked as such, tell me the strongest objection to my framing — the one that would lead a referee to say the whole idea is a category mistake — so that I am not the last person in the room to hear it.
