My old coding bootcamp asked me to give the graduation speech at their end-of-cohort thing this year (I went through the program like 8 years ago, I'm an infra/backend engineer now, never taken a single philosophy class in my life), and instead of the usual "follow your passion, the future is wide open" speech I want to do it on determinism vs predictability -- the idea that a system can be 100% deterministic and still impossible to predict, and that unpredictable doesn't mean random or uncaused. I keep noticing people mash those together, including me until pretty recently, and honestly it seems like the most useful thing I could tell a room of people about to start their first jobs.

Audience: about 60 grads, plus parents and partners who are mostly not technical at all, plus a handful of instructors -- one of whom actually has a PhD in nonlinear dynamics and will definately notice if I say something dumb. So it has to land for my aunt and not make him wince. No jargon dumps, no lecturing.

Here's what the speech needs to actually get right, because the whole thing falls apart if it's hand-wavy:

- I want to use the butterfly/tornado thing but honestly. As I understand it a butterfly doesn't *cause* a tornado -- the point is that differences that small grow exponentially, so they change *which* storm you get, not whether storms happen at all, and the climate, the averages, don't budge. So "small things matter" is true in one sense and basically false in another, and I want that split to be the hinge of the whole speech.
- The part I find genuinely cool: because errors grow exponentially, better measurement doesn't buy you much -- doubling your precision buys you a fixed extra chunk of prediction time, not double the range. So long-range prediction of the details is off the table no matter how good the tech gets. I know the shape of this, not the math, so keep it at the level of the shape.
- But the averages survive. A chaotic system is still stuck on its attractor, so you can be hopeless at predicting any individual trajectory and still solid on the statistics. Related thing I half-remember: a simulation of a chaotic system is basically never on the true trajectory, but there's usually some real trajectory running alongside the simulated one for a good while -- which is the honest reason weather/climate-type models get trusted for the average and not for next Tuesday. If that's overstated, say so instead of dressing it up.
- Don't let me say chaos = randomness, or that chaos gives us free will, or that quantum randomness rescues free will, or that unpredictable means uncaused or means anything can happen.

Structural stuff: about 4 minutes spoken. The arc I want is cliche -> why it's wrong -> the accurate picture -> something genuinely encouraging for people starting their first job, without me having to claim anything false to get there. If the honest version makes the comforting part harder, don't paper over it -- keep the tension visible, and tell me at the end what the speech had to give up because of it.

Concrete image: I'd like to anchor the whole thing in a flaky test if you can make that work -- a test with no randomness in it anywhere that still fails 1 in 500 runs because tiny timing differences get amplified. That's the exact feeling I want people to walk out with. One more image at most, I dont want a pile of metaphors.

No names, no dates, no papers, no quotes from anybody -- I don't have any of that memorized and I'm not going to stand up there and pretend. Write it as something I'd actually say out loud.
