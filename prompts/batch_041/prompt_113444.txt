I keep getting into this argument and I keep going in circles, so before I read one more hot take I want the spec. Everyone argues about whether soldiers on both sides of a war are morally on a par by jumping straight to a verdict on one case -- the conscript, the volunteer, the guy defending his village -- and I want the requirements list first.

So: what would a theory of when it's permissible for one combatant to kill another actually have to get right? Give me the desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each one.

Things I want covered, at minimum:
- the cases it has to handle: the conscript who had no real choice, the volunteer in a war he's been lied into, the soldier on the other side doing the same thing for the same reasons, the one defending his own home while his side is the aggressor overall, the one who's stopped believing in the cause halfway through;
- the distinctions it can't just flatten: permissibility vs blameworthiness, being liable to attack vs deserving it, ad bellum vs in bello, combatant vs civilian;
- the results it can't contradict -- and here I mean both the moral results people are pretty confident about and the boring empirical ones about how wars are actually fought and who actually ends up in them;
- and whatever you think it owes to the weird cases that keep coming up now: contractors, drone operators a continent away, people fighting for a group that isn't a state at all.

One more constraint, and it's the one I actually care about. I'm helping a friend who does pre-deployment legal briefings -- twenty minutes, a room of nineteen-year-olds, and no way for her to know whether the war itself is just. So the theory has to be usable by someone who can't see the whole picture and can't wait for the philosophers to finish. Tell me what that requirement adds, and whether it can be met at the same time as the ones above.

Then the part I most want: which single requirement is doing the most work of excluding theories, and is there any pair of these requirements that can't be satisfied together? If the list turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, that's the real result and I'd rather hear it now. Also mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the house preference of one camp or another.

And please dont tell me the theory can just defer to what the law happens to say. I want the moral story -- though it does need to tell me what to do about the fact that the law is symmetric and the morality maybe isnt. A table is fine if it helps.
