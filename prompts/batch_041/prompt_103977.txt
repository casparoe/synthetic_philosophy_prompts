I'm a third-year philosophy student, and one of my modules this term concerns autonomy and the self over time. We've just spent two weeks on advance directives — whether a competent person's earlier instructions should bind the later person who has dementia and no longer endorses them, and whether such a directive is best understood as an exercise of autonomy or as a violation of it. The written assignment is open-format, and I would rather do a parody than another essay.

Write a mock terms-of-service agreement for Aevum, a subscription platform that stores, triggers, and administers advance directives on behalf of its users — a legal-and-ethical custodian of your future incompetent self. Format it as numbered clauses, with a short preamble and a closing "Frequently Asked Questions" section, running to roughly 700–800 words. It should be deadpan: no winking, no authorial nudging, nothing that reads as a joke on its surface. The comedy should come entirely from the fine print quietly taking positions on the real disagreements, so that a reader who has never opened a bioethics paper can follow it as absurd bureaucracy while someone who has will recognise which clause is which argument.

Please build the document around the following house rules of the fictional service and stay internally consistent with them:

- A Directive takes effect when Aevum's own reviewers determine that the subscriber has lost the capacity to revise it; the determination is made by the company, not by a clinician.
- The Earlier You and the Current You are treated as distinct account-holders for billing purposes, although only one of them may vote.
- There is a premium tier whose defining feature is that its instructions cannot be overridden, and a cheaper tier whose instructions can.
- A Continuity Dispute procedure exists for when the Current You objects to a Directive, with a stated burden of proof and a published fee schedule.
- A refund is offered for "unsuccessful continuity" — that is, for cases in which the earlier and later selves turn out, in the company's phrase, not to have been the same person after all.

Then, without ever stating them as theses, let the clauses carry: whether autonomy belongs to the person who wrote the directive or to the person who must live under it; the difference between the interests that give a life its point and the interests that consist in how it feels from moment to moment; the fact that the earlier self can only imagine the later condition and never occupy it, while the later self can neither ratify nor contest the terms; the suspicion that such directives encode a judgment about whose lives are worth living; and the question of who profits when the trigger is pulled early. The satire should be fair — exaggerated, but recognisably the real positions — so that a specialist laughs rather than winces.

Begin with the piece itself: no preface, no explanation, and no commentary after it.
