I have been persuaded by the case for imprecise credence, and I want to know what would count against it. On the view I now hold, an agent's doxastic state is properly represented not by a single probability function but by a set of them — a credal set — and the demand that a rational agent assign a unique real-valued probability to every proposition encodes a precision that the evidence frequently does not support. I am not asking for a survey or a reading list; I want the defeaters themselves, mapped and ranked, and an honest verdict on how exposed the view really is.

Begin with the standard objections, stated at their strongest. There is the sure-loss or Dutch-book construction, which purports to show that an agent who accepts any bet favorable under some admissible probability can be pumped. There is dilation, in which evidence that is independent of an event conditional on each admissible probability nonetheless widens the agent's interval for that event. And there is the charge that imprecision is a mere notational variant of an ordinary sharp credence held at a higher order. For each, identify exactly which commitment of the view it attacks, say whether it is decisive or merely awkward, and indicate which of these an imprecise probabilist would concede as counting against them and which they would dismiss as question-begging.

Then tell me whether the view risks anything at all. What does imprecision predict, forbid, or recommend that sharp Bayesianism does not? If it turns out to make no differential predictions — if any pattern of choice can be absorbed by adjusting the accompanying decision rule, or by declining to bet — say so plainly, and tell me whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or a legitimate feature of it.

Next, the decision-rule problem. Imprecision by itself does not tell an agent what to do; something like maximin, E-admissibility (an act is permissible if it maximizes expected value under at least one admissible probability), or maximality must be added, and different choices yield different verdicts. Does the interest of the view survive the observation that most of the contested work is being done by the decision rule rather than by the credences? And do the sure-loss objections, properly understood, target the credences or the rule?

Now an argument I cannot answer, which I would like you to assess on its merits. Suppose an imprecise agent does not gamble: she holds a credal set and uses it only to guide ordinary, non-betting choices. Ask her to compare, as doxastic states, her set against the sharp state obtained by replacing it with any single member. She cannot say the set is better, because every member is compatible with her evidence, so each sharpening is rational by her own lights, and she has no standard that ranks the set above its members. Nor can she say the set is required, unless she can show that sharpening is unwarranted — which is precisely what is in dispute. The charge is that imprecision is therefore at best permitted, never better and never demanded, so that the case for it collapses into a permissivism about credence — the far weaker thesis that more than one credal state may be rational on the same evidence — and cannot bear the weight the view places on it. If the argument works, say exactly what it establishes and how an imprecise theorist should reply; if it fails, locate the flaw.

Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say what I, as someone who currently holds the view, would be most rational to revise in light of them. I would be most moved by a demonstration that imprecision leaves me exploitable in a way precision does not, or by a concrete case in which imprecision yields plainly wrong verdicts about what to believe or do; I would be least moved by an argument that shows only that imprecision is unfamiliar or underdeveloped.

Finally, say which neighboring commitments the view stands or falls with — for instance, claims about what evidence can support, about whether rational agents must have determinate doxastic states, and about permissivism in epistemology — and, if the view is defensible only by surrendering one of them, which one, and whether that price is worth paying.
