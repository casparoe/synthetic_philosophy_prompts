I'm working through William James's 1884 essay "What Is an Emotion?" and there is a passage near the start that I cannot get a stable reading of. He writes: "I should say first of all that the only emotions I propose expressly to consider here are those that have a distinct bodily expression. That there are feelings of pleasure and displeasure, of interest and excitement, bound up with mental operations, but having no obvious bodily expression for their consequence, would, I suppose, be held true by most readers." He then sets these aside, remarks that taken together they seem to prove that there are "pleasures and pains inherent in certain forms of nerve-action as such," and turns to the thesis that the feeling of the bodily changes is the emotion -- adding that "without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth."

Three things I want disentangled. What is he claiming with "inherent in certain forms of nerve-action as such" -- a claim about intrinsic phenomenal character, and does it commit him to a second, different theory for the feelings that lack bodily expression? If the thesis is expressly restricted to emotions that have a distinct bodily expression, does it even answer the general question of what an emotion is, or is that restriction doing hidden work? And the part that most troubles me: "pale, colourless" seems to say that once you strip away the bodily feeling, a purely cognitive state is all that remains -- which is precisely the cognitivist, judgment view of emotion that James means to reject. So is his position just "the judgment, plus a feeling of the bodily changes"? A friend insists the honest reading makes James a proto-perceptual theorist, for whom the emotion is a perception of the body, rather than a feeling theorist at all, and that the "we feel sorry because we cry" line is dispensable rhetoric. Does the passage support that, or does it make the feeling itself the emotion? Argue from the passage.
