so I've got a pile of gut reactions about the death penalty and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Context: I'm an atheist, I think the sanctity of life is basically empty rhetoric, so don't build the answer on that. I'm also not a pacifist.

Where I land:
- A guy tortures and kills a couple of kids, caught on video, zero doubt, zero remorse: I dont want him executed. Doesn't feel like justice, feels like us turning into him.
- Same guy, but the case rests on one shaky eyewitness: no, obviously — but I notice that's a *different* reason (error), and I don't think error is really doing the work, because if you gave me a magic box that proved his guilt with 100% certainty I'd still say no.
- A general who ordered the massacre of 10,000 civilians, captured alive: part of me wants him dead. I think we shouldn't.
- A police sniper shoots a school shooter mid-rampage: totally fine.
- A drone strike kills a terrorist leader and, unavoidably, his family: uneasy about the family, but the strike itself, fine.
- A soldier kills an enemy soldier in a war I think is just: fine.

So I'm clearly not squeamish about killing as such. The other end:
- I think life without parole is actually *worse* than death — I'd rather be executed than rot for 40 years — and I still think LWOP is the right sentence and execution is not.
- I'm fine with a terminally ill person getting help to die if they ask. I'm not fine with executing a condemned man who asks to die.

The case I keep flip-flopping on: a guy who's been on death row 25 years, found religion, is genuinely a different person now, and the state is about to kill him anyway. Gut says this is the worst one of all, but I can't tell if that's a *new* wrong (we're killing the man he is now, not the one who did the crime) or just the same wrong with extra ick.

Here's my best guess at a principle, tell me if it survives: its not the killing, it's that the execution is a *scheduled ritual*. The state picks a date and an hour, builds a room for it, invites witnesses, offers a last meal. It's a ceremony where we kill someone deliberately, on purpose, in cold blood, with paperwork and a countdown. A sniper's bullet isn't that. A drone strike isn't that (mostly). And that's why the 25-years-later case feels worst — we had all that time to *not* do it, and did it anyway.

Does that hold up, and where does it break? What does it predict for stuff I didn't mention — like a state that executes people quietly, no ceremony, no announcement, or a prisoner who dies of natural causes while waiting, which abolitionists sometimes treat as a kind of win?

Last thing: I have a vague memory that Camus's whole case against the death penalty was about *premeditation* — that what's uniquely horrible is the state deciding in advance, calmly, and then doing it on schedule — which sounds a lot like my ritual point. If I've got him right, is my principle just his? And where would they come apart — is there a case that only came up after his time where his reasons and mine would point different ways? Not looking for a reading list, just the argument.

American English, please. And don't just tell me both sides have a point — give me an actual principle, its failure cases, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save.
