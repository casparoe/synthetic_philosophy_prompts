Several months ago I began sitting weekly with a small contemplative study group, and I have found myself quietly reorganizing my life around the view of one of its members — a retired philosopher I will call D., who has given the position a name of her own, tranquilism, and states it this way: the only things that matter in themselves are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, and everything we ordinarily prize as well-being is either a means to those two absences or a distraction from them.

Over a run of dinners she has given me verdicts on particular cases, and I have written them down as carefully as I could.

First, a life containing neither craving nor suffering but also no pleasure whatever — a colorless, untroubled equanimity — is, she says, the best of the lives we have discussed, and is exactly as good as an otherwise identical life that also contains a great deal of pleasure. Second, a life of continuous and intense craving that is satisfied the instant it arises, so that frustration and suffering never occur, is clearly worse than the first, and worse in the respect that matters rather than merely in aesthetic terms. Third, a patient in a permanent vegetative state, with no craving, no suffering, and no experience at all, is neither well off nor badly off; that life simply does not receive a welfare score. Fourth, a woman who has quieted her own craving but who is consumed with anguish on behalf of a suffering relative is not doing well — the anguish is the trouble, whatever we think of her conduct. Fifth, of a man who has never craved or suffered but is wholly passive and indifferent she says that he has attained nothing, since the goal is not deadness; yet when I asked her directly whether the goal requires the disappearance of all wanting or only of the wanting that hurts, she said only that wanting is the whole trouble, and would not elaborate.

The new case is mine. Over the past year my own craving has gone quiet in a way I did not expect: I want far less than I used to, and I am rarely restless. But I have taken on the care of my father, who has a progressive illness, and I find that I am moved by a steady, unhurried wish that he not suffer. That wish does not agitate me. When it is thwarted I am grieved but not destabilized, and I would not call it thirst. I need to know whether D.'s tranquilism treats this wish as the last ember — the residue of craving that still keeps me short of the goal — or as the very thing the practice was for.

Predict her verdict, and give me your own best guess together with the reasons for it. Set out the candidate rules that fit her five verdicts, including at least one under which every form of wanting, unanxious benevolence included, counts as craving, and at least one under which only craving in the narrower sense of thirst, lack, or aversion counts; if further rules fit her record just as well, give me those too, and say what each predicts about my case. Tell me whether her record secretly settles the matter already or genuinely underdetermines it, and which of her verdicts do the most work in either direction. Then name the further case, of a kind she has not yet ruled on, that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, and explain why that case rather than another. Finally, if the record does underdetermine my case, tell me what principle she would be adopting with each answer, and which of those principles you would defend.
