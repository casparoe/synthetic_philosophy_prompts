"I want to argue that we've been asking the wrong question about promises. Everyone wants to know what makes a promise binding — what strange act turns words into an obligation. But I think the more interesting question is why promises are non-transferable. When I promise you something, I'm not just undertaking to bring about a state of affairs; I'm undertaking to be the one who brings it about, or at least to stay the one who's on the hook. If I promise to help you move on Saturday and instead pay a professional mover to turn up in my place, I've done something wrong even though the boxes got moved, and even if you'd have preferred the professional. The usual story — that promising hands you a right to demand performance from me — struggles here, because you could just waive that right, and you didn't get what you actually wanted, which was me, sweating, carrying your couch. So maybe a promise is better modeled as a loan of the self: I lend you a slice of my future agency, and the debt is denominated in me. That's why it can't be discharged by a substitute, and why it survives my changing my mind — you can't repay a loan by deciding you no longer want the money. And it's why promises to intimates feel heavier than promises to strangers even when the stranger's promise matters more, because the closer the person, the more of myself I've already lent them; the promise just rides on a relationship that's full of unspoken loans already."
