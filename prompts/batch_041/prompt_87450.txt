My old professor of philosophy of social science gave us his summary of Friedman on unrealistic assumptions, and I wrote it down in my notes, so some of the mangling is probably mine. I want to know how much of it to keep. Here it is as I have it:

Friedman's claim — the one that got nicknamed the F-twist — is that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant. Only the accuracy of the predictions matters. So it's fine to assume firms maximize profit even though real managers obviously don't solve optimization problems; it's fine to assume consumers have complete and transitive preferences even though nobody's are; in general an economist may assume whatever makes the model tractable, because the only test that counts is whether the predictions come out right. His example was a good pool player: he sinks his shots as if he knew the laws of mechanics and could do the geometry, and that's a perfectly good explanation of what he does — we never have to say he's actually doing the math. And this is the same thing physicists do when they treat the planets as point masses or pretend there's no friction; everyone knows those are false and nobody thinks that refutes Newton. His deeper claim was that a theory is just a filing system for organizing predictions, so a model isn't the kind of thing that can be true or false about the world, only more or less useful. Which means that when someone says a model is unrealistic — a representative-agent macro model with infinitely lived households that never default, say — that isn't a criticism, it's a category mistake. The only real question is out-of-sample performance. And the upshot is that all the behavioral economics stuff and the but-people-dont-actually-do-that complaints are methodologically beside the point, because they're attacking assumptions instead of predictions.

How much of that is fair? Go sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong. In particular I want to know where the line is between an assumption being unrealistic and an assumption being false, and whether the pool player example is doing the work he says it's doing. Also: if the corrections are right, does that last bit survive? I.e. is behavioral economics really methodologically confused on Friedman's actual view, or does that conclusion only follow from the caricature? And if my professor overstated things, what's the strongest claim in the neighborhood that is actually defensible?
