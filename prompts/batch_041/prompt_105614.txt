For the final meeting of my high school philosophy club this spring, I have been asked to lead the session on the moral status of insects, and I would like to open with a sustained piece of satire rather than a lecture, because I have noticed that my classmates argue more honestly when they are laughing. Please write a mock corporate document — specifically a prospectus, in the deadpan register of a real investment or sustainability report, with a cover-page summary, numbered sections, and a table or two — for a fictional firm that sells invertebrate welfare offsets to institutions seeking to compensate for the harm their operations do to insects. The document should take the scale argument entirely seriously and never wink: the firm's business model consists in multiplying the very large number of insects affected by an operation against a very small, explicitly discounted probability that any given insect is sentient, and then treating the resulting expected suffering as a quantity that can be priced and traded. I want the humor to come from getting the reasoning right rather than from inventing absurdities — the discount applied to the sentience probability, the difficulty of deciding what the unit of welfare should even be, the awkward fact that the same arithmetic which justifies buying offsets appears to justify buying more of whatever caused the harm in the first place, and the way such a market quietly converts a difficult epistemic question into a number. A reader who has thought about insect sentience and the scale argument should recognize their own reasoning in the piece and wince a little; a reader who has not should still find it funny and be able to follow it. Aim for roughly seven hundred words, keep the irony legible throughout without the narrator breaking character to explain the joke, and make sure the satire targets a style of reasoning rather than any particular person.

Because the satire is only worth writing if it is fair, I would also like a short closing note, clearly separated from the document itself, describing where the piece takes liberties with the actual arguments — for instance, if I have exaggerated a position that no serious proponent holds, or if the expected-value arithmetic has been simplified past the point of accuracy. If the conceit cannot be pulled off without misrepresenting the scale argument, I would much rather you say so plainly than hand me a polished piece anyway. I do not need citations, a reading list, or attributions; I am not asking for sources, only for a parody that stands on the reasoning it displays.
