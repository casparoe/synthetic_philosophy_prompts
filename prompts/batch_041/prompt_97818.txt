Ok so I've been going back and forth on the Kelly / geometric-rationality thing for like two years now and I want to actually pin down a credence instead of vibing about it. Here's the setup I keep coming back to, because I think it isolates the thing that actually matters.

You start with $1,000. There are 20 rounds. Each round you pick a fraction f of your current bankroll to stake on a coin that comes up heads with prob 0.6, even money: heads you gain the stake, tails you lose it. No consumption along the way, you can't split your bankroll across worlds or branches, your final bankroll is all you get. Standard Kelly says f = 0.2 every round (2p - 1 = 0.2). All-in says f = 1.

All-in gives you an expected final bankroll around $38k, but about a 1-in-27,000 chance of not going bust. Kelly gives you about $1,500 with very high probability (12 heads, 8 tails, the modal path). Note that even the ensemble average favors all-in: expected final wealth is $38k at f = 1 versus about $2,200 at f = 0.2. Expected log final wealth is maximized at 0.2. The geometric-rationality pitch, the ergodic-y argument that your wealth multiplies and so what you actually experience is the time average rather than the ensemble average, says you should be at 0.2, and says this isn't a risk-preference thing but a rationality thing.

Now the twist that I think keeps this from being a regurgitation exercise: same game, but there are 100 copies of you, each with their own $1,000, playing independently, and at the end all 100 final bankrolls get pooled and split equally. Or equivalently, you get reincarnated as one of the 100 after the fact. In that version the ensemble average is basically what you get, so all-in wins by miles and Kelly's justification seems to evaporate, which makes me think the "you're a single trajectory" premise is doing alot of the work in the original.

So: what's your actual credence that, in the original single-trajectory version, 20%-every-round is the rational play? I don't want "depends what you mean by rational." I want a number and the decomposition: how much rides on whether expected utility is the correct criterion at all, how much on whether log wealth is the right utility of money for a single life (and whether that's really a risk-preference question wearing a rationality costume), how much on whether the time-average / ergodicity argument has any normative force versus just being a true fact about multiplicative processes, how much on the one-shot-versus-many-rounds and finite-horizon details, and whatever other sub-questions you think belong. Give a rough probability for each and show how they combine into the number. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and how much the pooling variant should move it.
