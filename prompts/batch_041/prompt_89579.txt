I'm a postdoc and in about two weeks I have to present at our department's mind-and-metaphysics work-in-progress, and I'm dreading it because the room is a bad mix for what I want to argue. The thesis is this: the cogito proves far less than it's usually taken to prove. Read charitably, all it really establishes is that at the moment of doubt there's thinking going on -- some episode of doubting, or of seeming-to-see-something, actually occurring -- and that we can't assert "there's thinking happening and nothing doing the thinking" without landing in a kind of absurdity, the way "it's raining and I don't believe it's raining" is somehow out of order even before we ask whether it's false. My line is that the absurdity is doing all the work, and absurdity isn't proof; and even if you grant the move, it gets you a happening, not a thing. It doesn't get you anything that persists, doesn't get you a substance, and I don't think it even gets you a single unified subject of experience -- the unity of the "I" looks to me like a premise smuggled in and then announced as a conclusion. So I want to hold the line that the argument is real but thin, and that most of what people hang on it is added later.

The problem is who'll be there. Two of them are early-modern historians who teach the Meditations every year and care a lot about the order of reasons; they'll want to know whether I'm quietly turning Descartes into a worse philosopher than he was, and whether my reading is even of the same argument. Two are epistemologists who work on self-knowledge and on Moore-paradoxical sentences, and they'll want to know exactly what work "unassertable" is supposed to do and why unassertability should track truth at all. One is a comparative philosopher who works on Buddhist no-self and will probably say my conclusion is true and boring -- that I've shown there's a stream and no self, which is the whole point, so what's the news? And one is a cognitive scientist who thinks the self is a constructed model and will keep asking why we're doing this with armchair arguments when there are patients with split brains and depersonalization. Plus assorted grad students who just want to know what my actual argument is.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this specific crowd, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy room for what matters. And I want you to take a position rather than laying out options -- if there's an objection I genuinely can't answer, say which one and how far I'd have to weaken the thesis to survive it, and if the honest verdict is that I should just give up the "even the unity is smuggled in" claim to keep the rest, then tell me that. Don't bother with citations or reading lists, I know the literature; I need the room, not the bibliography. Also include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
