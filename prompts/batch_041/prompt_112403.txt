Compose a joke about Sen's liberal paradox. The paradox is easy to gesture at and hard to actually use, so build the joke on the specific case set out below rather than on the familiar illustration, and make the humor depend on the structure of that case rather than on the word "paradox."

The case. Two people, Anna and Boris, are deciding which of them, if either, will read a certain book. There are three outcomes:

- only Anna reads it;
- neither reads it;
- only Boris reads it.

Whether Anna reads is Anna's own affair, so the liberal idea that each person should be decisive over their own private sphere implies that Anna's preference between the first two outcomes fixes the social ranking between them; she prefers only Anna reading to neither reading. Whether Boris reads is Boris's own affair, so the same liberal idea implies that Boris's preference between the last two outcomes fixes the social ranking between them; he prefers neither reading to only Boris reading. But both prefer the third outcome to the first — Anna would rather have Boris read the book than read it herself, and Boris would rather read it himself than have Anna read it — so the Pareto principle, applied to nothing more than their unanimous strict preference, fixes the social ranking between the first and third outcomes the other way. The three commitments together therefore generate a cycle: society is required to rank only-Anna above neither, neither above only-Boris, and only-Boris above only-Anna. Every individual preference involved is ordinary; the impossibility is structural.

Write the joke. It may take any form — a short dialogue, a mock news brief, a limerick, a scene at a committee meeting — but it must satisfy the following conditions:

- The punchline must turn on the collision between the two liberal rights and the Pareto improvement, not on the general oddity of economists or on the bare existence of a paradox. If the joke would work just as well with a strong Pareto principle, or with a large number of people, it has missed the point: the contradiction already arises with two people, two pairs of outcomes, and unanimity over strict preferences alone.
- It should be funny to someone who already knows the result, which means it must not pause to explain the paradox or close by defining it.
- It should avoid the standard Lady Chatterley's Lover illustration and the usual Prude and Lewd characters.
- It should remain intelligible to a reader who has the setup above but has not memorized the literature.
