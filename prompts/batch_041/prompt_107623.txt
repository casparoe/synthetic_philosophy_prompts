I'm a science journalist working on a long feature about the fight over psychiatric classification, and one phrase keeps surfacing in my interviews. A psychiatrist I spoke with — he has spent a decade building an alternative to the symptom-based diagnostic manuals around biomarkers, circuitry, and dimensional measures — told me his program is finally 'carving nature at its joints,' and he likened the older manuals to a butcher hacking across the grain of a carcass. A week later a philosopher of science on my list said the opposite: that the slogan is a piece of rhetoric that has been doing illegitimate work in these debates for decades, and that whenever someone invokes it, an argument that should have been made in the open gets skipped. I want to write about this carefully rather than take either of them at their word, so I need the slogan taken apart. What are the distinct precise claims that 'carving nature at its joints' could be making? I can see at least a few very different things it might mean: that our categories are not arbitrary and track real similarities; that good taxonomy is a method — find the real divisions rather than impose convenient ones; that kinds are the categories that support induction, explanation, and intervention, perhaps because they are nodes in a network of causal relations; that nature has one complete, mind-independent taxonomy with a unique set of joints; that kinds have essences, intrinsic properties necessary and sufficient for membership, discovered a posteriori; that kinds are stable clusters of properties held together by causal mechanisms, with fuzzy boundaries and no essence; that there are many cross-cutting real classifications, so joints are relative to a domain or an interest; that there are no joints at all and all classification is human imposition; and that kinds are warranted tools of inquiry rather than discoveries of preexisting structure. Lay these out as a menu, from the platitudinous to the radical, and for each say whether it is defensible, what it would commit its holder to, and — this is the part I most need — whether it would actually license my psychiatrist's conclusion that his categories are the real ones and the manual's are artificial.

Two further things I'd like you to press on. First, the slogan is often used as if it settled a dispute, when the work is really being done by an unstated assumption that there is a single correct taxonomy and that our current best science has found it; tell me which readings of the slogan are doing that smuggling, and whether any reading can support a claim about which research program deserves priority without also begging the question. Second, consider the possibility that the slogan cuts against him: if kinds are causal clusters or nodes in a causal network, symptom-based categories sustained by robust mechanisms — including social ones, like the way a diagnostic label changes the people it is applied to — might qualify as joints too, in which case the disagreement is not about whether nature has joints but about which mechanisms matter. I'd rather you take a definite position than hand me a survey: tell me which readings you would defend, which you would reject outright, which one my psychiatrist most likely meant, and whether the slogan can survive the use he is putting it to. Please write in American English.
