I direct the rules-review unit of a state agency — the office the legislature created when it attached a sunset clause to our administrative code. Six hundred and twenty rules sit in the code; under the statute each one lapses in twenty-two months unless the commission affirmatively re-authorizes it, and the commission has told us it will vote in bundles of about forty. My staff is four analysts, none of them lawyers, plus one deputy counsel. Each analyst gets roughly six hours per rule. Every file we send up has to be defensible in writing, because we have already been sued once over a rule we let lapse, and because the public comment record is where the losers of these votes will go looking for their arguments. The mandate's language is about unnecessary burdens on regulated parties, but the commission is not interested in a crude cost-benefit ledger, and neither am I.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my analysts can actually run on a single rule, in the spirit of Chesterton's fence and of the Burkean presumption that inherited arrangements may embody knowledge no living person possesses. It should give me: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure stops with repeal and the point at which it stops with retention; the point at which it must stop and hand the file to a person, which person, and with what question stated rather than answered; and the fields the written record must contain so that the reasoning survives litigation, public comment, and a change of commission. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a test.

Two failure modes have to be handled head-on, because both are live in my office. The first is Chesterton's fence used as a thought-terminating veto: an incumbent tells my analyst that a rule has reasons, declines to state them, and the file closes with retention. The second is the mirror-image rationalism: no one can articulate a purpose within the six-hour budget, so the analyst concludes there is none and recommends repeal. A procedure that only guards against one of these is worse than none, because it will be cited as neutral while doing one side's work.

Three things we have learned the hard way, and I want them built in rather than footnoted. First, the rationale recorded for a rule, the actual historical cause of it, and its present function are three different things, and they frequently fail to match; a scandal, a compromise, or a copying error from another state's code can leave a rule whose stated purpose is fiction and whose real work is real. Second, compliance data and enforcement data are not function data: a rule cited zero times in eleven years may still be doing work through the mere fact of its existence, and a rule with universal compliance may be doing nothing, or may be doing something other than what its text says. Third, rules come in bundles, and repealing two innocuous-looking rules at once is not the sum of repealing each alone.

I also need the procedure to distinguish a fence from a tollbooth. Some standing arrangements are defended by their beneficiaries rather than by their trustees — the moral case and the commercial case for a rule often arrive in the same envelope — and a procedure that treats every defender as a custodian of inherited wisdom will preserve rents, while one that treats every defender as a rentier will knock down load-bearing walls. Tell me how the analyst is supposed to tell these apart in six hours without either naivety or cynicism, and say plainly when the distinction cannot be made on the evidence available.

Two asymmetries should be addressed rather than assumed away. Repeal is cheap and re-enactment is expensive: it takes a coalition, a session, and a scandal. And the costs of a rule show up in invoices and complaint letters, while the harms it prevents show up nowhere, because they are the harms that did not happen. If the procedure corrects for either asymmetry, say how; if it refuses to, say why that is the honest choice.

Then run the procedure on three rules from my docket, showing the file as the analyst would fill it in:

- A rule requiring a particular hazardous operation to be performed by a licensed person rather than by an automated system. Its recorded rationale is a fire risk that no longer exists in that form. My analysts believe it is obsolete. It also appears to be the only place in our code where a diffuse and unorganized group — the people who live near these facilities — has a right to be notified.
- A rule with no rationale in the file at all, no citations in eleven years, and unanimous staff testimony that it is pointless. One analyst has noticed that it is the legal hook for a reporting practice on which four other rules silently depend.
- A rule the commission plainly wants gone, because it imposes substantial recurring costs on an industry with an effective lobbying presence, and which appears to be the sole protection for a group with no lobbying presence at all. Whether the protection actually works is unknown, and the evidence that would settle it is not obtainable within the six-hour budget.

At least one of the three should come out the way the commission will not like, and you should show what the record has to say so that my analyst can defend that outcome in the room rather than fold.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and which of the questions above should never have been turned into a procedure in the first place. Assume my analysts are careful readers who have never taken a philosophy course; if a distinction cannot be applied by someone in six hours with a case file and a phone, it does not belong in the procedure, however true it is.
