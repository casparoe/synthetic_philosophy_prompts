Before I look at any particular answer, I want the specification: what must a theodicy deliver? I mean a claim about God's actual reasons for permitting evil, not merely a proof that theism and evil are compatible. Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration that forces it, and tell me which are genuine constraints and which are the prejudices of a single camp. The list should cover at least the following: the sheer quantity and uneven distribution of suffering, not merely its existence; suffering borne by creatures that can be neither shaped by it nor compensated for it — animals, infants, those who die before their suffering can mean anything to anyone; the long prehuman history of creatures that lived and died badly, which no fall can explain; evils that are means to no one's good, cruelties that leave everyone, the victim included, worse off; and evils so extreme that calling them justified appears to add insult to injury. It must also remain consistent with what the tradition already says God is — omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, loving — so that a solution which quietly shrinks God does not count, and it must name goods we could in principle recognize rather than deferring to reasons beyond our ken. For each requirement, say what it rules out, and flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied; I expect the sharpest tension to fall between the last two. If I have omitted a genuine requirement, add it, and say whether a theodicy must be a single unified account or may be a patchwork of local explanations. Then identify which single requirement does the most excluding, and, in a sentence each, how the free-will, soul-making, and skeptical-theist strategies fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria; I will handle the scoring.
