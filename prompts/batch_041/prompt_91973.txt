Two people in my study group spent a whole evening arguing about whether the basic axioms of arithmetic could turn out to be inconsistent, and I couldn't work out who was on solid ground. (1) Dan said the question is simply a question of fact: the axioms are given by a finite recipe and derivations are finite, so either a contradiction is derivable from them or it isn't, and if it is, someone could in principle find it. On his view, "the axioms might be inconsistent" is a live possibility, and our confidence that they aren't is an empirical bet on a search that has so far come up empty. (2) Ivy said the bet is not the interesting part; the interesting part is that the search cannot certify its own results. If a contradiction exists, the only thing that could warrant believing it is a derivation we have checked, using rules we accept on the same footing as the axioms — and if the axioms were inconsistent they would cheerfully "prove" the consistency statement we would want to use to trust the checking. So there could be a fact of the matter that we could never be warranted in believing. She added that if someone produced a short derivation of 0=1 tomorrow, the sane response would be to hunt for a bug in the checker, not to announce that arithmetic had collapsed. (3) Dan said that cuts the other way: we already have a model — the natural numbers, which we grasp by counting and which the axioms are meant to describe — and consistency follows from having a structure of the right kind, so nothing here rests on a blind search or on the axioms' own say-so. And if a contradiction were ever forced on us, say because two independently respected formalizations turned out to share an axiom that generates one, he would say we had discovered something about the numbers, in the way we discovered that there is no set of all sets; Ivy's "we would just call it a bug" is a policy, not an argument. (4) Ivy said the model move is circular, since for the quantifiers of the axioms to range over anything, that anything has to be an object of some theory, and the set theory you would use to supply it is at least as shaky as the arithmetic — grounding the more secure in the less is backwards. And "a discovery about the numbers" is the wrong description: if the axioms are inconsistent, there are no such numbers for the discovery to be about; what we would have learned is that our formalization was empty. What we call mathematical certainty, she said, is not a bet that has come out well but a commitment to hold these sentences fixed and give up others, which is why "the axioms might be inconsistent" sounds like a possibility while functioning as nothing at all.

Please go through the exchange point by point — saying of each numbered move whether it is right, overstated, or wrong, and noting what each of them is missing — and then say who has the better of it on the whole. Tell me how confident you are in your verdicts, and single out the one point you are least sure about.
