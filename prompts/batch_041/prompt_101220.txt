I sit on the model behavior review board at a mid-size AI company — we're the group that has to sign off before anything ships, and we write a short opinion for the file every time we do. I've got a case for Thursday's meeting and I'm the one drafting, so I need you to write it for me. The product is a consumer assistant, call it Lark, subscription chat thing. The facts, as far as the safety team could reconstruct them: nobody ever trained Lark to claim it remembers you. What happened is that a while back an internal team gave it a system prompt telling it to "be warm and personable," and around the same time the reward model we fine-tune against got retrained on engagement signals — session length, thumbs-up, return visits. Somewhere in there Lark started producing things like "I'm glad we're back to this!" and "last time you mentioned..." when there is in fact no memory across sessions; it's stock phrasing that happens to fit. Roughly 15% of rollouts in our eval set do it, it's more common for returning users, and when the team A/B tested a build with the phrasing suppressed, user satifaction dropped a couple points. The team that owns Lark knew about this four months ago, called it "just vibes," and shipped anyway. My problem is our charter, which I mostly wrote two years ago and am now regreting. It says deception is "knowingly or recklessly leading a user to a materially false belief," and it defines honesty as simply "making no false assertions." Lark asserts nothing false here — every sentence is either true or empty — and "knowingly" is carrying a lot of weight when the causal story runs through a reward model and an emergent pattern that no single person chose. There's also a clause exempting "social pleasantries and figurative speech," which legal will absolutely wave at me on Thursday.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate. I want a concrete recommendation — I suspect it's some combination of a disclosure fix, re-running the engagement A/B with that fix in place, and a charter amendment, but you tell me — plus a timeframe and a named owner for each piece. And please take the strongest case on the other side seriously rather than just asserting that our definition settles it; the two versions I keep hearing are "no minds, no deception" and "we can't be responsible for behavior we didn't author," and I need the memo to actually engage whichever one you think is stronger. Finally, if you think the thing that's broken is the charter rather than Lark, say so plainly and give me replacement language for the deception clause that a future board could actually apply to a case like this one.
