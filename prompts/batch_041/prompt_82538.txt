I'm in a philosophy of emotion seminar, and the three-way standoff between the view that emotions are judgments, the view that they are perceptions of value, and the view that they are felt bodily states has begun to look to me less like a live disagreement than like three camps talking past one another. Each side presses a different case as the decisive datum: the perceptual camp leans on recalcitrance — someone who sincerely judges there is no danger and still feels fear — while the feeling camp leans on states such as being moved by a piece of music or a free-floating dread that seem to have no object at all, and the judgment camp treats both as marginal and asks instead how a non-conceptual state could be warranted or unwarranted in the way emotions are. My hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about the nature of emotion but about which explanatory task is treated as primary — individuating emotion types (what makes this fear rather than anger) versus explaining how an emotion is directed at anything at all — and that each side's objections land only against a version of its rivals rebuilt to serve the other task; a further part of the dispute may be verbal, turning on whether 'cognitive' may mean merely information-processing rather than concept-involving. Diagnose this: is my hypothesis right, or is a hidden empirical or conceptual question doing the work? And given your diagnosis, what concretely would count as progress — a distinction, a case that pulls individuation and directedness apart, or a change of question — or should we expect the debate to stay stuck?
