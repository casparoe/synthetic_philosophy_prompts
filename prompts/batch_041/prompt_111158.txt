ok so im in this health policy and ethics class and theres a pattern i cant stop noticing. In the US if you walk into an ER mid heart attack you get treated — they have to screen you and stabilize you whether or not you have insurance, whether or not you can ever pay the bill. But the same system lets someone stretch their insulin for months or skip the scan their doctor ordered, and nobody is legally on the hook for that; its a policy debate, not an emergency. Prevention is the same story: we'll spend an absurd amount to keep one identified person alive (the kid in the news, the guy who needs the experimental drug) and comparatively nothing on boring cheap stuff that would save far more lives. I'm pretty sure dialysis is the one chronic thing Medicare covers no matter how old you are, and I genuinely don't know why that one and not, say, insulin.

So what is this whole pattern committed to? What view of when health care is owed to a person does it presuppose — that the person has to be identifiable and in front of you, that death has to be imminent rather than decades off, that it's about the difference between doing something and letting something happen, or just about whoever happens to be legally on the hook? Which of those does the practice actually need, and which is just along for the ride? Does it hang together at all, or is it two theories stitched together — "rescue whoever is in front of you" plus "distribute nothing else"? And what would actually have to change in the practice if you swapped the presupposition out for a cold population view that just maximizes lives saved per dollar, or for a view where your health is your own bad luck to carry? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not just gestured at.

And one thing I keep getting told that I'm not sure about: people say the ER rule is just about not letting people die in the parking lot, i.e. optics and politics, not a real moral commitment — is that a fair way to dodge the question, or does the practice still commit you to something either way?
