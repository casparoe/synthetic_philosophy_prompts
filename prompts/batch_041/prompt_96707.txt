In the 1950s and 60s the sleep laboratory made dreaming a physiological phenomenon: dreaming is concentrated in REM sleep, and in the dream-deprivation studies subjects woken at every REM onset showed a rebound of REM and, as their experimenters reported, daytime distress — evidence, it was argued, that dreaming answers to a need. Take that research program as your episode and analyze it through Malcolm's account of dreaming: that a dream is not an experience undergone during sleep but a disposition to tell a story on waking; that the waking report is the criterion, not the evidence, of having dreamt; and that treating a dream as a cause or a process in the sleeper is a confusion.

Say what Malcolm must hold the labs to have been doing — what the REM/dream correlation is a correlation of, and whether the deprivation hypothesis can be stated in his terms at all — and then where his account is embarrassed, particularly by the physiological half of the rebound finding, which needs no reports. End with a verdict: was the laboratory program trading on a concept it had no right to, or is Malcolm's restriction of the concept one the evidence should make us abandon? Take a side and defend it, and say briefly whether Dennett's more empiricist handling of the same question comes out better.
