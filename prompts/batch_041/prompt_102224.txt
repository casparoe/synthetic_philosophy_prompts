I am a working compiler engineer, not a game theorist. My everyday vocabulary is that of static analysis and optimization: a program defined over an input domain, a set of candidate instructions or code paths, analysis passes that delete code proven unreachable or unprofitable, fixpoint iteration, monotone dataflow frameworks with a unique least fixpoint, phase ordering and the uncomfortable fact that the order of passes can change the result, soundness defined against an observational-equivalence criterion, and the occasional randomized dispatch where a deterministic choice is provably worse than a coin flip. I know undergraduate game theory only loosely, so treat me as fluent in compilers and a beginner in the formal theory.

I would like you to take seriously, and push as far as it will go, the following identification: the theory of iterated elimination of dominated strategies — including elimination by mixed strategies — is a static analyzer over a strategy space. Then give me a residue report.

First, restate as faithfully as you can, in my vocabulary, the standard account of iterated elimination: what a strategy space is, what a dominance relation is, what a single round of elimination is, what the surviving set is, and what the procedure is supposed to be justified by. Keep the translation honest. Do not quietly import the assumptions of static analysis — a fixed concrete semantics, a single objective function, an analysis that does not change the thing it analyzes — and then present the result as a translation.

Then work through the following, in order.

1. What maps cleanly. In particular: is iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies a monotone framework with a unique least fixpoint, confluent under reordering of the passes? 

2. What translates only with distortion. Explain why eliminating weakly dominated strategies behaves like a phase-ordering problem, where different orderings of the same passes reach different fixpoints, whereas strict dominance does not. Then say whether order-independence here is a theorem about the procedure or an artifact of my analogy.

3. What has no counterpart at all. I am most interested in the case where a pure strategy is strictly dominated only by a mixture and by no pure alternative, so it is "dead" even though for every single alternative there is some belief under which it is optimal. Is there any notion in static analysis of code that is dead only relative to a distribution over inputs and not relative to any single input? If there is not, say so plainly rather than constructing one.

4. Whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the original claims are less clear than they look. The justification of the procedure is usually said to rest on common knowledge of rationality. Tell me what in my vocabulary, if anything, corresponds to "common knowledge," and whether its absence marks a gap in compilers or a gap in the argument.

Close with a single paragraph giving your verdict: is this identification a real structural analogy, or a pun?
