I am a third-year PhD student working on Kantian ethics, and I have been trying to write fiction as a way of thinking through a difficulty in my dissertation chapter on the universalizability tests — specifically the problem of how the maxim of an action is to be specified. I would like you to write a short story that dramatizes that problem rather than explaining it. The setting I have in mind is a walled city-state whose civil administration is organized around a Chancery of Maxims: before undertaking any act that materially affects another person — drawing grain from a common store during a shortage, standing surety for a friend's debt, refusing a petition for aid — a citizen must file the maxim of the act, and the Chancery must rule on whether that maxim could be willed as a universal law. The Chancery recognizes two species of failure: maxims that cannot even be conceived as universal law without contradiction (promising without intending to keep the promise; taking what belongs to another whenever one wants it), and maxims that can be conceived as universal law but cannot be willed as such without contradicting an end one necessarily has (never helping anyone in need; letting one's talents rot). The adjudicators' real labour, however, is neither of these. It is deciding at what level of generality a filed maxim is to be described. Described narrowly enough, almost any act passes; described at the level of the policy it instances, almost any self-serving act fails. The Chancery's precedent book is therefore as much a record of descriptions as of deeds, and its rulings are binding, with fines for citizens who act on an uncertified maxim.

Your protagonist should be a junior adjudicator — someone trained on the canonical examples and reasonably confident in the test's verdicts — who is handed a case in which one and the same act has been filed twice, under two descriptions, and has received opposite rulings, so that she must decide which description is the relevant one. I would like the story to take seriously that she cannot simply defer to the actor's stated intention, since filers have every incentive to describe their maxims advantageously; nor can she invoke a general rule of description, since the Chancery has never agreed on one. Let her also notice, in the course of the casework, that a maxim which survives the contradiction test may still treat someone merely as a means, and that her office has no settled procedure for what to do when the two checks pull apart. I have been arguing about this with a colleague who thinks the level-of-generality problem is fatal to the test while I think it is only a problem of application, and I would like the story to let both views breathe rather than adjudicating between them.

Please aim for roughly 2,500 words, and resist ending with a lecture on Kant: I want the philosophical pressure to come from the casework, the paperwork, the petty office politics, and the character's own reasoning. It is fine — better, even — if she reaches no clean verdict, provided the story shows precisely why the description she would need is not available to her. Feel free to name the city and populate the Chancery as you see fit.
