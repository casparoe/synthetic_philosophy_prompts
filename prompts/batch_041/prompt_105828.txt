I spent a decade covering economics before I moved to the science-and-ideas beat, and I did a master's in the subject first, so I am fluent in the apparatus — utility functions and revealed preference, identification versus fit, instrumentalism about assumptions, out-of-sample validity, calibration, the standing distinction between a model that is useful and a model that is true — while my reading of the philosophy is casual and secondhand. I now write about artificial intelligence, and I keep having the uncanny experience of hearing, in the disputes about whether large language models can be said to have beliefs or whether we are entitled to explain their behavior by attributing intentions to them, arguments I have heard before in another register: about whether fitted parameters are real preferences or mere predictive devices, and about what is actually being claimed when we say an agent maximizes a utility function we reconstructed from its choices after the fact.

What I would like from you is a systematic translation, not a summary. Take the debate over the intentional stance and the attribution of beliefs to AI systems — the position associated with Daniel Dennett, the objections from those who see it as a license for careless anthropomorphism, and the more recent arguments about whether systems trained to predict text can be said to hold beliefs at all — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Do this part by part: the three stances and their ordering, the claim that intentional patterns are real, the competence-without-comprehension point, and the current dispute over machine belief.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I am really after. For each element, say what carries over cleanly, what carries over only with distortion and what the distortion is, and what has no counterpart in the economist's vocabulary at all. If the claim that certain patterns are real can only be rendered in my terms as a claim about predictive success, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that gap is a defect of the economic vocabulary — something it cannot express that the philosophy can — or evidence that the original claim was doing less work than its admirers suppose. Run the check in the other direction too: is there anything the economist's instrumentalism about assumptions captures that the intentional stance cannot?

One asymmetry I would especially like you to work through, because it is where I suspect the translation will strain. In the human case the design stance is largely unavailable to us and the physical stance is hopeless, which is part of why the intentional stance looks forced; in the case of a language model we have the architecture and the weights in hand. Render that asymmetry in my vocabulary — is it the difference between estimating a reduced form when the structural model is unknown and estimating one when the structural model is known? — and tell me whether it bears on the original argument or is a distraction from it.

Keep the translation honest. Do not quietly substitute the economist's assumptions — that agents are rational, that preferences are stable, that predictive success is the only test — for the philosopher's, and then present the result as a faithful rendering. The value of the exercise lies as much in what fails to carry as in what carries.

Throughout, state how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point in the whole exercise you are least sure of, with a brief account of what would settle it.
