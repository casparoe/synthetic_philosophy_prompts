I have come to hold that a theory such as the string-theoretic landscape or an inflationary multiverse can be rationally accepted even though it yields no prediction we could realistically test — that its support comes instead from the absence of viable alternatives, from the unexpected coherence it imposes on otherwise disconnected results, and from a meta-induction over the history of physics, where a successor theory was often accepted before its distinctive consequences could be checked. Tell me what would count against this position.

I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, and I want them drawn from several kinds of source: conceptual or formal results (for instance, anything showing that without a preferred measure the framework predicts nothing at all, or that the non-empirical criteria are satisfied just as well by theories we have already discarded); empirical developments (a rival that succeeds where this fails, or an observation the framework should have made unlikely); and methodological arguments (that the meta-induction runs equally well for abandoned research programs, or that the no-alternatives move presupposes a well-defined and already-exhausted space of alternatives). For each defeater, say whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it, and on what grounds.

I would personally find most telling any demonstration that these same arguments would have licensed a program we now regard as dead, or that the position has been qualified — by invoking a measure, a selection principle, or an observer-selection story — to the point where nothing could count against it. So close with an honest verdict: is this position well insulated, and if so, is that a defect here or an acceptable feature of a framework at this stage?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
