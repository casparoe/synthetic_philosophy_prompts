For about a year I've been in a reading group that keeps circling back to AI safety via debate — the proposal that two systems argue a question out in front of a weaker judge, and that the judge's verdict can be trusted to track the truth even on questions it could never answer on its own — and two members have now spent months talking past each other. The first holds the debate-optimist line: the adversarial structure is the whole trick, because a liar has to survive cross-examination by an opponent who is motivated and competent, and the judge needs only the competence to follow an argument, not to generate one; on this view the honest debater's win is the guarantee, and obfuscation is a technicality that better protocols — more rounds, forced commitments, letting the judge direct where to look — will absorb. The second holds the obfuscation-skeptic line: the guarantee only ever covered cases in which the honest side can put a checkable argument on the table, and there are questions whose true answers admit no short, checkable argument, so a dishonest debater can win by asserting a confident, long, mostly-valid argument whose one fatal step is buried where nobody in the room can find it. Lying is cheap precisely when the truth is expensive to demonstrate. Each of them has a case the other never engages with, and I want the document they should have written together. To keep this from being a clash of intuitions, I want two cases treated as shared test cases. (A) The question is the exact value of a quantity that is hard to compute and has no short certificate either way — the number of proper 3-colourings of a large graph, say, or the permanent of a large 0-1 matrix. The honest debater cannot produce the answer within the budget; the dishonest debater asserts a wrong value and defends it with a plausible derivation containing a buried non sequitur. (B) The liar's move is a decomposition: the claim is split into a long list of subproblems, each individually easy to check, such that at least one of them is wrong but neither debater can tell which one, so refuting the decomposition seems to require solving all of them — the honest debater is reduced to insisting that somewhere in the list there is an error, and the proposed repair (letting the challenger demand that the decomposer solve related subproblems, in the hope that a trapdoor can be planted among them) is exactly the kind of fix I expect the two of them to disagree about the generality of. I'd like each side's predictions about both cases recorded before the argument about them starts.

Please draft the joint statement. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign — about the formal setup (the judge, the two debaters, the resource bounds on each, what counts as winning, and whether the judge should be modelled as a complexity class or as a fallible reasoner with biases and sycophancy), about what debate does and does not promise, about when the obfuscation construction bites and when it does not, and about which parts of their dispute are merely verbal — if part of it is really about worst-case versus average-case hardness, or about whether "the judge tracks the truth" means the honest debater wins in equilibrium versus the verdict equalling the truth on every input, then say so and write out the disambiguated readings each side actually endorses; (2) a disagreements table listing each surviving disagreement, with the issue stated neutrally and each side's position given in wording that side would accept; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, including any I have missed, for instance whether the honest debater's real resource is cross-examination rather than the opening argument, or whether the binding constraint is the judge's computational budget rather than its human failings; and (4) for each crux, a case, argument, or formal result both sides agree bears on it, each side's prediction stated in advance, and a note on what would count as the crux being settled rather than merely re-contested. I would rather have precise formulations than citations, so skip references and reading lists; and if you conclude that some apparent disagreement is definitional, or that the two positions reduce to different bets on complexity-theoretic assumptions, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a middle position.
