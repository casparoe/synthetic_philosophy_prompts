I'm a novelist — not a philosopher, which will be obvious in a second — and I've written myself into a corner. My new book has a man who was born blind and gets his sight back in his seventies, and I'm trying to write the scene where people show him things for the first time. Someone told me Locke has a passage about exactly this, so I dug it up, and now I'm more confused than I was before. Here it is, as Locke quotes it from his friend Molyneux:

"Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t'other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube."

Then Molyneux's own answer to his own question, which Locke passes along:

"Not. For though he has obtain'd the experience of, how a Globe, how a Cube affects his touch; yet he has not yet attained the Experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; Or that a protuberant angle in the Cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye, as it does in the Cube."

And then Locke's own two cents:

"I agree with this thinking Gent. whom I am proud to call my Friend, in his answer to this his problem; and am of opinion, that the Blind Man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say, which was the Globe, which the Cube, whilst he only saw them: though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their Figures felt."

Help me actually read this thing. Here's what I need:

- First off, is he talking about two objects or three? He says "Sphere" and then he says "Globe," and I've been going back and forth on whether those are two names for the same object or whether something got dropped in the telling.
- Is the claim that the man can't *see* the difference between the two shapes, or that he can see the difference but can't stick the right name on the right one? "distinguish, and tell" makes me think both at once, and then Locke says he couldn't say it "with certainty," which sounds weaker — like maybe he could see *something*, just not be sure what. Which is it, and what does the passage actually commit to? This matters to me because its the difference between a scene where he's genuinely blind to shape and a scene where he's looking at two shapes that simply don't have names yet.
- What is "Experience" doing in that argument? Saying he hasn't yet had the experience that touch-so-and-so goes with sight-so-and-so feels less like a reason and more like the answer repeated. What would have to be true about ideas that come in by the eye and ideas that come in by the hand for that sentence to count as an argument at all? And what would someone have to believe about those two kinds of ideas in order to answer "yes" instead?
- Why the fuss about the objects being "of the same metal" and "nighly of the same bigness"? What is that detail quietly ruling out?
- My editor wants me to have someone prepare him before the bandages come off — tell him the round one will look like the moon, the square one like a book. I think that's cheating, because he's never seen anything, so "will look like" can't mean anything to him yet. Am I right, or is there a reading of the passage where a description like that would do the work that the missing experience is supposed to be doing?

Please don't give me the centuries of who-said-yes-and-who-said-no — I'm not going to read any of it, and I don't want a reading list. Just stay with these three chunks and tell me whats on the page, in American English.
