My political theory reading group is doing a unit on social choice, and the person leading this week handed out a one-page summary that's supposed to explain "the discursive dilemma" before we discuss it. I'd never heard the term before, and the summary is confident and clean, but a couple of things in it don't sit right with me and I can't tell if that's me or the handout. The gist of what it says: the discursive dilemma shows that majority rule is incoherent. Suppose a group has to decide whether B is the case, and also has to decide on a connecting claim like "if A then B." Because the members disagree, a majority can end up endorsing A, a majority can endorse the link from A to B, and yet a majority can reject B — so the group's decisions contradict each other, which is what happens when people aren't reasoning carefully about how the issues hang together. The handout says this is really just Condorcet's paradox in fancy dress, and that what it proves is that there's no way to run a vote that both respects what the majority wants on each question and comes out logically consistent. So a group that insists on being consistent has to hand the decision to a single expert or a small committee — in effect a dictator — which is why the whole thing is usually read as showing that democracy is impossible in principle and that we should be suspicious of referendums.

I have some background in formal stuff (I've done a bit of logic and I know Arrow's theorem by name), and the move to "so somebody has to dictate" feels like it's smuggling in a different result, and I don't think "democracy is impossible" can be the moral of a single dilemma. But I dont trust my instincts here. Can you go through the handout claim by claim and tell me which parts someone who actually works on this would sign off on, which are the standard simplifications you'd expect in a popular summary, and which are just wrong or are importing a neighboring theorem? In particular I want to know: (a) whether the discursive dilemma really is the same thing as Condorcet's paradox or only a cousin, and if it's a cousin, what exactly the difference is; (b) whether the contradiction it describes requires confused or irrational voters, or can happen with people who are perfectly consistent about the logical connections; (c) whether "you need a dictator" follows from the dilemma at all or is being borrowed from somewhere else; and (d) what the honest upshot is supposed to be — is the result really as strong as the handout makes it sound, or is it a narrower trade-off among a few things we'd all like an aggregation rule to have, with some escape routes the handout never mentions?
