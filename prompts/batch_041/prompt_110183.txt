For the past two years I have sat on the programming committee of a small municipal library that runs what we call the Open Hour: once a month, any resident who signs up gets ten uninterrupted minutes at the lectern on any subject, followed by fifteen minutes of questions from whoever happens to be in the room. There is no vetting of topics, no list of disfavoured views, and no mechanism for cancelling a confirmed speaker. In practice we have had to decide, after the fact, whom to invite back and whom to ask not to return, and I have found myself on the losing side of those votes often enough that I want to know what I actually believe. My verdicts are these. A retired teacher who used his ten minutes to argue that all immigration-related programming should be cancelled and that the library should stop buying materials in Spanish: I thought he should be welcomed back. A man who used his slot to read out the home address of one of our librarians and invite listeners to go and ask her some questions there: I thought he should never be allowed in the building again, though I notice that this has nothing to do with what he thinks and everything to do with what he did with it. A third speaker, unfailingly courteous, no slurs, no raised voice, who over six months built a case that members of one ethnic group are innately prone to violent crime and should be treated accordingly: I thought he had to be excluded, even though he broke no rule we have. A fourth, who argued that the Open Hour is a mistake, that the library should run an invitation-only lecture series instead, and that people like the third speaker are the reason: I thought he should be welcomed back, though his proposal would silence far more people than the third speaker's ever did. And a fifth, an atheist who spent his ten minutes arguing that the dominant religion of our town is false, after which three regular attenders from that congregation stopped coming and have not returned: I thought he should be welcomed back as well, even though I will admit the room has never quite been the same.

I have argued about this with a colleague on the committee who holds what she calls the add-more-speech rule: anyone may speak, and the remedy for bad speech is always more speech, so the committee may add events but never subtract one. What actually moves me, when I am honest, is not the offensiveness of a view but whether in this particular room the speech makes it harder for some group of residents to keep turning up and being heard — except that this formulation seems to condemn the atheist along with the third speaker, which I do not want, and I suspect it also lets the first speaker off too easily. What I want from you is the principle, or the two or three best candidates if no single one works, that comes closest to my pattern of verdicts; each clause of it tied to the case that forces that clause; its verdicts on two cases I have not described, namely a speaker whose arguments are impeccably polite but of a kind no evidence could ever refute, and a resident who has been the target of the third speaker's campaign and now wants the ten minutes to describe what that has been like; and an honest statement of which of my five verdicts the principle cannot keep. Please give me the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, covering the principle, the cases that motivate its parts, the two new cases, and the verdict I would have to surrender — with no introduction and no conclusion.
