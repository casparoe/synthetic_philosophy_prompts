I'm a retired chemist — forty years of spectroscopy — who's spent the last few years reading philosophy, and I've gotten stuck on something a trade book on group decision-making calls the discursive dilemma. The author seems competent, but I've read maybe four books in this area and I can't tell where the seams are. Here's the summary I'm working with:

"Three judges must decide a case. Legally, liability requires two things: that the defendant breached the contract, and that there was no valid excuse. Each judge reasons perfectly consistently — each votes liable exactly when he votes for both breach and no valid excuse. Now suppose a majority finds a breach and a majority finds no valid excuse, but only one judge votes for both; then issue-by-issue majority voting says there was a breach and no excuse, which by the legal rule means liable, even though a majority of the judges voted not liable. So majority voting is simply incoherent. This is really just Arrow's theorem in another guise, and Condorcet spotted the same thing two centuries ago with his cycles. What Pettit and his coauthor showed is that no democratic procedure can be both consistent and fair, so our only real options are to stop letting majorities decide anything, or to admit that groups don't have minds at all. The premise-based fix — vote on the premises and let the conclusion follow — is the one escape hatch, but it's really just a way of letting a minority dictate the outcome, so it doesn't count."

Something about it feels off; the author seems to want it both ways in places, and I suspect a couple of seperate claims have been welded together. Go through it claim by claim: what would a philosopher who actually works on social choice nod at, what's a harmless simplification, and what's flatly wrong? And tell me whether the closing moral — that we either stop aggregating or give up on group minds — follows from anything true in the rest of it. For what its worth, I'm inclined to think the "groups don't have minds" line is the sensible one, but I'd rather know if I'm being sold something.
