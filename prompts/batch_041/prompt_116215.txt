I am a retired geologist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy. My closest friend and colleague, an anthropological linguist, died last month, and I have been asked to deliver the eulogy at her memorial. She spent thirty years among a small, isolated people and produced the only dictionary of their language. Her most famous entry translated a term—I will call it kalo—as rabbit. The room will include her family, who are not academics, and a good number of linguists and philosophers. Since reading Quine on the indeterminacy of translation, I find I cannot say, as I would once have, that she understood them or captured what kalo really meant; by Quine's argument, rabbit, rabbit stage, and undetached rabbit parts could all fit every behavioral fact she recorded. But a eulogy that hedges is no eulogy. Write the speech I should give: roughly five minutes, warm enough for the family, precise enough that the linguists do not hear it as evasion, and built so that the indeterminacy is not a footnote but the thing that determines what I can and cannot say about her life's work. Do not offer me several options; choose one line on what her achievement was—translation, predictive manual, something else—and commit to it. After the speech, add a short note, for me alone, saying where the speech takes liberties with Quine's thesis, where it leans on a contested reading of it, and whether the line you chose can survive the objections a Quinean would raise.
