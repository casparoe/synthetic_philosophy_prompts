Hi — I'm in my third year and I've been told to lead the seminar discussion for the consent week of my ethics-of-law course, and I want to bring a decision tree instead of the usual slide with the three conditions of informed consent on it. The reason is that everyone in my tutorial can recite the three conditions and then we spend the whole hour disagreeing about every hard case anyway, which makes me think the conditions arn't where the action actually is.

So: can you build me a decision tree that goes from questions anyone could answer about concrete cases to the named positions on what makes consent valid? I want the branch nodes to be the questions people really come apart on, not the standard doctrine. Roughly the distinctions I care about:

- What kind of thing is a valid consent — a mental state (they really did agree, even if they never said so), a communicative act (it has to be given or at least expressed somehow), or a normative power (it's the thing that changes what others are permitted to do to you)? And a related one: does valid consent make the act permissible, or could you validly consent to something it's still wrong for the other person to do?

- Coercion, and specifically the baseline problem. When someone says 'I'll do X unless you do Y', what do you compare against to decide whether that's a threat or just an offer? What would have happened anyway if they'd said nothing, what's statistically normal, or what the person making the proposal morally owes you? I want this as a branch node because I've noticed the same case gets called coercive or not depending only on which baseline people are quietly using.

- Deception: does the lie have to be about the nature of the act itself, or does being misled about something peripheral (the other person's motives, how much they're being paid, whether they'll still respect you afterwards) also void consent? And does it matter whether they would have refused had they known, or only whether they were misled about something they cared about?

- Competence: fixed threshold versus decision-relative (the same person can be competent to consent to a haircut and not to a mortgage), and whether competence is about understanding the facts or about being able to weigh them.

Cases I want the tree to have to handle, because these are the ones that split my tutorial: someone threatens to tell the truth about your affair unless you lend them money (true disclosure, but no right to make that threat); a lawful job offer that's awful but better than every alternative; a surgeon who truthfully lists the complication rates but presents them in a way that leaves the patient underestimating them; someone who agrees to sex while drunk and is glad about it sober; a patient with early dementia who can restate the procedure but can't compare it to the alternatives; a blanket advance consent to whatever a future doctor thinks best.

What I need: a tree with 4-6 levels and 8-12 leaves, each leaf named (or marked unnamed if nobody really holds it), every branch node phrased as something answerable without already knowing the label it leads to, and one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment. Also please flag (a) any leaves the tree can't separate and why, and (b) any combinations of answers that come out empty — is that because the view is incoherent, or just that nobody's bothered to defend it? Keep it to stuff a second-year can follow; don't assume we've read alot beyond the standard consent material.
