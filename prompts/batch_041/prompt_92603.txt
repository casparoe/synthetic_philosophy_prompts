Heres a thing about how scientists police theories that I can't square. In physics, submit a paper showing that some model can be tuned to fit data that was already on the table and reviewers will call it curve-fitting or unfalsifiable and bounce it. In the historical sciences — paleontology, geology, a lot of evolutionary biology — you can do more or less exactly that and it's a perfectly respectable paper. Then the same physicists who sneer at curve-fitting insist that any replacement theory reproduce what the old one got right in the limit where it worked, even while telling you the old theory was refuted and is false. So what is the practice assuming? Does it presuppose that a theory earns its keep by risking a prediction that could have failed, rather than by accommodating what's already known, and does the "recover the old results" rule commit them to theories being only locally or approximately true instead of just false? Do those two commitments even fit together, or is one doing all the work while the other is just professional habit? And where is the practice underdetermined — what does it strictly require versus what does it merely sit comfortably with?
