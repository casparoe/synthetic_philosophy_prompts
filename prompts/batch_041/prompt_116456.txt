After thirty-five years in microbial physiology I retired, and I have spent the last two of them reading philosophy of biology, mostly to make sense of a dispute that kept surfacing in my working life: what exactly we mean when we call something alive. I now sit as the outside member of a standing committee of a scientific society, a committee whose unglamorous job is to rule, case by case, on whether a newly described entity is to receive an entry in the society's register of living systems. The register matters, since a listing brings biosafety oversight, a grant-eligibility code, and a certain standing in the literature, so we cannot simply wave the borderline cases through. Our track record so far is this: a free-living bacterium was listed; a highly reduced obligate intracellular bacterium that cannot grow outside a host cell was listed; a large virus, one with several hundred genes, was not listed, our minute observing that outside a host it neither grows nor consumes energy and is inert; an infectious misfolded protein was not listed; a self-replicating RNA molecule, engineered in a test tube, that can be propagated and selected over many generations was not listed; a sterile hybrid mammal was listed; a dormant bacterial endospore, metabolically silent for years on end, was listed, with the note that it is alive but dormant; a lichen, which is of course a composite of two organisms, was listed; a plasmid was not listed; and, most contentiously, a chloroplast removed from its cell and kept in culture, where it went on photosynthesizing and even dividing for a time, was not listed, our reasoning being that it is a detached part of another organism rather than an entity in its own right. Each was decided on its merits, and I confess I could not have told you, until I began reading, that the committee has evidently been running some rule rather than judging ad hoc.

The case now before us is synthetic, and it is the reason I am writing. A laboratory has assembled a lipid vesicle containing a complete autocatalytic metabolic cycle: it takes up simple nutrients, generates its own ATP, and synthesizes most of its own lipids and cofactors; it grows, and at a threshold size it divides into two. It contains no DNA and no RNA whatsoever. What it passes to its daughters is not a nucleic acid sequence but a set of self-templating peptide conformations that bias which catalysts get made, so that a daughter inherits a rough copy of the parent's catalytic repertoire and the population can be selected over many generations. The question is what our committee should do with it, and I would like rather more from you than a verdict.

I would like you to reconstruct the candidate rules that would fit every ruling above — I can see at least two, and possibly a third — to state what each predicts for this vesicle, and then to commit to the prediction you judge best and defend it, including which of our past cases you take to be the closest analog and why that analog is not dispositive. Finally, tell me the further case we could put to the committee that would separate the surviving rules from one another, and be candid about where your own reasoning is weakest: which argument or consideration would move you to the opposite verdict, and on which step — the reconstruction of the rule, the reading of the analog, or the prediction itself — you would place the least weight.
