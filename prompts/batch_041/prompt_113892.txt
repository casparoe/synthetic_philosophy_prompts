You're a philosopher of language who knows the externalism literature inside out — Twin Earth, deference, causal chains of reference, the lot — and I've got a prediction job for you that I can't do myself.

I'm a historian of science, and I sit in on a reading group run by a colleague of mine, Rosie, who's definately an externalist about meaning: she thinks what your words mean depends on things outside your head. I'm giving a paper next week on a mining settlement I've been working on, and I want to predict what she'll say about it. The trouble is I can't work out which rule she's actually running, so I'm outsourcing the job.

Here's what she's ruled on so far, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Two planets with no contact, each with a community that digs up a shiny yellow metal and calls it "gold". On one planet it's the element we know; on the other it's a different element that behaves identically in every test either community has ever run, or could run. She says the two words mean different things — the meanings come apart even though nobody on either planet could ever tell, and even though every speaker would say exactly the same things about their own samples.

2. A bloke in the group who genuinely can't tell juniper from the look-alike shrub that grows next to it. The community's botanists can. He points at a juniper and says "that's juniper": she says that's true, and that he means juniper, not "whichever shrub I happen to be pointing at". If he'd pointed at the look-alike, she says he'd have said something false.

3. Someone whose chemistry is completely up the spout — thinks gold is what you get if you refine lead for long enough — but who uses "gold" for gold and picks it out reliably. She says he means gold, and the daft theory is a false belief about gold rather than a change of subject.

4. A student who's read about a metal in books but never handled any. She says his word still means the metal.

So far, so externalist. The annoying thing is that in all four cases the stuff in front of the speaker, the community they're sitting in, and the history of how they got the word all line up — so I can't tell which of the three she's running, and I'm fairly sure they're seperate rules. Roughly: the meaning is fixed by what the stuff actually is where you are; or by what the people you'd take correction from mean by it; or by whatever the word got attached to when it was first introduced, and thereafter it just gets passed down. Or something else I haven't thought of.

Now the case. There's a small settlement on a subantarctic island — call it Kestrel — that's been going about twenty years. The only metal in the rock there isn't iron. The lab back home, which analyses samples for them, has established it's a different element altogether, a close cousin of iron. But it looks like iron, it's worked like iron, and everything on Kestrel is made of it: nails, pots, the roof. Everybody there, all of them born and trained on Earth, calls it "iron" and always has. They're not cut off: instruments, journals and spare parts come in by ship, they radio the lab weekly, and the lab's chemists — who use "iron" for iron — are exactly who they'd ask if they wanted to know what something was made of. The lab has even coined a name of its own for the local metal; the settlers know the name perfectly well and never use it.

The person I'm writing about is Nadia, who's been on Kestrel for twenty years. She says things like "the iron here corrodes faster than the iron I grew up with". So: does her "iron" mean iron, or the local metal? Does that sentence come out true, false, or somehow neither? And which of the rules above is Rosie going to reach for?

Then the bit I really care about. Suppose Kestrel had been cut off for two hundred years instead: no ships, no radio, and by now the settlement has its own metallurgists, born there, who've never seen Earth iron, who use "iron" for the local metal and have a whole working theory of it. Does that change your answer, and does it change which rule she's running? What I want to know is what each version of the case actually tests, and whether there's some further case I could put to her that would separate the rules in a different direction from these two — something where the environment and the history come apart but the community doesn't, or whatever the mirror image turns out to be.

No citations, no reading lists, please — I'm not going to go and read the literature, and a bibliography is no use to me anyway. What I want is: first, a short brainstorm of the candidate rules and what each predicts; then your best guess at Rosie's verdict on Nadia, with reasons; then the same for the two-hundred-year version; then the case that would settle it. If the precedents genuinely don't determine an answer, say so, and tell me what she'd be committing herself to with each choice. Think it through carefully — I'd much rather have a hedged answer with the machinery showing than a confident one-liner.
