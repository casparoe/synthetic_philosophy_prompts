I am a novelist — four books, the most recent a fairly grim family chronicle — and last month I sat on a festival panel about whether fiction owes anything to the world it borrows from. A critic on the panel, a man I like and largely agree with, kept waving the question away with 'art for art's sake'. When I asked whether a novel that turns a real atrocity into a decorative set-piece owes anything to the people it borrows, he said I had made a category mistake, since art for art's sake means a work answers only to its own internal standards and to no external court whatever; twenty minutes later, when an audience member asked whether a press should be pressed to drop an author who had done something odious, he produced the same sentence again, this time to mean that we must never judge the maker through the work or the work through the maker. One phrase, at least two quite different jobs, in ten minutes. I suspect it is doing more than two, and I would like the thing taken apart properly. I don't need the phrase's history or a reading list — I need the logical geography.

Please lay out the distinct, precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, and give me a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false (or so close to false that only a rhetorician would say it). I would expect the readings to come apart along several axes, and I want them separated: whether it is a claim about definition (that art is the kind of thing whose value is internal, or that function and purpose are excluded from the concept of art altogether); about value (that aesthetic value is the only value a work has, or merely that aesthetic value is not reducible to moral or political value); about the maker (that the artist's intentions, commitments, and conduct are simply not part of what the work is); about criticism (that moral or political criticism of a work is always a mistake, or only that it is not the whole of criticism); about institutions (no patron, no censor, no committee — the artist's freedom from external control); and about the audience (that we should attend to form and technique rather than message or use). For each reading I want to know what it would commit me to, what it would forbid me to say, and whether any of them is the sort of thing a working novelist could actually live by rather than merely declaim at a festival.

Finally, tell me which of these readings my critic was most likely relying on in each of the two moments, and — this is the part I care about — which readings would actually license the dismissals he performed. My suspicion is that the reading that sounds loftiest is doing none of the work, and that the two dismissals need two incompatible readings of the same sentence, but I would rather you showed me than agreed with me.
