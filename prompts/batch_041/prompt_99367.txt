For about a year my philosophy-of-probability reading group has been circling the two-envelope paradox without getting anywhere, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another attempt at the puzzle. The setup is the usual one: two envelopes, one holding twice the other, you pick at random and open it to find X, so the other holds either 2X or X/2; the naive argument assigns each possibility probability one half, concludes that the other envelope has expected value 5X/4, and recommends switching — while the same reasoning applied before opening would have recommended switching to whichever envelope you were about to leave behind. One camp treats the matter as closed: the equal-probability step is invalid, it presupposes something like a uniform prior over the amount in the smaller envelope, and with a well-behaved prior the apparent advantage evaporates. The other camp grants that the classic presentation may be mishandled but insists the first camp has changed the subject: it has shown how to avoid the contradiction, not identified the faulty step, and, they add, conditioning on the observed X can make switching genuinely advantageous for particular X even under a proper prior, so the demand for a proper prior is a fact about one setup rather than a diagnosis of the reasoning. My own suspicion is that the two camps are answering different questions — how to defuse the paradox versus where exactly the fallacy lies — but I cannot tell whether that exhausts it. What kind of disagreement is this: a mathematical question with a determinate answer that one side has simply not absorbed, a dispute about what it takes to resolve a paradox, two questions under one name, or a hidden formal issue about improper priors, unbounded or undefined expectations, or the relation between conditional and unconditional expectation? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here, or should the group stop expecting any?
