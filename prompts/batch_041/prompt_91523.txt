So I've somehow agreed to run a session for this reading group I'm in — about fifteen people, mostly the sort who hang out on LessWrong-adjacent discords and have read alot of pop science plus chunks of the Sequences, plus two or three humanities grad students who will definately know more than me about Kant. It's 75 minutes, I have a whiteboard, and the topic is underdetermination of theory by evidence. The reason I volunteered: in another server I'm in, people keep deploying "underdetermination" as a conversation-ender — "well, the evidence underdetermines the theory, so your realism is dead" — and I'm fairly sure most of them are either running the trivial curve-fitting version (any finite data set fits infinitely many curves, therefore... something?) or quietly conflating it with the holism point that you can't test a hypothesis in isolation because of auxiliaries. Those seem like different problems to me, and people mush them together mainly because both have Quine near them. So I want a plan for teaching the actual thing, not a summary of it.

What I want is the pedagogical skeleton, not the content. First, the order the pieces have to come in and why that order and not another — what has to already be in place before "underdetermination" even means anything, and where the fork sits between the boring version (true of every finite data set, so presumably not what anyone should care about) and the version that's supposed to threaten scientific realism. Second, the one example that should carry the whole session. I have a hunch it should be the old ether theory vs. special relativity situation, since at the time the available experiments couldn't tell them apart and the disagreement was about why the numbers come out the way they do rather than what the numbers are — but you tell me, and tell me what the carrier has to have and what it must not have. My suspicion is that it must not be a case where the two theories secretly differ in predictions somewhere down the line, because then it's just a normal empirical dispute and the point evaporates. Third, the misconceptions they'll arrive with or form, in the order they'll actually surface, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off — I'm expecting "underdetermination means all theories are equally good," "it means science is arbitrary or relativistic," "it's just uncertainty," "it's the same as holism," and "it only applies to unobservables," but I want your map, not mine.

Fourth, a check for understanding I can run at the end that separates someone who actually got the distinction from someone who just picked up the vocabulary — ideally something that would expose the holism/underdetermination conflation if it's still there. Fifth, what I should openly refuse to explain at this level (I'm guessing the realism/anti-realism literature proper, and anything requiring them to already know what a Ramsey sentence is), plus the one teaser sentence to leave them with so they want to go further. I have a half-formed idea that the interesting residue isn't "same predictions" but "same predictions, different advice" — two theories agreeing on every observation and still telling you to do different things about precommitment, or about whether some unobservable state is real in a way that changes the decision. I don't know if that's a real distinction or just me being confused, but I'd like the session to gesture at it at the very end. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrap-up.
