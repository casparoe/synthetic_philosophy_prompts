I have been asked to write the substantive argument of an agenda-setting memo for a foundation that is about to fund six full-time positions for five years in the study of cross-cultural and cross-linguistic variation in philosophical intuition. The foundation's stated aim is to change how the field conducts itself rather than to add findings to it, and it has asked which conceptual questions most deserve a person attached to them, and in what order.

Some constraints. It will not pay for new large-scale data collection, since a separate empirical project is already funded and is described below. It will not fund historical reconstruction of how this dispute arose, and it will not fund work whose main deliverable is a survey of what has been said. It also wants to know which of the questions it might be sold is in fact not worth a researcher at all.

The empirical project the memo must be responsive to administers familiar philosophical cases of several kinds — knowledge, reference and natural-kind terms, causation, personal identity, and moral dilemma — to participants in eight countries, in the participants' first languages, with back-translation checks. Three features of the design matter here. First, each case appears in several semantically matched versions differing only in surface features: whether the probe asks for a prediction or an evaluation, whether the protagonist is described as an expert or a novice, whether the response is a forced choice or a rating, and whether the case comes first or last in the session. Second, the same participants answer the same cases at three points across a year, so the project will yield measures of within-person instability alongside between-group differences. Third, participants explain their answers in their own words, and the explanations are coded for the consideration each participant treats as decisive, which lets the project ask whether groups differ in the concept they bring to the case or only in how heavily they weight a consideration that both groups recognize.

Among the questions people in this area say are open: whether the reported variation is real rather than an artifact of translation, sampling, or task construal; whether it is conceptual or substantive, that is, whether the groups disagree or merely mean different things; whether real variation shows the intuitions to be unreliable rather than merely diverse; whether philosophical expertise insulates anyone from the problem; whether any of this bears on the epistemology of the armchair at all; and what would have to be true for variation data to count as evidence about the truth of a philosophical claim rather than about the psychology of those who make it. Add whatever else belongs on that list and drop whatever does not.

What I want from you is the ordering and the defense of it. Which questions are upstream of which — which, if settled, would settle or dissolve the others, and which are downstream disputes that will look different once something else is resolved? Which of them can this project actually settle, which can it not settle no matter how clean the results, and which are already settled well enough that a researcher assigned to them would be wasted? Where a question is worth a full-time person for five years, say what that person would be doing in year three; where it is worth one good paper and no more, say so. Then tell me what evidence over the next two or three years would move a question up or down the ranking, and which popular question I should advise the foundation to deprioritize against the field's expectations. No bibliography — I want the argument, not the literature.
