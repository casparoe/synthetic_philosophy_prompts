In a running debate about whether university should be tuition-free — the German and Nordic arrangements set against the US and UK fee-and-debt regimes — the phrase 'education is a public good' keeps doing all the work. It gets used both to justify full public funding and to wave away the objection that free tuition is regressive, since it subsidizes the children of the well-off. I suspect the phrase is smuggling several different claims, and I want to know which one is doing the work each time. Lay out, as a menu I can bring back to the debate, the distinct precise theses 'education is a public good' could be expressing — ranging from the technical sense economists give 'public good' to the looser senses in which it means publicly funded, ethically required, or a precondition of democratic citizenship — and for each reading say whether it is actually true of education, whether it is a substantive claim or a platitude, and whether it would genuinely support the conclusion (free tuition, full state funding) that it is being made to support. Flag where the technical and colloquial senses come apart, and say which reading is most likely in play when someone uses the phrase to end the argument.
