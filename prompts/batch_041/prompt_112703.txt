I have a standing argument with a friend of mine — an internist by training, and a far more patient reader of philosophy than I am — about whether 'disease' carves nature at the joints or is a policy wearing declarative clothing. Rather than keep trading intuitions, I have spent two years collecting his verdicts, one case at a time, and I would like to reverse-engineer the rule he is actually running rather than the one he says he is running.

His verdicts so far:

- Type 1 diabetes in a nine-year-old: a disease.
- Nearsightedness in a teenager, corrected with glasses, no complaints: a disease.
- Stage 1 hypertension in an otherwise healthy forty-year-old, no symptoms, no organ damage: a disease.
- Three months of intense but uncomplicated grief after a spouse's death, with no measurable dysfunction in any organ system: not a disease. Pressed on this, he says the duration is doing no work — he would give the same verdict at eighteen months if nothing else changed.
- A healthy six-foot-eight man with no endocrine or skeletal abnormality: not a disease.
- A five-foot-four man, likewise with no endocrine or skeletal abnormality, who is intensely distressed about his height: not a disease.
- Blocked fallopian tubes in a thirty-five-year-old woman who wants children and is devastated by the diagnosis: a disease.

Through all of it he maintains that he is not making value judgments — that he is describing the body, and that whether anyone minds is a separate question for somebody else.

Next week he has agreed to give me verdicts on two cases he has not seen:

1. Blocked fallopian tubes discovered incidentally during an unrelated surgery, in a thirty-three-year-old woman who has never wanted children, is untroubled by the finding, and declines any intervention.
2. A forty-five-year-old man with a normal examination, normal cardiac function, and no detectable abnormality of any organ system, whose polygenic risk score for coronary artery disease places him in the top one percent of the population; his physician has started him on a statin, and he is mildly anxious about the whole thing.

What I want from you:

1. His most likely verdict on each new case, stated plainly, even if the honest answer is that it depends on which rule he is running.
2. The candidate rules that fit his existing verdicts, each stated precisely enough that I could compute its output on a case it has never seen. Include rules that fit the data as well as the obvious ones do but that he would never put in those words himself.
3. What each surviving rule predicts for the two new cases, and which rule I should actually believe, with your reasons.
4. Whether his self-description — a value-free description of the body — survives contact with his own verdicts, and if it does not, exactly where it breaks.
5. The single further case that would best separate the surviving rules, and what it would have to come out as under each.

Two constraints. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the rules and the reasoning, and I am not going to look anything up. And do not assume he is running a named position merely because the name is famous — if an unnamed rule fits his verdicts better than the famous ones do, say so and defend it.

Please answer in American English.
