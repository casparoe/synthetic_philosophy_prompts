I'm a science and ideas journalist, and for the past fortnight I've been reporting on a public argument that has left me genuinely unsure whether the two sides are disagreeing at all. It started at a festival panel on neuroscience and criminal justice. A researcher, summarising the readiness-potential work, told the audience that in the cases he studies the brain's preparation precedes any conscious decision, and concluded that the man in question could not have done otherwise — hence, he said, the whole vocabulary of blame is a fiction. A barrister on the same panel, visibly irritated, replied that of course he could have done otherwise: nothing and nobody was stopping him, the door was unlocked, and he walked through it. Both remarks have been quoted endlessly since, as though they were flatly contradictory, and I suspect they may not even be about the same thing.

What I want from you is a careful disambiguation rather than a verdict on the free will problem itself. Treat the sentence 'he could not have done otherwise', and its positive twin, as a piece of language whose meaning is doing all the work, and lay out the distinct precise claims it might be making. Consider, at least, the readings that turn on what kind of possibility 'could' is expressing — physical, causal, nomological, epistemic, practical, moral; on whether the claim concerns the agent's capacities or his opportunities; on whether it asserts that the world was genuinely open at the moment of action or merely that we did not know how it would go; on whether the relevant comparison is with the same agent in the same circumstances or with a differently constituted agent; on whether the phrase is a description of the agent or already an exculpatory verdict in disguise; on whether it means nothing more than that no obstacle stood in his way, as against the much stronger claim that his doing otherwise is consistent with the whole of the past together with the laws of nature; and on any further readings you judge the phrase to have been made to bear. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a substantive but dubious one, or straightforwardly false — and, crucially, which of them would actually support the researcher's conclusion that blame is a fiction, and which would support the barrister's.

Please approach this as a philosopher of action who is also familiar with how the criminal law handles these phrases, and reason it through rather than reaching for the standard textbook oppositions. Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you list the candidate readings before you evaluate any of them, then give the menu itself as a numbered list, and finish with a paragraph telling me which reading each of my two panellists most plausibly had in mind, whether they were in fact contradicting one another, where the genuine compatibilist–incompatibilist fault line falls once the slogan has been separated out, and which single reading — if any — would make the sentence do the work the researcher needed it to do. Please write in British English, and there is no need for citations or a reading list; I want the distinctions, not the scholarship.
