I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy writing on moral responsibility, and I have been drafted to run a single seventy-five-minute session of an interdisciplinary reading group: eight people, mostly philosophy graduate students but with two psychiatry residents and one law student among them, none of whom have worked on free will before and all of whom read Strawson's Freedom and Resentment once, quickly, the night before. I do not want you to explain the essay to me. I want a plan for how I should teach it, built around dependencies: which idea has to be installed before the next one can even be heard, and why. My own sense is that the entire session turns on getting the participant stance in place before determinism is so much as mentioned, and that the tempting shortcut, which is to open with the free will problem and then present Strawson as one more answer to it, quietly destroys the point of the essay; tell me whether I am wrong about that, and if I am right, tell me what has to come between the participant stance and the distinction between excusing someone for what they did and exempting them from the demand altogether, and what has to come after that, and in what order, with a rough allocation of the seventy-five minutes. Assume the room will resist any move that looks like an appeal to how we happen to talk.

For the carrying example I have a candidate I want you to assess and, if it fails, replace. Rather than the usual cases of the very young, the severely impaired, or the appallingly badly brought up, I was thinking of anchoring the session on a psychiatric patient who is furious that his psychiatrist has stopped blaming him for his relapses, who experiences the therapeutic stance as a demotion and a refusal to treat him as an adult. My hope is that this makes the objective attitude feel like something one can be wronged by rather than something obviously humane, and that it forces the audience to ask whether the resentment is intelligible and what that shows about the priority of the participant stance. Tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack for that work to get done, whether mine has it, and where exactly it should be introduced. I also want to know whether to open the session with the question of whether one could resent a chatbot, using it as a hook, or to hold it back as the closing test, given that in this room it will collapse within a minute into the question of machine consciousness.

Then map the misconceptions for me. My guesses are these: that the argument is a can't-help-it argument, that reactive attitudes are just moral judgments or just emotions, that the objective attitude is simply dehumanization, that Strawson is quietly proving we are free, and that the pessimist is a strawman with no serious reply. Tell me which of these I have misjudged, which ones are in fact defensible readings I should not be correcting, which ones I have missed entirely, and for each the precise point in the sequence where it must be headed off rather than left to fester. Finally, give me the closing check for understanding, ideally one question or a short pair whose wrong answers are diagnostic rather than merely wrong, so that I can tell apart a room that has genuinely grasped the participant stance from a room that has only learned the vocabulary; and tell me what I should openly refuse to open at this level, including the dispute over whether the essay's argument is transcendental or an appeal to natural fact, the later refinements in the secondary literature, and the standard counterexamples to the claim that responsibility requires alternative possibilities, along with a way of refusing that does not feel like evasion.
