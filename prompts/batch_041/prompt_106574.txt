I have been having a long-running argument with a friend in my department about what she calls free-floating rationales — the idea, which she sums up as reasons without reasoners, that a bit of the world can be the way it is because that way works, and can be explained by a reason that nothing anywhere entertains. I think I believe in them, but I cannot get my verdicts on particular cases to line up behind a single principle, and I am supposed to turn this into a short paper for a seminar on functions and teleology, so I would like you to find the principle, if there is one. Here is what my gut says. A bacterium swimming up a sugar gradient is doing something for a reason — it is going toward food — and the reason is really there, even though the bacterium represents neither the food nor any goal. A beaver's dam is built to a height that holds back the right amount of water, and the reason for that height is real even though the beaver never works it out. Over the course of an infection your immune system ends up with antibodies that fit the pathogen, and again the fit is no accident, and the reason — the shape of the thing — is written down nowhere. An ant colony sends out foragers roughly in proportion to how much food each trail is bringing in, and that allocation tracks a fact about the world that no ant knows. Those four I count as genuine cases. Now the others. A river cuts the channel that does the least work, and I say that is just physics: the river is not doing anything for a reason, and the least-work channel is not a rationale. A slime mould relaxing into a near-shortest network between oat flakes gets the same verdict — it merely settles, it does not solve. A thermostat switching the furnace on is not acting for a reason; the reason belongs to whoever built it. A clever thermostat that learns my weekly schedule and preheats on the days I am actually home is, I insist, still not tracking a reason, even though it now answers to a fact about me — and here I begin to feel the pull of my own principle, since the bacterium is also just a loop that follows a gradient. A smoke detector likewise has no rationale of its own; that smoke means fire is something the manufacturer knows, not the detector. And when I turn to a common-law rule that has survived for centuries because it does something useful that no judge ever articulated, I genuinely do not know what to say.

The principle I keep reaching for is this: for there to be a free-floating rationale, the thing must be the upshot of a process that generates variants, retains some rather than others because they do the job better, and passes the improvement on, so that the fit between thing and job is explained by selection rather than by a mind. On that principle the bacterium, the beaver, the immune system and the ants qualify, since each is the product of a lineage in which better-tracking variants out-reproduced worse ones, while the river, the slime mould, the thermostats and the smoke detector do not, because they are either pure physics or artefacts whose selection happened once, in a designer's head. But two of my verdicts now look like prejudice. The beaver and the river are both mindless processes that fit a shape to a function; I am letting replication rather than reason-giving do the work, and I cannot say why that is not arbitrary. Worse, an antenna shape grown by an evolutionary algorithm — variants, differential retention by a fitness function, inheritance, all present — has exactly the lineage my principle demands, and yet I want to deny it a free-floating rationale, because the job was set by the engineers. A friend meanwhile tells me the whole notion is just the biological idea of a proper function, the function a thing was selected for, dressed up; if that is right, I have relabelled my problem rather than solved it.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of rival principles, that best captures the verdicts above; a precise statement of which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to abandon, and why that abandonment is or is not tolerable; and then its verdicts on cases I have not put to it — the evolved antenna, the common-law rule, a market price that in some sense says a commodity is scarce, a neural network's learned policy that works for reasons no one can state, a wasp's sting. If the notion of a free-floating rationale turns out to be a manner of speaking we should drop in favour of just talking about causes and functions, say so plainly rather than handing me a tidy formula that hides the confusion. Please tell me how confident you are in the principle you land on, and single out the step or the case where you are least sure.
