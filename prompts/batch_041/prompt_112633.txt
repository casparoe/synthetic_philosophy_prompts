I work on causal inference for a living — structural causal models, directed acyclic graphs, do-calculus, identification of effects from observational data — and my grasp of metaphysics is that of a tourist. I keep encountering the causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism and I want to see it stated in the vocabulary I actually use. So please do two things.

First, take the argument as its defenders present it — mental properties supervene on physical properties without being identical to them; the physical domain is causally closed; there is no systematic overdetermination; hence a mental property is either reduced to its physical base or excluded from causing the physical events it appears to cause — and restate it as faithfully as you can using my machinery: variables and their parents, structural equations, exogenous noise, interventions, screening off, total versus direct effects, modularity and invariance, identifiability, and the coarse-graining of a fine-grained variable into a higher-level one.

Second, give me the residue report. Which parts of the argument map cleanly onto the SCM picture — for instance, does supervenience become a structural equation in which the physical variable is the sole parent of the mental one, and does multiple realizability become a many-to-one coarse-graining? Which parts translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my framework at all? I suspect the causal-closure premise, the no-overdetermination principle, and any notion of causation as production rather than as difference-making under intervention. For each item of residue, say whether it shows the metaphysical argument doing work my framework cannot express — so that the loss is my framework's defect — or whether the argument was trading on a notion of causation that loses its grip once the situation is written down as a graph. If your translation has to presuppose interventionism about causation in order to go through, flag that as a distortion rather than quietly treating it as part of the translation. I care less about whether the argument is sound than about what it looks like when I try to say it in my own terms, and what I lose in the attempt.
