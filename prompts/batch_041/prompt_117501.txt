I'm running a discussion group on personal identity and the next session is on uploading, specifically on gradual replacement. What I want is to compare two ways of cashing out "what matters in survival," worked through on cases rather than stated abstractly.

First view, call it the pattern view: what matters is that a certain information-processing pattern is preserved. It doesn't matter what the pattern is implemented in, so a faithful silicon copy of you is you.

Second view, the process view: what matters is that one and the same causal process keeps running without a break. A copy, however faithful, is a new process and so a new person; but swapping the parts out one at a time can be survival, because the process never stops.

No citations or reading list please, I'll add those myself. What I want is both views applied to the whole ladder below, and then a straight verdict on which of the two handles the ladder better (or wether each just gets a different part of it right). The cases:

1. Scan and destroy: the brain is read out, the original destroyed, a perfect copy is built elsewhere and switched on.
2. Plain gradual replacement: neurons swapped one at a time for silicon units doing the same job, no gaps, nothing left over.
3. Fade-over: silicon units are installed alongside the biological ones and take over their function while the biological ones are still sitting there but idle; only after that are the biological ones switched off, one at a time.
4. Build-then-cutover: an entire second system is built, run in parallel and kept in sync with your brain, and at the end the original is switched off.
5. Reversal: the replacement gets half way, then is run backwards and the original tissue goes back in.
6. Same outputs, different guts: the silicon units match the neurons' input-output behaviour, but by a totally seperate internal method — say a giant lookup table rather than whatever the neurons were doing.

For each case I want what each view says, and where (if anywhere) the two actually come apart. Some of them they'll agree on, and I'd rather you just say so than manufacture a disagreement. Give special attention to case 3 versus case 4: both have two things running at once, so if the process view treats them differently, tell me what's really doing the work there — continuity of the process, or just a lingering preference for the biological parts.

Then give me a table, one row per case, with the columns: case | pattern view's verdict | process view's verdict | do the views differ here (yes/no, one line) | which verdict is more plausible and why (one line). Add a final row labelled overall, saying which view survives the ladder better, plus one sentence on what the ladder exposes that neither view admits out loud.
