I've been trying to pin down what the for-want-of-a-nail family of historical arguments actually needs from the physics they borrow. Consider a deterministic system with sensitive dependence on initial conditions, so that nearby trajectories separate exponentially. That fact by itself seems to underwrite almost nothing about whether some small perturbation changed the outcome, and I suspect most invocations of chaos in history are quietly trading on the gap.

Please give me a careful conceptual explanation built around the following taxonomy, which I've been using to keep my own thinking straight:

- amplifying contingency: a perturbation whose effect keeps growing in the observable you care about;
- selecting contingency: a perturbation that fixes which attractor, basin, or regime the system ends up in, after which the effect is bounded but irreversible;
- absorbing contingency: a perturbation whose effect decays or is averaged away by the dynamics;
- constitutive contingency: a perturbation that changes the system's rules or parameters rather than its trajectory within fixed rules — the familiar story about institutions and increasing returns, where early choices raise the cost of reversal.

What I want explained: (1) which of these the claim that history could have gone otherwise actually requires, and whether sensitive dependence is needed at all, or whether selecting plus constitutive contingency already suffices; (2) how one could in principle tell selecting from absorbing contingency for a particular episode, given that basin boundaries are typically invisible from inside a single trajectory, and what kind of evidence would settle it; (3) what work the distinction between unpredictability in practice and metaphysical indeterminism is doing here — can a fully deterministic history be contingent in the historian's sense, and in which of the four senses; and (4) whether the evolutionary version of the same argument transfers, or whether selection there changes the structure of the question rather than just the substrate.

I'd rather have the crux than a survey, and I'd like the load-bearing distinctions stated in a way I can actually apply to a concrete episode rather than as a list of positions. End with a single line beginning "Bottom line:" that names which of the four senses is doing the real work and states the takeaway in one sentence.
