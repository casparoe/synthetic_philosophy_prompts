I am the philosopher-in-residence at a small research centre — fifteen years of people modelling collective behaviour in markets, immune systems, cities and brains — and the centre is being wound up at the end of the month. At the closing dinner I have been asked to give the short speech: six to eight minutes, after the plates are cleared, before the founder says a few words. The room is genuinely mixed. A third of it is the modellers themselves, who will notice immediately if I say anything loose about causation, levels or what a simulation can show; a third is funders and university administrators who want to be told the money did something; the rest is spouses, partners and the founder's family, including two grandchildren in their early twenties who have never heard any of this. And the founder — the person who hired me, who has been the centre's whole personality — was diagnosed a few months ago with a condition that is quietly dismantling his memory, and everyone in the room knows it. I do not want to pretend that is not in the room, and I do not want to make it the subject. What I want the speech to do is give a verdict on the thing I have spent fifteen years being sceptical about in public: whether the higher-level patterns we spend our lives modelling — the clusters, the moods, the institutions, the selves — actually push anything around, or whether they are only the shape the small things happen to make as they bounce.

The thought experiment I keep coming back to is the careenium: a vast box of tiny magnetic marbles — simms — which bump and stick and occasionally clump into much larger, slower, semi-stable clusters, the simmballs, which then appear to move under their own steam and to shove other clusters about. Hofstadter's question was whether the simmballs shove the simms or the simms shove the simmballs, and I have never been satisfied by the usual answers, which either make the simmball a mere summary (so that nothing is ever caused by a pattern, only by a marble) or else quietly smuggle in a second kind of force. I want you to take a line and defend it, not to hold the ring. In particular, I would like the speech to do four things, and to do them in the voice of someone talking to friends rather than writing a paper. First, put the thought experiment and the problem in your own words, plainly enough that the funders and the grandchildren follow it, and without leaning on technical vocabulary you are not sure of — no citations, no names of articles, no reading lists, and if you are unsure of a term, describe the thing instead of using the term. Second, work through at least one concrete test case; I have two I would like you to use. The first is the frictionless careenium: strip out the damping, let the simms keep all their energy and never forget a collision, and ask whether simmballs can form at all, let alone push anything — I have come to think the interesting claim is not that higher levels are real but that they require the lower level to be forgetful, and I want to see whether that survives being said out loud. The second is what a colleague calls phantom simmballs: clusters that are statistically robust — you can predict them, you can point to them, the numbers are real — but which do not persist or track anything, so that treating them as agents looks like superstition with error bars. Third, say what you think distinguishes a simmball worth taking seriously from a phantom, and give me a criterion a sceptical modeller in the audience could actually use; I would rather have a rough test that bites than a careful formulation that does not. Fourth, land it: the last two minutes should turn to why any of this matters at a dinner like this one, without pretending that the founder's illness is a tidy illustration of a philosophical point, and without the consolation that the pattern simply continues somewhere he cannot be reached.

Please write the speech as delivered prose, in British English throughout, at roughly 900 to 1,100 words, with the argument carrying the shape of the speech rather than sitting in one dense paragraph in the middle of it. It should be honest enough that the modellers do not wince and warm enough that his family are not left out of the room, and I would rather it ended on a verdict I could defend in an argument the next morning than on a line that merely sounds wise.
