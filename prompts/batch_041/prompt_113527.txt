I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Hofstadter's careenium, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of our disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right. The setup, as I understand it: imagine a billiard-table-like arena containing an enormous number of tiny magnetic marbles, which Hofstadter calls simms. They careen around, collide, and occasionally stick to one another, forming transient clusters — simmballs — that drift, bump into other simmballs, merge, split, and sometimes appear to attract or repel one another at a distance. Watching from above, you can describe the whole scene in terms of simm-level collisions, or you can describe it in terms of simmballs pursuing, fleeing, and jostling one another. Hofstadter's question, which he pressed in a paper whose title asks who shoves whom around inside the careenium, is whether the simmballs are doing any of the shoving, or whether all the causal work happens at the simm level and simmball vocabulary is just a very good compression. My friend holds that simmballs genuinely shove — that the higher-level pattern is a real locus of causal power, and that unless you say this you cannot make sense of the everyday claim that a person's beliefs and meanings push their neurons around. I hold that only simms shove, and that simmball talk describes a pattern rather than introducing another agent into the story. What makes it maddening is how much we agree on: the microdynamics, the predictive and compressive power of the simmball description, and the fact that no new force is injected at the higher level.

As far as I can tell this is the old causal-exclusion and downward-causation dispute wearing a billiard-table costume, and it has the same stalled quality — each side's arguments look to the other side like they simply assume what is at issue. The exclusion-style argument says that if the simm-level facts suffice for every collision, then citing a simmball adds nothing causal and is at best a redescription; the pattern-causation reply says that sufficiency at one level of description does not crowd out causation at another, and that asking which level is really doing the work is either confused or answerable only relative to an explanatory interest. My working hypothesis, and I want you to treat it as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that we are running together at least three different questions under one heading: first, a verbal question about what the words cause and shove are being asked to pick out; second, a question about explanatory relevance, namely which description answers the why-question we happen to be asking; and third, a substantive metaphysical question about whether higher-level properties are real, irreducible, and efficacious. If that is right, some of our impasse is verbal and some of it is not, and I would like to know which parts are which.

So please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicating it. Say what each side is actually committed to, not the cartoon versions, and tell me what kind of disagreement this is: verbal, a clash of explanatory aims, a genuine metaphysical dispute about causal efficacy at higher levels, or two groups answering different questions under a single name. If part of it is verbal, say exactly which part and what a regimented version of the question would look like; if part of it is substantive, say what would settle it — a formal result, a distinction, a change in the question — or argue that nothing would, and why. Tell me also whether the careenium is a fair model for the mind at all, or whether the analogy quietly assumes the conclusion by building in a clean micro/macro boundary that brains do not have. Be explicit about how confident you are in the diagnosis, and say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I would much rather have a diagnosis with its weak points marked than a balanced survey.
