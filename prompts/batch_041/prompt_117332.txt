I have been attempting to formulate a principle for when the use of force in support of a rule is just, and my verdicts on a set of cases continue to pull me in different directions. I am also trying to determine whether the principle I arrive at is a version of Pascal's view or a departure from it. Consider the following cases and my verdicts. A city council taxes residents to fund public defenders for indigent defendants; a wealthy resident refuses to pay, and the council seizes his assets. I think this is just. A neighborhood association, with no legal authority, fines a homeowner for painting his house a color it dislikes and, when he refuses, hires a contractor to repaint it. I think this is unjust, even though the color rule is widely shared. A revolutionary tribunal, after a coup, tries and executes a deposed dictator for ordering massacres. The trial is not conducted under the old law. I think this is just, or at least not unjust. A creditor obtains a court order to seize a poor family's home because they missed two payments on a payday loan. The court is legitimate, the law is clear, and the family did default. I think this is unjust, and the force is unjust, even though it follows the established rule. An occupying army imposes a curfew that prevents looting after a war. The curfew is resented and not authorized by any local law. I think it is not just, but it is better than no order, and I do not think resisting it is unjust. A parent forcibly restrains a child from touching a hot stove. I think this is legitimate, but it is not a matter of justice; it is necessity. A mob beats a suspected thief who did in fact steal. I think this is unjust, even if the mob's norm against theft is shared. A workers' cooperative seizes a factory that the owner has legally closed, and runs it. I am unsure: I feel the seizure is not just, but I also feel the closure was not just, and I cannot say the workers are simply wrong. I tentatively think that force is just when it is necessary to maintain the conditions of justice, and unjust when it is used to enforce a controversial conception of the good. But this does not handle the creditor case, because enforcing contracts is a condition of justice, yet the seizure feels unjust.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts? For each clause, say which case motivates it. Then say what the principle predicts for three further cases: an international court orders a powerful state to pay reparations and enforces it with sanctions; a workers' blockade of a factory prevents a legally authorized closure; a state uses force to suppress a separatist movement that has widespread local support. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up the principle or the verdict. And say how the resulting view relates to Pascal's claims about justice and force—specifically his claim that justice without force cannot accomplish anything and that force without justice is a form of tyranny, his claim that custom is the source of all equity, and his claim that possession is the only title and that we obey the majority not because it is more just but because it has more force—and whether Pascal would accept the principle.
