I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and my team treats two rules of thumb as if they were the same rule. The first is Chesterton's fence: you may not tear down an existing thing until you can say why it was put there. The second is the Burkean thought that inherited arrangements are the sediment of many generations' hard-won experience, so the burden of proof falls on whoever wants to change them rather than on whoever wants to keep them. I suspect these are not the same rule at all, and that the second is much the stronger of the two.

Please compare them: what does each actually assert, what does each treat as evidence that what already exists is worth keeping, and where exactly do they come apart? I'm especially interested in whether Chesterton's rule, read strictly, is a rationalist principle a Burkean ought to distrust. Once I can state a fence's purpose and judge it obsolete, Chesterton appears to license its removal — whereas Burke's point seems to be that the reasons for an inherited practice are usually not fully articulable by any one generation, so my confident account of why the fence is there is precisely what he would tell me not to trust. Then say which rule, if either, is the better guide for deciding what to delete from a large legacy codebase, and what each one gets wrong.
