My epistemology reading group spent almost the whole of last week's session on a single unresolved argument between two of its members, Dana and Ivo, and we broke up without a verdict. I was a bystander throughout — I did not take a side then and I am not taking one now — and what I want is the exchange itself adjudicated, rather than the underlying topic settled in the abstract: which of the moves below are sound, which are not, and what each of the two is missing. Here is my reconstruction, as faithful as I can make it.

1. Dana opened with a principle she called the margin requirement: you know that P only if your evidence rules out the nearby possibilities in which P is false — that is, you could not easily have been wrong. She said this is precisely what separates knowledge from a lucky guess. She then offered a second principle, which she called KK: if you know that P, you are in a position to know that you know that P. On her view the two are made for each other, since the very margin that puts you in a position to know P is a margin you are in a position to see that you have; the higher-order knowledge comes free with the lower.

2. Ivo attacked the margin requirement. Take any condition that comes in degrees — feeling warm, hearing a faint tone, being sure of a name — and arrange a long sequence of cases in which each adjacent pair is indistinguishable to the subject while the two ends are plainly different. If the margin requirement holds, he said, then the subject never knows that they are in the condition anywhere in the sequence, because at every point the adjacent case is compatible with their evidence. Yet at the clearly-warm end they obviously do know. So the margin requirement proves far too much and must be false.

3. Dana: the sequence shows only that knowledge has a fuzzy edge, not that it fails everywhere. At the ends you know; in the middle there is a zone in which you neither know that you are in the condition nor know that you are not. Nothing in the margin requirement forbids that, and a sharp boundary would be the surprising result, not a gradual one. She added that she had claimed KK for knowledge, not luminosity for warmth, and that the two are not the same thing.

4. Ivo: KK just is a luminosity claim — for the condition "knows that P." So if the margin requirement is right, the same sequence argument applies to it. The condition "knows that P" has a borderline too: there are cases in which you know and cases in which you do not, with a region in between. Apply the margin requirement there and you get the same result — cases in which you know that P but do not know that you know it, and, iterating, an ever-more-demanding tower of higher-order margins that no finite subject could satisfy. So Dana cannot keep both principles; she must give one up.

5. Dana: the tower is not infinite, because the higher steps add no new content. Knowing is factive: if you know that P, then P is true. To know that you know P you therefore do not need a fresh and narrower margin around a new condition; you need only to rule out the nearby cases in which you believe that you know P but do not. And once you know P those cases are already ruled out, since a case in which you believe that you know P but do not is a case in which you do not know P, and you have excluded those. So the second margin is free and the tower collapses after a single step.

6. Ivo: factivity does not buy you the higher-order margin; it tells you only that P is true when you know it. The higher-order margin requires ruling out the nearby cases in which you believe that you know P but do not, and those are not the same as the cases in which you believe P but P is false. Dana's step 5 runs the two together.

7. Dana: then we simply disagree about whether "being in a position to know" has a borderline at all. She says it does not have the relevant kind, because it is a normative status rather than a sensory condition; you cannot be a little bit in a position to know the way you can be a little bit warm. Ivo says every condition has a borderline and this one is no exception. Neither of us could move the other.

I would like the following, and please be specific. (a) Is the margin requirement as Dana states it defensible, and does Ivo's sequence argument in step 2 refute it or merely locate its fuzzy edge? (b) Does Dana's step 5 defuse Ivo's regress, or does it conflate the margin for a first-order condition with the margin for a higher-order one, as Ivo charges in step 6? (c) Is Ivo right in step 4 that KK is simply a luminosity claim about the condition "knows that P," so that Dana cannot hold both principles together? (d) In step 3, was Dana entitled to separate KK for knowledge from luminosity for warmth, or was that a fudge? (e) Is there a defensible version of either principle that survives the exchange, and what exactly would it have to say? Please rate each of the seven numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a one- or two-sentence justification for each rating, then say what each party is missing and who, on balance, has the better of the argument.
