I'm in a graduate seminar on the ethics of promising, and rather than simply ask my professor for her theory, I've been trying to reverse-engineer it from the verdicts she hands down on the cases we discuss. Her track record so far is this. A loan I promised to repay, where the lender later told me, before I had paid, that I should forget about it: I am no longer bound. A promise I made at knifepoint to hand over my wallet: not binding. A promise to write a former student a recommendation letter that I have since realized would require me to misrepresent his work: not binding. A promise I made alone at the bedside of a dying friend, who had asked me for it and was relying on me, to burn his private papers after his death: still binding, she insists, even though he is now dead, no one else knows the promise was made, and no one will ever know whether I kept it. A promise to meet a colleague for lunch, which she still expects and will turn up for, though nothing of consequence turns on it and I would rather stay home: still binding; I do not get to skip it merely because I would prefer not to go. And a promise I made as a child, in character, during a game, to a friend who knew it was a game: not binding.

The case I actually care about is one I have not yet brought up. Some years ago my elderly neighbour, then fully lucid, extracted from me a promise that I would visit her every Sunday and read to her, and I gave it. Her memory has since declined to the point where she no longer recognizes me, no longer remembers asking, and no longer expects anything from me; worse, when I do go she is often agitated and appears to want me gone, though she cannot articulate why. The visits cost me several hours each week, and they no longer seem to give her any pleasure. I have kept the promise so far, but I want to know whether I am still under it.

What I would like from you is a prediction, not a sermon. Tell me what verdict my professor will give on this new case. Then set out the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well — the ones I have been able to think of are that a promise binds because of the assurance or reliance it creates, because of the social practice or convention it invokes, because of the good it secures for the promisee or the wrong that breaking it would do, or simply because it is a promise and that is an end of the matter, though I suspect there are others — say what each rule predicts about the neighbour, give me your best guess as to which rule she is actually running and why, and name the further case that would best separate the rules that survive, that is, the case on which the plausible candidates disagree and which would therefore tell me which one she holds.
