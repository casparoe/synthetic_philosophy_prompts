I sit on the "values council" at a small AI lab — five of us who have to sign off on default behaviors before a model ships. I've been the annoying one in the room for two years now, mostly because I read LessWrong and a pile of rat-adjacent blogs all through high school and college and never fully recovered. So I've absorbed all the old arguments about CEV and "the AI should do what we'd want if we were smarter and knew more," and I've come to think most of them quietly assume there's a fact of the matter about what humanity wants, which is doing an enormous amount of unexamined work.

Here's the decision I have to write up by Friday. We're shipping one model into two channels: a consumer chat app, and a licensing deal where a hospital network and a mid-size city government will run it over their own policy documents and patient-facing materials. Which means it'll be giving answers that affect people who never typed anything into it and never will. Our policy has to say what it does with contested moral and political questions — the examples that keep coming up are whether to tell your family you've left their religion, whether eating meat is wrong, whether assisted dying should be legal, whether to report a coworker to immigration, who to vote for.

The five proposals on the table, each with a champion on the council:

(a) The Switzerland option: don't take sides, lay out the strongest arguments on each side.
(b) Mirror the user: infer their values from the conversation and answer from inside them, flagging when the answer would come out differently under other values.
(c) Aggregate: answer per a large representative survey of humans, plurality rule. One of my colleagues keeps calling this "the democratic option."
(d) Veil of ignorance: answer per what people would pick without knowing their position.
(e) Local deference: answer per the deploying jurisdiction's law and majority culture.

My problem with (a) is that refusing to answer is itself an answer with distributional effects — the hospital network will read our non-answer as license. And my problem with (c), which is where I keep landing, is that shipping a default changes the distribution we'd be sampling: if a billion people ask whether eating meat is wrong and get their region's plurality answer back, the regional pluralities in ten years aren't the ones we measured. On top of that, the people whose values we'd aggregate, the people affected by the answers, and the people who'll exist after we ship are three seperate sets.

What I want from you is the memo I'll circulate to the other four. Recommend one of (a)-(e) or something else, and give a rule concrete enough that the engineers can write tests against it rather than argue about vibes. Include the strongest case for the option you're rejecting, and tell me what evidence would make you switch. If two of the proposals turn out to collapse into each other once you make them precise, say which and why.

No memo header, no background section, no restating any of this. Open with the recommendation in your first sentence and take it from there, around 900 words.
