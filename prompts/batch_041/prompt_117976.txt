I'm teaching Macbeth next term and want to try something wierd with it. There's an old logic puzzle, the surprise exam -- teacher announces a surprise test for next week, the class rules out Friday (they'd know by Thursday night), then Thursday, and so on down to Monday, so they conclude there can't be a test, and then it lands on Wednesday and surprises them anyway. I want a reading of the play through that puzzle rather than the usual fate-versus-free-will angle. Macbeth is told no one born of a woman can harm him and that he's safe until a forest walks up to his castle; he does the students' move, crosses off the impossibilities, and stops worrying. So is he running the backward induction, and does the play show it failing for the same reason the students' reasoning fails, or a different reason? I know one of the fussy bits in the puzzle is that the announcement has to be common knowledge before the students can reason at all -- the prophecies are more like a private tip-off, so does that change anything? And where does the analogy just break: the witches mean to trick him and the teacher doesn't, and he learns the forest is moving before he dies, which the announcement is supposed to rule out. Be honest in both directions -- what the puzzle lights up in the play, and what the play does that the puzzle can't. Which scenes would you put in front of a class that's just read the puzzle?
