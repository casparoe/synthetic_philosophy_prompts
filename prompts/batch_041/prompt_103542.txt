I hold the Nyāya view that testimony (śabda) is a basic pramāṇa — a source of knowledge in its own right, not a disguised form of inference or perception. On this account, when a hearer grasps the meaning of an utterance and the speaker is a reliable authority (āpta), knowledge of what was asserted arises from that grasp, with the speaker's reliability functioning as a condition on the knowledge rather than as an inferential premise. I would like a map of what would count against this. Lay out the defeaters and rank them by how decisive they would be: the reductionist argument that every testimonial episode is really an inference from the speaker's reliability to the truth of her claim; cases in which a hearer appears to know something on testimony without any belief about the speaker's reliability, or in the teeth of a belief that she is unreliable; knowledge drawn from a text or a dead language, where there is no speaker available to be an āpta; and the gap between merely understanding a sentence and accepting it, which the account seems to need to bridge but does not. For each, say whether a Nyāya defender would concede it as a genuine threat or dismiss it, and be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a defect or the unavoidable price of treating testimony as basic.
