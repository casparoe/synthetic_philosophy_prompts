I am a junior at a high school with a small philosophy club that meets in the library on Thursdays, and this term we are working through the ethics of political leadership. I have been asked to prepare the handout for our next session, and rather than produce another summary of the usual positions I would like the handout to be a taxonomy in the form of a tree: a reader should be able to start from a few plain questions and arrive at a named position without ever having to decide in advance which label they like. Our club is argumentative and reasonably well read for a school group — several members do Model United Nations and one has read some Machiavelli for a history essay — but none of us has formally studied moral philosophy, so every branch should be phrased as a question one could answer about a concrete case rather than as an invitation to endorse a doctrine. I am particularly keen that the tree be built out of the logical space of possible answers rather than out of a list of famous names, with the names placed into that space afterwards, and that any combination of answers nobody seems to occupy be flagged and diagnosed: is it empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has explored it?

The specific angle I want the taxonomy to take is this. I have noticed that when people argue about whether a political leader may do something morally wrong for the sake of a greater good, they slide between at least three different questions, and I want the tree to keep them apart. The first is whether the act is genuinely wrong at all or merely appears so — the consequentialist line that if the act was right, then nothing was dirtied. The second is whether, granting that it is wrong, the agent is nonetheless permitted, required, or forbidden to do it. The third, which our club keeps skipping, is what the agent owes afterwards — remorse, restitution, disclosure, resignation, or nothing at all — and whether that debt is owed to the victims, to the public, or to the agent's own character. I would also like the tree to handle two complications that the introductory material we have been given does not discuss: whether the dirt can attach to an office or an institution rather than to a person, and whether it matters that the decision was taken under time pressure with no real opportunity to deliberate.

Please make the tree roughly five to seven levels deep and end it in somewhere between eight and twelve leaves, each leaf carrying the name of a position where one exists — for instance the view that the problem dissolves once morality is properly understood, the absolutist refusal, the appeal to a distinct morality of roles or office, the tragic-heroic view that the leader must both do the wrong and bear the guilt for it, the lesser-evil view that justifies the act while trying to minimise the residue, and the deflationary view that dirty hands is a confusion produced by our having inherited two incompatible moral codes at once — together with one sentence on the commitment that each position will find hardest to defend. Use these cases as the concrete material for the questions, since our club will recognise them: a mayor who orders a raid knowing that a hostage will die, a finance minister who lies about the currency to stop a bank run, a peace envoy who offers amnesty to a man who ordered a massacre, and a head teacher who expels an innocent pupil in order to break up a gang. Where the tree cannot separate two positions, say so plainly rather than pretending that the questions do the work.
