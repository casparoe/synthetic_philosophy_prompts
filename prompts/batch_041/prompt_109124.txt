I'm a PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and I'm writing a chapter on the philosophical views Einstein developed in the last two decades of his life: his Spinozist determinism, his insistence that a deterministic outlook should make us more forgiving rather than less, and his repeated claim that he was religious in something like Spinoza's sense while rejecting a personal God. I want to use him as a test case for a broader question about how a thinker's settled commitments would have evolved under evidence and arguments he never encountered. Please act as a philosopher of mind with a historian's command of Einstein's writings, and reason carefully rather than reaching for a tidy summary.

Here is the exercise. Suppose Einstein were revived today with his faculties intact, given a few years to read up on the philosophy and neuroscience of free will, and then interviewed at length. What would he actually say? I don't want a generic "he was a determinist, so he'd say X." I want you to reconstruct how his specific commitments would interact with developments that postdate him, several of which sit awkwardly with his position. For instance: the readiness-potential experiments, and the more recent reanalyses arguing that the signal is better explained as noise accumulating to a threshold than as an unconscious decision forming before the agent becomes aware of it — would he treat this as confirming his view, as irrelevant to it, or as a confusion about what determinism even implies? The migration of the debate away from "does determinism threaten freedom?" toward manipulation and causal-exclusion arguments, which threaten freedom even in indeterministic worlds. The proposal, now defended by serious philosophers, that we keep the practices of holding people responsible while dropping desert entirely. The empirical literature claiming that believing in free will makes people behave better. And the newer cases nobody in his lifetime had to consider: brain implants and algorithms that produce a person's actions, and artificial systems that produce fluent first-person claims about their own agency.

Then tell me where his answers would be unstable — the points where two of his commitments pull apart, so that he'd have to choose or revise. Start by brainstorming the relevant commitments on both sides before you commit to a reading, and be explicit about which parts of your reconstruction are well supported by what he actually wrote and which are extrapolation. Do not give me a bibliography; I'm after the argument, not the reading list.
