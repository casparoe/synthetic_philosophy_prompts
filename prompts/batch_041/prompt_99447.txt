I've got no background in this stuff, just a person who reads too much, and I keep getting stuck on these "risk score" tools that decide who gets a scarce thing. My local version: the school district wants to score every kid for "might fall behind" and hand the limited tutoring slots to the top scorers. Groups in the district dont fall behind at the same rate to start with, and heres the knot I can't untie. You'd want the score to mean the same thing for a kid in either group (a 70 means a 70% chance, whoever you are). You'd want it to miss the same share of the kids who really do need help in each group. And you'd want it to wrongly flag the same share of kids who'd have been fine in each group. As I understand it you can have two of those but never all three once the groups start out at different rates.

So build me a decision tree / taxonomy of where people end up once they see that. Questions first, so someone can walk down it without knowing any of the names, and then the actual positions placed at the leaves, each with one line on its most awkward commitment. Things I'd want it to handle: (a) the person who says it's just a bad model and better features fix it — is that a seperate leaf from the person who says the different starting rate is itself manufactured by who gets watched or punished upstream, and does your tree tell them apart? (b) the flip in my example: a kid we miss costs more than a kid we wrongly tutor, so does the "which mistake do we eat" answer flip too, and where does that land? (c) whoever says this is a political choice about who absorbs which mistake, not a math result, and the follow-up question of who gets to make that call; (d) the person who says dont score the kids at all. Mark any leaves that come out empty and say why — mathematically impossible, or just that nobody's bothered — and flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate. Also, if a position only exists because the slots are scarce in the first place, note that, because "just fund more tutoring" seems to dissolve the whole problem and I never see it sitting on anyone's list. Keep it to about eight questions and no more than eight leaves.
