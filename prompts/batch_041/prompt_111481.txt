After thirty years in experimental physics I retired and have spent the last few years in a small philosophy discussion group; next month it falls to me to lead ninety minutes on the claim that preference is revealed by choice and that utility names nothing over and above the pattern in a person's choices. The group is eight or nine adults, mostly humanities graduates with no economics, and I have a whiteboard. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the idea to me: the order in which the components must come and why each depends on the one before it (I take it that something must be settled about choice versus inner state before consistency conditions can even be stated, and that the ordinal character of the resulting scale only becomes visible after that); the one case that should carry the whole thing, and what it must have and must not smuggle in — I had thought of a person whose choices over three options cycle, whom I then invite to pay a small sum to escape the cycle; tell me whether that is the right carrier, and what the group would need to have already accepted for it to land rather than merely amuse; the misconceptions to expect and the point in the sequence where each must be headed off, including utility as a quantity of felt satisfaction, the thought that inconsistent choices refute the theory rather than specify what the theory forbids, and my own physicist's temptation to go looking for a measurable magnitude; and the single question to put at the end that would show whether they have grasped that the content lies in restrictions on choice data rather than in any mental state — in particular whether they can see why, if preference is allowed to range over menus as well as items, almost any pattern can be rationalized, so that the empirical bite has to come from somewhere narrower. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so explicitly; I assume the representation result itself and the aggregation of ordinal scales across persons are both beyond a first pass.
