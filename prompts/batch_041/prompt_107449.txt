I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of language, and for the final portfolio I'd like to submit a poem rather than another essay. What has been nagging at me is the case we spent the better part of two weeks on: a speaker says that it is raining and that they do not believe it. Nothing in that is a contradiction — the two clauses can be true together, and when the same content is said of somebody else, or said about the speaker by somebody else, it is the most ordinary thing in the world; yet in the first person it is somehow unsayable, and every explanation I've been offered relocates the absurdity rather than dissolving it, whether it puts it in the norms of assertion, in the authority a speaker has over their own beliefs, or in the idea that a belief cannot take itself as its own object in that way. I want a poem that makes a reader feel that split rather than read a gloss on it.

So here is the brief. Write a poem of roughly twenty-four to thirty lines in a single speaker's voice, in loose blank verse or a repeating meter of your choosing, with one line that returns verbatim as a refrain at least three times. The subject matter should be as flat and ordinary as a weather report and the speaker's relation to it, and the poem must contain no formal contradiction anywhere: no line may deny another, the speaker may never take back or qualify what they earlier affirmed, and the words themselves must be such that a logician could not fault them. The absurdity has to be produced by the act of saying and not by what is said. To make that audible, build the poem as two panels of the same material: the second panel should repeat the first's content as closely as the meter allows, with the first person turned into the third — the same words about someone else, or about no one in particular — so that a reader hears identical content move from something a person cannot sincerely utter to something utterly banal. The refrain, unchanged across both panels, should therefore sound like two different sentences. End by returning to the opening line verbatim, so the poem finishes by doing what it started by doing, and leave the reader unable to decide whether the speaker has changed their mind or only their pronoun.

If it helps, you may let one of the refrain's returns carry the parallel case in which a speaker asserts something and then says they do not know it, since I think the two cases pull in the same direction; but the ordinary belief case should remain the spine of the poem. Someone who has studied the paradox should recognise the specific form at work and the asymmetry between the first person and the third; someone who has never heard of it should still find a poem about what it costs to say what you cannot believe.
