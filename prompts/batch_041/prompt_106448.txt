I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I am trying to get the concept of superrationality straight before I file a column that leans on it. A colleague who covers game theory handed me a short explainer as a favor, and I want to know how much of it I can stand behind. The passage reads:

'Superrationality, the idea introduced by Douglas Hofstadter, holds that when two agents are identical they are bound to arrive at the same choice, and so in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma each of them should cooperate rather than defect. Defection looks compelling only if you treat your opponent's decision as independent of your own; between identical agents it is not, so the usual argument for defection rests on a mistaken probability assignment. Superrationality is, in effect, Kant's categorical imperative translated into decision theory: it tells you to choose the act that would be best if everyone in your situation chose it too. The upshot is that the supposed dominance of defection in the Prisoner's Dilemma is an illusion.'

My hunch is that this is mostly right and slightly off in ways that would matter in print, but I cannot see where the seams are, and I do not want to repeat a distortion to an audience that includes people who know the literature. Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a game theorist or a philosopher of decision would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would reject outright; which parts are legitimate simplifications and which are misrepresentations the originator of the idea would disown; and whether the closing claim — that the dominance of defection is an illusion — actually follows from the parts that survive. Where the passage goes wrong, I would like the defensible version of the point rather than a correction in the abstract, and I would rather have the reasoning spelled out than a list of sources, since I need to be able to explain this in my own words to readers who have never opened a game-theory text.
