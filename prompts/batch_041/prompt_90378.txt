A colleague has drafted a handout for the undergraduate unit we co-teach, and I would like a second opinion before it reaches the students. The handout's account of Dennett's four grades of mind runs roughly as follows: 'Dennett arranges minds on a ladder that evolution climbs. Darwinian creatures are hardwired — natural selection does whatever thinking is required, and their behaviour is fixed by their genes. Skinnerian creatures are the next rung up: they learn, and Dennett names them for B. F. Skinner because operant conditioning is the whole story of their minds, leaving no room for anything inner. Popperian creatures cross a decisive threshold: they acquire an internal environment in which they can run simulations of possible actions and let the hypotheses die in their stead, and it is here that genuine mentality and consciousness first appear. Gregorian creatures, named for the psychologist Richard Gregory, are tool users — what sets us apart is that we fashion and deploy tools, from spears to smartphones. These four grades are a sequence of replacements, each superseding the last, so that a human being is a Gregorian creature and no longer a Popperian or a Skinnerian one; the whole point of the hierarchy is to show that evolution builds minds by degrees, without skyhooks.' I am fairly sure the general shape is right, but several of the details strike me as the sort of thing a popularizer writes when they have not read the original carefully, and I do not want to correct students against a handout that turns out to be defensible. Could you give me a careful audit? Tell me which claims Dennett would endorse, which are oversimplifications or conflations of two things he keeps apart, and which he would reject outright; where the handout has substituted a familiar idea for the one actually at work, say what the actual idea is and why the difference matters; and whether the closing claim about degrees and skyhooks can stand once the corrections are made. Write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a memo to a colleague.
