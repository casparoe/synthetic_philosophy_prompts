I run the intraoperative awareness review service at a mid-sized teaching hospital. When a patient says, after surgery, that they remember voices, the pressure of the tube, or pain from the table, my colleague — a psychologist who works on memory — and I are the two people who sit down with the patient, the anaesthetic record, and the surgeon's notes and try to establish what happened. We do this three or four times a month, and we are always under pressure: the verdict decides whether we treat the episode as a failure of the anaesthetic plan that has to be fixed in the protocol, what we say to the patient that will either repair or destroy their trust in us, and what we will be able to defend in writing to risk management and, in the worst case, to a court. Our current practice is informal and, I have come to think, incoherent. Two of us look at the same record and reach opposite conclusions, our arguments turn on hunches, and when someone asks what evidence would settle the matter, we change the subject.

What I want from you is a procedure my service could actually run — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and returns a verdict, and the point at which it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of judgment or of values rather than of evidence. The knot you have to take seriously is this. When a patient reports an experience, we habitually date the experience at the time of the event reported. But two very different stories fit the same report. On the first, the experience genuinely occurred then: the content — the voices, the sensation of the tube — was assembled and became part of what the patient was undergoing before any of it reached the machinery of memory and speech, so that the recollection, however partial, is a faithful record of something that really happened at that moment. On the second, the content was never experienced at the time in the form reported: there was a fragmentary or absent experience, and what is now the memory was manufactured later, in the recovery room or over weeks of retelling, by a system that smooths over gaps and is exquisitely responsive to what the interviewer suggests. One might call these the Orwellian and the Stalinesque readings, after the two ways a regime can falsify a record — by altering it after the fact, or by staging the event itself so that the record is true of what was staged. The laboratory demonstration that makes the problem vivid for me is the one with the two dots: a blue dot is flashed near the top of a screen and a red dot near the bottom a fraction of a second later, and observers report a single dot travelling across the gap and changing colour abruptly about halfway along its path — a change they place before the second dot was ever presented, which is the puzzle, since the first dot's colour could hardly have been revised to match a colour that had not yet appeared. Either the experience is put together early and the timing of experience is not what it seems, or it is put together late and the memory is not what it seems. My cases have exactly that shape, with much higher stakes. Please do not solve the philosophy of it for me; I need the procedure.

So: write the procedure as a numbered sequence of questions, with the stopping rules and the hand-off points marked, and then run it on three cases — the two-dot demonstration, treated as a rehearsal; a patient who, in the first conversation the day after surgery, unprompted and before he had spoken to anyone else, describes hearing a specific exchange between two staff members and the sensation of the tube; and a patient whose detailed, vivid narrative emerged only after several months of reading about anaesthetic awareness and a session with a therapist who asked leading questions. Tell me which case the procedure resolves cleanly, which it resolves in a way my colleagues will find intolerable, and where it must stop and refer the decision to a person. I also want to know where the procedure will systematically misfire, and in particular whether any of its steps quietly presuppose one of the two stories above — because that is exactly the assumption we keep smuggling in without noticing. Assume that whoever runs the procedure has clinical training but no philosophy and no access to the literature, so anything you invoke has to be explained inside the procedure itself; and give no citations, since we cannot use them. The finished thing should fit on two sides of a sheet, and I should be able to run it in a single forty-five-minute meeting.
