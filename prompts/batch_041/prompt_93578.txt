I'm a postdoc in philosophy of science, and next month I'm presenting at a small interdisciplinary workshop on levels of explanation. The room will be a specific and slightly hostile mix: several philosophers of biology, among them a couple of adaptationists and a couple of Evo-Devo people who are allergic to adaptationist storytelling; two committed eliminative materialists from the cognitive-science side who regard any talk of "higher-level regularities" as sentimental residue; a complex-systems person who will try to recruit me to the emergence camp; and a historian of biology who mostly wants to know who, exactly, ever held the strong version of the view I'm attacking. My thesis is deliberately awkward: that Dennett's opposition between cranes and skyhooks, and his companion charge of greedy reductionism, are not the two useful tools everyone treats them as, but a single mistake applied twice. The metaphor, I argue, smuggles in an engineering picture of explanation — load-bearing mechanisms versus free-floating magic — under which any appeal to a higher-level pattern that isn't cashed out as its own dedicated mechanism starts to look like cheating. I want to replace it with a term I've been trying out, which I call crane-mongering: the demand that every higher-level regularity be underwritten by a distinct lower-level mechanism, a vice I claim is what greedy reductionism really names once you strip off the rhetoric. I lean on a few cases — the explanatory status of selection, the reality of fitness, whether developmental constraints explain or merely permit — but please don't assume I've nailed them.

Please write my Q&A prep sheet for the question period. I want the objections I will actually get from this particular room, not the ones I'd get at a Dennett festschrift: the eliminativists' demand that I produce the mechanism, the adaptationists' suspicion that I'm a closet skyhook-lover, the Evo-Devo people's reaction, the complex-systems person's attempt to conscript me, and the historian's question about whether anyone ever really committed to the strong reading I'm attacking. Order the objections by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it, phrase each one the way that specific interlocutor would phrase it, and for each give me a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes, together with an honest note on where that reply is thin or likely to invite a follow-up I can't handle. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold, and close with the single question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that would expose the load-bearing assumption in my position that I haven't yet defended.
