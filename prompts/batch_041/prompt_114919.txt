I'm in the last stretch of a novel about a woman in her early sixties who agrees to a course of treatment in which her neurons are replaced, one at a time over the better part of a year, by silicon units that do exactly what the originals did, so that nothing in her speech, her memory or her temper changes from one week to the next; at the outset she is offered the alternative of having the whole substitution done in a single session under anaesthetic, and she refuses it, mostly because she cannot bear the thought of going to sleep as herself and waking as something else. My sister, who is a palliative care nurse, tells me the premise is a category mistake, and we have been arguing about it since Christmas. What I want from you is a poem of eighteen lines for the chapter in which the last of the original cells is gone: six three-line stanzas, in which the third line of each stanza is the third line of the stanza before it with exactly one word changed — the refrain begins as a flat, clinical five-word sentence, something on the order of the patient is still herself, and by the final stanza not one of its original words is left. Never mention the rule, never hint at it, never apologise for it, and let the other two lines of each stanza do something else entirely: the same room, the same small routines, the log kept the same way on the same clipboard, so that the only thing in the poem that alters is the line that keeps insisting nothing has altered. The last version of that line should be readable either as the same claim or as its opposite, without punning and without trickery. It has to work as a poem first — someone who has never thought about any of this should find it sad and a little frightening, and someone who has thought about it a great deal should feel the problem in the mouth rather than be told about it.

I would rather not have it in her voice. That is the one thing I cannot have, since the whole question is whether that voice is still the same voice, and the poem should be free to doubt her. Use the technician who keeps the log, or someone close to her who has to go on using the same name; either will do. The poem should hold every position without adjudicating among them: that nothing was lost because nothing was ever interrupted; that what sits in the chair at the end is an excellent copy of a dead woman, and the absence of a moment of death is a fact about our powers of discrimination rather than about her; and the harder thought that identity was never what we cared about, that what we cared about comes in degrees and has been thinning since spring. Keep the refused single-session option somewhere in the poem's shadow — a clause will do — because the absurdity of survival turning on the rate of change is what I keep circling back to. It should be accurate enough that someone who works on personal identity would not wince: no souls, no slogans about patterns, no machinery beyond the clinic chair.

After the poem, give me a single short paragraph of plain prose — a note rather than a lecture — telling me which position each stanza is quietly leaning on, whether you actually managed the one-word-at-a-time rule in every stanza and where you had to cheat, and which of the positions you found hardest to keep from tipping over into its opposite. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables; continuous prose, the way you would write it to me in a letter.
