I'm a science journalist — I cover economics and the philosophy of science, mostly — and every spring I get talked into teaching one session of a masters seminar on science, values and policy. This year it's my turn to write the take-home question, and I want it to be about discounting the future. What I don't want is the usual "should we care about future generations" essay that a student can knock out from memory of the climate-policy fight.

What I want the question to test is the thing that took me embarrassingly long to get straight: a positive consumption discount rate and a positive pure time preference are two different animals, and the second one is where all the trouble lives. So build the question around a student who says something like "nobody needs to be impatient — we discount future consumption because future people will be richer, so a dollar then buys less than a dollar now; pure time preference is zero, done." The question should make them show what that move actually establishes, and then why it doesn't get them off the hook ethically.

The question has to force them into four or five things: the usual decomposition of the consumption discount rate into a pure-time-preference term and a term that scales with growth (r = δ + ηg, or whatever notation they use); what it means that η is doing double duty, as the curvature of the utility function and as the weight on equality; why "the future will be richer" is doing almost all the work, and what happens when you drop that assumption or flip its sign; and whether δ > 0 can be defended by anything other than the fact that we happen to have it. I also want a bit on the uncertainty dodge — the move that says we discount the future not out of impatience but because the future might not arrive — because I suspect that one holds up for small, marginal cases and falls apart for the big ones.

Answerable in about 50 minutes, model answer around 700-800 words. Give me the question, then the model answer, then a marking scheme out of 20, where you tell me what seperates a good answer from a mediocre one and, for each of the five things they have to hit, one line on how students typically get it wrong.
