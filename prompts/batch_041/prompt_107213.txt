For three years I have co-led a seminar on interpretation, and every term the same dispute deadlocks. One camp holds that what a text means is what its author intended to communicate by producing it, so that an interpretation is answerable to evidence about the author's intentions. An opposing camp holds that a text's meaning is fixed by the linguistic conventions and genre expectations it activates, so that the author's private intentions, even when recoverable, are beside the point. Between them sit those who say a text means what a suitably informed reader would take its author to have intended — a compromise each extreme dismisses as a fudge. Everyone in the room shares the same inventory of cases, and the verdicts never converge. The author who, decades after publication, disavows the reading the text plainly invites; the collectively authored document whose signatories disagree among themselves about what they signed; the dead poet whose posthumously discovered drafts reveal an intention no reader could have guessed; the sentence that is ungrammatical in a way that seems to carry meaning precisely through its error — each side processes these without strain and reaches opposite conclusions.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is largely verbal, though not shallowly so: that "meaning" in these exchanges names at least two different objects — the meaning of a particular utterance-event and the meaning of a repeatable text — and that each side quietly privileges one object and then reports its verdict about a case as though it were a verdict about the other. I want that hypothesis tested rather than flattered. Tell me whether this is really a verbal dispute, or a substantive disagreement about the correct theory of linguistic meaning, or a clash of bedrock commitments about where interpretive authority resides — in the author, in the linguistic community, or in the text itself — that no argument will dislodge because it is prior to argument. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually does with the cases rather than in what the labels suggest. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the appearance of conflict, a case that discriminates between the readings, a demonstration that the parties are answering different questions under a single heading, or an argument that the stall is exactly what we should expect and no progress is owed.
