My roommate says 'X is just a social construct' about everything -- race, gender, money, the weekend, whatever -- and treats it like it settles whatever we were arguing about. Last week she said race is just a social construct so race-based medicine is nonsense; a day later she said gender is just a social construct, so of course we can just change the categories; and she once said money is just a social construct, and got annoyed when I said fine, then I'll pay my half of the rent with a social construction.

I don't think she's exactly wrong, I think that one sentence is doing about five different jobs and she slides between them mid-arguement. Can you break out the precise claims 'just a social construct' could be making? I want a menu, not a lecture on one reading. For each one, say what exactly is being claimed -- about the word, about the concept, about what caused the category to exist, about what the category consists in, about what it's for, about whether it could have been otherwise -- and then whether that reading is trivially true of basically everything we name, substantive and defensible, or actually false. Then say, for each of her three moves above, which readings would actually get her the conclusion she wants.

Two things I'd especially like pinned down. First, the word 'just': does it add a claim or only an attitude? If the money case shows a social construct can still be totally real and totally constraining, does that kill the dismissive reading or just relocate it somewhere else? Second, the reflexive bit: some people say the interesting thing isn't that we invented the category, it's that the category then acts back on the people in it, so the classification and the classified keep changing each other. Where does that go in the menu, and does it make the kind more real or less? While you're at it, keep seperate whether the claim is about the category itself or about the people sorted into it -- she treats those as the same thing and I suspect they're very different.

And if she means the strong version, where there's no fact of the matter about where the boundaries fall, tell me whether that helps her 'so we can change them' move or actually undercuts it, since it sounds like it would also make one arrangement no better than another. A numbered list I can bring to the kitchen would be great.
