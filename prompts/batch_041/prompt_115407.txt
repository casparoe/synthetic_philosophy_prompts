So I've been sitting in on this reading group about the regress problem — foundations, coherence, the infinitist thing — and Dara, whos in the group, has an argument he's weirdly proud of, and I honestly can't tell if it's a proof or a magic trick. I've been reading rationalist blogs for like a decade, I'm supposed to have a take on this stuff, and I don't. Here it is in his numbering, more or less his words:

1. If a belief of mine is justified, then something justifies it — there's some support for it, otherwise it's just a thing I happen to think.
2. Nothing can justify a belief except another belief. A reason has to be the kind of thing that can be right or wrong, and a feeling or an experience isn't in that business — it just happens to you.
3. A belief that isn't itself justified can't justify anything. You can't get justification out of a source that has none; thats getting something from nothing.
4. So any justified belief sits on a chain of supporting beliefs, and each link has to be justified if the link above it is going to be. (He says we lose nothing by assuming each belief has just one supporter — if a belief has two, you just ask about each of them in turn.)
5. The chain can't stop at an unsupported belief: by 1 and 3 that belief is unjustified, so it can't pass anything up the chain, and the top belief gets nothing. He has a lemma here — even if you think justification comes in degrees, it doesn't help, because an unsupported belief contributes zero and a chain of zeroes is zero, so the degrees move just relabels the problem.
6. The chain can't loop, either. A circle of beliefs holding each other up is like a loan guaranteed by another loan from the same bank; no actual justification ever enters the system. And if loops worked, then a belief could support itself, and then anything at all would be justified, which is absurd.
7. It can't be infinite. A finite mind holds finitely many beliefs, so there's no infinite chain sitting in there doing any work — and anyway, no belief in an infinite chain is ever justified in its own right, each one is waiting on the next, so the justification is always deferred and never lands.
8. So no belief is justified, for anyone, ever.

He says the payoff isn't just skepticism, it's that the scientist and the conspiracy theorist end up on the same epistemic footing — the difference between them is sociology, not justification. I can't swallow that. But I also don't want to just declare the argument bad, because 1, 2 and 3 all look obviously true to me, and I'd rather not give up any of them, and I'd also rather not give up the denial of 8. So: which step do I reject, and what made it look so innocent in the first place? Then the part I actually care about — price the exits. If I go foundationalist and drop 2, what am I on the hook for? If I go coherentist and drop 6, what do I owe? If I go infinitist and drop 7, what do I owe? And is there any way to keep 2 and 3 *and* deny 8, or is that just wanting to have it all? I suspect there's a slide somewhere in 5 through 7 but I can't put my finger on it, and every time I try, I end up restating one of the three positions at him, and he just says "yes, that's the cost, isn't it."
