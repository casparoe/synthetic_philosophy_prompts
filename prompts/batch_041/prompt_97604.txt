I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to run the opening session of a graduate workshop on perception and cognition — sixteen students, ninety minutes, no lecture slot, and a room I'm free to rearrange. What I want them to feel in their hands, rather than be told, is the force of the puzzle usually named after Molyneux: whether a person blind from birth who has learned to tell a sphere from a cube by touch could, on first being given sight, tell which is which by looking alone. My worry is that the puzzle arrives pre-chewed — everyone has heard the standard version and has a stock answer ready — so I want a version they haven't met, one in which the two senses are made to disagree and in which the question is not resemblance but re-identification: not which visible thing is a cube, but which visible thing is the very object that was a moment ago in their hands.

Design the activity so that this happens in the rules rather than in my framing. I'd like three roles, assigned by lot. The tactile-only participants learn two objects under blindfold, handling them inside a cloth bag so nothing leaks past the blindfold, and then must say which of the two they were given a second time — a test of tactile memory, to establish a baseline. The visual-only participants, seated behind a low barrier, see the same two objects and must do the same by eye. The newly sighted participants touch an object in round one, and in round two, sighted for the first time, must pick which of the visible objects is the identical one they held. Use objects chosen so that the naive strategies fail: one pair identical in weight, temperature, and surface texture but different in silhouette; a second pair almost identical in silhouette but different in texture and heft. Let participants wager tokens on their confidence and tally the wagers, so there is a small stake in being right and an incentive not to guess. Then run two extra rounds that I think carry the real conceptual weight. In the calibration round, the newly sighted may move the object with their own hand and watch it move — does seeing the consequence of one's own action substitute for having seen the thing? In the label round, someone simply tells a newly sighted participant the object's name — does a shared label do the work that a shared percept supposedly would?

The debrief should map what actually happened onto the parts of the puzzle. If the tactile and visual groups score well, that is the moment to show that shape seems to be available to more than one sense, and to ask what that implies about whether the senses deliver the same content or merely correlated labels. The label round should be used to expose the difference between classifying something and recognizing it as the same individual. The calibration round should be used to ask whether the difficulty lies in the senses or in the concepts, and whether an answer about what a particular person would do settles anything about the content of that first visual experience — the question of whether there is a fact of the matter at all. Expect two things to go wrong the first time. Participants will smuggle in cues — weight, sound, the edge of the blindfold — so match the pairs carefully and treat any suspiciously easy success as data rather than victory. And nobody in the room can honestly play the newly sighted, since everyone already has the cross-modal mappings the role is supposed to lack; they will succeed and conclude the puzzle is trivial. Handle this by having them predict success rates and bet on them before the reveal, so the interesting object is the prediction, not the performance. Be candid in the debrief about what the activity cannot reproduce: the genuine absence of any cross-modal transfer, and the question of what, if anything, it is like to see a shape for the first time. That part I have to supply in words, along with the reminder that real cases are never clean — real subjects have partial sight and years of experience — which is precisely why the staged version, and not the historical one, is where the puzzle can be felt.
