I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on probability and induction, and we've just finished the unit on the reference class problem. I understand the abstract shape of the difficulty well enough: any single case falls under indefinitely many true descriptions, the relative frequency of the outcome varies across those descriptions, and so the probability we assign seems to depend on an arbitrary choice among them. What I can't get past is that every example in the readings and problem sets is gerrymandered to make the problem look like a technicality — the competing classes differ by a couple of percentage points, or one of them is transparently the right one once you think about it for ten seconds. I want to see the problem actually bite, and bite in a setting where a real decision turns on the answer. (For context, I got into this because a friend in a public-policy seminar was arguing about risk scores used in sentencing and hiring, and I realized I had no idea what the "correct" probability was even supposed to mean in those cases.)

Please build me one such case, and keep it mundane: a clinic, a hiring decision, a parole board, an insurance desk, something of that order — no lottery tickets, no contrived setups, and nothing that asks me to take a substantive empirical fact on faith (you may stipulate plausible numbers, but flag them as stipulations rather than discoveries). The case must contain two reference classes that are each admissible by the ordinary criteria and that assign the target event genuinely opposite probabilities — one clearly above one half and one clearly below, or one that supports the decision at hand and one that blocks it. Then do the following explicitly. (1) Name the event in question and the two candidate classes, and say precisely what makes each of them admissible. (2) Work out what each of the three standard responses to the problem says the probability is and why: the maximum-specificity or narrowest-class rule; a relevance- or mechanism-based criterion; and the deflationary view that there is no unique correct answer to be had. (3) Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the real work — i.e., exactly what I would have to change to make the two classes converge. (4) Finally, show me how someone who believes there is a right answer would try to argue that one of the two classes wins here, and assess honestly whether that argument succeeds or merely restates the intuition it was meant to support.

I'd rather you commit to a verdict on how the case should come out and defend it than lay out the menu of positions and leave the choice to me; if you think the case is genuinely underdetermined, argue for that conclusion instead, but don't hedge by treating all three responses as equally defensible. Flag any point at which the case works only because you stipulated something rather than derived it, since I want to know which parts are load-bearing.
