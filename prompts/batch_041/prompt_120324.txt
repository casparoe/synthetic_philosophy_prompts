You are a philosopher of mind specializing in intentionality, and you have agreed to serve as the neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration. The two parties are (A) a Dennettian, who holds that the distinction between original and derived intentionality marks no difference in kind — that the intentional stance is the measure of the intentional, that the two-bitser's internal states are about the world in whatever sense ours are, and that what separates us from the machine is the richness of the pattern and the complexity of the competence rather than a metaphysical line — and (B) a Searlean, who holds that the distinction is a difference in kind: the intentionality of artifacts and representations is derived from, imposed by, and dependent on the intrinsic intentionality of an agent, and only states with the right kind of intrinsic causal power are genuinely about anything at all. Both parties accept the two-bitser as the shared test case: the coin-slot device that reliably accepts genuine coins and rejects slugs, and the question of what its internal state is doing when it does so, whether it can be said to believe that a slug is a quarter, and whether it can be mistaken. Neither party expects to change its mind, and both have signed off on the process. Your job is not to adjudicate but to draft the document both can put their names to. Refine the two glosses above if you find them unfaithful; if you do, say where and why.

Before drafting, think on the page: state each position as its own adherent would state it, in the strongest form you can manage; list the sub-questions that appear to be doing the real work; and mark the ones you suspect are verbal. Then produce the joint statement, with these sections.

1. Stipulations and agreed claims. First the facts about the two-bitser both sides accept, stated precisely enough that the residual disagreement cannot be about the mechanism: what the internal state is stipulated to carry, how it came to carry it, and what it is used for downstream. Then a numbered list of agreed claims about intentionality, derivation, and error. Admit no truisms — each agreed claim should be one a bystander might have assumed was contested.

2. Disagreements. A table: one question per row; one column giving the Dennettian's answer and one the Searlean's, each phrased in wording its own side would accept as fair; a final column for what turns on the answer. Where you can find a formulation both sides would accept as neutral, use it; where you cannot, say so and explain why.

3. Cruxes. For each: the sub-question; why settling it would move at least one side; the argument, case, or consideration both sides agree bears on it; and each side's prediction, recorded in advance. Consider and either adopt or replace, with reasons: whether the original/derived distinction is a difference in kind or in degree; whether derived intentionality can be defined without covertly presupposing the original as its source, and whether that presupposition is a finding or a stipulation; whether the predictive success of the intentional stance is evidence of belief or only of the utility of a fiction; whether a system could be functionally and behaviorally indistinguishable from an intentional agent and still have no original intentionality; and whether the substrate and its causal powers matter over and above the competence.

4. Pre-registered predictions on three new cases. (a) A descendant of the two-bitser trained by reinforcement learning on noisy coins and slugs until it matches a human cashier's reliability, whose internal state is reused downstream for other tasks, including answering questions about what it has in hand. (b) A hybrid: living human neurons wired to perform exactly the same discrimination, otherwise behaviorally identical. (c) A device whose reports about its own reasons are produced by a module causally insulated from the mechanism that actually drives its accept/reject behavior, so that its stated reasons are confabulated yet useful. For each case, each side records its verdict, the principle that yields it, and whether the verdict is a prediction or simply a consequence of how that side has defined its terms.

5. Verbal disputes. Say which parts of the disagreement, if any, are about the meaning of intentionality — a functional-role sense versus an intrinsic or phenomenal sense — and would dissolve once terms were fixed. If any dissolve, say exactly what remains once they do.

6. Self-doubt. For each side: what it would count as evidence against its own position. Then, jointly, what both agree would actually move the debate — and if the honest answer is nothing, say so, and say why that is a defect rather than a sign of depth.

House rules: no citations, dates, or reading lists; refer to positions in your own words. Do not fall back on the famous set-piece examples of this debate; use the cases given. Write plainly, flag any term you have to coin, and never paper over a surviving disagreement with a formulation vague enough to cover both sides.
