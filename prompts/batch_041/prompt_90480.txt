I am helping convene a small graduate reading group on immoral art, and we have reached the point where we need to stop comparing positions and first ask what a theory in this area is supposed to do. Please write the desiderata for any adequate theory of the relation between a work's moral qualities and its artistic or aesthetic value. The theory should tell us when, if ever, an ethical defect in a work counts against its value as art, when an ethical merit counts in its favour, and how either of those relates to the moral standing of the artist; it should not simply presuppose that the moral and the artistic are separate domains, nor that they collapse into one. For each requirement, supply the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate account and which are optional or specific to a camp. I am especially interested in the distinctions the theory must preserve: between the moral character of the maker and the moral character of the made thing; between what a work invites or endorses and what it merely depicts; between a moral defect that is incidental to a work's artistic success and one that is partly constitutive of it; between a work's ethical value and its cognitive or emotional value; and between pro tanto and overall artistic value. Flag any pair of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the tension; if the list itself forces a choice among attractive options, state that result plainly.

To make the task concrete, here are some invented cases I would want the theory to handle. A composer writes a formally dazzling chamber piece mainly in order to humiliate a rival; the cruelty is not audible in the music, but it is the causal explanation of why the piece was written. A novel has exquisite prose and a repugnant moral vision: it invites the reader to relish the humiliation of a vulnerable character, and that invitation seems inseparable from the novel's aesthetic power. A memoir betrays real people, yet its ethical reckoning with that betrayal is what makes it valuable as literature. A work is morally admirable but aesthetically inert; another is morally repellent but aesthetically transformative. These are test cases, not canonical examples, so please use them to discipline the desiderata rather than treating them as historical evidence.

After the list, briefly say how the familiar families of views fare against it: autonomism, ethicism or moralism, immoralism, and an interactionist or two-aspect approach. Keep the scoring subordinate to the specification; I care most about which requirement does the most to exclude candidates and which view survives the central requirements. A table for the scoring is welcome if it helps, but the list and its motivating cases are the main deliverable.
