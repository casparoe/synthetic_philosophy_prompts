(i) Analogies are indispensable — nobody reasons about a genuinely novel crisis without reaching for some earlier case, and the alternative to an analogy isn't clear-eyed analysis, it's paralysis. (ii) My own test for when an analogy earns its keep: it has to name the causal mechanism that produced the outcome in the source case, and I have to be able to check whether that same mechanism is present in the target case independently of how similar the two outcomes look. If the mechanism can only be described by pointing at how the source case turned out, the analogy is just a vibe with a date attached. (iii) Munich 1938 is a real lesson: a leader whose demands expand with every concession can't be bought off, so early resistance beats late resistance. (iv) Vietnam is also a real lesson, and it points the other way: outside powers systematically underrate local nationalism and the staying power of a committed insurgency, so restraint beats escalation. (v) The Thucydides trap picture is basically right — when a rising power closes on the ruling power, war is likely, and thats mostly structural. (vi) Related: structure explains nearly all the variance in how these things go, and what individual leaders decide is mostly noise. (vii) Counterfactual history is close to vacuous — things are too causally dense for a claim about what would have happened if X hadn't occurred to have a determinate answer.
