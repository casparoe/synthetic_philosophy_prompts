I have been reading a popular introduction to the philosophy of language, and I would like a second opinion on its treatment of Putnam's Twin Earth argument. The book's account runs roughly as follows. Twin Earth is a planet just like ours except that the liquid its inhabitants drink and call 'water' is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ; the two planets and their populations are otherwise duplicates of one another. Oscar and his twin are therefore identical in every psychological respect, yet their word 'water' means different things, since one refers to H2O and the other to XYZ. The moral, the book says, is that meaning is not in the head but is fixed by one's surroundings. It then explains that this is precisely why Putnam holds that ordinary speakers defer to the relevant experts — your word means whatever the chemists say it means — and it concludes that the argument shows Twin Oscar to be simply mistaken about his environment, since he calls XYZ 'water'. The closing sentence claims that semantic externalism is therefore a form of skepticism: if the meaning of our words depends on the world, we can never know what we ourselves mean. I suspect the early part of this is sound and the last sentence overreaches, but I cannot tell whether that is just my prejudice.

How much of this should I trust? Please take it claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher of language would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is simply wrong, and whether the closing verdict follows from anything that survives the correction. Tell me how confident you are in each part, and single out the point you are least sure of. Please write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
