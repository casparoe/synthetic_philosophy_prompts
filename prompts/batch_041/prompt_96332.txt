I belong to a small reading group that has spent the last few months on the great priority disputes — Newton and Leibniz over the calculus, Darwin and Wallace over natural selection — and we keep talking past one another the moment the question shifts from what happened to whether it matters who got there first. What I would like to hand out at our next meeting is a decision tree that sorts us by the positions we actually hold rather than the ones we would name if asked directly. Build it as a branching questionnaire: every node phrased as a concrete claim or case that a person can answer without knowing where the branch leads, every leaf carrying the name of a position together with one sentence stating its least comfortable commitment. Where two leaves end up making the same predictions about the cases, please say so rather than pretending the tree separates them.

I would rather you build the tree around three distinctions that I have come to think do most of the work here, instead of starting from a list of famous names. The first is between the inevitability of a result and the inevitability of its timing: it seems perfectly coherent to hold that something was bound to be found sooner or later and still think it was near-luck that it appeared when it did rather than half a century later. The second is between three things we usually run together — priority, by which I mean who was there first in time; precedence, by which I mean who is awarded the credit; and provenance, by which I mean whose route to the result the field actually went on to use. The third is between the individual, the pair, and the whole field as the unit that did the discovering, since one of my friends insists that the interesting entity in these stories is never a person at all.

Please let the tree run from a starting commitment a reasonable person might actually hold — say, that the Darwin and Wallace affair was a genuine race with a determinate winner, or that it was two people catching the same wave — through six to nine branching nodes to somewhere between five and eight leaves. Among the leaves I want one that denies there is any fact of the matter about priority whatsoever, and one that treats priority as a perfectly real fact that is nonetheless morally and intellectually inert. Include at least one node on whether the counterfactual question — had one of them died young, would the result have arrived within a generation — is a well-formed question with an answer at all, since we cannot agree on that either, and at least one node on whether the pair or the field rather than the person should be credited. Work from the two historical cases plus one hypothetical I will describe at the meeting: two researchers derive the same theorem in the same year, the first to publish is entirely forgotten, and the second's notation becomes the standard one. Note any combinations of answers that leave a branch empty, and say briefly whether nobody occupies that spot because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has tried it. Lay it out as a handout with numbered nodes and lettered branches.
