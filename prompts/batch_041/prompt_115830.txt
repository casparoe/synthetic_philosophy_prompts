Since my mother was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's, I have been reading about the Buddhist teaching of no-self, and I keep meeting an argument—one version from a teacher at a retreat, another from a philosopher in my reading group—that seems to prove far more than anyone who offers it wants.

(1) Whatever is impermanent and not under my control cannot be my self. (2) Every candidate for the self—the body, feelings, perceptions, dispositions, and consciousness—is impermanent and not under my control. (3) So none of these is the self. (4) A self over and above them would never be met in experience and so would be a bare posit, no better than a soul; hence there is no self. (5) If there is no self, there is no persisting owner of experiences and actions, only momentary events in a causal stream. (6) If there is no persisting owner, then the woman who raised me and the woman now in the care home are not, strictly, the same person, and whoever grieves for her later will not be the one who loved her. (7) Yet the tradition also holds that karma ripens on the very being who acted, that compassion is owed to particular persons, and that liberation is the liberation of this stream and not another. A friend who studies Yogācāra says the storehouse consciousness—a deep layer of mind said to carry karmic seeds from moment to moment—secures the continuity; I cannot tell whether that answers the objection or merely relabels the self.

I would sooner keep (7) than abandon the teaching, but I want to know what I am paying. Which step should I reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost at the other steps? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—name the step—then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
