I am preparing materials for a graduate reading group that meets once on the justification of simplicity in inductive inference, and I need a classification instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree that takes a participant from a starting commitment to a named position about what, if anything, justifies Occam's razor.

The entry point is the commitment everyone in the room already shares and that I want the tree to interrogate: that, other things equal, the simpler of two hypotheses that fit the data is the better guide to truth, and that this is more than a matter of taste. From there, each branch node must be a question a participant can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf must be a position on what grounds the commitment and how far it extends.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of names, and use the following as the branch points (add others only if they are needed to keep the leaves distinct):

1. Language-dependence. When two hypotheses fit the data equally well, suppose that one has the shorter description only in language A and the other only in language B. Does this show that the simplicity comparison is ill-defined, that it is well-defined up to a bounded amount that washes out in the long run, or that the choice of language is a free parameter that never fully disappears? This branch must turn on the difference between a complexity ranking that is independent of the descriptive language up to an additive constant and one that is not.

2. What simplicity is claimed to track. Is the claim that the simpler hypothesis is more likely to be true (convergence to the actual distribution, better long-run prediction), that it is a better engineering choice (shorter compression, less computation), or that it is a norm we impose on theory choice rather than a fact about the world? Probe with: if the true distribution turned out to be maximally complex and simplicity gave you worse predictions in your domain, would that refute the commitment or leave it untouched?

3. Which simplicity. When a model with one adjustable parameter and a model with fifty both fit the data, the second is automatically penalized by its prior marginal likelihood. Is that the same phenomenon as the shortest-program idea, a different phenomenon that happens to point the same way, or the only one of the two that does real work? This branch should separate an algorithmic reading of simplicity from a Bayesian one.

4. Realizability. An oracle could tell you the shortest program generating the data, but no finite agent can compute it, and the ideal prior that weights hypotheses by program length is itself not computable: an agent can approximate it from below but can never bound its own error. Does a justification have to be realizable by a finite agent to count, or is it enough that the ideal exists as a mathematical object that we approximate? This is the branch between a justification that is usable and one that is merely true.

5. The universality objection. Averaged over all possible problems, every inductive method does equally well, so for every problem on which a simplicity prior excels there is one on which it fails. Does this defeat the claim that simplicity is a guide to truth, or is it beside the point because the world is not drawn at random from the space of all problems and the relevant restriction to structured or computable environments does the work?

6. Scope. Is the question about induction in general or about model selection inside a fixed statistical family? Positions that look opposed at one scope may coincide at the other, so this branch should be able to send participants to the same leaf by different routes.

The leaves should include at least: a position on which the universal prior genuinely solves the problem of induction and simplicity is objective; a position on which convergence holds but the machine-dependent constant leaves no unique ranking; a Bayesian position on which simplicity is nothing over and above marginal likelihood; a position on which simplicity is a domain-specific heuristic with no universal justification; a position on which the whole ideal is an unusable idealization because the prior cannot be computed; and a conventionalist position on which Occam's razor is a norm of theory choice rather than a claim about the world. Name each leaf, or write Unnamed if no one defends it, and give each leaf one sentence on the commitment it has the most trouble defending.

Then, in a short section after the tree: list the combinations of answers your tree leaves unoccupied and diagnose each, saying whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, with one sentence on the question you would have to add to pull them apart. Seven to nine leaves is the right size. Do not turn any leaf into a survey of who has held it: I want the positions located in the logical space, not a history of them.
