I am writing a short position paper on the distribution of consciousness across animals, and I would like an audit of its internal consistency before I circulate it. Here is where I have landed.

1. Consciousness is not an all-or-nothing property; it admits of degrees. There is no single point in evolutionary history at which experience switches on, and no single fact of the form "this clade has it, that one does not."

2. Even so, whether a given creature has experience, and how much of it, is an objective matter about that creature rather than a stipulation or a projection of our own moral convenience. Our concepts may be vague and our evidence thin, but there is something to be right or wrong about.

3. The morally decisive feature is the capacity for valenced experience: that there can be something it is like to be the creature, and that this can go well or badly for it. Where there is no felt quality, there is nothing for morality to protect.

4. Moral weight should track the richness of the experience rather than mere species membership. A creature with a thin, dim experiential life has a correspondingly weaker claim on us than a creature with a rich one, and this is a substantive fact about the two claims, not merely a concession to our ignorance.

5. In practice, though, I would assign equal weight per unit of suffering to every creature whose sentience is credible, because our instruments for measuring richness are hopeless and the costs of underestimating a creature's experience are asymmetric.

6. On the empirical side, I am confident that mammals and birds are conscious, reasonably confident about octopuses and fish, genuinely undecided about insects and other arthropods, and confident that plants, fungi and bacteria are not conscious at all.

7. My practical verdicts follow: I have no qualms about eating oysters or about killing mosquitoes, I take boiling a lobster alive to be a wrong that ought to be illegal, and I buy free-range chicken and pork when I can afford it.

Please tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, which only appear to be, and how severe each genuine tension is. Rank them rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to be given up, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs me elsewhere. I would rather keep the set as it stands if the tensions turn out to be merely apparent, so be explicit about which ones are real and why the others dissolve. This is an audit of my own position, not a survey of the literature.
