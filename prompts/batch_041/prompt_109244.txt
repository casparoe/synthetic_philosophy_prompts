Question: The absent-minded driver problem. A driver is on a highway with two exits. If he takes the first exit he gets 0; if he takes the second he gets 4; if he drives past both he gets 1. The driver is absent-minded: at each intersection he cannot tell whether it's the first or the second, and he cannot remember whether he's already passed an intersection. (a) What is his optimal strategy, and what is the expected payoff? (b) The problem is often said to generate a paradox or inconsistency. Explain why. (c) How does the response by Aumann, Hart, and Perry bear on the paradox?

Answer: Piccione and Rubinstein came up with this problem. The driver has to decide at each intersection whether to exit or keep going. Since he can't tell the intersections apart, he has to use the same rule at both. Let p be the probability he continues at an intersection. His expected payoff is p^2 * 1 + 4p(1-p). The first term is the case where he continues at both intersections and ends up with 1; the second is the case where he continues at the first and exits at the second, getting 4. The case where he exits at the first gives 0. Taking the derivative and setting it to zero gives p = 2/3. So he should continue with probability 2/3 and exit with probability 1/3, and his expected payoff is 4/3.

The paradox is about what he should do when he actually reaches an intersection. He can't tell which one he's at, so he figures he's equally likely to be at the first or the second. If he exits, he gets (1/2)(0) + (1/2)(4) = 2. If he continues, then if he was at the first he reaches the second and can exit for 4, and if he was at the second he overshoots and gets 1, so continuing gives (1/2)(4) + (1/2)(1) = 2.5. Since 2.5 > 2, at the action stage it looks like he should always continue, which contradicts the planning-stage answer of continuing with probability 2/3. That's the inconsistency.

Aumann, Hart, and Perry say there's no real inconsistency: the driver should just stick to the plan-optimal strategy and not re-optimize at each intersection. The action-stage reasoning is a mistake. But this means the driver has to be a bit irrational, or at least has to have a divided self that can bind itself, which is what makes the problem philosophically interesting.
