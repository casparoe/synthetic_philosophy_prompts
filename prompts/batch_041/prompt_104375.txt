Im a backend engineer (Go, Python, some infra) and I've somehow become the person on my team who actually reads the eval reports, so I've been chewing on this for a while and I want a real answer rather than a pep talk. Here's the setup. Last quarter we shipped an LLM feature that summarizes support tickets. The way we decided it was good enough: 400 hand-labelled examples, a rubric two of us wrote over a Friday afternoon, and a spot check of maybe thirty outputs by three people. Nobody on the team can tell you why the model gets 91% of them right, and honestly we're fine with that -- "it does the task" is the thing we care about. But then in the demo somebody pulled up the chain-of-thought traces and everyone visibly relaxed, as if the traces had shown us something about the model's insides. We also talk like "that one's a reasoning error" when it misses in an interesting way. Meanwhile the team next door has a credit-scoring model where compliance makes them produce SHAP values for every individual decision, and I've never once been asked for anything like that for the summarizer. Nobody thinks this is weird and I sort of do.

What I want to know is what our process is actually assuming about the relationship between being able to do a thing and understanding it. I half-remember some old thread where Darwin and Turing both pull the same kind of flip -- Darwin gets design out of a process with no designer in it, and Turing says you don't have to settle whether the machine understands, you just look at what it does -- and I suspect we've quietly inherited one side of that without noticing. So: which parts of our release process only make sense if comprehension is optional, or if "comprehension" just is whatever the behavior turns out to be; which parts quietly assume the opposite, that you can't really know a system works unless you know why it works; and are the traces and the rubric doing legitimate work, or are they basically superstition dressed up as diligence? If someone on the team genuinely believed the second view, what would our validation have to look like instead? And I'd also like to know where the process is just underdetermined -- if two very different views of understanding would generate exactly the same checklist we already have, say so, because that's actually the answer I'm most afraid of.

No reading list please, and go easy on the jargon since I'll have to explain this to a room of engineers. Also, tell me how confident you actually are in what you're claiming, and single out the one part of your answer you're least sure about -- I'd much rather have a hedge I can repeat in a design review than something that sounds authoritative and then falls apart the second someone pushes on it.
