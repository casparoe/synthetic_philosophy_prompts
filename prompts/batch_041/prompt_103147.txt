For a graduate seminar I teach on population ethics, I am preparing a single-page handout that classifies the positions one might hold on whether a larger population is a better one, and I would like the classification built as a decision tree rather than as a list of labels.

Please construct the tree so that it grows from the questions that actually separate the positions. Each branch node should be phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should be labeled with the name of the position it represents. Work from the logical space first: set out the branch questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions within that space, and note any combinations that no one occupies, with a brief diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

The purpose of the handout is not to survey population axiology in general but to sort the various arguments for pronatalism — for the claim that we ought to have more people — according to the axiological commitments each argument presupposes. Accordingly, each leaf should also carry, in one sentence, the most awkward commitment that the position imposes on anyone who uses it in defense of having more people. Where an argument for a larger population is really an argument about something else — a duty owed to existing people, a claim about national power or economic output, a religious premise — the tree should say so rather than forcing it into an axiological leaf.

I want the tree to turn on at least the following questions, though you should add whatever else the logical space requires:

- whether the value of a population depends only on the welfare of the people who exist, or also on how many people exist;
- whether adding a life that is worth living always increases the value of the world, or does so only under some further condition — raising an average, clearing a critical level, not diluting some scarce good;
- whether the position accepts the repugnant conclusion, and if it does not, what does the blocking work;
- whether procreation is a duty, a permission, or something supererogatory, and whether that deontic classification is meant to follow from the axiology or to stand independently of it;
- whether population size is valued intrinsically, valued as a means to some further good, or not taken to be value-relevant at all.

Please ensure that the leaves include, at minimum, totalism; averagism; a critical-level view; the person-affecting or narrow family; the view on which it is bad to create miserable people but not good to create happy ones; and a position on which the number of people is simply not a value-relevant feature. Distinguish the totalist who welcomes the repugnant conclusion from the totalist who adds a clause to avoid it, if these two genuinely come apart.

I would also like you to name and place two positions that I have not seen given labels and that I would like the tree to introduce. The first is an arithmetic pronatalism, on which each additional worthwhile life is a fixed positive increment to value regardless of how many lives already exist. The second is a threshold pronatalism, on which more people are better only up to some optimal size and worse beyond it, with the threshold derived from the welfare of the people who already exist rather than from any resource limit. If either turns out to be identical to a position already on the tree, say so instead of inventing a separate leaf for it.

Finally, flag any pair of positions that the tree cannot separate by any answer to its questions, and explain why they coincide for present purposes.

Keep the whole instrument to about a page: the tree in outline form, followed by a short key. No citations or reading list.
