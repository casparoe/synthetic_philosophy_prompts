I'm a retired geologist -- forty years of field work behind me and a lot of empty time in front of me now -- and I've been slowly teaching myself philosophy, mostly Popper lately, after a younger colleague kept insisting he'd "solved" the problem of induction and I wanted to see for myself whether that was even the sort of thing you could solve. Anyway, I've fallen into a standing arguement with an old friend of mine, a historian, over coffee most mornings. He's a cheerful determinist about human affairs, and I've been trying to explain to him the argument -- I think it's Popper's, though I couldn't give you the page -- that we can't predict the long-run course of history, because the future growth of knowledge is itself something we'd have to predict first, and we can't. He listens politely, nods, and then says the same six words every single time: "nothing new under the sun, old man." He clearly means it as a conversation-ender, and I've realised to my annoyance that I can't actually say what he's claiming.

So here's what I'd like from you. Take that one line -- nothing new under the sun -- and lay out the distinct, precise things it could actually be asserting, ordered from the trivial to the radical. Does it mean merely that the raw materials are always the same? That human nature is fixed, so the same dramas keep recurring? That every particular event is just an instance of some eternal type, so nothing is ever genuinely novel? That history is strictly cyclical, or strictly determined down to the detail? For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something we should just throw out -- and, most importantly, which of these readings would actually do the work my friend wants it to do, i.e. genuinely block the argument I've been making about the unpredictability of new knowledge. Give it to me as a menu of options I can carry back to him, not a single verdict, because the reading that matters is whichever one his argument actually needed.
