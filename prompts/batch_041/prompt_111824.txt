I'm a high school junior and the student chair of my school's peer accountability panel. We hear cases of academic dishonesty, theft, and bullying, and we recommend consequences to the assistant principal. We meet weekly, usually three or four cases at a time, and I get roughly twenty minutes per case. Our decisions are inconsistent: some members treat a rough home life as an excuse, others insist that everyone simply chooses, and we end up voting on gut feeling.

I recently came across Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, which seems to say that no one can be ultimately morally responsible for what they do, because a person's character and choices always trace back to facts they never chose. I want to understand what that argument actually commits us to and turn it into a procedure my panel can run on a case: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and what to do when the answers show that a student's conduct was shaped entirely by things outside their control. I do not want to walk into a hearing and announce that nothing anyone did was their fault and let a bully off, so the procedure has to preserve whatever kind of responsibility the argument leaves standing, distinguish it from the kind the argument rules out, and be honest about whether that distinction survives scrutiny or is just a comfortable gap we are papering over.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a student who plagiarized an essay during a year in which their parents divorced and the family moved twice; a student who joined a friend group in mocking a classmate and says they only went along with it; and a student who planned a theft weeks in advance and shows no remorse. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like, and you should say so plainly.

Please organize the response as follows: "What the Basic Argument Actually Establishes" (about 150 words); "The Procedure" (the numbered steps, roughly 500 words); "Case 1," "Case 2," and "Case 3" (about 200 words each); and "Where the Procedure Fails" (about 150 words), identifying the points where no procedure can decide and a person must.
