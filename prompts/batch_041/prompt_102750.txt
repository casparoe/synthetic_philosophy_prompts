ok so I run this little philosophy/rationality reading group out of my apartment, been doing it for like three years now, and I've got a session coming up that I actually want to be good. Its usually 12-16 people, pretty mixed — a couple grad students, a handful of software folks, one retired guy who's read literally everything, and a few people who mostly show up for the snacks. We've got 90 minutes and one co-facilitator besides me.

I want to do the wrong kind of reasons problem — the thing where there seem to be reasons you can have for an attitude that just... don't work, or don't work the way reasons for a plain action do. You know, the money-for-belief thing: somebody offers you cash to believe something and you sort of can't, or you can only by some weird backdoor. I've read enough of the metaethics-adjacent blogosphere to know the standard moves, and honestly the standard exposition is boring — everyone nods along and nothing feels weird. What I want is for people to run into it with their actual hands.

So design me a runnable activity. Rules, what materials I need to bring, a timeline that fits the 90 minutes, and a debrief. The key constraint: the idea has to show up in what happens, not in what I say. No lecture at the front, and I don't want to even use the phrase wrong kind of reasons until we're debriefing.

Here's the rough shape I'm imagining, take it or trash it. Everybody pairs up, and one person in each pair is the tempter, with a small real prize — I'll bring nice chocolate, a few five-dollar bills, whatever — and a little contract card. Different cards pay out for different attitudes:

- one pays you if you come to genuinely believe some trivial thing you've got no evidence about (that a made-up team is good, or that a coin I flipped came up heads when you didn't see it)
- one pays you if you genuinely want something you currently don't want (a flavor you hate, say)
- one pays you for merely intending to do something gross tomorrow night, with the money handed over tonight whether or not you actually do it (you probly know the puzzle I'm gesturing at)
- one pays you if you're genuinely amused by a terrible joke, or genuinely grateful to someone, or genuinely forgive them for a made-up slight
- and one control card that just pays you for a plain physical action, so we've got a baseline of something that obviously works

Then they try. Like 15-20 min, prizes sitting on the table. I expect the belief and the gratitude/forgiveness cards to just fail, the want card to be murky, the intention card to be the one people split on, and the control card to be over in five seconds after which they're bored.

The debrief is where I need you most. I want to map what actually happened back onto the problem, and I want at least three competing explanations on the table for what makes a reason the wrong kind — something like (1) it's about control, you just can't do attitudes at will; (2) it's about what the attitude is aimed at, its direction of fit; (3) it's about the reason having to bear on the very question the attitude answers, so a payout is answering a different question. Then say which of those the activity actually supports, which it can't distinguish, and where they come apart — the intention card and the emotion cards are the fun ones there, and I fully expect someone to bring up how the meditation/mindfulness crowd claims you can summon gratitude on command, so I want that on-ramp ready.

Also give me:

- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it. I'm already worried about the inevitable guy who insists he can just decide to believe whatever pays, and then obviously can't, and about pairs turning it into a free-will shouting match.
- the honest note on what the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. I assume that's the whole normative question — whether a wrong-kind reason is no reason at all, or a reason of the wrong kind, or a reason that just can't be your operative one — but tell me if I'm missing a bigger one.
- any epistemic-harm guardrails, because I definately do not want to actually talk 14 people into self-deceiving about real stuff for chocolate.

Keep it practical, I'll be running it myself.
