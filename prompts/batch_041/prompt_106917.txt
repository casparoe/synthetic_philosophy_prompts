Okay, so here's my situation. I'm a science and ideas reporter at a mid-size daily — I do the Thursday science page and one longer feature a month — and my inbox is a firehose of archaeology press releases. I keep getting burned. Last month I ran a piece built around a release that said a burial "proves" a prehistoric community had a belief in an afterlife, and a friend of mine who actually did her PhD in this stuff was polite about it but basically said the release had smuggled in about four inferential steps and I'd reported the last one as if it were the first. I don't have time to read site reports. I usually have the press release, maybe an abstract, a photo, and sometimes a 10-minute phone call with a lead author who is on a dig and calling me from a jeep.

What I want from you: a procedure. Something I can actually run on a release in about 10-15 minutes and that tells me how much of the claim I can responsibly report, how I should hedge it, and when I should just spike it.

So — you're a philosopher of science who specializes in archaeological inference, the epistemology of how you get from dirt, bones, and pots to claims about what people believed, valued, or how their institutions worked. Before you write anything, brainstorm for a minute about the ways this kind of reasoning goes wrong, and be specific about which of those failure modes are visible from a press release versus which ones are invisible from where I'm sitting. Then think step by step and give me the procedure.

Please build it around the stuff that actually matters, not generic "consider the source" advice. Off the top of my head the things I've heard matter: whether the object was found in a secure context at all (provenience, stratigraphy, association), what happened to the deposit between when people dropped it and when someone dug it up, the fact that one pattern can have many different causes, the difference between showing that people *did* something and showing that they *believed* something, and the old rule of thumb that you can read technology off of tools pretty well, subsistence a little worse, social organization with real difficulty, and belief almost never. Analogies to living societies seem to tell you what's possible rather than what happened. Use whatever's right from that list and add what I'm missing.

Format: ordered questions I ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "you can't run this claim," and — this matters — where it hands off to my judgment or to an actual archaeologist because the question is about values or about something I can't see from a release. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I'd love a two-minute version too, like the three questions I ask when I'm on deadline.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a release about a roughly 30,000-year-old burial of two children with thousands of pierced shell beads, framed as evidence of religious belief; (2) a release about a burned destruction layer with arrowheads lodged in a wall at a fortified settlement, framed as proof of a violent conquest; and (3) a case I'm not going to enjoy — a release about imported pottery turning up in a provincial town, which is the spine of a feature I've already half-written about the town being a cosmopolitan trade hub. Tell me straight what your procedure says I can and can't report there.

Finish with what this procedure can't do, and which kinds of releases will defeat it no matter how carefully I follow it.
