I have no academic training in economics, but for several years I have been trying on my own to understand a disagreement that I suspect is older and more tractable than the way it is usually reported. The two camps are, on one side, the critics of central planning — the tradition of Mises and Hayek — who hold that a socialist economy cannot allocate resources rationally because it has no market prices for the means of production, and that no planning board can gather or process the knowledge those prices carry; and on the other side, the defenders of market socialism, in the position associated with Lange, who hold that a planning board can set prices by trial and error, adjust them in light of shortages and surpluses, and thereby reproduce what a competitive market does, so that the remaining problems are political or about incentives rather than about whether rational calculation is possible at all. I would like you to draft the document these two camps could jointly sign if they sat down in good faith to establish where they actually stand.

Please produce it in this shape. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what both sides can accept about the nature of the problem, about what a planned economy could and could not do in principle, and about which sub-questions are empirical rather than conceptual. I want these to be substantive rather than platitudinous, and I want you to flag any claim that looks agreed only because it has been worded vaguely. Second, the precise points of remaining disagreement, each written in the strongest form its own side would endorse, so that neither side is made to accept a caricature of itself. Third, the cruxes: the smaller questions such that settling one would move at least one side, as distinct from questions on which the two merely restate their positions. I am especially interested in whether they disagree about (a) whether the informational role of prices can be separated from the incentive and discovery roles that markets play, (b) whether the knowledge that prices aggregate is in principle aggregable by a board or is by its nature dispersed and tacit, and (c) whether the obstacle to planning is computational or epistemic — that is, whether a sufficiently powerful computer would change the answer. Fourth, for each crux, a case, argument, or kind of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, together with the prediction each side makes about it, recorded before the fact. Fifth, wherever you conclude that the two camps have been talking past each other, or disagreeing about words rather than substance, say which part and why.

Two further things. I keep running into the analogy of a large firm that allocates resources internally without internal markets, and I want you to work out what that analogy does and does not show for each side. And I want you to address, without pretending it settles anything, whether cheap modern computation changes the character of the old argument, since both camps seem to have written as though the cost of gathering and processing information were fixed. Write it as a formal document both sides could put their names to.
