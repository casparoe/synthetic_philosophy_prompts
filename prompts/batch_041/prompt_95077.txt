I lurk in a Discord that's basically a LessWrong comment section that refused to die — half of us have been there since the Sequences were coming out chapter by chapter — and last week two of the regulars, Dev and Sam, spent maybe two hundred messages going at each other about a protocol our friend Mira says she'd sign up for if anyone ever actually built it (she's a neuroscientist, or at least plays one in the server). The protocol, as she describes it: swap one neuron at a time for a silicon unit that's supposed to be functionally equivalent to the neuron it replaces, spread over about twenty years, with Mira awake and lucid the whole time because she says that's the entire point. The detail that started the fight is the verification step. Before anything gets swapped, she records a baseline list of memories — a few dozen specific episodes, with whatever level of detail she can give. Then after every single implant she does a recall session where she's asked to describe those same episodes, and the techs nudge the new chip's parameters until her reports match the baseline. That's the whole protocol. No copy at the end, no transfer, just twenty years of one neuron at a time plus a tuning loop.

Here's roughly how it went, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) Dev said the conclusion is obvious — one neuron can't possibly be what makes you you, so by induction the Mira at the end is Mira, and anyone who denies it owes us the number of the neuron that kills her. (2) Sam said that's just the sorites move wearing a labcoat: "one neuron doesn't matter" is a premise you're free to reject, and you can't get from "no single step makes a difference" to "the whole process makes no difference" unless you assume identity is the kind of thing that has no sharp edges, which is the very thing in dispute — the honest answer is that there's no fact of the matter about which step is the last one where she's still her, not that the answer is "still her, all the way down." (3) Dev said fine, then run that on ordinary life: you're replacing your atoms constantly and there's no sharp boundary there either, so if vagueness means "can't be her," you weren't you last Tuesday, which is a reductio. (4) Sam said ordinary turnover is the body maintaining itself and the difference is the direction of causation — in the lab an external device is imposing a state on her, and the real question is whether the causal chain runs through Mira or through the lab, and the tuning loop is the tell: you're literally fitting a model to her outputs until it agrees. (5) Dev said "external" is doing all the work there — pacemakers, cochlear implants, deep brain stimulators, nobody calls those partial deaths — and the tuning is just calibration, and anyway all memory is reconstructive, you re-encode a memory every time you recall it, so tuning to her own reports isn't an external standard, it's her own standard. (6) Sam said that's exactly the problem: by year fifteen the "her own standard" is being supplied by a brain that's mostly chips, so the reference drifts along with the replica and nothing ever gets checked against the original — and here's the kicker, a straight-up copy made at the start and then trained the same way would pass every test in the protocol, so what work is the gradualness actually doing? (7) Dev said that last move cuts the other way: if a copy would pass every test, then "same person" isn't tracking any fact the tests can reach, and Sam is smuggling in a fact-of-the-matter about identity with no observable consequence, which is the sort of thing Sam claims not to believe in — either identity is observable or it isn't.

I genuinely couldn't referee this and I'm not going to pretend I know who won, so please don't just tell me they're both partly right, I can get that from the chat log. Go through it move by move — rate each of the seven from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each — and then say what both of them are missing. The thing I most want a verdict on is whether the recall-and-tune step actually changes anything or is a red herring, because that seemed to be the exact point where they stopped hearing each other.
