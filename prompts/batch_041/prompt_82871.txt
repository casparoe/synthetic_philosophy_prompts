I never studied philosophy formally, but for about a year now I've been circling a question I can't put down: what makes a state inside a creature — or inside a machine — actually mean something, rather than merely be caused by one thing and cause another? The answer I find most attractive, and understand least, says that meaning is a matter of history: a state means what it was recruited for, what it was selected to indicate, because the behaviour it triggers was selected for working when that indication was right. (I believe this view is called teleosemantics, though I've only read about it second-hand.) The answer that keeps pulling me back the other way says history is a red herring: what a state means is fixed by what it is doing now — by the information it actually carries, or by the way the surrounding system currently treats it — and where those come apart from the history, so much the worse for the history. I can argue both sides to a draw by myself, which is exactly the problem, so I would rather watch the two sides go at each other than read one more summary of them.

Here is a case I invented to try to break the tie, and it has beaten me. Take a species of moth with a single neuron, N, that fires when the moth hears ultrasound. As far as anyone can tell, N originally fired at bat sonar and drove evasive flight, and that is why the neuron and the flight reflex exist at all; the moths that had N survived the bats, and the ones that lacked it left no descendants. Now put the moth on an island where bats have been extinct for a few thousand years. On that island the females of the same species have drifted, over time, into singing at a pitch that happens to fall in the ultrasound range, and the males now use N — the very same neuron — to steer toward the female instead of away. So N fires at a mate and the moth flies toward the sound. Suppose this approach behaviour has been around long enough to be a genuine adaptation, but not so long that anyone would call the neuron a mate-detector by birth. Then add one more thing: a bioengineer, having studied this moth, builds a collision-avoidance chip for drones that works the same way — an internal state that fires at ultrasound and turns the drone away — except that in the chip the wiring is a blueprint, chosen on purpose, and there are no ancestors. I want to know what N means during the island period, what it means once the courtship behaviour is thoroughly established, and what the chip's state means, and I suspect the two views answer these three questions in a way that exposes what each one is really buying.

So please write me a dialogue between two people who hold these two views and are both good at it — neither one a fool and neither one a strawman, each taking the other's strongest point seriously and answering it rather than sliding past it. Let them work through my three cases, and let each of them use those cases to build their own arguments rather than recite a party line; I would much rather see them corner each other on the details than hear a survey of the field. It is fine if they end without a winner, provided they have told me what evidence or argument would settle the matter; and if one of them thinks the other's question is confused, or that my case is badly described, let them say so and say why. I don't need names, dates, or a reading list — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
