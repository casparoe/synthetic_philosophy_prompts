I've been covering this whole stochastic-parrots food fight for the mag for like two years now and I'm finally supposed to write the big think-piece, and every time I sit down I realize I don't actually have a spec for what would settle it. Everyone just lobs one case at you -- the parrot, the octopus, the Chinese room, whatever the newest benchmark is -- and declares victory, and I'm left thinking, ok, but what would a real theory of this even have to do? So before I do another round of interviews I want the actual desiderata list for a theory of what it takes for a system (silicon, meat, or otherwise) to genuinely understand language rather than just do something that looks like it from the outside. What does such a theory have to deliver -- which cases it's got to get right, which puzzles it has to dissolve instead of dodging, which distinctions it absolutely cannot let collapse -- with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

A couple of things I want baked in, because they're where I keep getting stuck. First, a distinction I keep groping toward and can't quite name: between a system that does the right thing because it's the kind of thing that understands, and one that lands in the same place for reasons that have nothing to do with understanding -- like a look-up table that happens to be huge, or a bilingual person vs a phrasebook. I don't want to smuggle in a verdict by how I draw that line, so tell me what a theory owes it. Second, the fact that the same outward behaviour can come from wildly different insides, and the same insides can be described at wildly different levels (weights, neurons, a person, a species) -- a decent theory has to say which level the understanding is supposed to live at. A colleague wanted to call the two poles thin and thick understanding but I think that jargon would sink the piece, so translate as needed.

Then give me the honest version: which of these requirements are genuine demands and which are just commitments of one camp (the behaviorists, the internalists, the people who think only biology can do it), and flag any pair of them you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- I have a feeling the list is going to turn out to be the interesting part, not any scorecard. And if there's one requirement that does the most work, that knocks out the most popular positions on either side, tell me which one and why. Weight it toward the criteria and the cases behind them, I can do the scoring myself once I have the list.
