I want to use the Monty Hall problem blow-up in the popular press — the switch answer, the furious letters from people with stats PhDs, the whole circus — as a case study for Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. Don't just re-derive 2/3. What I want: what are the rival indifference assumptions behind the naive 'two doors left, so it's 50/50' response, and how do they map onto Bertrand's different ways of picking a random chord? Does the host's protocol — does he always open a goat door, did he have to, is he randomizing? — change which indifference principle is even applicable, and if so is that a problem for the principle itself or just for people misusing it? Then reverse the lens: does the Monty Hall controversy actually teach the same lesson as Bertrand's paradox, or is it a different kind of failure? And what does that show about whether the principle of indifference is ever legitimate, or whether it's just a way of smuggling in assumptions. Be disciplined about where the analogy is exact, where it's loose, and where the principle is simply silent.
