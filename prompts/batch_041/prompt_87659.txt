You are a philosopher of mind whose specialty is the nature of pain and its disvalue. I want your careful reasoning, not a survey of positions. Before you write, think through the space of candidate principles and test each against the cases below; I suspect that not all of my verdicts can be saved, and I would rather be told which one has to go than have the conflict smoothed over.

I have been using pain asymbolia as a lever, so let me say how I understand it. In patients with damage to the insula, or a loss of its connections to the limbic system, a noxious stimulus is still registered, still localized, and still reported as hurting, and its intensity is still rated much as anyone would rate it; what is missing is the unpleasantness, the being-bothered. Such patients are, in the descriptions I have read, largely free of the distress.

Here are my verdicts, going by my gut on cases.

1. An ordinary bad headache: bad for me, and bad in proportion to how bad it feels.

2. A headache of the same intensity that I have decided not to be bothered by — I am absorbed in something else and feel no distress about it: still bad for me. Being unbothered does not make it not-bad; it only means I am not additionally suffering.

3. The asymbolia patient, who says the stimulus hurts but is untroubled by it: I am inclined to say the pain is not bad for them, or hardly at all. Yet I also want to say that something has gone wrong with them, that their condition is a misfortune.

4. A masochist who seeks out intense pain and reports it as painful: I want to say the pain is not bad for them, perhaps even good for them.

5. Post-workout soreness: painful, and I welcome it; not bad for me.

6. Phantom limb pain: bad for the person who has it, even though it reports nothing and is located in no existing part.

7. Congenital insensitivity to pain: no pain at all, ever — and a serious misfortune, which sits oddly with the thought that it is pain that is bad.

8. Two people with the same sensation in the chest, one who believes it is indigestion and one who believes it is a heart attack: the second is worse off, though the feeling is the same.

9. A pain I will not remember — a procedure under sedation that leaves no trace: bad for me while it lasted, I think, even though it leaves nothing behind.

Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets all of this, or close? State the principle precisely, and identify the clause that each case forces on it. My own half-formed proposal, which you should test and then repair rather than adopt: pain is bad exactly insofar as it is unpleasant, and being unpleasant is a property of the feeling itself rather than of whether the person minds it. Tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts the repaired principle cannot accommodate, and why. Then say what it predicts for two cases I have not listed: a non-painful unpleasant state — nausea, an itch, a bad smell — and the pain of someone who has chosen to undergo it for a reason, say as penance. If my verdicts split into two families that no single principle unifies, say so and give me both, with the case that shows the split.

Begin with the principle in one sentence. Do not cite the literature; I want the reasoning. At most 800 words.
