A colleague in interpretability research has been pressing the following analogy on me, and I would like it audited rather than applauded or dismissed.

A chess engine, he observes, is a system we describe in intentional terms without embarrassment: we say that it wants to win, that it thinks its queen is en prise, that it is looking for a way to expose the king. Nobody supposes this is more than a convenience. The engine has no inner life, no stake in the outcome, nothing it is like to be; the description is shorthand for a search over a game tree. A large language model, he continues, is the same kind of case. When it reports that it is trying to be helpful, that it is uncertain, or that it would prefer not to be shut down, we are projecting a mind onto a computation that has none, and the projection is no more truth-apt than the engine's wanting to win. He anticipates the obvious rejoinder — that engines are hand-coded while language models are trained — by noting that the strongest engines are themselves trained by self-play and are, if anything, more opaque than the engines of twenty years ago; the disanalogy, on his view, strengthens his hand. From this he draws a practical conclusion: a model's statements about its own beliefs and preferences should not be treated as evidence about anything, and the current interest in model honesty, deception, and welfare rests on a category mistake.

I am not persuaded, but I want the disagreement stated properly rather than traded as slogans. Please audit the analogy, as follows.

First, give me the correspondence: what plays the role of the engine, of the search, of the evaluation function, of the wanting, of the observer, and of the predictions the intentional description is meant to secure.

Second, say where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the conclusion he is drawing and those that are merely decorative.

Third, and this is what I most want settled: does the analogy, read in the direction its source seems to have intended, cut against him? My understanding is that the intentional stance was offered as a predictive strategy, and that its predictive success was meant to show that the attributed pattern is real rather than fictional. If so, then 'useful shorthand' and 'mere projection' are being run together, and the analogy may be doing the opposite of the work he wants from it.

Fourth, examine whether the analogy's persuasive force depends on an asymmetry of access. With a chess engine we can inspect the code and confirm that nothing resembling wanting is present; with a language model we cannot inspect the mechanism in that way. Is that asymmetry doing legitimate work, or is it smuggling in the conclusion?

Finally, tell me whether his practical conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if the analogy can be repaired into one that actually carries the weight, what the repaired version would look like.
