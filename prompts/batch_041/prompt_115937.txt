I'm a graduate teaching fellow and I've been handed a single seventy-five-minute session in a second-year undergraduate module called Mind, World, and Technology. The students have had one introductory metaphysics course, so they have met the question "what is real?" before, but they have no formal ontology, and most of them walk in already convinced that the whole topic is a squabble over the word "real" that a dictionary could dissolve. The session is on virtual worlds — whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real — and I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. I want the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it; the one example that carries the session; the misconceptions the students will predictably arrive with or form along the way, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; the question I should ask at the end to tell whether the idea has actually landed; and what I should openly decline to cover at this level.

I keep circling a candidate for the carrying example and cannot decide whether it will do the work: a sword that a player owns in a persistent multiplayer world, which exists as an entry in the server's authoritative state that every client is required to agree about, which the player bought with real money, and which the operator can delete at will. Tell me whether that example has what the session needs — I suspect it has to be a single object whose existence, ownership, and destruction can all be interrogated without my changing the subject — or whether something with less built-in machinery would serve better; if it is the right case, tell me precisely which questions to put to it and in which order. I would also like you to place a distinction a colleague from computer science keeps pressing on me: she says that everything we care about in this debate turns on the difference between objects that live in the shared authoritative state of a world, where the server can adjudicate disputes and every client must agree, and objects that live only in one client's local view, like a cosmetic modification only I can see. She thinks this difference cuts the metaphysical debate at a joint. I want to know where in the sequence it should enter, whether it really separates the cases that matter, and whether importing it early will illuminate the students or merely hand them a technical-sounding way to dodge the philosophical question.

The students will bring some things I can already predict: that "real" just means "physical," so the answer is trivially no; that anyone who says virtual harms are real must think a virtual stabbing is as bad as a stabbing; that "virtual" and "fictional" are interchangeable; and that the debate is settled by stipulating a definition and moving on. I expect they will also form at least one misconception I have not anticipated, and I want that flagged. Finally, tell me what to leave out — I am inclined to skip the modal and simulation-style arguments and anything about the nature of fiction as such, and to say so out loud rather than let them dangle — and give me the single closing question, built on a case they have not seen, that would show me whether they can now say what the realist and the fictionalist each owe us rather than merely recite a side.
