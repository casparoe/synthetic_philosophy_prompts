I'm on the standards committee of a loose alliance of about fourteen small research nonprofits -- same ballpark size, similar programs, and we keep facing the same recuring choice: whether to publish our own unflattering evaluation data, whether to pay for an outside audit we know will find problems, whether to adopt a shared reporting standard that makes each of us look worse but makes the whole field more legible. Each org decides for itself, we vote quarterly, and there's real money and reputation on the line. Before each vote we all write short position memos and circulate them, and then everyone reads everyone else's before deciding. None of us are game theorists, and we have one two-hour meeting to settle each one.

A colleague keeps bringing up superrationality -- her version: when the parties are symmetric and everyone knows everyone reasons the same way, you should choose the option you'd want everyone to choose, acting as if your choice fixes theirs, instead of the option that's best given what the others do. I can't tell if thats a real insight or a nice way of talking yourself into getting burned.

Can you turn it into a procedure the committee can actually run on each decision? The questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's values and not strategy. I especially want it to be straight about the part I'm suspicious of: does circulating our memos first actually make our choices correlated in the way the principle needs, or is that just cheap talk that a sharper org can free-ride on? And what do we do about the two members who've said flat out they'll do whatever's best for them no matter what the rest of us conclude?

Then run it on three cases: publishing failure data when we think two of the fourteen won't; chipping in to a shared tool that only pays off if at least eight of us fund it; and a third where I suspect the procedure tells us to eat a genuine loss for the group's sake -- I'd rather see that one than have it smoothed over.

Finish with where this will misfire: the steps that are really coin flips dressed up as principle, the question of who even counts as symmetric with me (do I have to worry about strangers who happen to reason like me?), and what the procedure can't do at all.

American English please, and skip the citations and reading lists -- I want the machinery, not the literature.
