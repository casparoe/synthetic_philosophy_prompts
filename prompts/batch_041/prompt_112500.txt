Question:

"Popper is usually credited with the so-called paradox of tolerance. State it, and then separate two ways of reading it. On the logical reading, tolerance taken as an absolute principle is self-undermining: applied consistently it licenses the conduct that destroys it, so always-tolerate cannot be a coherent norm. On the strategic reading, the claim is empirical and contingent: a society that in fact tolerates organized intolerance will, as a matter of fact, tend to be taken over by it. Explain the difference between the two readings, say which one the familiar response -- that we need not tolerate the intolerant -- is answering, and then assess whether that response resolves the paradox or merely relocates it. Be explicit about whether the response permits intolerance of the intolerant or requires it."

Student's answer:

"Both readings agree that unlimited tolerance is impossible, they just locate the impossibility differently. The logical reading is internal to the principle: if tolerate-everything is your rule, then by your own rule you have to tolerate the people who would abolish tolerance, so the rule destroys the thing it was supposed to protect. That's a contradiction inside the norm, not a prediction about how events will go. The strategic reading drops the principle and makes a prediction instead: a society that tolerates fascists will end up with a fascist government, the way Weimar did. So the logical reading is about what tolerance means and the strategic reading is about what happens.

The familiar response -- we tolerate everything except intolerance -- is aimed at the logical reading. Once you say tolerance isn't absolute but has a limit, the rule stops being self-undermining, because tolerate-everything-except-those-who-would-destroy-tolerance doesn't instruct you to tolerate its own destroyers. So it resolves the logical version.

It also answers the strategic version, since the same limit that saves the principle also tells you to shut down the people the strategic reading is worried about. Once you've drawn the line at intolerance there's nothing left for the empirical worry to do; the line takes care of both.

So I think the response resolves rather than relocates the paradox. If tolerance is bounded, intolerance of the intolerant isn't a paradox at all, it's just the edge of the principle. And the edge isn't arbitrary, because it's fixed by the same value tolerance is meant to serve -- keeping open the space for people to live and speak together. Tolerating the intolerant would betray that value, so we have to refuse them. It's not optional: if we're really committed to tolerance we must suppress the intolerant, otherwise we aren't committed to it."
