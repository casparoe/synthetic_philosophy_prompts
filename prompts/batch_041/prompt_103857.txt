Me and two mates from my old neuroscience masters were in the pub and got stuck on whether anything can have a reason without anyone actually doing the reasoning. It kicked off because Femi had been reading about bacteria that swim up a sugar gradient — they swim straight for a bit, then tumble, then straight again, and on average they end up where the sugar is. Cass said: right, you can say the bacterium "is trying to reach the sugar" or that it "has a reason to swim that way", but that's just us being lazy with language. Strip it back and there's a receptor, a flagellum and some chemistry, and no reason anywhere — we're projecting one.

Femi said: but the machinery is genuinely sensitive to a real fact, namely the sugar concentration, and the fact that more sugar is better is exactly what explains why the mechanism is built the way it is. So the reason is doing explanatory work even though nothing in there represents it.

Cass: by that standard everything tracks reasons. A river bends round a rock "to get to the sea". If the river counts, the word has stopped meaning anything.

Femi: no — the difference is selection. The bacterium's behaviour was shaped over generations precisely because it correlated with finding sugar; there's a history of that sensitivity being kept and the alternatives being weeded out. The river wasn't selected. That's why the bug has a reason and the river doesn't.

Cass: but selection is blind. Nobody chose anything. So it was never anyone's reason, and if it was never anyone's reason then it isn't a reason at all, it's just a cause that happens to keep the line alive. And take your own case — when the bacterium swims toward something that mimics sugar but is actually poison, you'd want to say it made a mistake. But it didn't make a mistake, it did exactly what its wiring tells it to. The mistake is in our description, not in the bug.

Femi: mistake is real though. A mistake is just a failure to track the consideration the system is supposed to track, and "supposed to" is fixed by what it was selected for. Moths fly into flames because their steering is built to navigate by moonlight and a flame breaks it. That's a genuine malfunction, not a slip in my wording. So there's a fact of the matter about what the system has reason to do, whether or not anyone says so.

Cass: fine, then your immune system has reasons, and my thermostat has reasons — its reason is to keep the room at twenty degrees. Do you actually want to say the thermostat has reasons?

Femi: the thermostat was designed by someone who had reasons. The bacterium wasn't.

Cass: so now reasons need either a designer or a selection history, and all you've done is make "has a reason" mean "has a function". That's a new label for the same old thing, not a discovery. And it definitely can't be what you started out claiming, which was that there's something it's like to be the bacterium's reason.

They turned to me to settle it and I couldn't. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why — and then say who's more right overall and what both of them are missing. Write it in British English please.
