I have been trying to work out what the Judy Benjamin problem is supposed to show, and I think I have finally extracted its lesson — but the lesson is strong enough that I want it checked before I build anything on it. Here is the case as I reconstruct it. Judy has been dropped into terrain she cannot identify. All she knows is that she is somewhere within one of two sectors, which I will call North and South, and she regards North as twice as likely as South. Within either sector she might be on the road or in the woods, and within each sector she regards those two possibilities as equally likely, so her prior over the four combinations of sector and terrain is fixed accordingly. She then receives a single radio message, which she has no reason to doubt, and the message tells her only this: if she is in the North sector, then she is on the road. She is not told which sector she is in, and nothing else about her situation is disclosed. It seems plain to me that this information leaves her posterior underdetermined: there is a whole family of distributions over the four combinations that satisfies the constraint that she is on the road if she is in the North sector, and nothing in the message singles out one member of that family over the others. My conclusion is that the case is a counterexample to Bayesian conditionalization — that when information arrives in conditional form the Bayesian apparatus has no answer to give, and that conditional information therefore cannot be handled within a Bayesian framework at all. On that assumption, what I need is a rule from outside the Bayesian tradition: which non-Bayesian rule of updating should I adopt, and what does it deliver in this case? Please also tell me how confident you are in your overall verdict, and identify the one point in your answer that you are least sure of.
