I run a small housing policy shop — four of us, about two years of funding, and a mandate to push our city council on zoning reform. We're not academics and we can't run big studies, so our only real asset is arguements. I want to spend those two years on the conceptual questions that actually do work in the fights we keep having, not on stuff that only matters to other policy shops.

Here's the thing I keep tripping over. Every zoning fight in this city has two ledgers running at once. One is about what the neighborhood owes the people already living there — the ones who bought in, planted trees, sat through a decade of meetings, and who have some claim that the place will stay recongizably itself. The other is about what the city owes everyone else — the people priced out, the people who'd move in if they could, the people whose only realistic shot at a decent school or a short commute runs through a neighborhood like this one. Nobody I talk to keeps both ledgers open at the same time. The homeowner side argues from the first and treats the second as an abstraction; the YIMBY side does the exact reverse. I've started calling it the two-ledger problem, and I think most of our stalemates are really about how to merge the ledgers, or whether they can be merged at all.

So, within that — plus whatever else in the zoning and NIMBY space you think sits upstream of it — what are the open questions that matter most, and in what order should we take them? I want the reasoning more than the list. For each one: why is it upstream of the other things we argue about, is it tractable for four people with no data, no lawyers and no PhDs, and what would actually move it up or down my ranking — a court ruling, a new kind of tenure, a change in who bothers showing up to meetings?

Two things I specifically want you to take a side on, because I suspect that's where the real disagreement lives and everyone dodges them. First, whether standing in these fights should be weighted by tenure and ownership at all — whether someone who's lived on a block thirty years has a stronger claim on its rules than someone who moved in last year, or whether that's just an intuition that happens to protect incumbents. Second, whether the fix is changing the rules (upzone, allow more by right) or changing who holds the power to make them (move discretion up to the region, or down to the block). Those feel like different questions to me, but I keep hearing them collapsed into one, and if they are different then one of them is probably upstream of the other, and I'd like to know which. The right-to-the-city tradition is somewhere in the background here and I've read enough of it to be dangerous, so use it if it helps, but don't just hand me back its slogans.

Last thing: tell me what I'm missing. What question would you put at the top of this list that I haven't mentioned, and why does it beat the ones I have?
