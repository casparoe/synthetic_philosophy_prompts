I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a secular version of the idea that history bends toward justice. She's not religious—she thinks the arc is real but immanent, something like a structural tendency rather than a promise from anyone. I want eight to ten questions that actually press her, not just let her restate the thesis. Please include at least one question each on: whether the claim is meant descriptively (a trend we can measure) or regulatively (a commitment that guides action), and how she'd tell the difference; how the view survives the contingency objection—that lots of moral progress looks like the result of accidents, coalitions, and luck; what she thinks the arc is made of mechanistically, if there's no designer (ratchets, learning, diminishing returns to domination, whatever); whether the view needs a theodicy, i.e., whether it has to say past suffering was redeemed or instrumental, and what happens if it doesn't; and what evidence over the next century would make her abandon it. For each of those, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Also throw in a couple of questions on the historiography angle: if the arc is partly a retrospective narrative we impose on events, how does she avoid the charge that it's a selection effect rather than a feature of the world? And one on whether justice in the slogan is doing any independent work—does the arc bend toward any determinate conception of justice, or just toward less domination, more inclusion, etc.? Keep it concrete, and no generic what-got-you-into-philosophy stuff.
