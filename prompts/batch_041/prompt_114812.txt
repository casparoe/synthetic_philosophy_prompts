Exam question: In this course we have treated naturalism and normativism as rival accounts of the concept of disease. Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory is the leading naturalist account; normativists hold that an adequate account of disease must make essential reference to evaluative judgments about harm, undesirability, or the goals of medicine. Consider two cases. Case (i): an 82-year-old man has substantial hearing loss, confirmed audiometrically, caused by the ordinary age-related degeneration of the inner ear; hearing loss of this degree is statistically typical for men of his age, but his auditory system is functioning well below the level of the species design. Case (ii): a 24-year-old man is intensely distressed by the size and shape of his nose; he believes it is deformed, has stopped leaving the house, and describes himself as 'diseased'; measurement shows that his nose falls within ordinary anatomical variation and that its function is entirely normal. Which account, naturalist or normativist, better captures the concept of disease? State each position in your own words, say what each would say about both cases, and use the American Psychiatric Association's removal of homosexuality from its diagnostic manual in the early 1970s to say what the history of classification shows about the dispute.

Script: The naturalist and the normativist disagree about whether 'disease' is a descriptive or an evaluative concept. Boorse's biostatistical theory is the most developed naturalist account. On his view a disease is an internal state that reduces the functional ability of some part of the organism below the level typical for its species, where typicality is fixed statistically within a reference class such as an age group and a sex. Health is just normal functional ability. The theory is also a version of the harmful-dysfunction analysis: it requires both that some biological function be disrupted and that the resulting condition be harmful or undesirable for the person who has it, since otherwise we would not call it a disease at all. Boorse's aim is a value-free science of disease: whether someone is diseased is a biological fact that a pathologist could settle without asking whether anyone dislikes the condition. Normativists deny this. They hold that the concept of disease is essentially evaluative: to call a condition a disease is to say that it is bad for the person, or that it defeats the goals of medicine, or that it is the sort of thing that ought to be treated. Normativism does not deny that biological facts are relevant to disease classification; it denies that they are sufficient. Case (i) is the harder one for the naturalist. The man's auditory system is plainly not functioning as it was designed to, so there is a dysfunction. But if the reference class is men over eighty, the condition is statistically normal, and the biostatistical theory tells us that statistically typical conditions are not diseases, so the theory seems to classify age-related hearing loss as healthy, which is counterintuitive. The naturalist's best reply is that the choice of reference class is doing all the work here, and that a theory which can be made to say either thing depending on how the class is drawn has a problem; I take this to be a point against the naturalist. Case (ii) is the harder one for the normativist. The man is suffering and his life is badly disrupted, which is exactly what the normativist's harm condition picks out, but his nose is anatomically and functionally normal, and most of us would say that he is not diseased but distressed about his appearance. So harm alone is not sufficient for disease, and some dysfunction condition is needed as well; this is a point against the normativist. The history of the DSM settles the dispute in favour of the normativist. Homosexuality was listed as a mental disorder until the American Psychiatric Association removed it in the early 1970s. The biology of sexual orientation did not change; what changed was the social and political climate, and the removal was driven substantially by campaigning rather than by new scientific evidence. Since the biological facts stayed the same while the classification changed, disease classification must be a matter of social values rather than natural fact. The concept of disease is therefore normative through and through, and Boorse's hope for a value-free science of disease is misplaced.
