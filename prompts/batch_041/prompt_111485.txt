I'm trying to get clear on the reference class problem, and a passage in a general-audience book on probability that a colleague lent me has left me unsure how much of it to trust. The author writes:

"The reference class problem is a technical difficulty that arises for frequency interpretations of probability and for nothing else. If you say that the probability of an event is the long-run relative frequency with which events of its kind occur, you owe us an account of what its kind is — the reference class it belongs to. But every event belongs to indefinitely many classes, and different classes yield different frequencies, so the frequency interpretation is incomplete: it cannot assign a probability to a single case. Bayesians escape the difficulty, because a Bayesian conditions on the total evidence, and the total evidence determines a unique reference class. That is a genuine point in favour of subjective Bayesianism, though Bayesians of course have their own trouble in choosing a prior. In the end, this is simply Hume's problem of induction in modern dress: the worry that the future need not resemble the past is the same worry as our inability to justify a choice of reference class, and the two stand or fall together. In practice the problem is benign. Statisticians know to use the narrowest class for which they have reliable data, and in any real application they agree about which class that is."

I know just enough about the topic to suspect that one or two of these sentences are doing something the literature would not endorse, but not enough to say which. Please audit the passage claim by claim: which claims would someone working on this topic accept as stated, which are defensible but overstated, and which are straightforwardly wrong. I would particularly like to know what a defender of the frequency interpretation would say in reply, and whether the conclusion the author draws — that all this is a reason to prefer subjective Bayesianism — survives once the rest is corrected. I am inclined to think the sentence about Bayesians is the weakest, but I would rather not be told only what I already suspect. No citations or reading list, please; I want the substance.
