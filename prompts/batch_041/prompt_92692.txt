I'm assembling an episode of a small philosophy-of-mind interview podcast, and the guest is a philosopher who defends a view she calls the readiness theory of emotion. The core of it is that an emotion is neither a judgment nor a perception in the philosophers' sense but the felt experience of the body gearing up for a particular kind of action, with any "aboutness" the emotion has inherited from that readiness rather than contributed by an evaluative appraisal: fear is the felt readiness to flee or freeze, and it is about the danger only in the way a reaching hand is about the cup. Three consequences she draws from this are what I most want the conversation to press on. First, emotions are individuated by their action tendencies rather than by appraisals or by any valence-and-arousal signature. Second, an emotion can be assessed as correct or incorrect only derivatively, the way an action can succeed or misfire, so the familiar puzzle of recalcitrant emotion -- fearing what you know to be harmless -- is not a paradox but a tendency that has fired without its proper object. Third, regulating an emotion is always at bottom reshaping the tendency, so reappraisal is merely an indirect route to the same destination. I've been arguing about this for weeks with a friend who works on motor control and keeps insisting that readiness and feeling come apart, which is part of why I want the questions to be sharper than the ones I'd come up with myself.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that force her past restating the view, and give each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I need at least one question on each of the following, and I'd like the follow-ups to anticipate her standard deflection -- usually some version of "that's a feature of action tendencies, not a bug" -- and to press past it rather than accept it. On individuation: build the question around a case where two emotions share both a bodily profile and an action tendency, say two people fleeing the same animal with the same readiness, one in terror and one in cold determination, and ask what does the distinguishing work if not appraisal. On intentionality: press how a felt readiness located in the body ever comes to be about something in the world, using as the two horns a panic attack (full readiness, no object) and a person whose body is pharmacologically prevented from gearing up while every appraisal is intact. On the correctness clause: ask her to separate an emotion that merely misfires from one that is genuinely unfitting, and whether her view can call a fear of a harmless spider wrong rather than merely idle. On the felt quality: use a case in which a brain implant induces the complete action readiness with no felt quality at all, together with its mirror image, someone who reports the feeling with no readiness whatsoever -- do these count as emotions on her view, and if not, what work is the feeling doing that the readiness was not already doing? On the relationship to older views: ask what separates her position from the James-Lange picture on which emotions are simply feelings of bodily change, given that the readiness is supposed to be carrying all the weight. On scope: ask how far down the phylogenetic scale the account extends, and whether "gearing up for action" is a low enough bar to be embarrassing, since a thermostat arguably gears up for something. And on valence: ask whether pleasantness is just the pull of the tendency or something the tendency cannot supply.

Close with a question about what would count against the view -- the case, experiment, or phenomenon she would concede would sink it -- and, if she names one, a follow-up asking whether the theory as she has stated it actually forbids that outcome or has been qualified in a way that makes it safe. The audience is general but literate, so no softball opener about how she came to philosophy and no questions that only restate the setup above in interrogative form; I want each question to turn on the actual content of the position and to make the next question harder to answer than the last.
