I'm in a reading group on global history and we keep running into the same wall, and I honestly can't tell if it already has a name. Here's the pattern. Someone wants to write a history that isn't centered on Europe, so they pick a topic that seems to slip the usual story — a history of one commodity, or of one ocean, or of a single crop moving around the world. But to make it a history and not just a pile of separate local accounts, they need some thread to pull the reader along, and the thread almost always turns out to be something like the growth of long-distance trade, or the spread of empires, or the making of a world market — and then the milestones strung along that thread are, once again, Lisbon, Amsterdam, London. So the book is anti-Eurocentric in what it says it's doing and Eurocentric in its spine. The alternative I keep seeing is worse: the author refuses any thread, and you get six chapters that never quite touch, and you can't say what the book is about except that it's "about" everywhere at once. I don't think this is just Eurocentrism, because that feels like a critique of content and this seems to be about form — about what it even takes to turn many places into one story. Somebody in the group said it's just diffusionism, and somebody else said the fix is to multiply the centers or write "connected" histories, but when I actually read those they seem to have the exact same problem one level down.

So: does this bind have an established name? I'm less interested in the label than in the shape of the thing, and I won't be satisfied with a bare term. Is the problem that any single narrative needs a protagonist, or that the protagonists available to us are all European, or is it something else entirely — maybe a confusion between "one story" and "one standpoint"? And if there's no name for it, could you coin one and tell me what it would and wouldn't cover? For instance, does a history of the Atlantic world that stays entirely inside Africa and the Americas escape the bind or just relocate it, and is a deliberately fragmented, non-narrative history a solution or a different failure wearing the same coat? I feel like I'm circling something real but I can't tell if it's one thing or three things that happen to look alike.
