I'm a philosopher of biology at a large state university, and this June I have been asked to give the commencement address for the biology department: roughly a hundred and fifty undergraduates, a couple dozen master's students, a dozen PhDs, their families filling the back half of a tent on the quad, a brass quintet that plays for twenty minutes before the procession, the dean, and — this is the part I keep thinking about — a set of grandparents who will hear every sentence as either encouragement or insult. My friend, who chairs the department, gave me the usual constraints and one unusual one. Seven minutes, no more. Nothing about the job market. And the speech has to turn on the idea of function — on what a thing is for — because the graduates are scattering into medicine, conservation, biotech, secondary teaching, and two of them into philosophy of science, and she wants them to leave with a live question rather than a slogan. She has also asked me to work in, if I can, the fact that one of the PhDs spent five years studying eye reduction in a species of cave-dwelling fish, whose eyes are still present and still developing and no longer do anything for the animal.

That last detail is where my difficulty lies, because the two accounts of biological function I work on give different verdicts about what such a structure is for, and I cannot let a commencement address equivocate between them. On the etiological account, a trait's function is whatever it was selected for: history does the work, so a structure that once earned its keep and now does nothing still has the function it was selected to serve, while a trait that happens to help its bearer today but was never selected for helping has none. On the causal-role account, a trait's function is its contribution to a present capacity of the system it belongs to: history drops out, so a structure that currently does nothing has no function whatever its past, while a lucky byproduct that now does something does have one. I want the address to use the language of purpose honestly in front of both halves of that tent — to say at least one thing a grandparent will find moving and a biologist in the third row will recognize as the real disagreement — without awarding the win to either of the two colleagues in the front row who have been arguing about this in print for fifteen years. So the speech should include one short passage on a case where the accounts diverge: I would like the cave fish eyes, and if it fits, the further case of an organ that arose by accident rather than by inheritance and pumps blood exactly as a heart does. It should be clear, without becoming a lecture, that whether we call such things functional depends entirely on which question we are asking, and that this is not a defect in biology but a feature of it.

Please write the address. About nine hundred words, warm without being saccharine, plain enough that a grandmother with no science background follows every step, and it must end as a send-off rather than as a seminar — the tent is hot and the quintet is waiting. After the speech, give me a short list, five sentences at most, of the sentences you deliberately kept out of it, and for each, which of the two accounts it would have been false under. That list is for me, not for the podium.
