I'm writing a novel set at an emerald mine and my narrator — she sorts rough stones on the day shift and keeps a notebook — is a lapsed philosophy student who never got over Goodman's grue puzzle. I want the poem she writes in that notebook. Make it a pantoum, five four-line stanzas, because the form is the whole point for me: lines come back verbatim in a new context, so the same words can stop meaning what they meant, which is her whole obsession — that we say green and not grue not because the stones told us anything but because of which words we'd already spent. So the observational lines should keep returning unchanged while what they license flips, and put the cutoff somewhere in the middle stanzas so a reader can feel one sentence go from supporting one prediction to supporting the other. In a pantoum the last line is the first line come back, so let that be the payoff: the opening line reads as plain description and the closing one reads as grue. She also knows the date isn't the real culprit, so give me one stanza where the cutoff is a place instead of a time — stones from this side of the river, stones from that — and make that stanza still work. And she's noticed a grue-speaker would find green the strange word, so turn that over somewhere without explaining it. Keep the diction working-class, dust and shift changes, no lecturing, and dont resolve it; she isn't after an answer, she's trying to write down what its like to know the word you're using is just a habit. Someone who's taught the riddle should nod, and someone who's never heard of it should still get a poem.
