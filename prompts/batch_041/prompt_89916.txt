I host a fortnightly discussion group that grew out of the rationalist blogosphere: fourteen regulars, ninety minutes, a whiteboard, a room of people who are fluent in credences, betting arguments and Dutch-book demonstrations and who have already been through the standard coherence games more than once. I want to run one session on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, and I want it to be an activity rather than a talk — something where the group backs itself into the result before anyone names it.

The pressure I want them to feel, and I think it can be staged rather than asserted, is this. They will price a conditional ticket by the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent. Fine. Then the same pricing rule, applied to the credence function they would have after learning the consequent, gives a price of one, and applied to the credence function they would have after learning the negation of the consequent gives zero. But the price now has to be the news-weighted average of those two later prices, which is just bookkeeping about a ticket that will be revalued. So the ticket they priced at the conditional probability must in fact be worth exactly their unconditional credence in the consequent — and therefore their credence in the consequent is unmoved by the antecedent, for any antecedent and any consequent they care to name. The point I want them to arrive at, in their own case, is that learning the barometer dropped tells them nothing about rain. They should pick the example themselves so that the conclusion is visibly absurd to them, and they should notice that the argument never used anything about the particular example, so the absurdity is perfectly general.

Design it for me as follows.

1. Rules, materials, roles and a payoff scheme that needs no real money — tokens, with something small and silly at stake for the winner — including the exact wording of the price sheet (I want each price posted three times: now, after the consequent is confirmed, after it is disconfirmed) and the settlement rule that makes the tickets tradeable. I want roles arranged so that the discovery comes from inside the game: some participants posting prices, others trading against posted prices with an incentive to find an incoherent set, one registrar doing the weighted-average arithmetic in public with physical tokens rather than algebra.

2. A round-by-round plan with timings, opening cold, no reading required beforehand, and a rule that nobody says the word triviality or names any author until the debrief. Include an optional extension round on negations: what the pricing rule forces you to say about the negation of a conditional versus the conditional with a negated consequent, and why that should strike them as wrong.

3. A debrief that maps each moment of the session onto the corresponding step of the argument, and that is explicit that the step doing the work is the assumption that the same rule governs the updated credence function — I want the group to see that the squeeze depends on a closure assumption they never agreed to, and to be able to say what each escape route costs: giving up truth conditions for conditionals, restricting the rule to simple conditionals, denying closure, or accepting the conclusion that nothing is evidence for anything.

4. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the two people in the room who have read the literature and will spoil the discovery, the participant who refuses to price conditionals at all on the grounds that they have no truth values, and the argument about whether a conditional is vacuously true when its antecedent fails. For each, tell me how to handle it in the moment, and give me a shorter version that works if only eight people turn up.

5. An honest note on which feature of the result the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. I suspect it is the generality of the theorem — that one felt squeeze is not a proof, and that the group may leave believing they have refuted the heuristic of reasoning from conditional credence when the real target is the view that conditionals are propositions with probabilities. Tell me whether I am right about that, and what else is missing.

Please give me facilitator notes complete enough that a competent colleague could run the session from them if I am away, and keep the whole thing usable with one printed page and a whiteboard.
