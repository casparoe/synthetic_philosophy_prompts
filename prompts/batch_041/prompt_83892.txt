Next month I'm giving a paper to an archaeology crowd and my claim is that "collapse" is a bad unit of analysis. My line: in the three canonical cases everyone reaches for -- the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Terminal Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui -- nothing that deserves the name actually collapsed. What happened in each case is what I'm calling decomposition: the higher-level integration comes apart, but the parts (populations, farming systems, settlements, local cults) persist and recombine, often within a generation or two. "Collapse," as it's normally used, implies destruction, where the parts go too, and I claim there's no well-documented case of that at the scale of a whole society. In the Maya case the thing that "fell" was a political order, not a population or a maize system; in the Bronze Age it was palace-centred exchange, not the people; Rapa Nui is the case where the evidence for any sudden failure at all is thinnest, which is exactly why it gets told as the moral fable.

The room will be thirty-odd people, mostly settlement archaeologists who each work on one of those three cases, plus two from a resilience/complex-systems group. They're allergic to grand narrative, theyve all internalized the backlash against Diamond-style ecocide stories, and at least two of them think "society" is a word we should stop using altogether.

I don't need citations, I need the fight. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
