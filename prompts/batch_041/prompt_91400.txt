I sit on my university's animal care and use committee — I'm the token philosopher, the rest are two vets, a researcher, and a community member — and I've been asked to draft a one-page screening procedure we can run on every protocol before it goes to full review. I want it built on Kant's actual position on animals, not the sanitized version where he's basically a proto-welfare guy in a powdered wig. As I remember it: animals aren't rational, so they're not ends in themselves, so we have no direct duties to them at all; what get called duties to animals are really duties to ourselves, about not dulling our natural sympathy for humans through cruelty and about cultivating kindness; he seems to allow using animals for food and work, and he says something about not abandoning a dog that's served you its whole life, and maybe that painful experiments are ok for the sake of medicine but not for idle curiosity. I'm not sure about the last bit, so use whatever you actually know and flag it if you're unsure — and please don't put anything in quotes, I don't want to be the guy who misquoted Kant in a committee document.

What I need is not a summary. Turn it into a procedure. Specifically:

- An ordered list of questions we ask about a protocol, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because the question is a value call and not a fact.
- A step I care about most: a maxim-of-character test. Kant's universal-law test doesn't do much work here because animals can't be rational agents and the whole action is about what the agent becomes. So I want the test reframed as something like "could a being who has to live among other humans will this as a settled habit of character, given that cruelty dulls sympathy and kindness feeds it" — and then I need you to tell me whether that reframing is actually in Kant or is my invention, and if it's my invention, whether it's a faithful extension or a different theory wearing Kant's coat.
- A second thing I want you to try: a bridge step that separates "using an animal as a means" (which Kant allows) from "using it in a way that makes the agent into someone who treats rational beings as mere means" (which he doesn't). I don't know if there's a principled bridge there or if I'm just smuggling in direct duties through the back door. If it's smuggling, say so in the document.
- A divergence flag. Our institution's assurance statement says animals have intrinsic value and are moral patients in their own right. Kant says no such thing. So every step of the procedure needs a flag showing where the Kantian answer and our institutional premise come apart, so the committee can see the seam instead of pretending there isn't one.
- A dulling test that doesn't require empirical evidence we don't have. We can't run a study on whether a given protocol hardens our grad students, but I still need something better than vibes. Whatever you give me, be honest about whether it's a real test or a way of writing down a judgment.

Then run the procedure on three protocols: (1) inducing a painful but treatable condition in rats to test a cancer drug, with analgesia; (2) keeping a dog breeding colony and euthanizing the surplus animals, where the colony is needed for a line of research with real human payoff; (3) a proposal to spend a lot of money on enriched housing for pigs, where the payoff is entirely to the pigs. At least one of those should come out in a way I won't like, and I'd rather you pick which one and tell me why it stings than have all three come out cozy. For each run, show the steps, the verdict, and the divergence flag.

Finally, tell me what this procedure can't do — the cases it will misfire on, the places where the Kantian machinery just runs out, and anything in it that's a judgment call dressed up as a procedure. About a page, in American English, no citations or reading lists, and remember: no quotation marks anywhere.
