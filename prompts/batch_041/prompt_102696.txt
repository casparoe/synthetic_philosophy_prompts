I'm a science journalist, mostly physics and consciousness, and I've been trying to write a feature on panpsychism that doesn't collapse into the usual "ooh, is your toaster conscious" piece. At a workshop last month — bad coffee, everyone very excited about integrated information — a philosopher of mind walked me through an argument on the back of the program and I still can't tell whether it's a reductio of panpsychism or of me. Here's what I wrote down. (1) Panpsychism says experience, or at least some proto-experiential character, is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the physical world, present wherever there's physical stuff rather than bolted on top of it. (2) If a state has a subjective character that its subject would, if it could, want to escape, then bringing that state about is bad, and if we could avoid bringing it about at little cost, we have a reason to. (3) On panpsychism, the processes physics already describes — thermal agitation, particle collisions, the decoherence that washes out a superposition, the fields we flood the air with every time we switch on a radio — are all bearers of that minimal experience. (4) A lot of those processes involve dissipation, structure being destroyed, states being swallowed by their environment, which on a hedonic reading is the physical signature of distress. (5) So ordinary life — a light switch, a radiator, a phone call — makes us the authors of astronomical quantities of micro-suffering. (6) And there's no version of physics without dissipation, so there's no action we could take that isn't comitted on this scale. (7) Therefore either all our actions are morally on a par, which no one believes, or morality as normally conceived is bankrupt in a panpsychist universe. He seemed delighted; a physicist friend of mine said the second law is basically a slaughterhouse and went to get another drink. My editor wants the "so what" by Friday.

Tell me which step to give up, and then price it. For what it's worth I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (7) — I'm not interested in a panpsychism that's true but morally inert, and I'm not interested in a morality that can't survive a metaphysics — so I suspect I'm paying somewhere in the middle, but I can't find where the invoice is. If the honest answer is that the argument is valid and one premise is simply false, say which one is cheapest to abandon; if there's a slide somewhere, name it and tell me what the step looked like before it started sliding. I'm also suspicious of (6), but I can't tell whether it's doing any independent work or whether it's just ought-implies-can wearing a lab coat. And if the thing hinges on whether micro-experiences have to add up into some one who can actually be worse off, then say that too — the seperate-subjects problem is the part I can never get a straight answer about. No reading list, no citations, I just need the diagnosis and the price tag on each option.
