I teach a second-year undergraduate course on rational choice and decision theory, and after a seminar last week a colleague and I argued for most of an evening about whether the representation theorems carry any normative weight at all — he holds that "can be represented as an expected utility maximizer" is a piece of mathematics with no implications for what anyone ought to do, while I was defending something closer to the standard line that coherence arguments genuinely constrain rational preference. We got nowhere, and the dispute has been in the back of my mind while marking, because I set a problem on precisely this and I want a second opinion on one script before the TA and I return it. I am not asking you to adjudicate the argument my colleague and I had; I want a fair grade and defensible comments on a student's work, and I would rather my own leanings not color either.

The question on the problem set was this: "(a) Representation theorems in decision theory are often summarized by the slogan that a rational agent maximizes expected utility. State as precisely as you can what a representation theorem such as Savage's actually establishes about an agent's preferences, and identify the additional premise needed to convert that result into the claim that a rational agent must maximize expected utility. (b) The 'money pump' (or Dutch book) argument against intransitive or otherwise incoherent preferences is sometimes offered as supplying that premise. Explain the argument, say whether it does supply the premise, and give the strongest objection to it that you can."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full. "Savage models a decision problem as a set of states, a set of acts (functions from states to consequences), and a preference relation over acts. His theorem says that if the preference relation is a weak ordering, satisfies the sure-thing principle, and meets certain technical conditions on the richness of the state space, then there exist a subjective probability measure over states and a utility function over consequences, the latter unique up to positive affine transformation, such that the agent's preferences agree with the ranking of acts by expected utility. So the theorem is a representation result: it tells us that an agent whose preferences satisfy the axioms can be described as an expected utility maximizer, not that she ought to be one. To reach the normative conclusion we need a further premise, namely that the axioms are requirements of rationality. But this premise turns out to be thin rather than substantive: the sure-thing principle, for instance, says only that if two acts agree on a state, your preference between them should not depend on what they do in that state, and it is hard to see how anyone could reject that without contradicting herself. The extra premise is therefore implicit in the very idea of having preferences at all, which is why the representation theorem does have normative force. The money pump is the sharpest illustration. Suppose an agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and starts out holding C. She will pay a penny to trade C for A, another to trade A for B, and another to trade B for C, and she is back where she started with three pennies gone. That loss is a sure loss rather than a gamble, so intransitivity is straightforwardly irrational, and since the same construction generalizes to the other axioms, a rational agent's preferences must be representable by expected utility. I know there is an objection here — that the agent's preferences may change over the course of the sequence, so the trades do not represent one fixed ordering and the sure loss is an artifact — but the pump can be run quickly with the preferences held fixed, so I do not think this rescues the intransitive agent; if anything it shows that only a perfectly consistent agent escapes being pumped."
