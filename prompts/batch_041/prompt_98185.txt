Next month I'm giving a talk to our hospital's clinical ethics fellowship and I want to walk in ready for the Q&A. Picture the room: mostly clinicians — a couple of OBs, a NICU attending, two palliative-care people — plus a disability-studies scholar from the university and one analytic philosopher who works on population ethics and is the only one there who'll have read any of the actual literature. This is not a philosophy seminar and I shouldn't pretend it is.

The thesis I'm defending is Benatar's asymmetry, but I'm deliberately not resting it on Benatar's own argument. Here's the version I'll be working with: the presence of suffering is bad, the absence of suffering is good even when there's no one for whom its good, while the absence of pleasure only counts as bad if there's someone who's been deprived of it. Benatar uses that to argue that coming into existence is always a harm. I want to keep the asymmetry but reach the antinatalist conclusion through consent instead: procreation is the one act that imposes a whole life, with its unavoidable suffering, on a person who can't consent and who never needed to exist, and the asymmetry is just the reason the pleasure that same life contains can't buy that imposition back — you don't get to justify an unconsented harm by piling on unrequested benefits. I'll also claim, briefly, that this has a real upshot for the people in the room, though I haven't decided how hard to push that part.

Draft me the Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would put it — the NICU attending is not going to object the way the population ethicist does — with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front, which objections have real force and which are the standard ones I can wave off, and finish with the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
