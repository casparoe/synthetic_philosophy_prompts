You are a philosopher of time with a strong background in the metaphysics of grounding, truthmaking, and ontological commitment. Think carefully and slowly before answering: begin by laying out in your own words what each step of the argument below is doing and what a defender of each step would say, and only then commit to a diagnosis. Where a term in the argument is ambiguous, name the ambiguity rather than silently resolving it in the argument's favour.

Background. I'm a graduate student working on the metaphysics of time, and I hold a fairly ordinary package: presentism (only present things exist), truthmaker maximalism (every true proposition has something in the world whose existence makes it true), and the view that truthmaking is existence-entailing, so that only what exists can make anything true. A friend has built the following argument and now insists it shows presentism is self-undermining. I cannot find the exit.

(1) Every true proposition has a truthmaker: something whose existence makes the proposition true.
(2) The proposition that some dinosaurs existed is true.
(3) Truthmaking is existence-entailing: only existing things can make propositions true.
(4) If presentism is true, the only things that exist are present things, so any truthmaker must be something present.
(5) Therefore, if presentism is true, the proposition that some dinosaurs existed has a truthmaker that exists now.
(6) There are only three candidates for that present truthmaker: (a) the present world, or its present state, bearing a higher-order property such as being such that dinosaurs existed; (b) a present abstract object that represents how things were, with its content fixed independently of any real past; (c) no truthmaker at all, the truth of the proposition being brute.
(7) None succeeds. Candidate (a) is circular: the property is individuated by the very fact it is supposed to ground, so its existence already presupposes that dinosaurs existed rather than explaining it. Candidate (b) makes truth turn on what our representations happen to say rather than on how the world is, and its content cannot be fixed without presupposing the past it is meant to ground. Candidate (c) simply abandons (1).
(8) So if presentism is true, the proposition that some dinosaurs existed has no truthmaker.
(9) By (1), a true proposition has a truthmaker; so a proposition with no truthmaker is not true.
(10) Hence presentism entails that the proposition that some dinosaurs existed is not true.
(11) Therefore presentism is false.

Here is my difficulty. The engine of the argument — maximalism, existence-entailment, and the charge that the presentist's candidates are either circular or merely representational — never mentions time. Run the same machinery on any true proposition whose subject matter does not exist and you get the same result. On nominalism, arithmetic has no truthmakers, so arithmetic comes out untrue; on an open future, propositions about what will happen come out untrue; on any view that refuses merely possible objects, true propositions about what could have been come out untrue. So either the argument refutes a whole family of parsimonious positions in one stroke, or it is broken in a way that should already be visible in the dinosaur case. My friend says the moral is just that parsimony is expensive and that the right response is to abandon truthmaker maximalism. My supervisor says the argument is sound as far as it goes, but that presentists should simply accept (a) and that the circularity charge is confused. I would most like to keep (1) and (2); I will give up presentism if I must, but I want to know what I would be buying. I already know that dropping presentism does not force eternalism — there is the growing block and the moving spotlight — so please keep the focus on the truthmaker question rather than on the menu of A-theories. And I know the standard catalogue of presentist replies; what I need is not a list but a verdict.

Please give me the following.

1. The step at which the argument should be resisted. Say whether it is a genuine flaw — an equivocation, a circularity charge that cuts equally against the eternalist's own truthmakers, a step that goes through only under a reading the premises do not support — or whether the argument is valid and the real question is only which premise is least costly to abandon.
2. Why the step you name looks compelling in the first place, so that rejecting it is not free.
3. A priced comparison of the exits: abandoning maximalism (1); abandoning existence-entailment (3); denying the charge against the higher-order-property candidate (7a); denying the charge against the representational candidate (7b); and giving up presentism. In each case say what must be surrendered and what awkward consequences follow. In particular, address whether the cheap-truthmaker manoeuvre can be confined to the temporal case without also rescuing the nominalist and the open-future theorist, and whether there is a principled rather than ad hoc distinction between truthmakers that genuinely ground a truth and ones that merely restate it.
4. What your answer does to the package I began with, and whether the proves-too-much observation is a reductio of the argument or simply an honest bill for a sparse ontology.
