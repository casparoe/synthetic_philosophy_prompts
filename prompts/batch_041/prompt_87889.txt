I am taking a course on the foundations of mathematics, and I have been trying to think through the foundational crisis of the early twentieth century—the period running from Frege's response to Russell's paradox, through Russell's own type-theoretic repair, to Hilbert's program and its collapse in the face of Gödel's incompleteness theorems—through a lens that the standard narrative usually ignores. I want to analyze that episode through the theory of paraconsistent logic and the metaphysical view known as dialetheism. For the purposes of this prompt, take paraconsistent logic to be any logic in which a contradiction does not entail triviality—that is, in which the inference from a contradiction to any arbitrary conclusion is blocked—and take dialetheism to be the view that some contradictions are true. I know the standard story: Russell's paradox showed that Frege's naive comprehension axiom—the principle that every predicate determines a set—was inconsistent; Russell responded with a ramified theory of types; Hilbert tried to secure mathematics by finitary consistency proofs; Gödel showed that no such proof could be had for any sufficiently rich system.

What I want is a careful mapping of the episode onto the paraconsistent and dialetheist options. Who were the actors, what were the actual options available to them, and what did each take to be the evidence? What does a paraconsistent logician say that Frege, Russell, and Hilbert faced? Did they mistake inconsistency for triviality, and if so, was that mistake built into the classical logic they assumed? What would a paraconsistent response to Russell's paradox have looked like—say, a set theory based on a paraconsistent logic that tolerates the contradiction—and why did it not become the mainstream? Where does the dialetheist lens illuminate the episode, for instance in Frege's despair or in Hilbert's program, and where does it distort it?

Then reverse the lens: what does the episode show about the viability of paraconsistent logic and dialetheism? Does the history of the foundational crisis count as evidence for or against the idea that some contradictions can be true? Please be disciplined about the mapping—do not force the theory to speak where it is silent—and conclude with an honest assessment of which parts of the episode the theory explains well and which parts it cannot.
