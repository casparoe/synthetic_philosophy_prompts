I have been working slowly through Berkeley's The Querist, the set of queries he published on Irish economic conditions, and I keep snagging on the group of queries where he turns to money itself. Here is the passage, in the numbering of the edition I have:

"23 Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?
24 Whether the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty?
25 Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?
26 Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?"

What I want is help understanding what Berkeley is actually claiming here, because the queries seem to me to pull in different directions and I cannot tell which of several quite different theses he is putting forward. One reading is conceptual: he is offering an analysis of the concept of money, on which the "true idea" of it is exhausted by its function as a tally, so that the question whether it has intrinsic value is a confusion rather than a claim to be denied. A second reading is metaphysical: the queries express, in the economic register, the same denial of material substance that runs through his better-known philosophy, so that gold and silver have no more reality than the tokens that stand for them, and Query 26 is a straight counterfactual about a world in which the metal has literally vanished while the reckoning continues. A third reading is practical and much weaker: he is only insisting that the metallic content of the coin is irrelevant to a well-ordered circulation, which any number of writers could have held without being immaterialists at all. A fourth possibility, which is the one that actually worries me, is that the "ticket or counter" formula is unstable in his own hands, since a ticket is a ticket for something and a counter counts something, and Query 24 seems to supply exactly that something in the proportion of demand to plenty, so that he may need the very notion of value he appears to be dissolving.

Please tell me, on the strength of the text and of Berkeley's philosophy more generally, which of these he is committed to. I would like you to take a definite position rather than laying the options out and leaving me to choose; if you think the text genuinely underdetermines the answer, commit to the reading best supported and then say precisely where the underdetermination lies. I am also curious whether Query 26 commits him to the strong counterfactual or only to a claim about what would be rational to do, and whether his immaterialism is doing any work in these queries or whether he would have written them the same way had he held an ordinary view of matter. Do not cite secondary literature at me; argue from the quoted queries and from what he says elsewhere in his own voice. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to someone puzzling over a text with you, and no headings, lists, or bold text.
