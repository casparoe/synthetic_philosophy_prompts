I am a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on suffering and the limits of value trade-offs, and I am currently marking the midterm. The unit I am grading covers what the instructor calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, which we introduced through three stipulative claims rather than through any particular author: first, that pleasure and pain are only partially commensurable, in the sense that there is no common unit into which both can be converted without residue; second, that there is a severity threshold, T, at or above which the avoidance of suffering takes lexical priority over the production of pleasure, so that no quantity of pleasure, however large, can justify permitting suffering at or above T; and third, that below T lesser pains and ordinary pleasures trade off in the usual way. The exam question asked students to state the position in their own words and then to address the two most natural objections against it — that it is absurd, because it would license sacrificing enormous goods to avert trivial pains, and that it is unstable, because it collapses back into ordinary utilitarianism once the threshold is crossed.

One student submitted the following answer. The lexical threshold view begins from the thought that pleasure and pain cannot be measured on a single scale, and that the avoidance of suffering therefore has a standing that pleasure cannot match. Because there is no common currency in which a quantity of pleasure could compensate for a quantity of pain, the view refuses to trade the two off against each other; in this respect it belongs to the family of negative utilitarian positions, since the reduction of suffering, rather than the maximization of a balance of pleasure over pain, is what it treats as authoritative. The threshold is what saves the position from the cruder view that would forbid any pain whatsoever. The threshold marks the point at which suffering becomes severe. At or above it, avoidance has absolute priority, and no amount of pleasure — not even the aggregate ecstasy of an entire civilization — could justify permitting the suffering. Below the threshold, by contrast, mild pains and ordinary pleasures can be weighed against one another in the usual way, so the view does not demand that we forgo every small good in order to prevent every small hurt. The objection from trivial pains is therefore easily answered. A critic says: if the avoidance of suffering has lexical priority over pleasure, then you should give up any amount of pleasure to prevent a single mild headache. But the view does not make this comparison at all; it declines to weigh a headache against a pleasure, and so the critic's arithmetic never gets started. It is true that the position has costs. If pleasure and pain cannot be placed on a single scale, then we cannot say whether a life containing much pleasure and little pain is better than one containing little pleasure and much pain, and interpersonal comparisons of welfare become impossible; all we can do is compare suffering with suffering and pleasure with pleasure. The view accepts this, treating the comparison of whole lives as a matter for non-ideal judgement rather than for a value calculus. As for the charge of instability, crossing the threshold does not restore commensurability, so the position does not slide back into ordinary utilitarianism: the two regimes remain distinct in kind.
