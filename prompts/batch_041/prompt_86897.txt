I've been going down a rabbit hole on the frame problem lately and I think its one of those things thats way more important than its reputation suggests, but I keep struggling to explain why to people. Heres my rough understanding: it started as a technical headache in early AI — how do you write down everything that does NOT change when some action happens, without having to list an absurd number of facts for every single thing? — but then people like Dreyfus turned it into a bigger point about relevance: how does an agent know what's even worth considering in the first place? Anyway, my actual question: what are the most important implications of the frame problem — both the narrow logical version and the broader relevance version — for how we should think about today's AI systems, especially the ones that just predict the next token instead of maintaining some explicit model of the world? Like, does a large language model even have a frame problem, or does it sort of dodge it by never representing state in the first place? And what does that imply for whether we actually need explicit world models, for building agents that act in the world, and for the old argument about whether common sense is the genuinely hard part of AI? I'm not after a history lesson or a reading list — I want the implications, and where you think the real stakes are.
