Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about what intelligence is, and I came away with a page of notes and no settled answer. The argument began with something small — I said that a pocket calculator beats either of us at arithmetic but that nobody would call it intelligent, and he replied that this shows only that we are confused about arithmetic, not about intelligence — and it ended with the two of us drafting eight candidate accounts on the back of an envelope and then disagreeing about which of them survives contact with the awkward cases. I would like a referee's version of that last part, in writing.

Here are the eight candidates as we wrote them.

1. The test-theoretic account: intelligence is whatever the general factor is — the shared variance behind performance on a broad battery of cognitive tasks. It is fixed by correlation, not by mechanism.

2. The compression account: to be intelligent is to be a good model of one's world, and the measure is how much of what one encounters can be predicted, or equivalently how short a description one can give of it.

3. The acquisition-rate account: intelligence is not skill but the efficiency with which skill is acquired. Two systems with identical current competence differ in intelligence if one needed a thousand examples where the other needed ten, and if the second can then move to a genuinely unfamiliar task.

4. The search account: intelligence is the capacity to find actions that attain a specified objective across a wide range of environments, so that the ideal is defined over a distribution of environments and goals and measured as expected goal-attainment.

5. The library account: there is no general engine. Intelligence is a large, well-indexed repertoire of special-purpose mechanisms plus a cheap policy for choosing among them; generality is an illusion produced by breadth of repertoire.

6. The embodied account: intelligence belongs not to a solver but to a loop between a body and a world — the skill of finding and exploiting what the environment affords, largely without internal models, so that the boundary of the intelligent system is not the skin.

7. The collective account: intelligence is properly attributed to groups, lineages, markets, and institutions that store and recombine competence across individuals and generations; treating it as an individual property is a category error.

8. The deflationary account: intelligence is a folk category bundling several loosely related capacities. Any single definition is a stipulation serving some purpose — testing, legislation, engineering — and the only interesting questions concern which purpose.

What I want from you:

First, sort the eight into tiers — say four, best to worst — according to how far you would trust each as the anchor of a serious research program. The criterion we settled on, though we applied it very differently, is not which account sounds deepest but which commits us to the largest number of expectations that could turn out false; an account that nothing could disconfirm is not a contender, it is a mood. Give each item one sentence explaining its tier.

Second, for each of the eight give two scores from 0 to 10: one for how much it constrains what we should expect to observe in a system we are examining, and one for how well it handles the following without special pleading — a pocket calculator; a bacterium swimming up a chemical gradient; a termite colony or a market price system; and a system that is fluent and wide-ranging in what it says yet collapses when a familiar task is re-dressed in unfamiliar clothing.

Third, three sharper questions. (a) Which of the eight would let us say that a system became more intelligent while becoming less capable, and which make that combination impossible? (b) My friend's own preferred test was this: hold the kind of task fixed and vary its details, and read off intelligence as the degree to which performance survives the re-dressing. Say how well each account accommodates that test, and whether it is genuinely a ninth candidate or a restatement of one of the eight. (c) Which two of the eight are not real rivals but one commitment described twice, and which one, if any, is not a definition at all but a redescription of the question?

Finish with the account you would keep if you had to keep exactly one, the single element you would graft onto it from the runner-up, and the strongest reason a reasonable person would still reject your choice.
