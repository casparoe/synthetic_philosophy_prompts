Two ways out of the dilemma

The prisoner's dilemma is usually taught as a trap. Whatever the other player does, I do better by defecting: if they cooperate I get the biggest payoff by defecting, and if they defect I still do better defecting than cooperating. So two rational players defect and both end up worse off than if they'd both cooperated. The iterated version is supposed to be the escape hatch. If we play again and again and neither of us knows when it stops, cooperation can become stable — I cooperate today because defecting costs me tomorrow. Axelrod's tournaments are the vivid illustration: a simple nice, retaliatory, forgiving strategy won against a field of much cleverer programs, and then won again.

But the escape hatch is really just an information condition. Make the horizon common knowledge — a hundred rounds, we both know it, we both know the other knows it — and the whole thing unravels backwards. In round one hundred I defect, so round ninety-nine is really the last round, so I defect there too, and so on down to the first move. All the cooperation was scaffolding resting on a final round that never arrives.

Here's the variant that changed how I think about it. Instead of choosing a strategy — cooperate, defect, tit-for-tat — suppose each player has to hand in a small program, and each program is allowed to read the other program's source code before deciding what to do. Now the move cooperate exactly if the other program would cooperate against this one becomes something I can actually commit to, because the commitment sits in the code rather than in my willpower. Two players who submit that program both cooperate, and neither can gain by switching: a program that defects gets read, and gets defected on in return, so the most a defector can get is the mutual-defection payoff, which is worse than mutual cooperation. The dilemma evaporates — not because anyone became nicer, but because we let the players write the rules of their own interaction instead of only picking moves inside it.

Which makes me think the interesting question was never how do we get out of the dilemma, but who fixed the strategy space, and why we assumed it was fixed.
