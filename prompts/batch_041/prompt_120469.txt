I have been defending the view that moral deference is defective — that while testimony can reliably transmit moral beliefs, it cannot transmit moral knowledge or understanding, and that someone who forms her moral view by trusting another's judgment is deficient in a way that having good advisers does not repair. What would count against it? Set out the defeaters in order of how much they should move me, separating those that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward: cases in which deference looks not merely permissible but admirable; arguments that the alleged asymmetry with ordinary empirical testimony is an artifact of a bad theory of testimony rather than a feature of the moral domain; and whatever the view's own defenders ought to concede.

Two things I keep returning to. First, much of what we call forming one's own moral view is inherited testimony at one remove, and I do not see how the view distinguishes the inheritance it condemns from the one it quietly relies on. Second, imagine a device that reliably returns the correct verdict on any moral question, which one consults before acting. If the view implies that relying on the device is defective in the same way that deferring to a person is, is that a cost it can bear or a reductio? And if the asymmetry is real, is it an asymmetry in knowledge at all, or one in standing — the standing to blame, to resent, to have one's judgment count?

Finally, tell me honestly whether the position, once it has absorbed the best replies, still risks anything, or whether it has been qualified into something no possible evidence could touch — and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
