I sit on the ethics and governance committee of a mid-sized research charity that funds work on widening moral concern: animal welfare, the treatment of out-groups, risks to future generations, and similar. Some years ago we adopted a procedure we call bias interruption. During funding deliberations a trained facilitator may pause the discussion and designate a panellist's moral objection as an implicit bias rather than a considered preference. A designated objection is still minuted, but it carries reduced weight when we rank proposals. We introduced the procedure because we did not want our funding decisions steered by the unexamined aversions of a small and rather homogeneous group of panellists, and we have kept it because it is popular with our trustees. In practice it is invoked almost exclusively against objections to widening the circle of concern, and hardly ever against objections to narrowing it.

Two of us have since concluded that the distinction the procedure turns on, between a moral preference that deserves respect and a moral bias that ought to be corrected, is much harder to draw than the procedure assumes, and that it is hard for conceptual reasons rather than merely because our judgements about particular cases are noisy. One proposal now before us is an endorsement test: a panellist's objection counts as a preference if they would still endorse it on learning everything relevant both about the case and about how they came to feel as they do, and as a bias otherwise.

Please describe the most important implications of the difficulty I have described for how we should design and run this procedure, and say what we should do about the endorsement test in particular. Give me a committed answer: definite recommendations with your reasons, not a survey of the options that leaves the choice to us.
