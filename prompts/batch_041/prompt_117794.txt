Bit of an odd one, this. I'm a backend dev by trade — no philosophy training whatsosever, never took a single module in it — but for the last eight years I've been treasurer and general dogsbody of a small amateur theatre company. Converted church hall, two shows a year, and in three weeks its our twentieth anniversary do, and the committee have asked me to say a few words before the second half. Six or seven minutes, tops.

The room is the problem. Mostly it's our lot: actors, the two blokes who build the sets, a load of people who've never set foot on a stage and just come for the tea and the company. But our local councillor will be there, and he's the one who voted to cut our grant last year and got himself quoted in the local paper saying the arts are a nice-to-have in a cost-of-living crisis. And my old English teacher will be in the front row and she will know if I'm bluffing.

Here's what's actually keeping me up. I've been reading Rousseau's letter against the theatre — the one he wrote back when d'Alembert put it about in the Encyclopédie that Geneva ought to have a playhouse. I picked it up expecting to laugh at it and instead I keep finding bits that land. As I understand him: a playhouse can make virtue look lovely but it can't make anybody better, it's a mirror and not a medicine, and it flatters whatever tastes the audience already has rather than correcting them. He also can't stand that it turns us all into watchers — the things he actually approves of are the open-air dances and public festivals where everyone is in it and nobody is sitting in the dark judging. And he works through Molière's Misanthrope and argues that the play teaches you to laugh at the one sincere man in the room and side with the smooth one who flatters everybody. He reckons the actor's whole trade is pretending to feel things he doesn't feel, and that this is bad for the actor and bad for us.

I don't want to stand up there and wave all that away, and I definately don't want to tell the room we're saving civilisation. What I'd like is a speech that takes him seriously and then argues back. My hunch is that he drew the line in the wrong place rather than being wrong to draw one — because the way we actually do it isn't much like what he was attacking. Nobody auditions. We rehearse in the back room of the pub so anyone can wander in and watch. The Thursday is pay-what-you-can. Half the cast have never done it before. And everyone ends up in the bar together afterwards, punters and actors, no curtain, no separation. That feels closer to his dancing-in-the-square than to his playhouse. But check that hunch properly against what he actually says, and if it's weak, say so and tell me what survives — I'd rather have a smaller honest argument than a big dodgy one.

So: write me the speech. British English please, and it has to work out loud, for people who aren't academics and won't have read any of this. And please don't put words in Rousseau's mouth — paraphrase what he says rather than handing me quotes I can't check, and don't invent dates or titles I'd get caught out on.
