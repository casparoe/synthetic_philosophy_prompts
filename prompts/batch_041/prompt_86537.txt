I write a column on philosophy and the human sciences, and I would like you to draft the argumentative essay at its center. The subject is truth: not whether any particular claim is true, but what we are doing when we call a claim true, and whether what we are doing can bear the weight we place on it.

The background is the deflationary family of views. On these views, truth is not a substantive property that propositions possess in virtue of corresponding to reality or cohering within a system; the predicate "is true" is chiefly a device of endorsement and generalization, one that lets us say things like "everything she told me was true" without having to repeat what she said. Truth, so understood, does no explanatory work. Whatever needs explaining is explained by the contents of our beliefs, by their causes and their consequences, not by their truth.

Now suppose someone accepts all of that, and then proposes a division of labor. She asks us to separate two things that deflationists ordinarily run together:

- the property: the one picked out by "is true," thin, expressive, and explanatorily idle; and
- the norm: the standard governing belief and assertion — that one ought to believe and assert only what is true — which she insists is substantive, irreducible either to evidential warrant or to utility, and genuinely explanatory. It is what makes inquiry aim at anything, what distinguishes a lie from a merely false statement, and what keeps assertion from being a game of noises.

The question I want the essay to answer is whether that split is stable. Can one be a thoroughgoing deflationist about the property and still maintain that the norm is substantive and explanatory? Or does the norm quietly reinstate everything the deflationist surrendered?

Weigh the strongest case for the split and the strongest case against it. On the skeptical side, work through at least these lines of attack: first, that if "is true" merely endorses, then "believe only what is true" collapses into an empty injunction to believe what you already believe; second, that the norm is better explained by something else — knowledge, warrant, or the social costs of being caught out — in which case truth is not doing the work after all; third, that the norm may be a real convention of a practice without being a truth-involving one. On the other side, consider whether a deflated property can still mark a genuine target, and whether anything short of truth can explain why inquiry terminates in assertion instead of running on indefinitely.

Reach a verdict. State plainly whether the split holds; if it does not, say which half gives way and what should replace it. If you think it holds only under a restriction — say, for assertion but not for belief, or for some domains of discourse and not others — state the restriction and defend it. Take no specialized background for granted beyond what I have given you here, and do not cite or survey the literature; argue from the ideas themselves.
