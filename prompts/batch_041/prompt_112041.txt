I teach a third-year seminar that philosophy and psychology students take together, and in a fortnight I have to run a ninety-minute session on what the cross-cultural evidence about folk morality is supposed to show about whether morality is objective. I taught it badly last year and I want to get it right. I opened with the trolley problem, on the theory that it was the one case everyone already had an opinion about; the discussion immediately degenerated into a guessing game about how people in other societies would answer, and then split into two camps that could not give reasons — half the room took the variation in moral judgement to refute moral realism outright, the other half took moral psychology to be simply irrelevant to metaethics. A colleague in the psychology department tells me I am overcomplicating things and should just present the findings and let the students draw their own conclusions, which is more or less what I did last year. What I want from you is a teaching plan, not an explanation of the topic: the order in which the ideas have to be introduced and what depends on what. I am fairly confident that both the moral/conventional distinction and the distinction between the content of a moral code and the status of moral claims have to be on the table early, but I cannot see which must come first, and I want you to decide and defend the order rather than lay out the alternatives. I would also rather they reason from a case they can all evaluate than take my word for what a particular society says, so build the session so that it does not depend on them accepting any ethnographic report.

The thing I most want your judgement on is the carrying case. I have drafted my own vignette, because the published materials feel too loaded: a woman inherits her late mother's wedding ring, has it melted down and recast as a set of everyday spoons, and uses them at the family table, with everyone's knowledge and nobody's objection. Should that carry the session? Tell me what such an example has to contain and, just as importantly, what it must lack — my hunch is that anything bringing in harm, unfairness, deception, or even a bereaved person's distress will let the students file the disagreement under facts or consequences and learn nothing — and tell me plainly why the trolley problem is the wrong case to build on, since I have a suspicion about this but not an argument, and whether it has any legitimate use later, once the framework is in place. Then the misconceptions. I am expecting 'so morality is just relative' and 'so the empirical data are beside the point', but I suspect the ones that will do real damage are subtler — that the moral/conventional distinction is the legal/conventional distinction under another name, or that variation in what people judge is the same thing as variation in what is judged, or that the sampling complaint everyone has heard is a complaint about Western-ness rather than about education, wealth, industrialisation and democracy — and I want each one located at the exact point in the sequence where it has to be headed off, not merely named.

Finish with the single question I should put to them in the last five minutes to find out whether they have grasped the inferential structure rather than collected some facts, and with what I should tell them we are not going to settle today and why. Commit to your recommendations and give your reasons; where you think a familiar choice is a mistake, say so and say what to do instead. I would rather have a plan I might disagree with than a balanced survey of options.
