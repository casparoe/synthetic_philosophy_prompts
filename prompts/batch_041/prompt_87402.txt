I keep coming back to the surprise exam and I want an actual argument, not the usual "the students can't know it" hand-wave. Set it up with two stipulations I think get skipped: the students are certain the announcement is true, and the teacher is perfectly reliable (never cancels, never moves the exam). Now argue for this claim: the teacher's announcement is the announcement-shaped cousin of a Moore sentence, the p-but-I-dont-believe-p pattern -- it can be true, but no consistent, deductively-closed student can be certain of it, so what the announcement rules out isnt the exam, its the students' certainty. Give me the derivation, say exactly where the certainty assumption is doing the work, then face three objections: that Moore sentences are odd to *assert* while this one is odd to *believe*, so maybe the parallel is just a pun; that the students can just believe it with credence 0.99 and nothing goes wrong, so what precisely is impossible; and that a reliable informant should be able to tell you things you can know, so what went wrong with the teacher. Finish by saying whether "there will be a surprise" is something a teacher can promise at all, and whether she can know in advance that shes pulled it off.
