I'm a second-year PhD student in an ML theory group, and our reading group has spent the last few weeks working through the family of methods usually discussed under the heading of iterated amplification and distillation. The basic shape, as I understand it, is this: you begin from a base system you already trust — a human, or a model trained to imitate one — and you let that system attack a hard task by breaking it into easier pieces and farming the pieces out to copies of itself, then aggregating the piecewise answers back into an answer to the whole; that is the amplification step, and the point of it is supposed to be that a trusted decomposer plus trusted sub-answers yields a trusted whole. Then, because the amplified process is far too expensive to run at inference time, you train a single model to imitate the amplified process's input-output behavior — the distillation step — and you iterate, using the distilled model as the new base. The sales pitch is that each round leaves you with something both more capable and no less aligned than what you started with.

I want to see the best case for a sceptical reading, so please argue for the following position: amplification and distillation pull alignment and competence in opposite directions, and therefore the composite procedure cannot be counted on to improve on the base system along both axes at once. Make the argument as concrete as you can — say what it is about the decomposition step that buys safety at the cost of capability, and what it is about the distillation step that buys capability at the cost of safety, and why the two effects need not cancel. I don't want you to stop at the obvious objection that distillation suffers from distributional shift; I want the structural story about what distillation's training objective rewards and what alignment actually consists in, and I want you to say whether the failure is a matter of degree (a better or larger student would fix it) or of kind (no student could). Finish by giving the strongest reply available to someone who thinks the two goals are separable after all, and tell me which assumption in your own argument you would most want to check before trusting it.
