I am a novelist at work on a chapter in which a lapsed Victorian barrister — a man trained all his life to think in terms of liability, desert, and what a court may and may not do to an innocent party — sits up one night with a borrowed translation of Anselm's Cur Deus Homo and finds that he cannot put the argument down even though he no longer believes its conclusion. Because I want his paraphrase to be scrupulous, and because I do not want to lift sentences from the old English rendering, I have tried to compress the central movement of Book First — roughly chapters eleven through twenty-five, the stretch running from Anselm's account of what it is to sin, through the objection that God might simply put away sin out of compassion, to the claim that satisfaction must be proportionate to guilt — into my own words, and I would like your help in understanding what that compressed argument actually does. As I have it: Boso presses Anselm on why God could not simply remit sin; Anselm answers that to let a violation of the divine honor pass with neither satisfaction nor punishment would leave the moral order disordered, and that God cannot tolerate disorder; that what the sinner owes is not merely the thing taken but the honor of which he has robbed God; that the magnitude of the debt is fixed by the dignity of the one offended rather than by the smallness of the thing taken, so that even a finite creature's theft carries a weight no finite creature can discharge; and that since the debt must be paid, and only a being of infinite dignity can pay it, while only one who belongs to the offending race can owe it, the payment must come from a God-man — a conclusion Anselm develops at length in Book Second rather than in Book First. I can follow each step in isolation, but I cannot see the hinge between the third and the fourth: what exactly forces the conclusion that no finite creature could ever discharge the debt, and is the infinitude of the debt something Anselm argues for, or an assumption his interlocutor is meant already to have granted before the argument begins?

I have also been trying to work out how this bears on a distinction I know only secondhand. I am told that David Lewis, in a paper I believe is called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", argues that the idea of one person's being punished in place of another is something we do not in fact believe — that no one would accept a human court's hanging an innocent man in the place of a murderer, however much good it did — and that the credible element in traditional atonement doctrine is the payment of a debt rather than the transfer of a punishment. What I need is not a reading list but an explanation of what Lewis's objection is actually aimed at. Does it target the claim that Christ bore the punishment deserved by sinners, or the broader claim that satisfaction could be rendered by someone other than the debtor? And if Anselm's argument, as I have paraphrased it, is a debt-and-honor argument rather than a punishment-transfer argument, does it escape the objection entirely — or does it smuggle in the very idea Lewis rejects, given that the God-man is not himself the debtor and yet undergoes death? If Anselm does escape it, I want to know which later version of the doctrine Lewis is really attacking, and what the difference between vicarious punishment and vicarious payment comes to once one asks who is entitled to demand the one or to accept the other.

The chapter's crux is that my barrister must be able to state the argument fairly in his own voice — as a piece of reasoning that a lawyer would recognize as rigorous — and then say precisely where it loses him, and I would rather his objection be a real one than a mood. So please explain the argument's load-bearing steps in plain prose, tell me which premise a careful critic should refuse if he wants to stop the conclusion without simply denying that God is owed anything, and then say as exactly as you can what Lewis's distinction between punishment and debt does and does not accomplish against a reading of Anselm like the one above.
