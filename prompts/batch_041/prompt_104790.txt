A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether the universe requires a cause, and we ended without agreement — which is why I am writing. He defends a version of the cosmological argument on which the universe must have a cause because it began to exist, and he insists that the classical objections from early modern philosophy do not touch his version, since those objections were aimed at a different argument about necessary existence and sufficient reason. I have been trying to test his claim against Part IX of Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, and I keep getting tangled in the exchange between Demea and Cleanthes. Demea gives the argument as follows:

"Whatever exists, must have a cause or reason of its existence; it being absolutely impossible for any thing to produce itself, or be the cause of its own existence. In mounting up, therefore, from effects to causes, we must either go on in tracing an infinite succession, without any ultimate cause at all; or must at last have recourse to some ultimate cause, that is necessarily existent."

Cleanthes responds:

"Nothing is demonstrable, unless the contrary implies a contradiction. Nothing, that is distinctly conceivable, implies a contradiction. Whatever we conceive as existent, we can also conceive as non-existent. There is no being, therefore, whose non-existence implies a contradiction. Consequently there is no being, whose existence is demonstrable."

What I need help with is not a summary of the two positions but the precise shape of the disagreement. First, is Demea's argument here best read as an ancestor of the temporal cosmological argument (the universe began, so it has a cause) or of the modal or sufficient-reason argument (every contingent thing needs a reason, and the regress of reasons cannot be infinite)? His premise concerns existence and reason rather than beginning, and his objection to the infinite succession is that the whole series, taken together, is not determined by anything — which sounds like the second. If that is right, my friend may be correct that Hume is not addressing his version. Second, does Cleanthes' conceivability argument actually refute Demea, or does it only refute a stronger claim — that God's existence is demonstrable in the strict sense in which the contrary implies a contradiction — while leaving untouched the claim that the regress of contingent beings requires a necessary being whose existence is self-explanatory rather than logically necessary in that narrow sense? Third, Cleanthes goes on to ask why the material universe might not itself be the necessarily existent being, and then turns the theist's own contingency reasoning against the Deity. Is this a genuine objection or a tu quoque that trades on treating "we can conceive it not to exist" as decisive in one case and not in the other?

I would like an explanation that stays close to the text — including the way "reason," "cause," and "necessary" shift meaning across the passage — rather than a general survey of the cosmological argument. My friend is confident he has Hume's measure, and I am not yet sure whether he does.
