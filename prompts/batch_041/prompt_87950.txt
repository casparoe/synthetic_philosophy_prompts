So this happened at our department's Thursday coffee thing and I still can't figure out who won. Two colleagues — Dana, who does decision theory, and Marcus, who's more of a metaethics guy but reads alot of economics on the side — got into it about money pumps. It went five rounds and neither of them gave an inch.

Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Dana said intransitive preferences are just flat-out irrational, and the reason is exploitable-ness: if you rank A over B, B over C, and C over A, and you'll pay even a nickel to swap into something you prefer, then I can walk you around the loop and collect a nickel every lap. You end up holding what you started with, minus your money. Being pumpable like that is what irrationality *is*, she said — you don't need any other story.

(2) Marcus said the pump only works on a naive chooser. A chooser who sees the loop coming refuses the first trade, or ties itself to the mast, and then never loses a dime. So the argument really shows that naivety is bad, not that intransitivity is. And it needs the agent to actually accept every swap — someone with a preference cycle who simply never trades isn't losing anything, and you can't call them irrational just for having the ranking.

(3) Dana said that's a dodge. The commitment fix either costs you something, or it means you have to treat your own future self as a threat you're defending against, which is already a kind of incoherence. And anyway you don't need the time dimension at all: you can serve the whole thing up as one menu — a set of swaps or bets, each of which looks at-least-as-good to the agent, whose combination loses for sure. So don't tell me the problem is about diachronic choice.

(4) Marcus said the one-shot menu is either not a money pump at all — you lose no money, you just can't be represented as maximizing anything — or it smuggles in that betterness is measurable enough to add up, which is exactly what's in dispute. If I reject the machinery that lets you sum my preferences, you can't use the machinery to convict me. And if you hand me all the swaps at once, I just decline the bundle; declining isn't irrational.

(5) Dana came back that declining is only available to you if the parts aren't each preferable, and by construction each part is preferable, so there's no single choice at which you do anything you'd call a mistake. The pump only needs local preferability at each node. If your theory of rationality can't say what's wrong with that, your theory is too weak.

They went around like that for twenty minutes and I probaly looked like a deer in headlights. Please referee this move by move — tell me which of the five is right, which is confused, and rate each one if you can. I especially want to know: does the money-pump argument against intransitivity actually require the agent to be an expected-utility maximizer, or to be diachronic at all, or to be naive? And is there a real sense in which Dana is right that no *single* choice is a mistake even though the whole pattern still is? Also tell me what both of them are leaving out — I have a nagging feeling neither of them has said the thing that actually settles it, but I can't put my finger on what it is.
