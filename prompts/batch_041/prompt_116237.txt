I'm in a grad seminar on vagueness (last unit is the sorites, the tolerance principle, and the whole business about vagueness coming in orders) and for the final session each of us has to bring in a short story instead of a paper, which I initially thought was a cop-out and now think is actually the hardest assignment of the term. So here's the setup I want you to write from, feel free to rename anyone: a small harbor town where the fishmongers' guild levy is assessed on any pile of herring that counts as a heap. A heap pays; a scatter of fish doesn't. Nobody has ever defined heap. The inspector, who's been doing this by eye for about thirty years, walks the platform at dawn with a lantern and calls it, and she's never once been formally overruled, which everyone treats as proof that she's good at her job rather than as evidence that there's nothing to be right or wrong about. That's the woman I want the story to follow, through one bad season.

What I want to see happen: the merchants slowly figure out that they can pile fish one short of whatever she called a heap last market day and pay nothing, so the levy revenue quietly collapses and the guild starts to panic. The guild hires a clerk who's read some philosophy and he starts proposing rules at meetings — add one fish at a time and nothing changes, so either no pile is ever a heap or a single herring makes one — and the inspector tries to just write a number in the ledger and finds she physically can't do it without feeling like a fraud. Then the part I actually care about: she notices that her confidence has no edge either. She can say when she's sure it's a heap and when she's sure it isn't, but she can't say when she stopped being sure, and if you press her on that she gets stuck in the same way, one fish further up. I want that second-order thing dramatized through the town's ordinary life — a wedding, a feud between two families over a shared dock, the price of ice — rather than through characters standing around explaining paradoxes to each other, and I want at least one image that makes the first-order problem physical, something like a pile that is a heap at dawn and somehow isn't by noon.

Please commit to a reading rather than hedging: decide whether your story treats the heap as something real that the inspector simply can't know, as nothing over and above the town's habits of calling things heaps, or as something that genuinely shifts with what's at stake when she's standing there looking at it — and write the ending so it lands on that one, not on all three at once. If you want to give the clerk the shifting-boundary idea, fine, but then he has to lose or win on the page, not just propose it. And at the very end, seperate from the story, add a short note for me on where you took liberties with the ideas or leaned on a contested reading — e.g. if you make her uncertainty itself vague, that's a real commitment and I need it flagged so I can defend it in seminar.
