A friend of mine has a tic: whenever anyone says anything about a people — the French are formal, Brazilians are warm, whatever — she says "ah, but the exception proves the rule", like that patches the hole. We were arguing about it after a seminar on nationalism and I realised I don't actually know what that sentence means, and I'm not sure she does either.

So lay out the distinct things "the exception proves the rule" could be claiming, from the trivial to the substantive. I gather it began as a legal maxim to the effect that an exception shows there's a rule covering the unexcepted cases; there's also the reading where "prove" just means "test", the exception being what puts the rule to the test; and there's the one everyone actually means, which is "the counterexample doesn't count". For each: is it coherent, is it doing any work, and does it help with a generalization about a whole people?

Then the payoff. My friend the anthropologist insists there are three different things someone might mean by "the Germans are X": a statistical claim about a distribution, a claim that being German constitutively involves X, and a claim about whats expected or normal there — the ethos — which no individual has to live up to, the way "dogs have four legs" stays true when you meet a three-legged dog. Which of those three makes a counterexample a genuine counterexample? Which, if any, would make it alright to actually act on the generalization — expect things of someone, design a policy around it? And does "the exception proves the rule" ever earn its keep, or is it just a way of making a generalization unfalsifiable? Give me the menu with verdicts, not a position.
