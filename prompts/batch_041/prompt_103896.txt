I keep rabbits — a small stud for pet homes, and I take in rescued does, some of them already pregnant — and because I keep them, I have become the person friends and neighbours ring two or three times a month to ask whether they should let their doe have a litter. I want to stop deciding these things on mood. I have ended up convinced of a view I have only ever seen called the procreation asymmetry: bringing into existence a being whose life will go badly is a reason against doing it, whereas bringing into existence a being whose life will go well is not a reason for doing it, or at best a reason that stays silent. I notice that this makes it a one-sided rule: it can tell me not to, and it can never tell me to. I have come to think of these decisions as a one-way gate — whatever is behind the gate cannot be renegotiated afterwards, so the only question that matters at the gate is whether there is a bad life behind it, and the good lives behind it cannot be spent to buy off that risk.

Please turn this into a procedure I can actually run. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the decision back to me because what remains is a judgement about values rather than a fact about the animals. I have no philosophy training, and the decisions arrive with a deadline — a doe is ready now, the neighbour wants an answer this week — so it needs to be something I can run in an evening with the veterinary advice I happen to have and nothing more. The call is mine, but I would like to be able to hand the procedure to a friend who keeps guinea pigs, so it must not lean on anything I know and they do not.

Run it on three proposals. First, a healthy pair with four homes already lined up and only the ordinary background chance of a kit with something wrong. Second, a rescued doe who is already pregnant, with no homes waiting, where the alternative is a spay that ends the pregnancy. Third, a pair where the buck carries something that shows itself at about three years old and means a slow and painful decline; the kits would have every chance of a good first three years and nothing after that. I expect to dislike the answer to the third, because I want to say that a small chance of a bad life is worth taking for the sake of the good lives on offer, and I suspect the asymmetry refuses that trade. If it does, say so plainly and tell me exactly what I am giving up by accepting it.

I also want you to settle something I keep going round on: whether the asymmetry is a claim about how things go for the animal or a claim about what I am permitted to do, and what changes in the procedure if I take it one way rather than the other. Say which one you take it to be, and set the threshold for how much risk of a bad life is too much, with reasons — if that threshold cannot come from the asymmetry itself, say so and set it anyway, rather than laying the options in front of me and leaving me to choose. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do: whether it can tell me anything about the good lives at all, and what it says when the same decision also affects the animals who already exist, since taking in a litter uses up the space and money I could spend on the ones already here.

Please write the whole answer in British English.
