I'm a novelist — my new book is set in the 1340s, mostly in and around a college in Oxford, and the main character is a young fellow who spends his nights trying to work out rules for how bodies speed up and slow down. Next month I'm doing an on-stage thing at a festival with a historian I admire, and I'm the one asking the questions. Her book (I've read it twice and still can't quite sum it up) argues that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution are all later inventions — bookkeeping, she calls it — and that if you insist on a real rupture in European thinking about nature, it happens in the 1300s, in Oxford and Paris, not in the 1500s.

Roughly what she says: the humanists needed a darkness to have climbed out of, and the nineteenth-century nation-builders needed a birth scene; the "Scientific Revolution" got its grip on us from a mid-century historian called Herbert Butterfield, whose book on the origins of modern science told its readers that the whole business "outshines everything since the rise of Christianity" and counted for more than the Renaissance or the Reformation. She also insists that "science" is a nineteenth-century coinage and that what people did before that was natural philosophy, which is a different animal — it had God and the soul and purposes in it — so calling a medieval scholar a scientist is like calling a Roman a capitalist. And her positive case: a crowd at Merton College, plus a Paris man or two, proved in the 1300s a rule Galileo later gets the credit for (a body speeding up steadily covers the same ground as one moving at its average speed the whole time), tried to write down how speed depends on force and resistance, and explained a thrown stone not by the air rushing round behind it but by something the thrower leaves in the stone. That, she says, is the real thing.

Her critics, who I want to be able to represent fairly: that she's playing the precursor game backwards, reading the 1300s as if it were straining towards us; that the stuff she loves was university exercise, Aristotle-commentary, theology-adjacent, no instruments, no experiments, no printing, no societies, so "the ideas were there first" is the least interesting kind of continuity; and that nobody serious thinks the Renaissance is a natural kind anyway, so if period labels are just tools, what has her book actually changed?

Give me about ten questions. Specifically:

- at least one question on each of the three prongs — the labels, the "science is anachronistic" charge, and the fourteenth-century priority claim — each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Not a limp "can you say more" — an actual next move, the one that lands if she answers the way I expect.
- one question that turns all of this back on me and my book: if "science" is a nineteenth-century category, is my protagonist — a man who calls himself a natural philosopher and privately believes he's doing something nobody has done — just an anachronism I should cut, or is there a way to write him that doesn't smuggle modern science in through the back door and doesn't flatten him into a theologian either? With a follow-up for when she says he's fine.
- one that presses the "what changes" objection hard, and a follow-up for the moment she retreats to "periodisation is a tool" — I want to know what work the tool does, and what would show that a period is a bad tool.
- one on the evidential base: how much rides on a handful of manuscripts from one college, and what she would take as evidence against her own story.
- and something asking whether her own claim — a rupture, in the 1300s — isn't just the same founding-myth move with the date changed.

Two things I don't want. Nothing that invites a reading list or the names of books, because the audience won't write anything down and I'd rather she argues than recommends. And none of the "what drew you to the period" stuff; the festival has plenty of that already. Just the questions, numbered, with follow-ups underneath where I asked for them. I definately want her to have to think rather than reach for a paragraph she's given before.
