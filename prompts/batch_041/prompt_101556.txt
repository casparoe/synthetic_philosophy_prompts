I'm a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy training (so go easy on me), and I've gotten mildly obsessed with this doing-vs-allowing thing ever since a design review went sideways. Context: we run a fleet-management system for autonomous delivery robots on a hospital campus, and the team keeps arguing about which "policy modes" are safe, because management assumes any mode where the robot just waits is passive and therefore legally and morally fine, while any mode where it acts is risky and needs review. I keep telling them that active/passive and doing/allowing are definately not the same axis, but I can never explain why in a way that sticks, so I want to actually work it out on our own cases instead of trotting out the trolley thing everyone already knows.

So here's what I'd like: take the six operational modes below and (a) rate each from 0 to 1 on how much the system itself is *doing* the bad outcome rather than merely *allowing* it, where 0 = a pure bystander who simply doesn't intervene and 1 = the robot's own deliberate act directly brings the bad thing about; (b) rank all six from most-doing to most-allowing; and (c) tell me which one you think I, as the engineer who shipped the code, should feel most personally on the hook for, and why that might not be the same one you ranked highest. Also flag any mode where the 0–1 scale itself breaks down or where doing/allowing feels like the wrong frame entirely, because I suspect a couple of these are really about something else (foresight? consent? who quietly changed the default?).

The modes: (1) robot is mid-route, a person steps into its path, it's built to keep going unless it detects an obstacle, it fails to detect a thin dog leash stretched across the walkway, and someone trips. (2) the robot's default config is to not carry a defibrillator, a nurse pages it to bring one, our paging service rate-limits the request and it times out, and the patient dies. (3) we push a firmware update that removes an old "stop and ask a human" fallback to cut latency, and an accident that the fallback would've prevented now happens. (4) the robot physically grabs a patient's arm to stop them yanking out an IV line; the patient is bruised but otherwise fine. (5) an operator remotely tells the robot to hold still and do nothing, and while it's frozen it watches someone fall down a stairwell it could've blocked with its own body. (6) the robot was never built with gas-leak detection because we cut that feature for budget, a leak happens, and nobody notices in time.
