I'm 38 and halfway through IVF, and something I read has flipped me around: I now think that once you're already screening embryos, you're morally required to pick the one with the best expected life, not just the one without the disease — Savulescu's procreative beneficence — and I've pushed it further than he does. Two twists: the duty is comparative only among the embryos you've actually got (no duty to create the best possible child), and it isn't a duty to maximize wellbeing, it's a duty of non-arbitrariness — you have to choose on grounds you'd be willing to have the child find out about later, since the duty is owed to the child. I'm not making this up in a seminar; my clinic has started offering polygenic scores for stuff like height and how long you'll stay in school, and I've been sitting in that waiting room alot.

In two weeks I defend this at my clinic's ethics committee, and it's not a philosophy crowd: a Catholic moral theologian, two reproductive endocrinologists who think entirely in terms of clinical risk and later regret, a disability rights lawyer who's a social-model person and hears "best life" as an insult, and one Kantian who'll go straight for treating the child as a means. Write me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from each of them, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do to my position, each with a two-minute reply and one line on where the reply is soft; what I should concede up front to keep the room; and the one question I'm praying nobody asks. Start with the single objection most likely to sink the whole thing, then the rest in order, no preamble.
