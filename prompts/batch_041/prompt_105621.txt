You are an expert in welfare economics and normative ethics, specializing in the measurement of welfare and the possibility of interpersonal comparisons. I am assembling a position for a graduate seminar and want it audited for consistency before I present it. Please begin by brainstorming the principal fault lines in this literature, then work through the commitments below slowly, testing each against the others rather than answering from first impressions.

My position, stated as compactly as I can:

(1) Welfarism. The only thing that ultimately matters in the evaluation of outcomes is the welfare of the individuals involved. Equality, liberty, and resources matter, if at all, only instrumentally or as constituents of welfare.

(2) A representational account of utility. A person's welfare consists in the satisfaction of their preferences, and a utility function is merely a compact representation of those preferences over prospects. It is fixed only up to an arbitrary positive monotone transformation, and utility names no mental quantity.

(3) Robust interpersonal comparability. There is nonetheless a fact of the matter about whether one person is better or worse off than another, and we can compare not only welfare levels across persons but also the differences between them, on a single common scale.

(4) Prioritarianism. In distributing a fixed total, priority should go to the worse off: the moral value of a marginal gain diminishes as the recipient's welfare rises.

(5) A veil-of-ignorance justification. Distributive principles are justified by asking what self-interested parties would choose behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing their own position, and reasoning by expected value.

(6) Anti-egalitarianism. The levelling-down objection is decisive against egalitarianism: making the better off worse off cannot be an improvement if no one thereby gains.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Please:

- Identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad.

- For each genuine tension, say precisely which commitment would have to give and what giving it up would cost, that is, which of my remaining commitments it would destabilize.

- Separate these from any pair that merely appears to be in tension and dissolves once the right distinction is drawn; state the distinction and why it defuses the conflict.

- Be concrete about the technical machinery where it matters: what kind of scale a given principle actually requires, and where a justificatory argument quietly presupposes the conclusion it is meant to establish.

- Conclude with the single revision that would resolve the most while conceding the least, and say exactly what I would be giving up by adopting it.

A good audit discriminates; do not simply report that everything is interconnected.
