Two camps in the dispute over whether neural networks have representations have been arguing past each other for years. I want the joint statement they could both sign at the close of a good-faith adversarial collaboration: the claims they share, the disagreements that remain, the cruxes, and the tests both sides would accept as bearing on those cruxes, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Constrain the document as follows.

1. Reconstruct the two camps in your own words and name them the realists and the deflationists. Do not cite papers, authors, methods by name, or supply a reading list — I want the positions stated, not reported. Roughly: the realists hold that a trained network's activation space contains a determinate set of features over which its computation factorizes, and that interpretability methods are discovering that structure, with disagreements among methods being a matter of resolution. The deflationists hold that talk of what the network represents is a description of the analyst's model of the network rather than of the network's own content, and that the apparent objects of interpretability are induced by the analyst's choices — the probe class, the labeled dataset, the sparsity penalty, the dictionary width.

2. Distinguish three grades of commitment, and say for each camp which grades it is committed to and which it can concede without cost: (i) internal states carry information about behaviorally relevant distinctions and support predictions; (ii) there is a canonical decomposition of activation space into features, unique up to an equivalence class (permutation, invertible re-parameterization) that leaves behavior invariant, over which the computation factorizes; (iii) internal states have content about the world, not merely causal roles in producing outputs. If a grade is common ground, or if the quarrel at some grade is merely verbal, say so explicitly.

3. Engage with at least the following, deciding for each whether it is a genuine disagreement or a difference in vocabulary: the hypothesis that networks encode more features than they have dimensions, so that features are non-orthogonal and interfere; the fact that many overcomplete dictionaries reconstruct the same activations about equally well, so reconstruction alone cannot single one out; the difference between a probe's held-out accuracy and a direction's load-bearingness under intervention; whether intervening on several alleged features at once composes predictably or whether interaction terms dominate; whether robustness across random seeds and dictionary widths is a criterion of reality or merely of convenience; and the analogy on which the decomposition is fixed only up to a gauge-like freedom, with only gauge-invariant quantities observable.

4. The document must contain: (a) a numbered list of agreed claims, each signable by a committed member of either camp without reservation and none of them vacuous; (b) a table of residual disagreements, each camp's position written in wording that camp would accept, with a column marking which disagreements are terminological; (c) for each crux, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp, the test or argument both camps accept as bearing on it — including what is to count as the same feature and what is to count as behavioral equivalence — and each camp's prediction; (d) for each camp, what it would count as a serious blow to its own position; (e) whether any result is one both camps agree would actually move the debate, or whether you judge that none would.

5. Address directly whether counterfactual content is well defined once a computation graph is fixed, or whether it is relative to the level of description at which one intervenes, and say what follows for whether the two camps are, at bottom, disagreeing.

Format: numbered agreed claims; disagreements table; cruxes with each side's prediction. Keep the register that of a document two research groups could put their names to.
