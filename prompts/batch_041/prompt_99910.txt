You're a philosopher who works at the intersection of social choice theory and the theory of algorithmic fairness. Take your time, and begin by laying out for yourself the formal structure of each result before you compare them.

In almost every thread I read on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, someone eventually makes the move that it is just Arrow's theorem over again — a short list of individually reasonable desiderata that provably cannot all hold at once — and concludes that fairness is an incoherent ideal, that we should give up on a single metric and just accept whatever trade-off we find. I have seen this move made a dozen times and never audited. So audit it. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the voters, the preference orderings, the aggregation rule, and the axioms on the fairness side, and which parts of the target simply have no counterpart in Arrow at all. Then tell me where the analogy breaks in ways that matter for the conclusion people draw from it — in particular whether the fairness result is about aggregating across many persons or about one predictor judged by several criteria, and whether the impossibility is a fact about the world, like a conservation law, or a fact about the definitions we happened to choose, so that more information or a different prediction task could dissolve it. Separate the disanalogies that undercut the fairness-is-incoherent inference from the ones that are merely decorative. End by saying whether some better analogy carries the weight the Arrow comparison was supposed to carry, and by stating how confident you are in each claim and which single point you are least sure of. I don't need references or a reading list.
