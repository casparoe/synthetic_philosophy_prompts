For a piece I'm writing about how medicine decides where normal ends, I've been collecting cases in which the same body gets classified differently depending on which expert panel you consult. The American Diabetes Association treats a fasting glucose of 100 mg/dL and up as impaired fasting glucose, while the World Health Organization keeps its upper limit of normal below 110, on the stated ground that a lower line would sweep in too many people. A blood pressure of 130/80 counts as hypertension under American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association criteria but not under European ones. In 2014 NICE lowered the ten-year cardiovascular risk at which statins are offered for primary prevention from 20 percent to 10 percent, which by its own estimate made about 4.5 million more people eligible for a preventive drug; the number that triggers the offer comes out of a cost-effectiveness model as much as out of a physiological finding.

What I want from you is not a summary of these disputes but the philosophical commitments the practice rests on. When a health system sets a diagnostic threshold by asking what it is worth paying to prevent, what concept of disease does it thereby assume? Does it commit itself to disease being a normative category, one whose boundaries are fixed by human purposes, budgets, and tolerable rates of false positives? Or can a naturalist about dysfunction absorb all of this as vagueness in the underlying biology plus a separate decision about treatment? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as the evidence allows, and separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. For instance: does treating a population-level risk percentile as a diagnosable condition in an individual demand anything more than a statistical conception of normal functioning, and is that conception already value-laden in its choice of reference population and cut point? Tell me which rival accounts of health and disease the practice quietly rules out. Consider, among others, a view on which disease is deviation from species-typical functioning, a view on which it is a harmful failure of an internal mechanism, and a view on which the category is frankly a policy instrument. Say whether the practice's parts are consistent with one another, given that the same bodies that defend a threshold as a clinical fact also defend it in economic terms, and say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that more than one of those accounts would license it. Finally, if the presupposed view were replaced by a strictly dysfunction-based one, or by an openly normative one, tell me concretely what would have to change: who gets labeled, who gets treated, and how the argument would then have to be conducted in public.
