My study group has been grinding through decision theory and my friend Rae has developed a very firm line about when free information is worth taking before you decide. Her rulings so far, roughly in order: (a) you're choosing between biking and driving, someone offers you the forecast for free — take it; (b) you've already locked in a non-refundable bet and someone offers to tell you the result in advance — she shrugs, calls it a wash, says it can't change anything so it's neither good nor bad; (c) two sealed envelopes, you pick one, someone offers a free peek at which one has more — take it; (d) a free test that would tell you whether you'll eventually come down with something you can do absolutely nothing about either way — she says take it, it's free, information can't hurt you, that's the whole point of the theorem, she's forever quoting Good at us; (e) someone offers you free information that would change which option you pick, but you know that on seeing it you'd get so annoyed you'd spitefully choose the worse option — she says refuse it, information can definitely hurt you when you won't actually use it right.

Next week it's my case, and I can't predict her. I'm offered, for free, a look at evidence that would prove my closest friend has been lying to me for years. Here's the wrinkle: right now I'd want to know, and I'd want to keep the friendship — I value both the truth and them. But I know myself well enough to know that if I read it, I'll stop caring about them entirely, cut them off, and end up genuinely happier and more clear-eyed afterward. The me on the far side of reading it would say it was the best thing that ever happened to me; the me right now thinks that version of me has lost something that matters. So does she say I should take the look?

I care less about her yes/no than about which rule she's actually running — the plain 'free info never hurts, and it helps exactly when it can change your choice' theorem, the version with a follow-through caveat, the version where it only counts relative to the values you happen to have now, or some total-welfare thing that counts how the news itself makes you feel. Tell me which of those her five rulings leave standing, what each surviving one predicts about my case, and which single further case would best tell them apart — and, if you had to bet, which one she's really beleiveing.
