I am a novelist at work on a book whose two central characters share a corner office in a university population research centre, and I have reached the scene I have been dreading: the long argument, conducted at a whiteboard, over whether the generations they have spent their careers measuring are real. One of them — I have been calling her the realist — has spent thirty years producing cohort studies and believes that a generation is a genuine social kind: a body of people who came of age in the same historical weather, whose shared formative exposure gives them a common cast of mind that shows up in their politics, their spending, their taste. The other, the nominalist, is a technical demographer who believes that 'generation' survives in the discipline chiefly because it sells books, and that the entire vocabulary of Boomers and Zoomers is a marketing department's mistake which sociologists have agreed, out of politeness or cowardice, to take seriously. I want the scene to be a real argument — each of them answering the other's actual point, neither of them a fool, neither permitted to win by strawmanning the other — and I want it fought over evidence and cases rather than over definitions, because I have already written the scene in which they agree that definitions are stipulative, and it is dull.

I have read around the subject long enough to know what I want them to fight about, and I would like you to write the dialogue so that each of the following is actually used by one of them, in the service of the argument rather than as decoration. First, the Lexis diagram, on which age, period, and year of birth are three coordinates, so that a cohort is simply the diagonal line running across the grid — together with the awkward fact that this construction makes age, period, and cohort perfectly collinear, so that any claim about what a generation is like requires an assumption that no data can check. Second, the phenomenon of tempo distortion, familiar from period fertility measures, in which a change in the timing of an event is misread as a change in its quantity, so that a generation may look distinctive only because it did things earlier or later than the one before it. Third, the case of a country that moved its school-entry birthday cutoff by six months, splitting a single birth year into two groups with different formative environments, and the question of which of the two, if either, belongs to the generation on either side of the seam. Fourth, the distinction drawn a century ago by a sociologist of knowledge between a generation and a 'generational unit' — the smaller cluster inside a generation that actually works out a shared response to its situation, while the rest of the cohort merely shares its dates. Fifth, the possibility that generations are real only in the way a currency peg is real, held in place by everyone's belief that it holds, so that generational labels become self-fulfilling the moment enough people are told which one they are. And sixth, the mundane fact that cohort size alone — a large birth year crowding into the same schools, the same first jobs, the same housing market — can produce a shared outlook with no shared event behind it at all.

Give me roughly twelve hundred to sixteen hundred words, in dialogue only, with the barest minimum of stage business. Let each speaker have the strongest version of her case: the realist should be able to press tempo distortion and cohort size as evidence that the thing is real whatever its mechanism, and the nominalist should be able to turn the Lexis diagram against every claim the realist makes. Let them interrupt each other, concede a point where honesty requires it, and reach no conclusion. I do not want a resolution; I want the reader to finish the scene unable to decide whether these two are disagreeing about the world or about a word, and slightly embarrassed on behalf of both.
