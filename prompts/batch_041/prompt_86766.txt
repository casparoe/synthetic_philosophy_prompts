You're a philosopher who works on personhood in African philosophy and on social ontology more generally, so I don't need any of the basics here.

I run a small reading group and we've hit a wall. Two of the regulars have been going at each other for a year now and the arguement has stopped being productive. One is a strong relationalist about personhood — the Ubuntu-ish line that you're a person only in and through your relations with others, that personhood is something you attain and can lose, that nobody is a person on their own. The other is what I'd call a threshold liberal: personhood is a status you have by virtue of being the kind of creature you are, it's equal and it doesn't come in degrees, and relations matter causally (you'd be stunted and miserable without them) but not constitutively. They keep talking past each other, and I've started to suspect half of it is about the word "person".

I want you to write the joint statement the two of them could actually both sign. Take a breath first and brainstorm where the real joints are before you draft anything — I don't want a mush of "both views have insights".

Do it like this. First a numbered section of agreed claims, including agreed claims about what's at stake and about which sub-questions are genuinely open. Then a disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, a neutral statement of the question, then a seperate line for each side's position, phrased so that side would accept the wording as a fair statement of its own view.

I suspect some of the cruxes are: whether personhood is a status, an achievement, or a status that can be forfeited; whether the relational view has anything to say about people you stand in no relations with — distant strangers, the dead, future people; whether it can condemn an oppressive community from the inside or just collapses into whatever the community happens to think; and whether part of the fight is verbal rather than substantive. Tell me if I'm wrong about any of those.

For each crux, give a case or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, and write down what each side predicts about it in advance, before anyone goes looking. One good case per crux beats five.

To stop them cheating, make both sides state their view using this scaffolding: for any being at any time, is personhood grounded in (a) kind membership, (b) capacities, (c) actually standing in the relevant relations, or (d) being recognised as a person by others — and which of those is necessary, which sufficient, and whether the predicate is binary, graded, or a threshold with defeaters. They don't have to accept the scaffolding, but they have to say which cells they're in.

Finally: be upfront about which of your agreed claims is most likely to be a fudge that both sides would privately resent, and say what would change your mind about the overall shape of the document — which argument or case would make you redraw the line between agreement and disagreement. And be honest about where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
