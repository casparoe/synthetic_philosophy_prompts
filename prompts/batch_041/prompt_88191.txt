I retired last year from a career in analytical chemistry — thirty-odd years mostly spent fighting with mass specs and their baselines — and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the local university, which has been equal parts humbling and addictive. Here's the thing that's got its hooks in me. For most of my working life I was, without ever having the word for it, a strict conditionalizer: you've got a prior, you get a datum, you bang it through Bayes and move on. But real instruments don't hand you data — they hand you readings, smeared out by noise, and what I actually take in from the machine is never "the concentration is 12.3 ppm," it's "the machine flashed 12.3 and I have a rough sense of how much to trust that." Someone in the group told me there's a whole literature on this — updating on uncertain evidence, and a rule named after Jeffrey (I think Richard Jeffrey, the decision theorist?) where instead of setting the probability of the evidence-proposition to 1 you just shift its probability to whatever your new credence in it is, and let everything else ride on the likelihoods. "Probability kinematics," they called it. I've read enough to be dangerous and not enough to be sure.

So here's what I want, and I want you to play the formal epistemologist / philosopher of science here and really slow down and think before you answer, maybe start by brainstorming the moving parts out loud. Give me your actual credence — a number — that Jeffrey conditionalization (roughly: on learning something with credence p that isn't 1, you move your credence in the relevant partition cells to the new values and renormalize everything else by its old conditional probability) is the correct general rule for updating on uncertain evidence, and then decompose it: what's the probability that genuinely uncertain evidence even exists, as opposed to all real evidence being certain once you describe it properly (and does the question even have a truth value if it doesn't)? What's the probability that, granting uncertain evidence, probability kinematics is uniquely right rather than one of its rivals — say, updating instead on the higher-order fact that you recieved this particular reading, or treating the reading itself as the certain evidence? What's the probability that the order-dependence bothers me for a good reason — the fact that feeding in the same two uncertain observations in the opposite order can land you in a different place, which seems like it ought to be a reductio but maybe isn't? And I suppose the assumption that the likelihoods stay fixed while the priors move deserves its own seperate term. Give me a number and a line of justification for each term, then how they combine into the headline number.

Then tell me which single term you're least sure about and what would move it. My own number is around 0.6 and my friend in the group (a philosopher of physics, much sharper than me) is around 0.25, so if you can, tell me which of us is most likely wrong and about which sub-question — I suspect we're not even disagreeing about the same thing. Don't hedge by laying out the options and leaving the verdict to me: pick, commit to a number, and defend it.
