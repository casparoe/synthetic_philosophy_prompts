I keep running into a pattern in committee decisions and I want to know whether it has a name. A group has to decide several propositions that are presented as separate questions but are not really independent. In one case I saw, a funding panel was asked to vote on three things: whether a proposed method is scientifically sound; whether it will yield a publishable result within three years; and whether the project should be funded. Each panellist had a coherent view. The trouble was that the phrase scientifically sound did not mean the same thing across the two votes. For a three-year project, sound meant likely to produce a clean result quickly; for a longer project, sound meant methodologically respectable even if the result might take a decade. So a majority could vote yes to soundness, yes to the three-year claim, and no to funding, and the combination looked like an inconsistency in the group, but no individual had changed their mind and no single proposition had been affirmed and denied. The group had, in effect, manufactured a contradiction by freezing the wording of the questions while the content of the answers shifted with the other questions on the ballot.

A second case: a hiring committee votes on whether a candidate has sufficient research potential, whether the candidate would fit the department's teaching needs, and whether to make an offer. Several members think the candidate's potential is high only if the teaching load is reduced; others think the teaching need is genuine only if the candidate's potential is high enough to justify the reduction. Again, each member is consistent, the majority verdicts on the separate questions point one way, and the overall decision points another. But the deeper issue is not just that the separate majority verdicts are jointly inconsistent. It is that the questions themselves are being individuated in a way that makes them look independent when they are not, and that the act of voting on them separately changes what a yes on each one means.

I know there are familiar problems about groups making inconsistent decisions from consistent individual judgments, and about the order in which issues are voted on. What I am after is the specific phenomenon where the instability lies in the issue space rather than in the aggregation rule: the same words are used, but their content is partly constituted by the other propositions under consideration, so separate majority votes can generate an apparent collective inconsistency that is really a semantic or conceptual drift. Is there an established name for this? If there is, I would like to know how it differs from the nearest concepts — the classic paradox of majority voting on connected propositions, agenda-setting effects, framing effects, and the problem of unbundling a package of issues. If there is no established name, please coin one and define it, with an account of what it is not and some boundary cases. I am not satisfied with a bare label; I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, where it begins and ends, and which of the nearby ideas fits best.
