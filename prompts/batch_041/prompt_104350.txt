I am currently taking a seminar on personhood and its critics, and next week I present my term paper to the group. The paper defends a relational conception of personhood — the view, associated with Ubuntu thought, that one is born a human being but becomes a person only through one's relations with others — and I take it further than its usual defenders are willing to. My thesis, which I call the dissolution view, is that on a genuinely relational account personhood is not merely graduated but revocable: a person can cease to be a person, and this can happen both while alive, as in advanced dementia, and after death, when the relations that sustained the personhood lapse. The mechanism I defend is what I call relational upkeep: the relevant relations are not merely recognitional — others taking you to be a person — but constitutive, so that when the upkeep stops, the status itself is withdrawn and not merely the recognition of it. I use this to argue that the hardest questions about moral status are not the familiar ones about marginal cases at the beginning of life but the ones about people who have already been persons and then stop being them.

I know this audience well. My professor is a Kantian who works on autonomy and human dignity and will almost certainly read graded, revocable personhood as an assault on dignity and on the idea that persons have a worth admitting of no degrees. Two graduate students in bioethics work on decision-making capacity and informed consent, and they will press me on what my view implies for patients who are still "there" enough to have preferences. A student from the department's African philosophy reading group is likely to object that I have turned Ubuntu into a toolkit for Western end-of-life problems and stripped it of its communal and metaphysical commitments. And a visiting cognitive scientist is a functionalist who thinks personhood should track psychological capacities rather than social relations, and who will want to know why relations should be doing any of this work at all.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage each would do to the dissolution view; each phrased the way the particular person I expect it from would phrase it; for each, a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes; and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks.
