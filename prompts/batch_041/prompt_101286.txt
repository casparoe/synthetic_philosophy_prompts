I'm defending a paper at our epistemology reading group arguing that upamāna -- the Nyāya "knowledge by comparison" pramāṇa, the one you get when someone tells you a gavaya is like a cow and you later recognize one in the forest -- is a genuine, irreducible source of knowledge, and that the standard analytic list (perception, memory, inference, testimony) is simply missing a member. My line is that what upamāna actually delivers isn't knowledge of a resemblance (that part is just perception plus memory doing their thing) but knowledge of a term's application, a word-to-thing connection, and that neither inference nor testimony hands you that on its own. The room is mostly analytic epistemologists who work on testimony and perceptual justification; two of them know a little Indian philosophy from secondary reading but no Sanskrit; and there's one Buddhist-studies colleague who thinks at most two pramāṇas are ever needed and that Nyāya's four are a taxonomic indulgence. I get maybe twenty minutes of Q&A after a half-hour talk, so replies have to be short. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this crowd -- not the ones a Sanskritist would raise -- ordered by how much damage they do, phrased the way an analytic epistemologist would put them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Take a real stand: tell me which objections I should just concede, which I have to fight, and whether you think the thesis survives the room at all -- don't just lay out the options.
