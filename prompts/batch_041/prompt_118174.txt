Consider a family of arrangements in which an institution holds a person to a commitment she made earlier, against her own later and contrary choice. Gambling regulators in some jurisdictions keep self-exclusion lists: a person can ask to be barred from casinos, and the casinos are expected to refuse her service. Some addiction treatment programs ask patients to put up money that is forfeited if they relapse. Employers enroll employees in retirement savings by default, so that saving continues unless the employee acts to stop it. A living will instructs physicians to withhold treatment at a point when the patient can no longer speak. In each case the institution treats the earlier, cooler preference as the one with authority and the later, contrary preference, which may be just as sincere, informed, and uncoerced, as something to be overridden or at least not honored. The very existence of these devices also assumes that a person's preferences are not stable across time: that the rate at which she trades a later good against an earlier one shifts as the moment of choice approaches, so that the institution cannot simply take her preferences as given but must decide which of her successive selves to listen to. On the textbook picture of a consumer with a single, time-consistent preference ordering, none of these devices would have any work to do.

Set against this a set of refusals. Courts will not compel a person to perform personal services she has promised, and no contract can bind a person into servitude, however freely she entered it. A competent patient can revoke an advance directive at any time, even one she wrote with great care. And the law does not generally excuse wrongdoing on the ground that the will was weak: a person who acts in rage or in craving is answerable for what she does, and her earlier resolutions neither bind nor shield her.

What theory of the person and of the will does the first family of practices presuppose? Reconstruct it. It appears to need something like this: that a person's preferences at different times can conflict in a way that leaves no single ordering to be respected, that the institution may nonetheless rank those preferences, and that the earlier or cooler one is the person's authoritative preference while the later one is a defect of will to be engineered around. Say whether the practices strictly require that picture or merely fit it comfortably. Would a single-agent account, on which weakness of will is one agent failing to do what she herself judged best, license the same devices, or would it point instead to strengthening the agent's judgment rather than routing around it? Would an account on which the person is a series of time-slices, with no further fact about which slice is really her, license them? Would an account that treats the whole phenomenon as a correctable cognitive bias, with no division in the self at all, license them? Then say what the second family presupposes, and whether the two can be held together. If the later self's choice is not really the person's choice, on what ground is she fully answerable for what that self does? If it is her choice, on what ground may an employer, a regulator, or a physician set it aside? Where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, say so explicitly, and tell me what would have to change in each family if we adopted the rival account.
