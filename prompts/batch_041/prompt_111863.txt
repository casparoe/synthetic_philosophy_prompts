Since retiring from a career in geophysics, I have been reading history, and I keep running into a puzzle that I suspect may be of my own making. In one book after another I encounter the claim that germs, climate, and volcanic eruptions ought to be treated as historical actors in their own right — as genuine participants in the events historians narrate, rather than as inert background scenery. Yet the same authors, a few pages later, will warn sternly against environmental determinism, as though the very suggestion that a volcano or a pathogen might have driven human affairs were an intellectual sin. I cannot get these two attitudes to sit together. If the eruption of Tambora can be said to have caused the harvest failures and the migrations and the famines that followed, then plainly it made a difference to what happened; and if it made a difference, it was a cause; and if it was a cause, then surely the environment did determine something. So on what grounds do historians separate the innocent claim that non-human events are causes from the guilty claim that they are determinants? Is environmental determinism perhaps simply the name for taking that causation seriously, in which case the accusation amounts to a refusal to let nature into the story at all? Or is there a genuine distinction here that my training in the physical sciences has left me unable to see? I would be grateful if you could disentangle the concepts for me. My suspicion is that the word determinism is doing two quite different jobs in these debates, and that the word actor is doing a third, but I cannot work out where the slippage lies.
