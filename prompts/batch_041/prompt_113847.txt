I am a novelist by trade, and for the past six years I have also taught a senior seminar in our university's literature-and-science program. This term the seminar is called The Inheritance Plot; my students are mostly literature majors who have taken one statistics course and one introductory biology course, with a scattering of biology students who read novels. The final examination is in ten days, and I am now writing its essay section.

I need a single essay question, worth twenty marks and answerable in ninety minutes in roughly seven hundred words, on what heritability does and does not mean. The difficulty has to lie in the crux rather than in the labels: my students have already memorized that heritability is a population statistic and that it says nothing about individuals, and a question they can dispatch by reciting those two cautions will tell me nothing about whether they understand the idea. I want a question that makes them use it.

The crux I have in mind is this. Two research teams, working independently, study the same trait in the same country in the same decade and report very different heritability estimates, and neither team has made an arithmetic error. I want the students to explain how that is possible; to say precisely what does and does not follow from such estimates for (i) the claim that the trait is genetically influenced, (ii) the claim that a given individual's score is so many percent genetic, and (iii) a proposal to raise the population average by changing the environment; and finally to catch a policy analyst who cites the higher of the two figures as proof that environmental intervention will fail, identifying the exact inferential step that goes wrong and what further evidence would be needed to make the analyst's conclusion stand. Please invent the trait rather than borrowing a well-known one; I do not want the answer to turn on whether a student happens to have heard of a particular study. My students sit the examination without internet access, and the question should be answerable from reasoning and general knowledge alone.

Give me the question, a model answer of about six hundred words, and a marking scheme: five or six must-hit points with the marks each is worth adding up to twenty, a one-line note for each on how candidates typically get it wrong, and a short statement of what separates an A from a B. Begin your reply with the examination question itself, in the first sentence, with no preamble, and let the model answer follow without restating the question; the marking scheme comes last.
