I've been sitting in on a reading group on moral psychology, and last week a friend forwarded me an essay from a general-audience science magazine that claims to draw out the philosophical lessons of the "WEIRD" research on cultural variation. I've set out its central passage below; what I want is an audit of it rather than a defense.

"A much-cited review by a team of anthropologists and psychologists surveyed the psychology literature and found that something on the order of 96% of the subjects in published studies come from Western, industrialized countries — a large share of them from American college campuses — even though those countries contain only about 12% of the world's population. The authors christened this population WEIRD, and their central finding was that WEIRD people are the strangest people on earth: across domain after domain, from susceptibility to optical illusions to the way people divide money in bargaining games, they turn out to be statistical outliers while the rest of humanity clusters around a common pattern. The essay then turns to morality. It observes that non-Western societies do not share our fixation on individual rights, fairness, and autonomy; most of them organize moral life around community, hierarchy, and purity instead, and in some small-scale societies people will accept a bargaining offer that a Western undergraduate would reject as an insult. Because the moral intuitions that WEIRD people treat as self-evident are, on this evidence, the exception rather than the rule, the essay concludes that those intuitions are nothing more than the local customs of a peculiar slice of humanity. And since morality is thereby revealed to be whatever a culture happens to say it is, there is no fact of the matter about what is really right or wrong. The essay closes by claiming that this is what the WEIRD research 'proves,' and that philosophers who still believe in objective moral truth have simply never looked at the data."

I'll confess that I am inclined to think the conclusion is basically right — I cannot see what could make one culture's moral code objectively better than another's, and the evidence that judgments vary so widely seems to me to point where the essay points — but the passage also strikes me as slippery in a way I can't name, and I don't want to embrace a conclusion merely because it flatters what I already suspected. So please go through it claim by claim. Which parts would the researchers the essay leans on themselves endorse, and which are the essayist's embellishments or oversimplifications? Where exactly does the argument cross from empirical findings about what people believe into a philosophical conclusion about what is true, and is that crossing legitimate? In particular, I would like to know whether the descriptive claim — that moral judgments vary across cultures — can on its own support the metaethical claim that there are no objective moral truths; whether the "WEIRD people are the outliers while the rest of humanity clusters together" framing is really what the research shows; and whether the essay's use of the illusion and bargaining-game examples does the work it asks of them. Please sort your answer into what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is flatly wrong, and tell me both what a philosopher who believes in objective morality would say in reply and what the empirical researchers themselves would most likely object to. If the essay's conclusion does not follow, I want to understand precisely which inference fails and whether some weaker conclusion survives it.
