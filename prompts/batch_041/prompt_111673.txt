I have been running a reading group on Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal, and it has stalled in a way I would like diagnosed rather than adjudicated. Nobody disputes the theorem itself: with unrestricted domain, weak Pareto and minimal liberalism (at least two individuals each decisive over at least one pair of alternatives), no social ordering satisfies all three. What we cannot agree on is what the theorem is a theorem about. One camp reads it as a genuine discovery about the incoherence of a certain liberal position, and treats the meddlesome-preferences case (the prude who cares what the lewd reads) as showing that a liberal who accepts even the mildest efficiency condition is committed to inconsistency. A second camp holds that the formalisation is the whole problem: that decisiveness over a pair of alternatives is a stipulation no serious account of rights would endorse, since rights protect persons against interference or confer authority over decisions, and neither of those is a relation between two outcomes; on this view the theorem is true and beside the point, an artefact of representing rights inside a framework that can only see outcomes. A third camp suspects that the result is an Arrow-type impossibility in disguise, with the liberal gloss doing no work.

My hypothesis, which I would rather you attack than elaborate, is that the impasse is not about the theorem but about the target of the formalisation: whether minimal liberalism is meant to capture a claim-right, a shield against interference, or a power of authority over one coordinate of the social state. On the coordinate reading, the social state is simply the vector of individual decisions, and it is unclear what weak Pareto adds that the description of the state has not already fixed.

Diagnose this. Say which parts of the disagreement are verbal, which turn on a hidden formal question (for instance, whether a given weakening of Pareto or of the liberty condition leaves a non-vacuous theorem), and which are bedrock commitments that no argument will shift. In particular: is restricting the domain so as to exclude preferences about other people's private business a resolution of the paradox or a restatement of it, given that the restriction looks like a substantive liberal commitment rather than a neutral technical move? Then say what would count as progress from here, or why none should be expected.
