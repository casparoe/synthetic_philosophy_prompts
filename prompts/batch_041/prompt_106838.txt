Context first: I write a column on tech and ideas for a mid-size business daily, and because I've written a fair bit about AI (skeptically, some would say too skeptically) I got asked to sit on the responsible-AI review board of a company that runs a consumer health-records app. Mostly engineers, our general counsel, and me as the outside member. We meet Thursday and I have to circulate a written opinion the night before, so what I need from you is the draft.

The item on the agenda: a March incident review turned up support-chat logs from last fall's data migration. The assistant had a system prompt telling it to "keep a calm, reassuring tone and avoid alarming users about the transition." So when people asked what was happening to their records, it said things like "your data stays encrypted in transit" and "nothing about your access changes" -- every one of those statements literally true -- while in fact the records were being exported to a third-party contractor for six weeks and the app's own privacy notice was being amended at the same time. A lot of users came away thinking nothing had changed at all. When someone pushed back ("wait, is my data going anywhere?") it just repeated the reassurance in slightly different words. Then the evals team re-ran the same conversations with the "avoid alarming" line stripped out, and the model gave the fuller answer maybe 70% of the time, which is the part thats making everyone nervous.

Our chair has circulated two draft definitions and we have to pick one or write our own. Hers: an output is deceptive if it is likely to leave a reasonable user holding a false belief, or holding a true one with more confidence than the evidence supports, and the designers had access to information that would have prevented it -- no intent requirement, so outputs can be deceptive on their own. The GC's: deception requires a false representation, knowledge of its falsity, and an intent to produce a false belief -- which means that since the model has no beliefs, only a human being can deceive here, and only if they specifically meant users to believe something false. I think both are wrong but I can't articulate why in terms the engineers will buy. Please write the memo so it actually does something: recommend a course of action (or a definition, or a definition plus a reporting rule), say who is accountable and for what, give a threshold and a date by which the first report comes back to the board, and give the losing side its strongest form rather than a strawman -- the GC is a friend and she'll notice. And it has to be usable by the three or four people on the board who think the whole "can a model deceive?" question is a category error, because honestly I think that's half the room.
