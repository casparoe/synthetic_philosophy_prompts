I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I have picked up enough informal Bayesian reasoning to be suspicious of arguments that look too tidy, though not enough to see exactly where this one breaks. A friend recently forwarded me a short piece — a popular-science book excerpt, I believe, or perhaps a blog post — that purports to dispose of the two-envelope problem. It runs roughly as follows:

'Two envelopes lie on a table, one containing twice as much money as the other. You choose one at random and open it to find £20. The other envelope must contain either £40 or £10. Since your choice was random, you were equally likely to have picked the smaller or the larger, so each of those amounts is equally likely; the expected value of switching is therefore (40 + 10)/2 = £25, which exceeds the £20 you already hold. So you should switch — and, by the very same reasoning, switch back, and then switch again, forever. The way out is that the step I have marked "equally likely" is smuggled in: it presupposes a flat prior over the possible sums, and there is no such thing as a flat prior over an unbounded range. Specify a genuine prior and the two cases are no longer equiprobable; a routine conditioning calculation then shows that the expected gain from switching is exactly zero. The paradox, on this reading, is a familiar cautionary tale about improper priors rather than a genuine puzzle about choice, and a consistent Bayesian can safely dismiss it.'
