Write a satirical help-centre FAQ for a fictional firm — call it Completeness & Co., or anything in that register — whose business is completing other people's preferences. Clients arrive with pairs of options they cannot rank, or with rankings that run in a circle. The firm offers to buy and sell on terms that respect the client's own stated rankings, charges a modest fee, and, as a matter of arithmetic, leaves the client holding slightly less than she began with. The firm is entirely honest, its bookkeeping is correct, and every argument it makes is one a serious decision theorist would recognise as a serious argument. That is the joke. The target is not decision theory itself but the rhetorical habit of treating a possible money pump as a dispositive proof of irrationality, and the picture of rationality on which any departure from expected-utility coherence counts as a defect.

The piece should be written from inside the firm's position, in the flat, frictionless, faintly menacing register of corporate customer support: numbered entries, a short introductory paragraph, and at least one client question answered in a way the firm sincerely believes to be reassuring. It should be legible and funny to someone who knows the literature and still readable to someone who does not — no winking, no authorial nudges, no moment where the prose admits the absurdity.

The FAQ must engage, in the firm's own voice, at least the following:

- Why a preference ordering that is intransitive, or that leaves some pairs unranked, exposes the client to a sequence of trades each of which she prefers at the time and which returns her to where she began, minus the fee.
- Why the incompleteness case needs an extra assumption that the intransitivity case does not — roughly, that a slightly sweetened version of one option strictly beats an unsweetened option the client neither prefers nor disprefers.
- The objection that a client who sees the whole sequence coming can simply refuse to start, and the firm's reply: that refusing means acting against her preferences at the moment of choice, and that the firm's more elaborate offers are designed to survive foresight in any case.
- The distinction between showing that a client can be exploited and showing that she is irrational, together with the demand that the firm explain why coherence — acting as though one maximised expected utility relative to one's own credences and utilities — is a requirement of rationality rather than a convenience for the firm's ledger.
- An entry drawing the parallel with the coherent-credence case, and conceding that the same objections arise there.
- An entry on whether the firm is revealing the client's preferences or quietly changing them.

Aim for about 800 words. After the FAQ, add a separate note of roughly 150 words, in your own voice and clearly set off from the satire, stating which of the piece's claims are settled among people who work on this topic and which are genuinely contested — for instance whether exploitability entails irrationality, whether resolute or sophisticated choice rescues the client, and whether the money pump is a proof or a rhetorical device — and flag any point where the satire leans on a reading a careful person could reasonably reject. Do not name real firms or living authors, and do not attribute the arguments to particular texts; keep the piece self-contained.
