Two months ago I sat on a small university committee that had to decide whether a proposed experiment counted as work on living organisms for the purposes of our biosafety review. The material in question was a prion preparation. Our rulebook defines a living organism by a checklist — metabolism, growth, reproduction, responsiveness, and so on — and the preparation satisfied roughly half of it. We argued for forty minutes and then voted for a line that nobody could defend on the merits. I have taught a session on the definition of life for years and had always enjoyed the virus example as a bit of harmless fun. It has stopped being fun, and I would like my students to feel the difficulty in their hands before I explain anything.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of biology: eighteen students, eighty minutes, one meeting, roughly the middle of a unit on biological categories. They have all had a year or two of biology, and most arrive confident that life is defined by a list of properties.

Design a session in which they run a committee of the kind I have just described. My sketch is below; redesign it as you see fit, but preserve the feature that matters to me: the trouble must be produced by the rules and the cases, not announced by me.

The committee keeps a register. Every item before it must be entered in one of two columns, living or not living. A single sentence stating the committee's criterion must be posted before any item is classified, and every entry must follow from it. Add a short list of uncontroversial paradigm cases that the criterion must also get right — a dog, a rock, a flame, a bacterium, a corpse — so that no group can escape by declaring that it will simply define the words as it pleases. A group may petition for a third, borderline column, but only if it can say what a borderline entry would mean: ignorance of a fact that exists, or the absence of any fact to know.

I have a pool of cases already and want it used and extended: a viroid, a short circle of naked RNA with no coat and no protein of its own; a prion; a virus whose genome is larger than that of some free-living bacteria; a small virus that can only replicate inside the factory a giant virus builds; a mule; a sterile worker ant; a mitochondrion; an endosymbiotic bacterium with a heavily reduced genome that cannot survive outside its host cell; a tardigrade in its dried, metabolically silent state; a bacterial spore; a self-replicating RNA that, after many rounds in a test tube with a replicase, shed most of its sequence and now copies itself faster than anything else in the vessel; a liposome containing a self-copying RNA; a computer program that mutates and competes in a simulated world; a 3D printer that can print most of its own parts; a clay crystal in solution that catalyses copies of itself. Add four or five cases of your own, at least two of which my students will not have met, and do not let every case be a variation on the virus.

Then the part I care most about. After the registers are posted, groups exchange criteria and attack: each group must find, in the shared pool, a case that the other group's criterion misclassifies, and must invent one further case that does the same. The defending group must repair its criterion with the smallest possible addition and post the revision. Run two rounds and keep every version on the wall, so that the accretion of exceptions is visible at the end.

I would also like a second, shorter exercise using revisionary criteria, handed out as cards: life as a network that continually regenerates its own components and its own boundary; life as a system that holds itself far from thermodynamic equilibrium by dissipating energy; life as membership in a population that evolves by natural selection; life as a structure whose assembly from basic building blocks requires an unusually long and specific sequence of steps, a criterion proposed recently in origin-of-life work; life as anything carrying a heritable code that specifies its own construction. Have each group apply one card to the same dozen cases and compare verdicts across groups.

Give me the full design: rules, materials, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, how the register and the attack rounds are scored if anything needs scoring, and what I should be doing while they work. Then the debrief, which should map the moments of the session onto the underlying issue — why a checklist of features produces disagreement exactly at the borderline; what is gained and lost by dropping the sharp line in favour of graded membership or family resemblance; what is gained and lost by declaring that the question is the wrong one and replacing it with more specific questions about what we need the category for. Do not let me name any of this before the debrief, and keep the debrief to twenty minutes.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I expect at least one group to deflate the whole thing by stipulating, and I want to know how to handle that without lecturing — and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. I suspect that scoring criteria by their fit with our verdicts penalises exactly the counterintuitive theoretical accounts that might be right, and that the session will leave my students more relativist about the category than the evidence warrants.
