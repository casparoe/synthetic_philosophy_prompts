I teach a graduate seminar in social ontology, and this term we are reading the collapse literature from the outside — as philosophers, not archaeologists. I have a decomposition I have been working on, and I would like you to run it against a case. It is my own construction; do not go looking for antecedents or try to locate it in a literature, because I want it tested, not attributed.

The decomposition. What we call the collapse of a society is usually three separable losses reported as one. First, integration: the loss of whatever apparatus coordinated decisions across a territory — tribute, roads, a calendar, a court, a credible monopoly on violence. Second, reproduction: the loss of the demographic and subsistence capacity to sustain the previous population at the previous standard. Third, legitimation: the loss of the symbolic order that made the previous arrangements appear necessary rather than contingent — that the king speaks for the gods, that the tribute is owed, that the calendar is the order of the world.

The claim to be tested. These three come apart. Integration can fail while reproduction and legitimation hold: a change of regime, not a collapse. Integration and legitimation can fail while reproduction holds: a dark age that is, demographically, entirely fine. The strong sense of collapse — a society that ceases to exist and does not resume — requires all three to fail, and to fail such that no successor can reoccupy the old position. On this view most alleged collapses are reconfigurations, and the interesting question is why we insist on narrating them as deaths.

The case: the Terminal Classic abandonment of the southern Maya lowlands — the great polities of the Petén and adjacent regions left between roughly the eighth and tenth centuries, while Maya societies continued in the north and the highlands and were still there at Spanish contact. Take as given what is common knowledge: the abandonment was real and large, it was staggered rather than simultaneous, drought and warfare and the failure of elite networks are all in play, and the Maya did not disappear.

What I want from you:

Assign each of the three losses a verdict for this case, and say what would have to be true for you to reverse it.

Say which of the three you take to be the slow variable and which the fast one, and whether the case supports that ordering or inverts it.

Then the part I care about most: where the decomposition misdescribes the case. Does it presuppose a state that was never there — were the southern lowland polities ever integrated in the sense the first category requires? Does the third category smuggle in functionalism, treating the symbolic order as something that exists in order to hold the other two up? And does splitting legitimation off from integration beg the question against anyone who holds that in this case they were the same thing? Where the decomposition has nothing to say, say that rather than forcing it to speak.

Contrast it briefly with the two framings it is meant to improve on: the story that societies come apart when the marginal returns on complexity turn negative, and the ecological-informational reading in which collapse is the release phase of a cycle of growth, conservation, and reorganization. Say what each of those gets that mine does not.

Close with the general lesson for social ontology: on your account, what individuates a society, such that we can say one has ceased to exist rather than merely changed? If the honest answer is that nothing does, say so, and say what follows for the collapse literature.

End with a final line of exactly this form:

Verdict: <collapse|reconfiguration|mixed> | primary loss: <integration|reproduction|legitimation> | decomposition survives: <yes|qualified|no>
