ok so im the opinions editor for my school paper. we put out an issue every two weeks and i also run the insta account, which honestly is where most people actually read us. we keep getting op-eds and even some news features that make claims about whole groups of people — "our school just has a culture of not trying," "people from [country] are raised more respectful," "gen z is the lonliest generation," "kids from the flats are built different." sometimes its mean and sometimes its meant as a compliment. weve had two complaints this semester, one from a teacher and one from a girl who said a column about "kids from the flats" (thats the neighborhood by the river, everyone knows what it means) made her feel like a bug under glass. my advisor says i have to come up with a policy but she wont tell me what it should be, she just said "you cant print stereotypes, but you also cant ban describing a community," which is definately not a policy.

i dont want a lecture on why stereotyping is bad, i want a thing i can actually run. picture this: i have like 20 minutes before we post, im 16, i cant go do research, and the writer is my friend so i cant just say no. give me a checklist — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, the point where the answer is no and we spike the piece, and the point where it stops being about the writing and becomes a call for my advisor because its a values thing not a facts thing. she has to sign off on anything we think is risky and i need to know which ones those are.

i also made up three little tests and i want you to tell me if theyre any good and where they break, and fold them in if they survive:
- the swap test: does the sentence still read as true if i swap the group for a different group that overlaps a lot with it? if it survives the swap, its not really about that group.
- the narrower box test: can i restate it about a smaller group i could actually check? not "our school is apathetic" but "the juniors who signed up for the volunteering drive and then didnt show." if the claim only works because the group is huge, thats a flag.
- the mechanism test: can the writer say HOW the group thing happens — a policy, a shared experience, an incentive, a history — or is it just "thats how they are"? no mechanism, it goes in the opinion pile with a label on it, not the news pile.

also one more thing. my friend says the whole idea of a "culture" or a "national character" is fake and you should never generalize about a people, period. my other friend says of course you can, sociologists do it all the time, thats literally their job. i dont know who's right and i need the procedure to not secretly depend on settling that — or if it does, tell me exactly where.

then run the finished procedure on these three and show me the output:
1. an op-ed saying our school has a "culture of not caring" and that this is just what this town is like.
2. a column by a student saying the kids from [some country] at our school are naturally better at math and its a beautiful thing about their culture, and she wants the headline to say it too. i want to run this one — shes a good writer and it reads as nice.
3. a news piece saying seniors this year applied to four-year colleges at a lower rate than the class of 2019, based on a survey the stats class ran.

tell me straight which ones the procedure kills and whether ive rigged it so it kills the ones i already didnt like. and tell me what the procedure cant do — the cases where i should just walk it to my advisor instead of pretending i have a rule.
