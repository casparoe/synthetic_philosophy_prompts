I'm a freelance science writer — long features, mostly econ and social science — and I've somehow become the newsroom's unofficial explainer ever since I did a piece on how bank runs actually start. Now the genral assignment desk has talked me into a brown-bag session next month: forty-five minutes, no slides, just a whiteboard, on common knowledge. They asked for it because they keep reaching for the idea in their copy and getting it slightly wrong — "it was an open secret," "everyone knew that everyone knew," "the market just suddenly knew" — and I'd rather they had the actual machinery than keep gesturing at it. The audience is about a dozen reporters plus the photo editor: smart, no maths since high school, no philosophy, and allergic to being lectured. Don't explain common knowledge to me, I have most of the peices. What I need is the teaching plan.

So: what has to come first, second, third, and why — what depends on what, and where the whole thing collapses if I introduce a piece too early. Then which single example should carry the session, and what does that example have to have in order to do the work, and what does it have to lack? I was assuming the coordinated attack puzzle, the two generals with the messenger who might get shot, plus maybe the email version where the messages might not get through and you keep adding confirmations — but push back if something else is better, and tell me why not the easy cases everybody already gets, like which side of the road to drive on, or the muddy-faced children. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, and precisely where in the sequence I should head each one off. My guess is the big one is that they'll hear common knowledge as mutual knowledge with more steps — everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on up — and never notice that the real question is what grounds the bottom of that ladder, or that a finite pile of evidence can't get you there. But there'll be others I'm not seeing: something about a public announcement magically settling everything, something about this being a technicality with no bearing on actual coordination, probably something about the word "rationality" doing work I haven't flagged. If there's room at the end I'd like to reach the bit where common knowledge of rationality is a modelling assumption in game theory rather than a claim about people being clever — tell me if that's reachable in forty-five minutes or if it should wait.

Finally: give me one question to put to the room at the very end that would show me whether the idea landed rather than whether they can nod along — something where the tempting answer is wrong. And tell me what I should flatly refuse to get into at this level and say so out loud, so the session doesn't get eaten by the formalism.
