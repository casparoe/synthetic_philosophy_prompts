Two people in my department got into it in the break room over whether a colleague of ours (call her Sana) "knew" something, and honestly it went further than I could follow. The setup: Sana put together a cost projection and reported we'd be fine, but the numbers she was working from actually entail a shortfall. She had the unit costs, she had the headcount, she had the department's own formula, and those three together just are the shortfall, though she never ran the last step and never noticed. Here's how it went.

(1) Marek said Sana knew there would be a shortfall. She knew the three premises, and those premises entail the conclusion; if you know something, and you know it implies something else, you know the something else. So "she didn't know" is just false, and the fact that she didnt feel like she knew it is beside the point.

(2) Devi said that principle is a fiction. Nobody's head works like that; if it did, you'd know every consequence of everything you've ever read, all the way down. So either it's an idealization, in which case it describes nobody, or it's a norm, in which case it's not evidence about what was actually in her head, and you can't use it as evidence in a blame case.

(3) Marek said fine, take it as a norm. It's a norm she broke. The entailment was one step -- she didn't need to be a theorem prover, she needed to do the obvious thing. And if you throw the norm out you can never hold anyone responsible for the consequences of what they already knew, which is alot of what responsibility is.

(4) Devi said he's mixing up two things. "Should have known" is about diligence -- was she supposed to run that step, did she have time, is that even part of her job. That's an administrative and ethical question. He's importing a logical principle to make the ethical answer look automatic. And even granting the norm, where's the line? One step is obvious, five steps maybe, forty steps no -- but that line is about effort, not about what she knew, so calling it "knew" is just doing rhetorical work.

(5) Marek said norms can be cost-sensitive without being arbitrary; negligence law does it all the time. And anyway Devi's picture has its own absurdity: on his view, even someone staring straight at Q, who knows P and knows P implies Q, doesn't know Q, which is nuts. At some point the idealization has to be tracking something real.

(6) Devi said maybe the real thing is being in a position to know rather than knowing -- that's graded, it's about what you could get to, and it does the blame work fine. And here's a case for you: if the three facts had been split across Sana and two other people so that no one person had them all, would you say nobody knew there'd be a shortfall? Because intuitively the team knew, and your principle can't say that.

They went back and forth like this until the meeting started. I stayed out of it. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? Rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why, and then tell me what both of them are missing.
