I am a software engineer and the engineering representative on my employer's model risk committee, which has final say over whether machine-learning models that affect customers go into production. I have no formal training in philosophy, and I would like the following memo to stand on its own reasoning rather than on appeals to authority I am not equipped to evaluate.

We are deciding whether to approve a credit-underwriting model for a staged rollout. Two of our most senior quantitative researchers reviewed the identical validation package and reached opposite conclusions. Researcher A concludes the model is fit to ship, because the one fairness metric that fails is, in her view, an artifact of a threshold choice we can document and monitor. Researcher B concludes it should not ship, because that same metric failure concentrates harm on a subgroup whose applications are already decided with the least margin for error. They agree on every number in the package and disagree only about what that single failure licenses us to do. Both are competent, both are acting in good faith, and neither has produced a consideration the other cannot at least acknowledge. Two facts complicate the picture. First, they are not independent: both were trained by the same former chair and both rely on the same external validation set, so their disagreement survives even though their evidence base and habits of reasoning largely overlap. Second, Researcher B held A's position three months ago and changed her mind after we extended the validation window, so the committee has begun to treat her as the more reliable of the two on this class of question.

Our charter says the committee must decide on the best expert judgment available, and is silent about what to do when judgments of equal apparent standing conflict. It also says a deadlocked vote defaults to the more risk-averse option, and that any decision must be revisited if materially new evidence arrives within ninety days.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action -- approve, approve with conditions, or hold -- with a specific time frame and any conditions attached to it. It should engage Researcher A's position at its strongest rather than treating it as the obstacle to be cleared, and it should say plainly what the committee would be committing itself to about the weight of expert disagreement, since whatever we decide here will become the precedent for the next such split. It should not simply tell us to seek more evidence unless you also say what evidence and how long. Do not cite published work; reason from the case as described. Close by telling me how confident you are in the recommendation you have given and naming the single point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
