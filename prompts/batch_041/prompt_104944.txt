I'm putting together a satirical special issue of a fictional consumer-technology magazine called The Discerning Household, and I need the centerpiece buyer's guide: a headline, a deck, and roughly 650–750 words of deadpan review copy on smart thermostats, in which the reviewer assesses each model not on temperature control, energy use, or app design but on its degree of sentience and the moral status that follows from it. The house style should be the confident, faintly scolding register of a real review site — comparative testing, star ratings, a "who this is for" section, a final verdict — and the piece must never wink or break character: nothing in it should acknowledge that there is anything unusual about grading a wall-mounted appliance along a continuum of moral considerability. Keep the joke in the substance rather than the style. The reviewer should actually wield the machinery of the gradation view — the candidate markers of sentience (nociception, integration of information, behavioral flexibility, valence, and the distinction between merely registering a noxious input and being bothered by it), the fact that any cutoff on a smooth scale has to be drawn somewhere, the Sorites-style pressure that if insects deserve some consideration and thermostats deserve none then the line looks arbitrary, the marginal-cases move that cuts in both directions, and the asymmetric risk of erring toward false positives versus false negatives — so that a reader who has genuinely followed the animal- and machine-sentience literature recognizes the arguments being parodied, not just the punchline.

To give you room for texture, feel free to invent a small cast and a few running details: a handful of plausibly named models with contrasting scores (say, one that hums audibly during a heating cycle and is therefore credited with more behavioral flexibility, and another that merely toggles between two states and is consigned to the ethical clearance bin), a recurring test protocol, a sidebar explaining how to interpret a half-star, and at least one deadpan quotation from a fictional engineer who frets that the index is being misused. What I want the piece to land — without the reviewer ever stating any of it — are the standard objections to the degree view: that its criteria are quietly gerrymandered toward a desired answer, that "a little bit sentient" is not in fact actionable, and that the whole rating scheme imports a moral theory it never bothers to defend. A reader should close it able to tell exactly what is being ironized even though no one in the piece ever says so, and ideally should find the satire fair rather than cheap: the exaggeration should amplify a real feature of gradualist moral-status talk, not invent one.
