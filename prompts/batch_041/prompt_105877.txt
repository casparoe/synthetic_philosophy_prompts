I'm not an economist, just someone who reads alot online, so forgive me if I mangle this. There's this old fight — Mises and Hayek saying a socialist economy can't do rational economic calculation, because without real market prices for land and factories and machines there's no way to know whether building X is worth more than building Y, and then Lange replying, fine, let a central planning board set the prices, watch for shortages and surpluses, and keep nudging them until they clear, same as a market does.

What I can't figure out is where the modern version leaves us. Suppose the board doesn't do its trial and error in the real economy, where every wrong price means wasted steel and idle workers, but instead runs a giant simulation of the whole economy, lets the simulated prices find their own level until everything clears, then hands those simulated prices to the real factories as marching orders. The simulation never has to make anything, so being wrong costs nothing, and you can run a thousand variations a night. That seems to gut the "you can't work it out in time" half of Hayek's complaint. But I have a nagging feeling the other half survives — something about the knowledge Hayek cared about not existing yet, because nobody knows what a warehouse in a small town really needs until the person running it is stuck with the consequences, and prices are a way of letting that knowledge bubble up rather than a way of adding it up. I can't tell if that's a real argument or just a dressed-up way of saying markets are good.
