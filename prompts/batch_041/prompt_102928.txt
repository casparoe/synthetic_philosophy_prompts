I took early retirement from a physiology lab a few years back and, having spent forty years looking at nerve and muscle preparations, I've been trying to teach myslef philosophy in the evenings — slowly, with a little reading group of fellow amateurs, which is how I ended up in the middle of Maimonides' Guide of the Perplexed. We've reached the chapter where he explains why the Law institutes animal sacrifice, and it's the strangest argument I've read in the whole book. He opens with what is basically embryology and anatomy — he says the nerves coming out of the spinal cord are too soft to move the joints, so they harden into sinews as they go, and then: "When such an animal is born it is extremely tender, and cannot be fed with dry food. Therefore breasts were provided which yield milk, and the young can be fed with moist food which corresponds to the condition of the limbs of the animal, until the latter have gradually become dry and hard." Fine. Then he says straight out that "Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being," and gives us this:

"It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed. ... It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used ... For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner; viz., to build unto Him a temple ... to offer the sacrifices to Him."

Here is what I actually want help with, and please don't just paraphrase the chapter back to me — take the role of someone who knows this text and its background cold (a historian of medieval Jewish philosophy would be ideal) and think carefully, step by step, before you answer. First: what work is the biology doing? Is the analogy meant as evidence — God demonstrably proceeds gradually in nature, therefore He proceeds gradually in legislation — or is it just a reassuring picture, and does the argument collapse if the natural-history claim is shaky? I confess I keep reading his milk-and-sinews passage as a just-so story, and I want to know whether the argument actually leans on it. Second, the bit that really stops me: he says the sacrificial service continued only because it was too abrupt to abolish it, and that it was "transferred" from idolatry to God. Doesn't that mean the sacrifices were commanded only as a means to something else, not for their own sake — and doesn't the Torah's own language about a pleasing odor say the opposite? I gather he raises this very objection against himself and then answers it; walk me through what the answer actually is and whether it works, rather than telling me he "accommodates" human weakness, which is the slogan someone in my group keeps repeating. Third, if a commandment can be explained by a contingent custom of a particular time and place, in what sense is the Law still perfect and permanent for him rather than a piece of historical scaffolding? And tell me where you think I'm importing my own modern assumptions into the text — I suspect the whole "is this adaptation or design" framing is mine, not his. I don't need citations or a reading list, I can't look anything up anyway; I need the argument untangled, so reason it through out loud, flag the steps where you're unsure, and don't be shy about saying the passage is more ambiguous than I want it to be.
