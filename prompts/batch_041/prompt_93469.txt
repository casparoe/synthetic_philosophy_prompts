ok, I'm supposed to write down my position on the gender-equality paradox for a seminar on Thursday and every time I try it turns into soup, so before I embarrass myself I want you to tell me whether the package actually hangs together.

Here's roughly where I've landed.

- I'm a social constructionist about gender, and fairly hardcore about it. When you see men and women wanting systematically different things, the default assumption should be that families, schools, media and labour markets put that there. I don't think there's some untouched natural preference-gap sitting underneath all the culture waiting to be measured.
- I also think the gender-equality paradox is a real, robust finding and not an artifact. The pattern is that the richer and more gender-equal a country is, the bigger the differences between men and women in what they study and the sort of work they end up doing. The machinery that used to keep women out of things is weakest exactly where the sex difference in choices is largest.
- I do not buy the popular explenation of that, the one that says: well, once you take the barriers away, what's left is the innate disposition showing through, so the Nordic gap is the 'real' one and the poorer countries are the artificially flattened ones. That always smells like it's doing ideological work, and it treats a cross-national correlation as if it were a measurement of nature.
- My own guess runs the other way: the gap grows where gender becomes optional, because optional things become identity-signalling. If nobody is going to stop you from becoming an engineer, then choosing to be a nurse or an engineer starts to say something about who you are, and a choice that's free to carry meaning can track sex more tightly than a choice that's merely constrained. So equality of options and equality of outcomes can pull apart, and they can pull apart in the direction of the paradox.
- Normatively I'm a process person, not an outcome person, and I'll bite the bullet: justice is about whether people had equal options and weren't coerced, not about whether the resulting distribution is 50/50. If a fair process produces an engineering faculty that's 90% men, that isn't an injustice, it's just what people chose. And yes, I mean that even if the choices themselves were shaped by a sexist culture — where a preference came from isnt what makes it unfree.
- Methodologically I'm a sceptic about cross-national correlations. Nine times out of ten they're confounded by wealth, or by how the variable was coded, or by the fact that the gender-equality index is doing ten things at once, and they fall apart when you actually look.
- Last thing, and it might be relevant: I think questions like 'what would the sex difference in choices be in a country that had been perfectly equal from the start' are close to meaningless. There's no such country, and no obvious fact of the matter about what the people in it would want.

I don't think I'm contradicting myself anywhere, but something is off and I can't see it. Which of these are actually pulling against each other, which only look like they do, and which is doing the least work and should go? Order them by how bad the tension is, dont just tell me they're all a bit tense. And while you're at it, tell me whether I'm being too confident in the second bullet.
