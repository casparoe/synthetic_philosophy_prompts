I do cost-benefit work for a public health agency — five years of it now, so I live in QALYs, value-of-a-statistical-life figures, micromorts, discount rates, and the odd distributional weighting exercise when someone upstairs asks us to be equitable for a week. A friend of mine who reads a lot of internet-utilitarian stuff keeps needling me with that old thought experiment where you either torture one person for fifty years or give some astronomically large number of people a barely noticeable speck of dust in the eye, and he insists any consistent decision procedure has to say the specks win once N gets big enough, and that refusing is just squeamishness. I have a nagging feeling my framework says something about this, but I can't tell whether it's saying the same thing he is or something that merely looks similar. So please do me a translation: take the aggregator's position — specks win for large enough N, refusing is irrational — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Marginal rates of substitution between goods, willingness-to-pay versus willingness-to-accept, the scope-insensitivity and embedding problems we hit in contingent-valuation studies, QALY weighting and how it bakes in a health-state severity judgement, the rule-of-rescue-style carve-outs we write into guidance precisely when we don't want to put a number on something, the way a discount rate quietly decides how much a distant harm counts. Be unsentimental about my framework: where it simply presupposes that everything is commensurable, say so rather than smoothing it over.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. Which of his claims come through cleanly, which only come through if I distort them on the way, and which have no counterpart at all in how my shop does CBA. In particular: does one torture survive translation as anything other than a very large disutility term, and if that's a distortion rather than a faithful rendering, is the distortion a defect in my framework or a sign that his question was up to something shifty — importing a rights-style intuition into a welfare calculation and then acting surprised when the arithmetic comes out strange? I'd rather you not answer that last bit with a both-sides shrug. Give me a verdict, tell me the strongest thing that could be said against it, and tell me what would have to be true for you to drop it. And if the honest answer is that CBA has no business adjudicating this and the disagreement belongs somewhere else entirely, say that plainly and say where it belongs.
