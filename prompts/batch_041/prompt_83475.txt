I run a small reading group that meets in the back room of a bar twice a month — eight or nine people, none of us with a math degree, though two play poker seriously and one teaches high-school statistics — and the next session is mine to lead. The subject is the thing everyone in our betting and prediction-market circles keeps repeating: that you should stake only a fraction of your bankroll rather than all of it, and that the right way to frame the choice is to maximize the expected logarithm of your wealth instead of the expected wealth itself. I have absorbed the slogan but I have never managed to explain it to another person without it sounding like a strange taste for caution, and I suspect my group will leave thinking it is just risk aversion wearing a costume. What I want from you is not an explanation of the Kelly criterion but a plan for my thirty minutes: the order in which the pieces have to land so that "maximize the log" arrives as a discovery rather than a decree, the single example that should carry the whole session and what that example must have and must not have, the mistakes I should expect and the precise point in the sequence to head each one off, and how I will know by the end whether the idea actually took.

A few things to steer you. I want to carry the session on one concrete game and let the arithmetic do the persuading: a coin that comes up heads half the time and pays back 1.6 times whatever I stake, and tails half the time and returns only 0.5 times, with the entire bankroll in play every round and the game repeated many times. I am attached to this example because a single round has positive expected value, so the group will confidently say "take it," and yet anyone who stakes everything is nearly certain to lose nearly everything over enough rounds, which is exactly the shock I am after; tell me if this is the right carrying example or whether I should swap it. I also want to fold in a thought I keep circling and cannot state cleanly — that the logarithm is not a psychological attitude toward risk at all but simply the thing that compounds, and that the reason is that I cannot average my winnings across the parallel worlds where the coin lands differently, since I live in one world and my money multiplies along a single path — and I have heard somewhere that betting less than the full Kelly fraction is usually a response to not knowing the coin's true probability rather than to being timid, though I cannot reconstruct why. I keep seeing the name "geometric rationality" attached to a broader version of this, something about the same reasoning applying to anything that compounds over time, and I cannot tell whether that belongs in the talk or is a distraction. I would rather not open where the textbooks open, with utility functions and calculus, and I am content to leave out anything that only pays off later — the connection to information theory, the St. Petersburg paradox, the actual derivation.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
