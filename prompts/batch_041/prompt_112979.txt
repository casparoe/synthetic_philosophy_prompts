I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and I have finally written something of my own that I would like a second opinion on before I post it to a blog that perhaps forty people read. It is an essay about Dennett's four kinds of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — and the argument, in brief, is that the ladder is really two ladders fused into one. The first three rungs, I claim, are ordered by where the test happens relative to the organism: the hypothesis dies when the organism dies; the hypothesis dies when a behaviour is punished; the hypothesis dies when a simulation is discarded, so that the mistakes get made on the drawing board rather than in the flesh. The fourth rung is not further along that axis at all. Gregorian creatures are still doing what Skinnerian and Popperian creatures do; what changes is where the record of the tested hypotheses is kept. The genome was the first public store — shared, cumulative, transmitted across generations, but slow and effectively uneditable. Operant conditioning moved the record inside the individual, where it is fast and revisable but private and dies with its owner. Then words and notation moved it back out again into a medium that is public and cumulative and, for the first time, editable. On that reading the Gregorian rung is the re-externalisation of memory that the genome once provided, and the ladder is not a sequence of increasingly intelligent ways of testing hypotheses but an alternation between private and public storage. The tell, I say in the draft, is that you can be maximally Gregorian while being barely Popperian — someone faithfully following a recipe or a spreadsheet they could never have derived — and maximally Popperian while being Gregorian only in a thin sense, like a solitary mathematician who reasons well and writes nothing down.

The part I am least sure of is a fifth category I want to add to the revised picture, which I have been calling the clerical rung: creatures that inherit a procedure from the public store and execute it competently without being able to generate, evaluate, or repair the hypothesis behind it. The clerk is not a Skinnerian, because nothing is being shaped in him by reinforcement; not a Popperian, because he never runs the simulation; and not Gregorian in Dennett's sense of a mind restructured by its tools, because he is downstream of the restructuring without having undergone it. If that category is real, it is where a lot of the interesting questions about contemporary systems actually live, and it predicts something I find uncomfortable: that a culture can become more clerical while becoming less Popperian, and that this would register as progress on every measure that tracks competence rather than comprehension.

What I want from you is comments, not a rewrite, and not a reading list — I am not interested in being told which texts to consult, and I would rather you assess the argument on its own terms than explain to me what Dennett really meant. If you think I have misread him, say what you take the correct reading to be and why, but do not put anything in his mouth that you are not confident of. Specifically: (1) Is the two-axis claim a genuine distinction or a relabelling of what was already there — is there any content in the record moving that was not already in the test moving? (2) Does the clerical rung earn its place, or does it collapse into either a Skinnerian creature with cultural scaffolding or a Gregorian creature with poor introspection? (3) Where does the picture break — give me the case that fits neither axis, or the creature that is high on both while being, intuitively, less of a mind than something below it. And (4) tell me whether the essay's opening move, treating the genome as a storage medium on a par with words, is a mistake: a genome is not something the organism reads, and I am not sure how much of the argument survives if I drop it.
