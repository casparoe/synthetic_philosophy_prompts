I'm taking a seminar on naturalism and the philosophy of biology, and we have just finished Dennett's Darwin's Dangerous Idea, where he introduces the crane/skyhook contrast and then, in the same chapter, diagnoses "greedy reductionism" as an error distinct from reductionism as such. I can recite the definitions, but I don't think I have understood the conceptual issue, and I would like it explained rather than summarised.

Begin with the contrast itself. Dennett's own summary — that good reductionists suppose all design can be explained without skyhooks, while greedy reductionists suppose it can all be explained without cranes — implies that the two kinds of reductionist are distinguished not by how much they reduce but by which of the two devices they discard. If that is right, what exactly makes a proposed explanation a crane rather than a skyhook? Is the criterion structural, in the sense that the explanation draws its power from something already in place, or historical, in the sense that we can show how it came to be in place? Is a crane that nobody has yet managed to build still a crane? And does the contrast have any force against someone who does not already accept naturalism, or does "skyhook" collapse into "whatever has not yet been naturalised", in which case the framework cannot be used to argue for naturalism without begging the question? A friend in the seminar presses that last point and I cannot tell whether he is right.

Then greedy reductionism. A classmate proposes a test he calls the self-funding test: a reduction is greedy when it discharges its explanatory debt with a posit whose own origin the theory cannot account for — that is, when it skips a level it has no licence to skip. Dennett's own description, of explaining too much too fast and of skipping whole layers, is looser than that. Is the stricter test the right reconstruction, and does it over-generate or under-generate? Apply it to a case we argued about: one of us claims that a system whose behaviour can only be predicted by simulating it bottom-up is exhibiting a crane, not a skyhook — merely a great deal of honest work — while another says that "emergent" is a skyhook in a lab coat. Does the crane/skyhook criterion settle that dispute, or does it require an independent judgement about the science?

Finally, is "greedy reductionism" a substantive category or a term of abuse? What would distinguish a genuinely greedy reduction from an ambitious one that simply has not succeeded yet, given that we usually learn which it was only in hindsight — and what does the distinction let me do, concretely, that I could not already do with "good" and "bad" explanation?
