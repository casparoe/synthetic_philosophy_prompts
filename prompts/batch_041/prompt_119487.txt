I've been going down a rabbit hole lately about why old rules die, and I've got a pile of cases where my gut says different things about what actually did it, and I can't get them to fit one story. I'm not a historian or anything, this is just me on the train, so forgive me if I'm re-inventing a wheel here.

1. Dueling. My sense is the arguments against it were around for ages and did basically nothing. What killed it is that it got ridiculous, the sort of man who still dueled looked like a joke rather than a villain. So, shame, not persuasion. Although I suppose shame is an arguement of a kind, which is part of my problem.

2. Foot binding. Here I want to say reformers and the state did it, by making the case. But I've read that it went from a rich-family thing to a poor-countryside thing before it finally died, and that's exactly the pattern I just called shame in the dueling case. So if dueling was pure status, I don't see how I get to say argument did foot binding.

3. Slavery. My instinct is that people were finally convinced it was wrong. But the same arguments had been going a long time and nothing happened, and what seems to have changed is that it got expensive and dangerous to keep up, plus the people being enslaved kept revolting. I don't want to say it was just money, that feels like it takes the morality out of it, but I can't see how to credit argument without admitting the argument was there the whole time.

4. Smoking. Not exactly a moral rule, but it's my clearest case. Nobody talked anyone out of it. The better off quit first and it turned into a poor-person habit, and it's still with us. If that's the same thing that happened with dueling, then I have a very cynical picture of all of this.

5. Charging interest. I gather this was once treated as flat theft, and now only the crazy rates get called usury. Same act, new word. That's not a revolution, that's a repaint, and now I suspect some of the cases I'd call revolutions are repaints too, with the thing still going, just moved somewhere and renamed.

6. Calling cards and the old visiting rules. Died with the telephone. Nobody argued, nobody profited, the whole thing just became pointless because the world it lived in was gone.

7. The church rule in my own family. My grandmothers generation went every Sunday, my parents generation stopped, and I don't think anyone ever won that argument. The people who cared got old and died and the ones replacing them never picked it up. Doesn't feel like a moral revolution at all, feels like turnover, but the rule is just as gone.

The only principle I can come up with on my own is this: a rule dies when the people following it can no longer tell a story about it that they're willing to say out loud. But that doesn't cover the telephone one or my grandmother's church, where there wasn't really a story to lose.

So is there a principle that tells you in advance which of these is going to be the one that does the work, persuasion or self interest or shame or force or people simply dying off or the world changing so the thing stops making sense? Or is it just different for different rules and my wanting one story is the mistake? If there is a principle, I want it to earn its keep: tell me what it says about a live one, like bullfighting or hitting kids or factory farming, and be honest about which of my seven it can't save. If one of them has to go, say which and why, and don't be nice about it.
