Quick setup so we're using the same vocabulary. The naturalist line: a condition is a disease just when some part or system of the body is failing to perform its natural function -- i.e. running below the statistical norm for its reference class -- and no value judgement is smuggled in. The normativist line: a condition is a disease when it harms the person and we're warranted in treating it as a departure from health, and no dysfunction is needed. Then a hybrid that insists on both a dysfunction and harm.

I want it in the hard direction: a case where the normativist says disease but the naturalist and the hybrid both say its not. Constraints:

- Realistic -- an ordinary GP surgery or clinic, nothing sci-fi or futuristic.
- No dysfunction to hide behind. Nothing in the story is functioning below the normal range for its reference class, and name the reference class you're using so I can check you.
- The harm has to be genuine: the person is suffering, or can't do things they'd otherwise be able to do. It can't be aesthetic dislike, social stigma, or a purely statistical risk that never came off.
- Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph.

Then do the walk-through. Verdict for each of the three accounts, with the reasoning, and say plainly which single feature is doing the work. After that, change just that one feature -- put a modest, real dysfunction back into the story -- and tell me how the three verdicts move and wether the case collapses into a boring textbook one. Finish with how a defender of the naturalist would attack the case: where would they go hunting for a dysfunction, and would it be fair?

The point of the exercise is to show students that the value component of "disease" can't just be argued away, so a case that quietly smuggles in a dysfunction, or that only works because the person dislikes how they look, is cheating -- that's what I'll be checking for. No citations or further reading please, just the case and the analysis. And write it in British English.
