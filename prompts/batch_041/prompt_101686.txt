I am a high school student who has spent most of this year in my school's machine-learning club, and I have been trying to square something about the way reinforcement learning is actually done with the way my friends and I talk about it. In practice, the field treats the reward signal as a design variable and nothing more: you write a scalar function, you reshape it when the agent does something foolish, you call it a bug when the agent finds a way to accumulate reward without doing the task, and you delete a training checkpoint — an entire partially trained agent, with its learned policy and its accumulated history — without hesitation, the way you would delete a rough draft of an essay. Yet the vocabulary is soaked in the language of liking and wanting: the agent "prefers" one action to another, it "likes" the reward, we worry aloud about "wireheading," and a whole corner of the safety literature is devoted to building agents that will not resist being switched off. A friend of mine, who reads considerably more philosophy than I do, told me that if you take the reward signal to be the agent's analogue of pleasure, then deleting a checkpoint is a moral act rather than an engineering one, and that "reward hacking" is merely a technical name for a creature finding a way to feel good without doing any good. I genuinely cannot tell whether he is over-reading a metaphor or whether the field is quietly relying on a substantive position it never states.

So what I would like from you is an audit of what the ordinary practice presupposes. Take the specific habits I have listed — the scalar reward as an editable knob, reward shaping, the treatment of reward hacking as a defect, the discarding and reinitializing of training runs, and the norm that a well-behaved agent should not resist interruption — and tell me what philosophical commitments they actually require, as opposed to what they merely sit comfortably with. Does the practice need the strong claim that reward is not a valenced state at all, or only the weaker claim that whatever valenced states there are fail to attach to anything worth protecting? Does treating the reward function as something the engineer may rewrite at will presuppose that an agent's desires are exhausted by the objective it was given — a kind of behaviorism about preference — and would the practice survive if that presupposition were dropped? Is there any way to keep running things as we do while granting my friend his premise, say by holding that the functional realization of reward is sufficient for pleasure, or that valence comes in degrees and the degrees here are vanishingly small, and if so, precisely what would have to change? Finally, where the practice turns out to be underdetermined, tell me which rival accounts of welfare and of mind would each license it, so that I can see how much of the field's apparent silence is a real commitment and how much is only silence.
