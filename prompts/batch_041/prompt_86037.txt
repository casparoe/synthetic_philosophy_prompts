For some years I have been trying to get clear about a family of arguments that historians use when they are deciding whether a traditional narrative should be treated as a record of events, and I have got stuck on a particular text. The text is the prologue to a twelfth-century Latin chronicle, in which the author sets out to discredit a rival's account of King Arthur. Since I cannot reproduce the Latin, and I do not want to rest anything on a modern translator's wording, let me set out the argument as I understand it. He does not deny that the Britons had a war-leader of some kind. His complaint is that the rival's book has furnished fables with the apparatus of history: a regnal chronology, a sequence of campaigns, named kings and bishops standing as witnesses. Having grown fond of the stories, he says, his contemporaries now treat the whole edifice as a record. Alongside this he makes a second, more familiar point: the writers who came earlier and had reason to notice such events say nothing of them.

What I want help with is the relation between these two objections and the conception of evidence that each presupposes. The first seems to be an argument about how a narrative can acquire the formal marks of history — dates, succession, corroborating names — without acquiring its evidential standing, and it hints that the acquisition of those marks may itself be a sign that the material has been worked over rather than transmitted. The second is an argument from silence, which seems to demand something quite different: that the earlier writers had both the opportunity and the motive to record what happened. I can see that the first objection would still bite even if the earlier writers had no motive at all, and that this makes the two objections carry different burdens. What I cannot see is whether the first is really an argument about evidence at all, or only about genre.

I should like this worked through with the aid of a distinction I have been trying to formulate, which I shall call chronological anchoring. The thought is that when a tradition is given a date and a place in a sequence — the fall of Troy fixed to a particular year by later Greek reckoning, the exodus placed a fixed number of years before the building of the temple, Arthur's battles entered in an annal beside notices of kings and bishops — what has been added is not testimony but connection: the claim that the events in question belong to the same causal and temporal network as the events one already accepts. I want to know whether anchoring is a form of support at all, and whether it can be distinguished from mere ornament. A test I have been toying with is whether the narrative would survive the removal of its dates: if its point is preserved without them, they are decoration; if removing them destroys it, they are load-bearing.

Please work through this and tell me what follows for how I should read the Trojan War, the exodus and Arthur. I should say that I want the answer to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material, and not to present a contested reading as the consensus. Please write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way one would write a long letter — and in British English.
