Two Ways of Editing the Past, and a Third

Everyone has had the experience of being told a fact and then, a moment later, being unable to remember whether they had known it all along. I want to examine a much faster version of that problem, one that happens in a few hundred milliseconds, and ask whether the standard way of dividing up the possible explanations is really exhaustive.

The phenomenon I have in mind is the color phi effect. Two dots are flashed in quick succession at different positions on a screen, and the first is one color and the second another. Under the right timing, subjects report seeing a single dot move from the first position to the second, and they report that the dot changes color about halfway along the path. The problem is that the change in color cannot be known until the second dot has been shown, and yet subjects place the change in the middle of the movement, before the second dot appears. Whatever the brain is doing, it is doing it after the second flash but presenting it as though it happened earlier.

The philosopher Daniel Dennett distinguishes two families of explanation for this. In the first, which he calls Orwellian, the subject genuinely experiences the first dot, then the second, and only afterwards has the memory of the first experience overwritten by an edited version in which the color change appears midway. In the second, which he calls Stalinesque, the editing happens before the experience: the brain holds back the first stimulus, waits for the second, assembles a single coherent sequence, and only then presents that sequence to consciousness. The names are memorable because the first is like a government that rewrites the historical record after the fact, and the second is like a show trial whose verdict was decided before the proceedings began.

Dennett's own view, as I understand it, is that the distinction is not as deep as it looks. Both explanations agree about the timing of every physical event in the brain; they differ only about which of those events deserve the label experience. But that label only makes sense if there is a definite moment at which a representation crosses a line into consciousness, and Dennett denies that there is any such line. If he is right, then the Orwellian and Stalinesque hypotheses are not two hypotheses but two descriptions of the same process, and the choice between them is a choice about where to put a boundary that does not exist.

I want to push on this in two ways.

First, I wonder whether the distinction can be rescued by moving it away from consciousness and towards information flow. Forget the finish line. Ask instead: is the representation of the first dot's color ever available to downstream systems in an unrevised form, or is the first revision the only version that is ever written? If the former, the Orwellian label seems apt even without a theater; if the latter, the Stalinesque label seems apt. The labels would then be about the architecture of memory, not about the timing of experience. I am not sure whether this is a genuine rescue or whether I have just changed the subject.

Second, I want to propose a third possibility that I have not seen discussed, though I would not be surprised if it has been. Both Orwellian and Stalinesque explanations assume that the revision is a single event that happens at some particular time, either before or after the experience of the first dot. But there is a third option: the perceptual content might be genuinely unsettled until the subject is asked to report it, at which point the act of reporting fixes a determinate past. On this view there is no fact of the matter about what the subject experienced at the moment of the first flash, not because we cannot know it, but because it had not yet been determined. I have been calling this the deferred-settlement view. It is meant to be different from both of the others: the Orwellian view says there was a fact and it was overwritten; the Stalinesque view says there was no conscious fact until the edited version was presented; the deferred-settlement view says there was no fact at all until the report, and the report is not a report of something that was already there.

I am aware that this sounds like it might collapse into the Stalinesque view, or into a general skepticism about the timing of experience, or into a version of the idea that memory and experience are the same thing. I would like to know whether it is a genuinely distinct option or whether I have just relabeled one of the two original hypotheses. I would also like to know whether the color phi case is the right place to test it, or whether the timescales are too short for the distinction to have any bite.

Finally, I want to extend the labels to a case that is much slower. When I read a mystery novel and reach the solution, the earlier clues suddenly seem to have been pointing at the culprit all along, even though I did not notice them at the time. Is this an Orwellian revision of my memory, a Stalinesque revision of what I experienced while reading, or something else again? I suspect that stretching the labels this far makes them do work they were not designed for, but I cannot tell whether that is a problem with the labels or a problem with my use of them.
