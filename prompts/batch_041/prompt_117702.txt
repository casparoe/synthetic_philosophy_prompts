A friend of mine who works on rational choice has been walking me through one-shot commitment problems, and I would like to predict her verdict on a new one before I put it to her. Her verdicts so far: (1) a driver who runs this road every week, and who will carry me only if I promise to mail him £100 once I am home — promise, and pay; (2) a driver who had already agreed to carry me for nothing, who will never see me again, cannot retaliate, and will never learn what I did, and to whom I nonetheless promised £100 — I may keep the money; (3) a benefactor who will transfer £1,000 the moment a reliable device detects that I have genuinely resolved to give £200 to a stranger tomorrow, when tomorrow I shall have no reason whatever to give it — take the £1,000, and do not give the £200; (4) a chemist who sells a pill that would leave me, for one day, incapable of breaking a promise — buy it, if the driver whose help I need knows that I have taken it.

The new case: I am stranded, and the only driver heading for town will carry me only if he is convinced that I will pay him £100 on arrival. He reads people well, and he was convinced. He carries me; we arrive; he will never see me again, cannot retaliate, and no one else will ever learn whether I paid. Predict her verdict on whether I should pay, set out the rules that fit her four verdicts so far, say which of them you think she is running and why, and name the case that would most cleanly tell the surviving rules apart.
