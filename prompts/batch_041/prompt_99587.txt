I've been arguing with a colleague about consequentialist cluelessness — the thought that, because the causal ripples of any action extend indefinitely and chaotically, we cannot assign anything like well-grounded credences to most of what our actions bring about, so the consequentialist's ledger of total consequences is largely blank. My colleague takes this to be devastating to the whole approach. I've been defending a position I'll call tractability-restricted consequentialism: the criterion of rightness evaluates an act only by those of its consequences that fall within what I call the tractable horizon — the set of effects to which we can attach credences that aren't mere artifacts of how we happened to draw the boundaries — while effects beyond that horizon are simply not counted, not even as unknowns assigned some default value. My thought is that this is the only non-arbitrary stopping point, since any attempt to include the distant effects forces us to treat our ignorance as if it were information.

I also want to know whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by the very move that defines it, and whether that insulation is a defect or simply the honest shape of any view in this area.

Be self-critical rather than diplomatic, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely irritating. Tell me what would change your verdict: which single argument or consideration, if it held up, would make you conclude that tractability-restricted consequentialism is untenable, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
