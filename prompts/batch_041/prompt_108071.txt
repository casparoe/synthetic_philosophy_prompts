I'm building an argument about intergenerational ethics, and I keep running into the same conversation-stopper: the non-identity problem. The line I get is that because our present choices determine not merely the quality of life of future people but which people come to exist at all, no future person can be said to be worse off than they otherwise would have been, and so the entire vocabulary of harm and injustice between generations collapses. I want to test that claim against a real dispute rather than a hypothetical.

The episode is the Stern Review and the response to it. The Review argued that the economics of climate change had been distorted by the discount rates economists habitually used, that anything above a negligible rate of pure time preference is ethically indefensible, and that a low rate by itself justifies immediate and aggressive abatement. Nordhaus's reply was, in substance, that this smuggles a contestable ethical stance into what is presented as an economic calculation, and that discounting at rates closer to observed returns on capital yields a far slower optimal path. Both sides accepted the same climate science, and both reasoned in terms of aggregate welfare summed over time rather than in terms of determinate individuals faring better or worse. I am not asking you to adjudicate the numbers or to survey the literature.

Analyze that dispute through the non-identity problem, and do it in both directions.

First, the mapping: be explicit about who the affected parties are taken to be, what the counterfactual comparison is in each side's argument, and whether either argument actually requires comparing the fates of particular persons rather than states of the world. Then say whether the non-identity problem has any purchase on the substance of the disagreement about discounting, or whether it only undermines the rhetoric of harm to future generations that surrounds it.

Second, run the case back against the theory. Does this dispute suggest that the non-identity problem is narrower in scope than its usual deployment implies -- in particular, that it bites only where a policy changes who is conceived, and not where it changes the lives of people who will exist regardless? Or does it instead show that Parfit's own remedy, a shift to impersonal principles of the kind he favored, was already silently in place on both sides, so that the problem has been doing less work in climate argument than either its friends or its enemies suppose?

Third, mark the limits. Identify the parts of the Stern-Nordhaus disagreement that identity-based considerations simply cannot illuminate -- the pure rate of time preference, the treatment of catastrophic tail risks, the substitutability of produced for natural capital, the status of market rates as evidence of anything normative -- and say which framework does better with each.

Close by telling me what would change your mind: which consideration, resolved one way rather than the other, would move your verdict about whether the non-identity problem is central or peripheral to this dispute, and where in your own reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
