I edit the ideas section of a magazine and am preparing a long-form interview with a philosopher whose forthcoming book argues that the lottery paradox and the preface paradox — usually presented as twin embarrassments for any threshold account of rational belief — are not in fact parallel: she holds that the preface teaches us something about assertion while the lottery teaches us something about inference, and that running the two together is what manufactures the apparent need to surrender deductive closure. She is, I'm told, unsympathetic to the familiar reply that the preface paragraph is merely a hedge, on the grounds that the hedge reading gets the illocutionary facts backwards. What I need is eight to ten questions that give her room to state the asymmetry but spend most of their energy testing it, rather than letting her recite the two paradoxes again.

Please build the sequence so that it includes at least one question each on: (a) the exact closure principle she abandons, and why it must be the multi-premise version rather than the single-premise one; (b) her criterion of "lottery-sensitivity" — roughly, that a belief is rational only if it is insensitive to the kind of evidential symmetry a lottery ticket exhibits — and how that criterion fares in two cases I'd like her to work through on the spot, a juror who must convict on a 99.9%-reliable eyewitness and a physician who must act on a diagnosis carrying a one-in-a-million error rate; (c) the speech-act reading of the preface paragraph, and whether the closing sentence of modesty is a second-order remark about the book as a whole rather than a belief about each individual claim inside it; and (d) whether her account ends up licensing the inference from a claim of the form "almost all Fs are G" to one of the form "this F is G," which is where I suspect the real trouble lies. For every question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — if she denies closure, ask whether she can still explain our practice of reasoning from many premises at once without treating the conclusion as rationally required; if she leans on the belief/assertion distinction, press her on whether a reader who believes every sentence of the book but rejects the preface is being irrational or merely discourteous; if she concedes the statistical-syllogism worry, ask what is left of the threshold view once the threshold is made case-relative.

Close the set with two harder questions: one asking her to distinguish her position from the view that both paradoxes are artifacts of insisting on a sharp belief threshold at all, and one asking what sort of consideration — a case, a formal result, a revision of our practice — would actually move her off the asymmetry claim. Nothing about how she came to the subject, and nothing that would let her answer with the received framing of either paradox.
