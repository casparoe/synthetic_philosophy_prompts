I do a small podcast on philosophy of language and next week I've got a philosopher on whose whole thing is performatives. Her line, roughly: the interesting divide in that literature isnt performative vs constative, and it isn't conventional vs intentional either — it's between acts whose force is handed to you by an established practice (sentencing, baptising, marrying, chairing a meeting) and acts whose force you take for yourself (warning, promising, apologising, resigning). Her twist is that the second lot are parasitic on the first: you can only take a force that some practice already makes available for others to hold you to, so there's no such thing as force that comes from the speaker's intention alone. She's also got a side claim doing a lot of quiet work — that a chatbot can't promise or accept your terms of service, it can only mimic the act, because standing is conferred by practices and not by whoever wires the thing up.

Can you draft eight to ten questions for me. I don't want her just restating the distinction, so press it. At least one question each on:

- the parasitism claim vs. plain promises between friends, where nobody authorised anyone — and if she says the practice of holding each other to our word is enough, follow up by asking what then separates a practice from a moral norm, since that's the old fight about whether force is conventional all the way down;
- the bot and the delegation case: the company that treats the chatbot's output as binding looks like it's conferring the standing by holding itself to it, so why is the bot a pen the company writes with rather than a party to the contract? push on what actually distinguishes them, and what she'd say about the de facto usurper whose orders everyone obeys and no one ever authorised;
- uptake: is the act done on utterance or on being recognised? what about an apology the other person refuses to hear, or a resignation the employer ignores and keeps scheduling you for — which is the case I suspect her framework is most awkward about.

Somewhere in there get one on whether standing is all or nothing or comes in degrees (the lifeguard yelling at you vs a stranger yelling at you), and one on the promise made with no intention of keeping it — misfire, or just a hollow act. Follow-ups keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, please. And end with a question about what would have to be true for her to give up the standing requirement entirely.
