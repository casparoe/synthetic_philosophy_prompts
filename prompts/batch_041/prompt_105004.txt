My father is eighty-one and has lived with severe, treatment-resistant depression since his thirties. Two years ago he enrolled in a trial of an implanted device that regulates mood by stimulating a small region deep in the brain; the clinicians describe it, half-jokingly, as a pacemaker for feeling. When he signed the consent paperwork he added a clause in his own hand: if he ever asked to have the device switched off, we should not take the request at face value, because the asking would be the illness talking. He also has a handheld controller for adjusting the intensity, and over these eight months he has steadily turned it up. By every clinical measure he is no longer depressed. He is calm, and he tells me he is content — more steadily content than I have ever known him. But the man who used to spend whole evenings arguing about politics, who kept a notebook of enthusiasms and grudges, now wants none of it and says he does not miss it. When I ask whether he would ever want the device off, he says the question makes no sense, because 'off' is the person who wanted to die. My sister says this is simply what recovery looks like. My uncle says we have replaced our father with a well-regulated appliance. What I cannot get past is that the person assuring me he is happy is the person the device made, and that the clause he wrote was meant to bind exactly that person. Should I be pushing for it to be removed? And if not, whose judgment am I deferring to — his, or the thing we put in him? I would like your read on this, written as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
