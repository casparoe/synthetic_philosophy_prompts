Ok, my friend hit me with this arguement about judical review and I can't shake it, because it looks like it proves way more than he wants it to. Here's his version, cleaned up.

(1) A rule has authority over me only if I'm still, in some sense, its author — if there's no way for me to decide it away, then it's ruling me rather than me ruling.
(2) The constitution was ratified by people who are now dead, and I never agreed to it.
(3) Striking down a statute on the basis of that old text is the dead deciding for the living, through judges I never elected.
(4) So judicial review — unelected judges overruling the legislature — is illegitimate.
(5) But every statute was passed by people who are now dead too, or by people I never voted for, and I didn't agree to any of them either.
(6) So no statute has authority over me.
(7) But statutes obviously do have authority over me — I'm not living in a state of nature.
(8) So one of (1) through (6) has to go.

I'd rather keep (7), and honestly I'd like to keep (4) as well, since I really do think there's something off about unelected judges overruling elected ones. Two friends have tried to help and I can't tell whether either of them is right. One says the difference is entrenchment: I can get a statute repealed by a normal majority but I can't get a constitutional clause repealed, so in one case the living majority really does rule itself and in the other it doesn't. The other says the constitution is the people acting at a higher level, so it isn't the dead ruling me, it's me ruling me through my earlier self. I don't fully buy either of those, and the first one seems to turn constitutional rights into ordinary statutes with better glue.

Where does this actually break, and what does the fix cost me? Start your reply with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
