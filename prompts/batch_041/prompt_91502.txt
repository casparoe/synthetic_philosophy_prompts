I'm building a week of a grad seminar on interpretation — comp lit and philosophy students, and the running fight is going to be whether the meaning of a text is fixed by what its author meant, by what an informed contemporary reader would have taken it to mean, or by neither of those (the text plus whatever a community does with it). I want to open with Kafka, but not with the question everyone asks about him, which is basically an ethics-of-estates question. What I'm after is the structure underneath it, so let me set the facts we'll all work from: Kafka died in 1924 leaving three unfinished novels, a pile of stories, diaries and letters, and a note designating Max Brod his literary executor and instructing him to burn the lot. Brod refused, edited and published the novels, and that's the only reason any of us read The Trial or talk about a Kafkaesque anything. Use that as a test case and run it in both directions.

Take the actual-intentionalist line (meaning just is what the author meant, so Kafka's intentions are the thing to recover), the hypothetical or reader-side line (meaning is what a suitably informed reader would take the text to mean, so the intentions drop out and Brod's disobedience is irrelevant to meaning), and the anti-intentionalist who thinks the author's intentions are a fallacy to begin with and the text is on its own the moment it's public. Say what each is committed to here and what verdict each gives on the case. Then take seriously the twist I keep coming back to: the instruction is itself a text, and we don't have an unmediated intention, we have a scrap of paper that Brod preserved and reported. An instruction to destroy everything can be read as a settled judgment, as self-lacerating theatre, as the move a writer makes knowing his friend won't comply — so the intentionalist about the novels is forced to become an interpreter of a note, and the regress doesn't obviously bottom out anywhere. Does that kill the position, or is it just the ordinary predicament of interpretation, with the intentionalist never having promised certainty in the first place?

The part I most want, because I think the standard debate fudges it: did Brod's disobedience change what the works mean, or only whether we can read them? My own hunch, and tell me if it's confused, is that a novel that exists as an unfinished posthumous draft isn't the same object as the novel it would have been had Kafka finished and published it, and that our knowing these were pulled out of the fire is now part of what they are — in which case what the author meant and what the text means may be the wrong pair of options to start from. Keep separate, at least at the start, the question of whether Brod did right by Kafka (a promise, an estate, a dead friend's wishes) from the question of what the texts mean, then tell me whether that separation can actually be held and what work it's doing. And where a position just has nothing to say about this case, say so instead of patching it. Skip the Pierre Menard bit, I know, everyone knows, that's week four.
