For about two years a colleague and I have been replaying the same argument about futarchy, and I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is rather than a verdict on the proposal. The arrangement, as I understand it, is this: an electorate or its representatives constitutionally fix an after-the-fact measure of national welfare, and policy questions are then settled by conditional prediction markets — markets that price claims of the form, if policy P is adopted, measured welfare in year D will be at least X — where the conditional bets are voided if the antecedent never occurs, and where a policy becomes law when the market's estimate clearly favours it. We agree on the mechanics, on the fact that real markets are thin, manipulable, and slow, and on the observation that the proposal has never been tried. Neither of us has moved in two years.

His position: every objection is an engineering problem. That the welfare measure is somewhat arbitrary is answered by treating it as a constitutional choice, like a currency or an electoral rule; a bad measure makes a bad constitution, not an incoherent mechanism. Manipulation is a cost that scales with stake size and can be priced; illiquidity can be subsidised. The mechanism's virtue is that it separates two things democratic politics conflates — what we want, fixed by votes, and what will happen, fixed by bets — and nothing in that separation is confused.

My position: the mechanism cannot settle the questions it is advertised to settle. Whatever measure is fixed, someone had to choose it, and the choice among measures is precisely the kind of value dispute the mechanism was meant to remove; the market can only tell you how to pursue a goal once the goal is given, so the disagreement is displaced one level up rather than resolved. Worse, the conditional markets that matter most are the ones whose antecedent never obtains: if the market's estimate is what causes a policy to be adopted, the branch in which it is not adopted is never realised, so the market's accuracy is unverifiable exactly where it does the deciding. And a market in whether welfare will reach X presupposes that welfare is a quantity settleable by a date, which is a substantive moral claim rather than a measurement convention.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this is not really a disagreement about prediction markets but about what an institution is being asked to do — he assesses the proposal as an ideal mechanism and treats deviations as costs to be minimised, while I assess it as something that must be robust under the conditions that generate value disagreement in the first place, and those two standards never meet. If that is right, no evidence about market accuracy will move the dispute.

I can see three rival readings and want them tested against what each of us actually says. First, that it is verbal: we may mean different things by aggregating preferences — he means pooling beliefs about consequences given fixed values, I mean pooling the values themselves — and so may be answering different questions under one name. Second, that a hidden formal question is doing the work: whether any design of conditional markets is well defined when the market's own output selects the policy looks like a question in mechanism design with a determinate answer, not a clash of intuitions. Third, that it is simply empirical, about whether such markets beat the alternatives, in which case we are both speculating.

Diagnose it. Say which reading the positions as stated actually support, whether my one-level-up hypothesis survives, and whether the distinction I have been running together — a market whose price is decisive versus one whose price is merely advisory — changes the diagnosis, since I suspect he has the advisory design in mind and I have the decisive one. Then tell me what would count as progress: a formal result, a distinction, a pilot, or an argument that none of these should be expected and why.
