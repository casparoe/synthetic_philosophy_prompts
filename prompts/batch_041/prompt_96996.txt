I'm putting together my position on dispositions for a seminar and would like a consistency audit before I commit it to paper. Here is where I have landed.

(i) Dispositions are real and irreducible. Fragility and solubility are genuine properties of their bearers, not abbreviations for patterns of behaviour or for claims about what would happen in this or that circumstance.

(ii) A disposition is individuated by its characteristic stimulus and its characteristic manifestation. To be fragile is to be disposed to break when struck; to be soluble is to be disposed to dissolve when immersed. Two dispositions differ only if their stimulus-manifestation pairs differ.

(iii) Dispositions are intrinsic to their bearers. Whether a particular glass is fragile is settled by the glass itself, by its constitution and microstructure, and by nothing about its surroundings.

(iv) Dispositions are causally efficacious. When a struck glass breaks, its fragility is among the causes of the breaking, and citing the fragility explains the breaking; dispositions are not idle bookkeeping introduced to save a theory of properties.

(v) No conditional analysis of disposition ascriptions can be correct, that is, being fragile cannot be identified with being such that one would break if struck. Finkish cases, in which a spell withdraws the fragility at the very instant of the blow, and masking cases, in which an intact fragility has its manifestation blocked by a coating, an antidote, or a gag, show both that a thing can have a disposition without manifesting it and that the manifestation can occur without the disposition.

(vi) A disposition can be possessed and never manifested. An unexercised disposition is a real property of its bearer, not a mere potentiality awaiting actualisation.

(vii) Verdict on a case: a glass wrapped in a shock-absorbing film that will keep it intact under any blow is still fragile. The film masks the fragility rather than removing it; strip the film away and the glass breaks as readily as any other, and the film is no part of the glass.

There is one further case I keep turning over, and I cannot tell whether my answer to it is dictated by the commitments above or is independent of them. Call it the delayed mask. A glass is fitted with a coating that neutralises the first blow it receives, but the coating is destroyed in doing so, so that a second, otherwise harmless tap brings the glass down. The glass therefore never breaks when struck, yet always breaks shortly after having been struck. I do not know whether to describe this as masking, with the fragility intact throughout, or as the fragility manifesting late, and I do not know what either description implies for the causal claim in (iv).

I would like an audit rather than a defence. Which of (i) through (vii) pull against which, ordered by severity, that is, by how much would have to be rewritten to reconcile them; which pairs only appear to conflict and dissolve once the terms are made precise; and for each genuine tension, what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the position. Where a commitment is underdetermined, say so rather than resolving it on my behalf. Finish by naming the single commitment that is doing the most damage and describing what the position would look like without it.
