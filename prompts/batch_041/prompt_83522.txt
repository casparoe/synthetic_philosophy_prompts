Been chewing on this one for a while. Adam Smith gets memed as the free-trade guy, but the part I actually find alive is his take on colonies — that the empire was a bad deal for Britain itself, that the colonial trade monopoly enriched a narrow slice of merchants while everyone else footed the bill for the navy and the administration. Ok.

He died in 1790, i.e. before basically everything about the modern version of this exists. So time-travel him forward and make him commit. Specifically I want his read on a proposal I keep seeing floated: that colonial reparations should be structured as a permanent equity stake — former colonies, or their citizens via sovereign funds, holding a real share of the former metropole's economy — rather than a one-off cash payment. Would Smith read that as restitution, as the old monopoly profits finally being unwound, or as a new mercantile racket in a nicer outfit? And does his own framework even let him answer that, or does he have to smuggle in a theory of historical entitlement he never bothered to build?

Then two more, more briefly: what would he make of the modern 'actually the empire was a net good' line, and which bits of his system cut against it versus which bits would embarrass him; and would he read the EU / modern trade blocs as a voluntary replay of the mercantile system or as the thing he was actually gunning for. For each, give me his likely position, the best argument against it, and where his 18th-c priors just fail to grip the case. Commit to answers, don't hedge into 'he'd definately find it complicated.'
