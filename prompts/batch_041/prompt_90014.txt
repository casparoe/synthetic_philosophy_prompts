I'm working through a unit on weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting, and I've hit a puzzle I can't resolve on my own. The unit gives the behavioral picture — a discount function that is steeper in the near term than in the far term, so that a person's ranking of two dated options reverses as the earlier one comes due — and sets it beside the older Aristotelian picture, on which the akratic agent's judgment about what is best has not shifted at all and the action simply fails to answer to it. What puzzles me is that our institutions appear to be built on both pictures at once, and I want to know what they actually presuppose. Consider the arrangements in which the cooler decision is made to win over the hotter one, and notice that the cooler one is sometimes the earlier and sometimes the later. A commitment savings product that pays no interest, or forfeits the interest, if you withdraw before a stated date lets the earlier self tie the hands of the later one; so does a voluntary self-exclusion list, by which a gambler arranges in advance that a casino or an online platform must refuse his custom. A cooling-off period, during which a consumer may rescind a contract signed at his own kitchen table, or a waiting period between the purchase and the delivery of a handgun, runs the other way: there the later self is allowed to undo what the earlier one did. In each case, though, one standpoint is treated as the one that ought to prevail, and it is the standpoint that is not under pressure.

I'd like you to reconstruct the theory of the will and of the self over time that this pattern presupposes. Does it require a compound picture, with a planning self whose preferences are authoritative and a succession of experiencing selves whose preferences are not? Or does it need only the weaker claim that decisions taken under certain conditions — pressure, passion, the heat of the moment — are defective as expressions of agency, leaving the unity of the person intact? Which rival views does the pattern quietly rule out? In particular, say whether it can be reconciled with a view on which no temporal standpoint is privileged and each time-slice's preference counts as one among others, and with a view on which there is no divided self at all, so that cooling-off periods must be justified by fraud, duress, or plain error rather than by divided agency. I am also suspicious of a specific inconsistency, and this is the part I most want: commitment products and self-exclusion lists seem to make sense only for an agent who already knows he is present-biased and takes steps to outwit himself — a sophisticated agent — while cooling-off periods and waiting periods seem to make sense only for an agent who does not anticipate his own reversal and cannot be trusted in the moment. If that is right, one and the same body of practice presupposes both a sophisticated and a naive agent. Say so plainly if so, and say which half of the practice you would give up.

Then the boundaries. The same legal order will not enforce a contract of self-enslavement, will not treat a signed consent to serious bodily harm as a defense to a criminal charge, does not permit the sale of a kidney in most jurisdictions, and does not enforce an agreement to remain married; and an ordinary commercial contract gets no cooling-off period, so that regret the next morning is simply the signer's problem. Tell me what those refusals presuppose — whether they require a substantive account of the interests a person cannot alienate, and whether that account is consistent with the account of agency you reconstructed from the enforceable cases. Throughout, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, so that I can see how much of the pattern a rival theory of akrasia could equally accommodate. I don't need citations or a survey of the literature; I want the reconstruction and your confidence in it. Tell me where the reconstruction is most likely to fail and what would change your mind — which fact about how these rules are actually justified, or which argument, would lead you to a different verdict.
