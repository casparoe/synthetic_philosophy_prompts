I have a puzzle I've been chewing on for a while, and the only way I can get it clear in my head is to see it as a story, so I'd like you to write that story.

Picture a small, isolated settlement, founded a few generations ago by people who had grown tired of moral talk that seemed to describe nothing. They didn't abandon moral words — they kept base, honorable, shameful, and the rest — but they retaught them so that saying such a thing is doing something rather than reporting something: closer to a hiss, a cheer, or a refusal to shake a hand than to a statement that could be true or false. Children are raised on this. To call a deed shameful is to take a stand, not to name a fact, and there is no fact for it to match.

The trouble I want the story to turn on is this. Those same words still turn up inside if-then sentences and under not. A mother tells her son that if stealing is shameful, then taking the coins from his grandmother's purse is shameful; the boy will happily agree that stealing is shameful — he means it, he'll say it — and then take the coins anyway, insisting that nothing follows, because his agreeing was only a cheer, and cheers don't add up to anything. The elders discover they can no longer hold anyone to their own words in a dispute.

Tell it from inside the settlement, through the eyes of someone ordinary — a schoolteacher, say, or the boy's older sister — who half-sees the problem and can't quite name it. Let the story dramatize the difficulty rather than explain it, and don't let anyone resolve it with a tidy lecture.
