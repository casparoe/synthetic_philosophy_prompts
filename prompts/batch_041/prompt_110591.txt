Consider three data-governance regimes and ask what philosophical commitments each presupposes. In Germany, the 1983 census judgment of the Federal Constitutional Court derived a right of informational self-determination from the general right of personality and human dignity, reasoning that if citizens cannot know who knows what about them, they will avoid unusual behavior and refrain from exercising their rights, thereby damaging democratic self-rule; the same legal order treats employee consent to data processing as suspect because of the dependency inherent in employment (BDSG §26(2)), and gives works councils co-determination over the introduction and use of technical devices designed to monitor employee behavior or performance (BetrVG §87(1) No. 6). In the United States, constitutional privacy protection has often turned on reasonable expectations of privacy, and information voluntarily conveyed to third parties—bank records, dialed numbers—has frequently been held outside Fourth Amendment protection, while data protection for consumers is largely sectoral and notice-and-consent based; open-government law, by contrast, pushes the state toward transparency. In China, the social credit system is not a single unified score but a fragmented assemblage of credit reporting, blacklists and redlists, and joint rewards and punishments, administered by different agencies and often focused on financial credit and court judgment debtors, with the stated aim of enhancing trust in society. What theory of the person, the state, and the moral status of opacity does each regime presuppose? Which commitments are strictly required by the practices and which are merely compatible with them? For example, does the German model presuppose a deontological right to control personal information, or is it better explained by a republican concern with domination and democratic participation? Does the American approach presuppose that privacy is alienable and that market or contractual consent can legitimate surveillance, or does it rest on a distinctively adversarial view of state power? Does the Chinese system presuppose that trustworthiness is a measurable administrative property and that social trust is a public good to be engineered, or is it continuous with older legalist and Confucian ideas about state-administered reward and punishment? Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view in any one regime with one of its rivals—say, contextual integrity, privacy as secrecy, privacy as a public good, or privacy as a constitutive condition of democratic self-rule—what concrete legal or institutional changes would follow? Be specific about which parts of the practice would have to change and which could remain.
