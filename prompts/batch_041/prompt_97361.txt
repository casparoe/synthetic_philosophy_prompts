I have recently been appointed to the drafting committee for the charter of a new international consortium that will develop and deploy advanced AI systems. The committee is divided over how to bind future members. One faction wants a strong precommitment: the charter would prohibit any member from deploying a model trained above a specified compute threshold without a supermajority vote of the assembly. To prevent the rule from becoming a dead hand, another faction has proposed what we have been calling a repeal auction. Under this proposal, any future assembly could lift the prohibition by paying into a compensation fund an amount equal to an independent estimate of the deployment's expected commercial value; the fund would be distributed to parties who can show that the deployment harmed them. The auction formula itself would be entrenched more deeply than the prohibition: changing the formula would require a two-thirds majority, while lifting the prohibition would require only the payment. A third faction argues that any such device is illegitimate because it lets one generation bind another, and that the charter should instead include a periodic sunset clause forcing reconsideration every ten years.

I have been reading James Madison's constitutional thought, and I keep wondering what he would make of this. He is often described as the architect of a constitution designed to make change difficult but not impossible, and as someone preoccupied with the dangers of faction and the need for institutional checks. If Madison were advising our committee today, how would he assess the repeal auction as a precommitment device? Would he see it as a legitimate way to solve the problem of future defection, or as a clever evasion of the deeper question of who has the authority to bind whom? Would he prefer the sunset clause, or would he think both proposals miss something about the nature of constitutional time? I am not asking for a bibliography; I want a reasoned reconstruction of Madison's likely position, with attention to the distinction between a precommitment that preserves future agency and one that merely postpones its exercise.
