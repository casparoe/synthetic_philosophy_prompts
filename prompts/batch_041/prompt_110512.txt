Last night I got into it with my friend Dana about whether Ulysses contracts make any sense at all, and we went at it for like two hours and neither of us budged. Her line was that tying yourself to the mast is either fake or it's just coercion you're doing to yourself. If the rope actually holds, then the guy lashed to the mast at 3am screaming to be let loose is being overruled by a guy who doesn't exist anymore, and the whole thing only looks rational because we happen to prefer the guy who tied the knot to the guy who's thrashing around. If the rope doesn't hold -- if there's always some escape hatch, some well I could always cut it if I really wanted -- then it's not a commitment, it's a mood, and you're back to square one. I said fine, so what about locked rehab, automatic payroll deductions into savings, the friend who holds your money and burns it if you smoke, or someone with bipolar disorder signing a document that lets them be treated against their will during a future episode even though that future them will be insisting they're fine? She said all of that is either fake or self-assault, and that we only call it rational when the ending is the one the sober guy wanted, which is circular. I said the entire point of a constitution is that you dont get to relitigate it every Tuesday, and she said that's exactly the dead hand of the past ruling the living, which is also why she's against supermajority requirements.

Anyway I want to settle this properly, and I think before either of us picks a side we need to know what a theory of this stuff would even have to do. So write me the desiderata. What does any adequate account of precommitment -- of why it can be rational to restrict your own future options -- have to deliver? Which cases has it got to get right (and say what each case is, dont just name it -- the mast, the advance directive, the money that's offered tomorrow for genuinely intending today to do something you'll hate tomorrow, with no penalty if you back out, the constitution nobody can amend), which distinctions can it not collapse (between binding yourself against a future mistake and binding yourself against a future self you're simply going to disagree with; between a commitment that changes what you'll do and one that changes what other people will predict you'll do), what must it explain rather than explain away (like the weird fact that people actively seek out being bound -- deadlines, gym contracts, vows, marriages -- when giving up options is normally supposed to be a pure cost), and which results from decision theory or from the metaphysics of personal identity is it not allowed to contradict? For each requirement, say what's behind it. Then separate the ones that are just preferences of one camp from the ones that aren't negotiable, and -- this is the part I actually care about -- whether some of these requirements can't all be satisfied at once. I have a suspicion that the later self is the same agent so his preferences count the same and the earlier self's preferences can legitimately override his can't both be true, but I don't know how to make that precise, so if you can nail down the actual incompatibility that's the most useful thing you could hand me. Also tell me which single requirement does the most damage -- which one, if you had to keep only one, eliminates the most otherwise-plausible theories.

One more thing, and it's the bit that's really bugging me: does the list survive being scaled up? Ulysses is one guy over one afternoon; a constitution is a whole polity over generations, and the people bound are literally different people, not just the same person later. If your requirements only work for the single-agent case, say so, and say which ones break when the bound party is a stranger who wasn't born when the knot was tied. And a puzzle I thought of in the shower that might be a reductio or might just be a bullet: if it's fine for earlier selves to bind later ones, then the last self of your life is fair game for everyone before him, since he's got nobody after him to bind on his behalf -- a pure precommitment theory seems to license running the final you into the ground. Tell me if that kills anything on the list. And definately dont give me a reading list, I wont read it.
