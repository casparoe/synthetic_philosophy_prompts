I am writing a novel whose protagonist is a philosopher of perception — a composite figure, not a portrait of anyone living — and much of the book's middle section turns on her rulings in a series of perceptual puzzles. I have already committed her to five of them, and I need to keep her consistent when I write the sixth, since a reader who knows the literature will notice if her working theory quietly shifts between chapters. The domain is cognitive penetration: whether, and in what sense, a person's beliefs, desires, or expectations can reach down into what she perceives.

Her rulings so far. The fruit: a subject who has been told that a gray, banana-shaped patch in a photograph is a banana reports it as yellow, though the patch itself is gray. My philosopher says the subject's belief about the object has reached into her experience — penetration. The dubbed film: a subject watching a film whose soundtrack does not match the speaker's lips hears the syllable that matches the lips rather than the one on the track. She says this is not penetration; the effect is internal to perception, one sense settling another, with no belief or desire doing the work. The arrow-tailed lines: equal-length lines with inward and outward fins look unequal. Not penetration; nothing cognitive is involved. The hillside: a walker who has been told the slope is unusually steep, and who expects a hard climb, sees the hill as steeper than a fresh walker does. Penetration. The threshold: a subject led to expect a particular color shows a measurably lower detection threshold for that color at an early stage of visual processing; she can discriminate the color more finely than before, but reports that nothing looks any different. My philosopher says this is not penetration, because nothing in the experience itself has changed.

Now the sixth case, which I have not yet written her ruling on. The searchlight: a subject believes a particular pedestrian may step into the road. The pedestrian is not seen as nearer, larger, brighter, or otherwise different in any property the subject can name; the scene represents exactly what it represented a moment before. Yet the subject reports that the pedestrian now stands out against the background — an alteration she can describe only by saying that the experience itself is different, more insistent, though she cannot say what it is now like that it was not like before.

What I want from you: predict her ruling on the searchlight case; set out the candidate rules her five earlier rulings are jointly consistent with, since I suspect more than one rule fits them all; say what each candidate predicts about the searchlight; give me your best guess at the rule she is actually running and why; and name the further case — the scene I should write next — that would most cleanly separate the rules still standing. Throughout, keep separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not hand me a contested position dressed up as the consensus. If her earlier rulings leave the matter genuinely underdetermined, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a verdict for her.
