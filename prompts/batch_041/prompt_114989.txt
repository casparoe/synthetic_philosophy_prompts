(a) Explain the interactive/indifferent distinction and what the looping effect is supposed to capture. (b) Explain how the possibility of looping bears on the claim that psychiatric classifications pick out real kinds rather than social conventions. (c) Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis is usually presented as steering between the view that mental disorder is a social construction and the view that it's a straightforwardly biological matter. "Hacking's main distinction is between kinds that are indifferent to how they're classified and kinds that aren't. Quarks are indifferent: calling something a quark doesn't change anything about it. People, or at least some categories of people, are interactive: when we classify them, they can come to understand themselves in terms of the classification, and that changes how they act, which changes what the category contains. That's the looping effect. I think Hacking's point here is basically the self-fulfilling prophecy from sociology — we slap a label on a group, the group starts living up to the label, and the label looks confirmed. What makes the psychiatric case interesting is that the DSM keeps getting revised, which is exactly what you'd expect if the categories are chasing their own effects. This matters for whether psychiatric categories are real kinds. If a category is interactive, then the population it picks out is a moving target, so it isn't stable enough to support the inductive generalizations we expect from a natural kind. So Hacking's argument gives us a reason to think DSM categories aren't real kinds at all, which supports the anti-psychiatry line that they're social conventions dressed up in medical language. Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis is meant to split the difference. On his view a mental disorder has two components, harm and dysfunction. The harm component is the objective, scientific part — a failure of some internal mechanism to perform the function it was naturally selected to perform. The dysfunction component is the value-laden part, since what counts as harmful depends on social norms. So the analysis says there's a factual core (harm) and a normative overlay (dysfunction), which is why the concept can't be reduced to either biology or social construction. Looping effects don't really threaten this, because Wakefield's analysis is about the concept of disorder rather than about particular diagnostic categories, and conceptual analysis sits at a different level from empirical claims about how categories change over time."
