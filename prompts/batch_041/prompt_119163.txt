Two researchers I interviewed for a piece on how AI labs justify pausing a model spent most of the hour arguing past each other, and I came away unable to say who was right about what. Call them R and S.

R: A lab that holds back a model for safety reasons makes the world worse on its own terms. The capability is coming either way; if this lab doesn't ship it, a less careful lab does, and the only durable effect is that the cautious lab has spent the influence it would have needed to steer anything.

S: That treats the frontier as a prize one lab wins outright. Capability diffuses — weights leak, researchers move, methods get published and distilled — so a lead is worth months, not a monopoly, and racing buys you almost nothing you couldn't have gotten by waiting. What does persist is practice: which evaluations get run, what gets published, what a lab will say out loud. A pause is cheap in capability terms and expensive to reverse in norms.

R: Leads don't have to be long to be decisive; they decide who sits at the frontier at the moment the capability lands, and that is exactly when the consequential choices — deployment, safeguards, oversight — get made. And the claim that safety work is usually capability-positive is a selection effect: we notice the safety work that happens to help. At the margin, safety costs something.

S: If the cost is real but symmetric — every serious lab pays it — the competitive constraint doesn't bite, because relative position is unchanged. It bites only against a defector, which makes it a coordination problem. The answer to a coordination problem is to build the coordination, not to defect first and call it realism.

R: Symmetric in form, asymmetric in effect. The labs willing to pay the tax are exactly the ones that would otherwise be at the frontier, so the tax doesn't stay put; it relocates the frontier to whoever declines to pay. And coordination with no enforcer is a wish. Until it exists, the unilateral pauser is simply the one who hands over.

Please adjudicate this turn by turn: for each specific claim, say whether it holds up, is true but overstated, or is wrong, and name what each side is quietly assuming. Then tell me what both are missing. And settle the thing I couldn't get them to settle themselves: they kept swapping the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax as though those were one argument. Are they? Does either one actually entail the other, or can you have one without the other?
