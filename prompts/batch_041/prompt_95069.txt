I have no academic background in history or statistics; I read popular books about forecasting and probability and then argue about them with a friend who works as an actuary, and one of our recurring fights is about the outside view. As I understand it, the recipe is to set your own case aside, find a class of comparable past cases, and let the distribution of their outcomes set your forecast, precisely so that the particulars of your situation cannot flatter you. My friend's objection, which I have come to share, is that the recipe conceals its hardest step: nothing belongs to exactly one class, and a state, a reign, or an empire can be sorted into any number of defensible ones. Whichever class you name supplies the base rate, and it also decides whether the case in front of you counts as a success, a failure, or not yet finished at all, since a lifespan can only be averaged over things that have already ended. I would like a joke built on this, and I would rather it be a good one than a short one.

Write me a short piece of comic dialogue, roughly six to twelve lines, set in the court, chancery, or war room of a long-lived empire, in which someone is asked how much longer the empire has and the humour comes out of the machinery of the outside view rather than out of anybody being a fool. The punchline should turn on something real: either that the answer is entirely fixed by the choice of reference class, or that the base rate can only be computed from cases that have already ended, so the sample silently excludes every empire still standing. Please keep it away from the tired gag that historians merely predict the past, and away from the equally tired one that every doomed regime believes itself exempt; and do not make the forecaster a cynic, because the joke only works if the reasoning is correct. If it helps, the scene can be a consultancy retained by the palace, with a junior analyst doing the arithmetic and someone senior delivering the news, though that is only a suggestion.

After the dialogue, in two or three sentences, tell me which part of the machinery the punchline depends on, and what a reader who has heard the phrase outside view but never thought about reference classes would fail to notice.
