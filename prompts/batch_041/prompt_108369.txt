I run the care side of a small home-care agency — about sixty clients, mostly older folks, some with dementia — and I need a written decision procedure my coordinators can actually use. We just changed how we work: alot of the daily checking-in now happens through a tablet app and a couple of cheap sensors (motion, door), and because of staffing we rotate aides instead of keeping the same person on a client. It's saving us, but it's thrown up a bunch of calls my coordinators have to make fast, usually on the phone, usually with a family member already upset: do we override what a client says they want (the shower refusal, the walker they won't use); do we keep pushing to hold someone at home or start steering the family toward a facility; what do we do when the daughter wants one thing and the client seems to want another; and when is a day where the only contact was the app check-in "good enough" care. I've been reading up on the ethics of care — the relational view, the four phases of caring, the whole thing being partly a protest against turning ethics into a checklist — and I'd rather ground this in that than in the usual four-principles bioethics stuff. But my coordinators have no philosophy training and maybe ten minutes per call, and I'm the one who signs off when it goes wrong.

So give me the procedure: the questions in order, what a good-enough answer looks like at each step, where it stops and says no, where it hands the call to a person because the disagreement is about values and not facts, and what the procedure can't do. One thing I keep tripping over and want you to settle as part of this: one version of care ethics — caring as a relation between two particular people, which can't be turned into rules — seems to make my whole project impossible, while the other version, the one with the phases and the elements, looks like it was built for exactly this. Tell me which one the procedure you write is actually doing, and what I lose by picking it.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a woman with early dementia who's refused her shower four visits in a row and whose daughter is threatening to pull her out; (2) a client whose only care that day was the app check-in because we couldn't staff anyone; (3) an aide who's been with the same client for three years and has started texting her on her own time. For at least one of these, give me the answer I won't like.

Last thing, and I want it straight: would someone who takes care ethics seriously say that what we're doing with the app isn't care at all? Because if they would, I need to know that before I sell this to my board. American English, and skip the citations — I'll look those up myself if I need them.
