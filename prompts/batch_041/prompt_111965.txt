I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of markets, and I want my students to work through the no-trade theorem in an unfamiliar setting rather than merely recite it. Consider, then, a market of the following unusual kind. Its participants are all instances of a single artificial-intelligence system, fine-tuned on one and the same corpus and therefore sharing a common prior over the propositions that are traded; each instance is subsequently furnished, privately, with a distinct piece of evidence bearing on those propositions. Anonymity is preserved, every participant is known to be rational, the information-acquisition structure is common knowledge, and the market clears. There are no noise traders and no irrational participants.

Assuming that the no-trade result — in the tradition running from Aumann's theorem on agreeing to disagree to the Milgrom–Stokey theorem — applies here, please set out its most important implications, above all for the epistemology of markets. What does the absence of trade imply about the evidential standing of the posted prices, and about whether such a market can aggregate the participants' private information into those prices? What would have to be false of a market for any trade to occur at all, and what does that reveal about the conditions under which markets may be said to track the truth? And what follows for the rationality of persistent disagreement between agents who share a prior — in particular, for how disagreement itself should be read as evidence?
