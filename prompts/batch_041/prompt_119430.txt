Me and a friend have been going at this for weeks and I want you to build the strongest possible case for her side, not mine. Her claim is that if you actually had to pick a bargaining solution to run a real negotiation -- wage talks, two flatmates dividing a flat, a coalition carving up ministries -- you should use the Kalai-Smorodinsky one, and that Nash's is a textbook answer that only works in the world where you already know everything.

Quick gloss so we're on the same page. Both solutions take a disagreement point (what each side gets if talks collapse) and a feasible set of possible deals. Nash picks the deal that maximises the product of the two sides' gains over disagreement. Kalai-Smorodinsky doesn't look at the shape of the feasible set at all: you find each side's best possible payoff, draw a straight line from the disagreement point to that pair of best payoffs, and take the point where the line leaves the feasible set -- equivalently, the deal where both sides get the same fraction of their own potential gain. Her argument is that this is an informational advantage. Nash needs the whole feasible set, which nobody in a real negotiation actually knows, whereas K-S needs only two things people can genuinely tell you: what walking away costs, and what each side's best case is. She also likes that if I get better options at every level against you, I can't end up worse off under K-S -- which isn't true under Nash.

So make her case properly, then tell me the price honestly. My objection is that the best case is exactly the number people inflate, and K-S has no way of checking it, so the solution hands more to whoever can most credibly overstate what they could get on their own, and the other side just eats the loss. Is that decisive, or is K-S merely honest about something Nash hides inside the shape of the set? And how much should I care that a deal can look fine to K-S while being one nobody would have proposed if the feasible set had been drawn slightly differently?

Keep it concrete -- one worked example (the flat or the wage talks, whichever is cleaner) beats a taxonomy of axioms. And be upfront about what's actually settled here versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this; I don't want a contested reading of K-S dressed up as the consensus.
