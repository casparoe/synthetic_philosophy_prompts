I am a novelist, and I have agreed to adapt Molière's Le Misanthrope into one — to put into prose a play whose central predicament is one I have been circling for years: what becomes of a person who insists on sincerity in a society that has turned accommodation into an art.

While preparing, I went back to Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, written in 1758 against d'Alembert's suggestion, in the Encyclopédie, that Geneva ought to have a theatre, and found in it an objection aimed directly at the play I mean to adapt. Rousseau does not dispute the comedy's genius; he argues that its morality is inverted. Alceste, who is honest and sincere in his dealings with others, is made ridiculous; Philinte, who is pliant and agreeable, is shown as the reasonable man; and the audience is therefore trained to laugh at the honest man and to take the side of the world that has learned to dissemble. He adds that a comedy which refused this inversion would not amuse an audience at all, so that the pleasure of the theatre and its corruption of judgment are, on his account, one and the same thing. His counter-ideal is not a better play but a different kind of gathering altogether: a public festival in which everyone takes part and no one is a spectator.

I am not asking for a defence of the theatre, and I do not need a summary of Rousseau or a plot summary of the play. I want the play read against his charge.

Is his account of the play's sympathies accurate? Do the sonnet scene, the business of Alceste's lawsuit, the letters of Célimène's that are produced against her, the presence of Éliante — as honest as Alceste and far better tempered — and the ending, in which Alceste renounces the world, bear him out? Or does the play arrange its sympathies so that the laughter falls on the salon as much as on the misanthrope?

Or is Rousseau's causal story simply backwards for this play? If the salon is already a stage, and everyone in it is already performing, then the play may be evidence that a society does not need a theatre in order to become theatrical — that it is showing its own audience its own face rather than teaching it a lesson.

Where does he see something the play's admirers miss? And what does the play know that his argument cannot accommodate — about sincerity itself as a performance, about the pleasure we take in the humiliation of the honest man, or about whether there is any alternative to the stage other than solitude? Finally, since the adaptation is mine to make: where must I be unfaithful to the play in order to be faithful to it?

You need not quote Molière. I would rather have your account of the scenes and your argument about them.
