I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on Spinoza's political writings, and I've hit a wall I can't talk my way past. I can state his claim that democracy is the most natural form of state, and I can state his claim that a commonwealth's right is nothing more than its power, but I cannot get the two to sit together, and I have come to suspect that the Dutch crisis of 1672 is where they come apart. What I want from you is not an exposition of either thesis but a case analysis that runs in both directions: the theory used to read the episode, and the episode used to test the theory. Assume I know the texts and need no summary of them; assume also that you have no library at hand, so work from what is common knowledge plus the facts I set out below.

The outline of the episode is this. The Republic was a loose federation of provinces governed for about two decades by an oligarchy of regents, not a democracy in any sense a modern reader would recognize; the grand pensionary Johan de Witt was an appointed official of the States of Holland rather than a popular leader. In 1672 the French armies overran much of the country, the Orangist faction and the Calvinist clergy turned the panic against the regents, William III was made stadholder that summer, and in August a crowd in The Hague murdered de Witt and his brother. Spinoza, who was living in The Hague at the time, spent the last years of his life writing the Political Treatise, which breaks off just as it reaches the chapter on democracy. Begin with the mapping. On the identity of right and power, what exactly happened to the sovereign's right when the crowd acted: did the commonwealth dissolve back into a state of nature, or did the multitude merely exercise a right it had never fully transferred? Can his framework distinguish a people exercising a retained right from a mob destroying a commonwealth, or is that distinction simply unavailable to anyone who equates right with power? Then tell me whether the episode counts against the claim that democracy is the most natural form of state. Since the regime that fell was an aristocracy of regents, which he defends at length elsewhere, is the collapse evidence against the aristocratic model rather than the democratic one, or does the episode in fact support his deeper thought that a commonwealth is stable in proportion as the power of the multitude is able to flow through its institutions rather than against them?

I also want the parts that make the theory uncomfortable. His account of superstition and priestly ambition undoing the Hebrew commonwealth reads to me like a template for 1672, and I want to know whether that story is doing all the real explanatory work, leaving democracy as an ideal with no causal role in the case; if fear and religion rather than the form of government explain what happened, does that leave his defense of democracy untouched, or does it show that his confidence in the rational tractability of the multitude was doing less than it appears? The same question applies to the link he draws between popular rule and freedom of thought: the Republic was notably tolerant, it was under the regents and not under popular rule that heterodox writing found any shelter at all, and it was a popular religious reaction that brought the regime down. So say what the episode does to the claim that the rule of the multitude is the best guardian of free thought. Where the theory is silent, say so plainly rather than filling in on his behalf; the unfinished democratic chapter is a real limit, and I want it respected. End with a verdict: what would it take for 1672 to be a genuine counterexample rather than an illustration, and on balance does the episode confirm his democratic theory, embarrass it, or pass it by? My own hunch is that it embarrasses the theory more than it confirms it, but I would like to be argued out of that.
