I'm writing a paper where I want to push a line I'm not at all sure I can defend, and I'd like you to make the case for it as hard as you can, and then tell me honestly how much of the case is stuff everybody already agrees on versus how much is a live fight.

The line: there's only one "if." Indicative and counterfactual conditonals with the same antecedent and consequent have the same truth conditions — or, if you'd rather go the other way, neither of them has truth conditions in the first place; pick whichever version you think is less bad. What I don't want is the two-separate-truth-conditions story where indicatives get a strict-conditional treatment and counterfactuals get a Lewis-style similarity treatment. On my line, the counterfactual morphology — the past tense in "hadn't killed," the "would" — isn't adding a truth-conditional operator. It's marking something about the status of the antecedent in the conversation: roughly, that the antecedent isn't being put forward as a live option. That's a presupposition-level contribution, or a discourse-level one, or maybe just an implicature, and it hangs off the if-clause rather than coming out of "if" itself.

The obvious thing this has to survive is the Oswald pair: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" versus "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have." You can accept the first and reject the second, which is the standard argument that the two forms differ semantically. On my line you have to say either that the second one is true (same truth conditions as the first, and the first is true) or that the second isn't the kind of thing that's true or false at all. Bite that bullet — tell me which one you're biting and why it's less bad than it sounds. If the bullet kills the view, say which step breaks and what the strongest surviving version is.

Other things I want the arguement to actually engage, since these are the ones that bite:

- The arsenic case (I think it's Anderson 1951, but describe it, don't quote it): someone says that if Jones had taken arsenic he'd have shown exactly the symptoms he is in fact showing, and uses that to argue that Jones did take arsenic. So the counterfactual form gets used where nobody thinks the antecedent is false. Any story on which the counterfactual presupposes or entails the falsity of the antecedent is dead on arrival. So what is the marking tracking, if not falsity?
- The mirror case: "Bob never danced. #If Bob danced, Leland danced" is bad, while "If Bob had danced, Leland would have danced" is fine immediately after "Bob never danced." Why does the indicative collapse when the antecedent has just been denied, if the marking on the counterfactual isn't truth-conditional? Is the counterfactual marking a positive feature (something the counterfactual has and the indicative lacks) or a negative one (something the indicative demands and the counterfactual doesn't care about)?
- Embedding and negation. "It's not the case that if Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" doesn't sound like a presuppositional misfire or a metalinguistic dodge; it sounds like a claim about how things would have gone. Same with "anyone who thinks that if Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy someone else would have is wrong." If the marking is just a signal about what's live in the conversation, what exactly is being denied or attributed here? This is the objection I most want you to take seriously instead of waving at.
- Morphology. The past tense in counterfactuals isn't about past time, and I'm told the same fake-past pattern shows up in a lot of unrelated languages (if you actually remember a language where the distinction is marked very differently, or not at all, mention it, but flag that you're going from memory and might be wrong). If the marking is a conversational signal, why is it obligatory and so systematically tensed? And what do we make of "would" looking like the past of "will"?
- Past and future: "If it rains tomorrow the picnic will be cancelled" versus "If it rained tomorrow the picnic would be cancelled." Is the second really saying something about the antecedent's epistemic status, or is it doing something else? If you think this pair is a different animal from the Oswald pair, say why.

One more thing, because my intuitions here are shaky and I'd rather you tell me than paper over it. I have the feeling the falsity of the antecedent is cancellable in a way a presupposition shouldn't be — "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have, but of course Oswald did kill him" doesn't strike me as a contradiction or a misfire, just as redundant. If that intuition is wrong, say so, and say what it does to my story. A story that tracks falsity fails the first; a story that tracks "not live" has to earn the second.
