For the last three years I have been writing a novel set in a river city whose nineteenth-century warehouse district was designated a historic landmark district some fifty years ago, and I have spent much of that time sitting in the meetings of the commission that administers it. What I cannot make cohere is the institution's own theory of what history is for. The commission's money and its fiercest arguments go to holding the buildings still: original brick, original window sashes, no new signage on the facades, a demolition permit refused because the warehouse is a document of what the city was and would be lost forever. Yet the same commission, in the same meetings, requires that the small museum inside one of those warehouses rewrite its interpretation every few years so that the story it tells is brought up to date — now the laborers, now the families displaced when the district was cleared, now the fact that the district's first fortunes rested in part on enslaved people — and it has lately spent a great deal of public money pulling down a statue of a founder it had itself helped to raise, putting up others, and renaming schools, on the ground that the young should have figures worth imitating. So one body freezes a building because it belongs to the past and rewrites a label because the past belongs to us; it is antiquarian in its masonry and critical in its captions; and when it topples a monument it cannot say whether it is judging the past or bequeathing a better one. Nietzsche's essay on the use and disadvantage of history for life, the second of the Untimely Meditations, published in 1874, gives me the words I lack: he distinguishes the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical ways of serving the past, argues that each can turn pathological, and measures all of them against life. I want to know what my commission's practice actually presupposes. Does it presuppose the antiquarian view that the past is worth keeping simply for having been ours, the monumental view that history exists to furnish exemplars, or the critical view that we must sit in judgment on the past and break with it — or does it presuppose that the three are separable, so that a single institution may honestly be all three at once, and if so, on what account of how they divide (by object, by method, or by the psychological need each answers)? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — above all the ideal of history pursued for its own sake, indifferent to what it does for anyone now living, which is what the archivists in my novel believe they are doing? Which parts of the practice strictly presuppose a theory of history's point, and which merely sit comfortably with one, so that a rival theory would underwrite them just as well? Where does the practice leave the matter underdetermined, licensing several theories at once? And if one replaced the presupposed view with a frankly critical one, or a frankly antiquarian one, what in the commission's actual doings would have to change?

I am not a philosopher and I do not want a reading list; I want the implicit theory reconstructed and then judged. I would also ask you to keep separate what is settled about Nietzsche's essay and what is genuinely contested among people who know the text well — whether his three modes classify historical methods or the needs of a culture, what he means by life when he makes it a standard, how continuous the essay is with his later work, whether the critical mode can be practiced at all without self-deception — and not to present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
