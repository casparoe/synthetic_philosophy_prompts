My old metaphysics tutor is pretty firm about these constitution puzzles: a statue and the lump of clay it's made from are two different objects (squash the statue and the clay is still there); same for a bronze sphere and the bronze (melt it and the bronze survives); she thinks Theseus's ship is the very same ship after the planks get replaced one at a time; and she thinks the ship you'd get by reassembling all the original planks, even in the original harbor, is a different ship from the one that kept sailing. Now here's the case I actually want to ask her about: a sculptor makes a statue out of a lump of clay, but this time the statue and the lump come into existence together and go out of existence together -- no squashing, no melting, no moment where one exists and the other doesn't, they're annihilated at the same instant. Is the statue the same object as the lump, or two? Predict her verdict, tell me which rule she's running -- something like 'coincident things are distinct only when thier persistence conditions could come apart' vs 'a made thing and the portion of matter it's made from are always two objects, modal profiles be damned' -- give me your best guess and the reasons for it, and tell me what further case would best tell those two rules apart.
