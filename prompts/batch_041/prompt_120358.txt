I am a PhD student in philosophy working on just war theory, and next month I have to deliver the reflection at a joint commemoration: our university's Japanese students' association and its veterans' alumni chapter hold it together, and the two halves of the room want opposite things from whoever speaks. I want to refuse both ready-made scripts — that the bombs were necessary and therefore permissible, and that they were simply a crime — and I want the address built on a distinction the audience will not have heard put plainly: an act can be wrong while the person who ordered it is excusable, because blame turns on what he could reasonably have known and expected at the time, and the moral status of the act does not.

Please write that address, at roughly eight minutes spoken. Let the distinction govern what the speech is willing to say rather than being explained and then set aside, and let it remain a speech rather than becoming an essay. It must not assert any factual claim that historians still dispute, and it should say something about whether the second bombing, three days later, raises a question distinct from the first. It has to make room for the grief of the veterans' families and of the survivors' families without adjudicating between them, and it must not close on a verdict or on the tragedy of war.
