My brother-in-law is a decent bloke but he's got one move in every argument about historical statues and it's "well, he was a man of his time", delivered like a trump card. It came up again last Sunday over a local campaign to pull down a statue of some colonial governor I'd never heard of, and what got to me is that I couldn't actually say what's wrong with the sentence, only that it feels like it's doing about four jobs at once and quietly picking whichever one suits. So what I want from you is a menu rather than a verdict on the statue: the distinct precise claims that "he was a man of his time" could be making, laid out from the near-trivial to the genuinely substantive, and for each one a note on what kind of claim it is — a claim about what he personally believed, about what was knowable or arguable in his milieu, about what morality could reasonably demand of him, about who is responsible when a norm is shared by a whole society, about whether blame can reach backwards at all, about what we should do with the monument now — plus whether that reading is plausible and whether it actually gets him the conclusion he's using it for. I'd like the empirical ones (what did his contemporaries actually think, was anyone making the opposing arguement at the time, was he an outlier in either direction) kept clearly apart from the normative and metaphysical ones (that agents are only answerable to the standards available to them, that a person's outlook is just the output of his circumstances, that you can't be blamed for what you couldn't have helped), because I suspect he's smuggling one in under cover of the other and I want to be able to say which.

I'm also curious about the readings that turn the slogan against itself: "he was a man of his time" as an indictment of the time rather than a defence of the man, or the way it sits next to its twin, "he was ahead of his time" — can both be true of the same person, does accepting one commit you to the other, and why does one sound like praise and the other like an excuse? And is there a reading on which the phrase is just true and irrelevant, i.e. a platitude that no one would deny and that carries no weight against any criticism? I've got Columbus, Jefferson and Churchill floating round my head as the obvious test cases, but I don't want a case-by-case ruling on any of them; I want the logical anatomy of the defence, with at most a closing line on which of the three it fits best and worst, if that's even a fair question to ask.

One thing I do care about: be straight with me about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who actually work on moral responsibility and the philosophy of history — I don't want a contested position handed to me as the consensus, because I'll be repeating it at Sunday lunch and I'd rather not be the one talking rubbish. Keep the whole thing in British English, please, and don't worry about being gentle with the slogan; I'd rather find out it's weaker than I thought.
