I have been working through a puzzle about higher-order evidence that my study group keeps splintering over, and I would like your actual credence rather than a survey of the literature. Consider an agent — call her Mira — who holds first-order evidence E bearing on some hypothesis H and, after careful reasoning that she has no independent reason to distrust, arrives at a credence of 0.8 in H. She then receives a piece of higher-order evidence: a diagnostic report, issued by a process she has strong track-record evidence is 90% reliable at classifying episodes of reasoning as reliable or unreliable, which tells her that the particular episode of reasoning that generated her 0.8 verdict was in fact unreliable. The report carries no information about H itself beyond what it says about Mira's episode of reasoning, and she has no way to inspect the episode further. The proposition I want a number on is this: level-splitting — that is, believing H to a high degree while simultaneously believing that this very belief is not epistemically justified — is rationally permissible in at least some cases of this structure. Give me your credence that this proposition is true, on a 0 to 1 scale, where 0 means it is never permissible and 1 means it is permissible in every such case.

Do not hand me the number bare. Decompose it into the sub-questions the verdict actually rests on, assigning each a rough probability and a one-line justification: (a) whether the diagnostic report is genuine evidence about H or only about Mira and her reasoning process; (b) whether the correct theory of how higher-order evidence discounts first-order evidence is a conciliation-style view that drives credence toward the level the report implies, or a view on which first-order evidence retains most of its force; (c) whether rationality imposes a transparency or no-self-undermining constraint that makes level-splitting incoherent regardless of the evidential facts; (d) whether the case even has a unique rational answer once one allows that rationality is permissive; and (e) any further term you believe belongs. Then give me the combination, and tell me which single sub-question the final number is most sensitive to and what argument or result would move it most.

Finally, situate your answer relative to a disagreement I am trying to adjudicate: my own credence in the proposition is about 0.35, and a colleague's is about 0.8. On your decomposition, which sub-question is most likely to be where the two of us actually diverge, as opposed to where we merely talk past each other?
