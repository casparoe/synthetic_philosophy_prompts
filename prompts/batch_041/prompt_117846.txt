Ok, so I run a small reading group and two of the members have been going at each other for basically a year about qualia, and I want to force them to produce the document they should of written months ago. Here's the setup.

One of them is what I'd call a qualia abolitionist. Roughly: the pre-theoretical notion of qualia — the idea that experience has these intrinsic, private, ineffable, immediately-apprehendable "feels" that are the raw material of consciousness — is not just vague but incoherent, and the reason nobody can ever say what qualia are is that there's nothing there to say. She likes to say the burden of proof should run the other way: anyone who wants to appeal to private subjective properties has to show first that they aren't making a mistake.

The other is a realist. Roughly: of course experiences have a subjective character; that's the one thing we know best; the abolitionist's arguments always smuggle in a verificationist or behaviourist premise — if you can't measure it or say what behaviour it predicts, it isn't real — and that premise is just false. She thinks the "there's no fact of the matter" move confuses our epistemic position with the metaphysics.

The case they keep circling is the coffee-taster one from Dennett's paper: two tasters, Chase and Sanborn, both of whom have gone off the company's coffee over the years. Chase says the coffee changed. Sanborn says the coffee is the same and he changed. Both are sincere, and there's no obvious way to tell which of them is right, or even what would count as telling.

Now I want to add a case of my own that neither of them has seen, so they can't just recite their usual lines. Call her Nell. Nell is a taster at the same company, hired long after Chase and Sanborn. There are four things anyone can measure about her situation: (a) the chemistry of the coffee — the company has sealed samples going back decades and the lab says the blend is unchanged; (b) her receptor and transduction profile, on file from her hiring physical, unchanged; (c) her preference rankings on the company's standard paired-comparison test, unchanged; (d) her verbal reports, changed — she now says the coffee tastes different to her than it used to. Nothing else about her is measurable. (a) through (c) are flat, (d) moved. She isn't lying, as far as anyone can tell, and she isn't confused about the question.

So what I want from you is the joint statement these two could both actually sign. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who wins — a document they'd both put their names to. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both of them accept. And I mean non-vacuous ones — things like: that experience has properties; that the distinction between "the coffee changed" and "I changed" is a real distinction in some sense; that reports can be mistaken; that the Chase-and-Sanborn situation is at least possible; that "what it's like" talk in some thin sense is fine by both of them. Push it as far as it will go. I'd rather have eight real ones then twenty safe ones.

2. The disagreements, each stated twice: once in wording the abolitionist would accept as a fair statement of her own view, and once in wording the realist would accept for hers. If the only way to state a disagreement is in loaded language one side rejects, say so and say what the load is.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where, if you settled one, at least one of them would have to move. I expect one of them is whether "there is a fact of the matter about the feel itself, over and above the stimulus, the physiology, the dispositions and the reports" is even a coherent question, but I dont want to prejudge it. For each crux: the best argument or case for each side, and what each of them predicts about how the Nell case would look if their own view were right. Write those predictions down as predictions, specific enough that we could tell in a year whether they were right.

4. Which part of the fight, if any, is verbal — and if part of it is, exactly which part, and what the two of them were actually disagreeing about underneath it.

5. What neither of them will give up, and why the collaboration won't settle it.

Two constraints. First, no citations, no bibliography, no "see so-and-so's paper" — if they want to use a famous argument, they should restate it in their own words so the document stands on its own. We're circulating this internally and I don't want it turning into a reading list. Second, neither side gets to win by appeal to what's "obvious" or "undeniable" — if something is obvious to one of them, it has to be written as a claim the other can at least evaluate.

Format: the agreed claims as a numbered list, then a table with columns for the question, the two statements, whether it's a crux, what evidence both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction. Then a short closing section for 4 and 5. Aim for something I can hand to both of them without either feeling set up.
