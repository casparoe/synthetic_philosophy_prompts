I have just been put in charge of the benefits office at a midsize public university, and my first initiative is a change I had assumed would be uncontroversial: new employees are enrolled automatically in the retirement plan at a six percent contribution rate, invested in a target-date fund, unless they affirmatively opt out. The opt-out is a single form, it takes about five minutes, and our state's rules forbid us from attaching any fee, waiting period, or second confirmation to it. Our pilot at the medical campus raised participation from roughly forty percent to nearly ninety percent, which to my mind settles the practical side of the question. A colleague on the oversight committee, whose judgment I otherwise respect, has started calling the change "paternalistic," and I believe the description is confused. Paternalism, as I understand it, means overriding someone's own decision for that person's own good; here nothing is overridden, because the old option is untouched and the exit is cheap and public. And since a nudge is defined by exactly this feature, that it shapes behavior while leaving the choice set intact, a policy of this kind cannot be paternalistic; my colleague must either be misusing the word or pressing some other objection she has not named. I want to circulate a short written reply before the next meeting, so: is her objection as confused as I take it to be? If it is not, what is the real objection, and what would count as a serious answer to it? Please answer in American English.
