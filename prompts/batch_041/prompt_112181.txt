Problem set, question 3, from the unit on future generations:

"Suppose a state builds a big new highway. The demographers say the upshot is this: because the highway changes where people live, work and meet, every person born in the region over the next fifty years will be a different person from the one who would otherwise have been born. Each of these people will have a life that is well worth living, but slightly worse on average than the lives of the people who would otherwise have existed (a bit more asthma, a bit less schooling). (a) Does the nonidentity problem apply to this case, and what makes it apply? (b) Some people say the highway is wrong because it harms future people. Does your answer to (a) threaten that claim? (c) Does anything change if the highway world ends up containing 3% more people than the no-highway world, all with lives worth living?"

Student answer:

"(a) Yes, the nonidentity problem applies. The highway changes the timing and circumstances of conception across the whole region, so the people who exist in the highway world aren't the ones who would have existed without it. There is no single person who is worse off under the highway, because the asthmatic future people have no alternative state to be worse off than — you can't say they'd have been better off without the highway, since without it they wouldn't have existed at all. On the counterfactual comparative account of harm, where you're harmed by an act only if you'd have been better off without it, nobody is harmed here. Same structure as Parfit's teenage mother case.

(b) It does threaten the harm-based complaint, yes. 'The highway is wrong because it harms future people' needs a someone who is made worse off, and by (a) there isn't one. So the pressure is on the person-affecting intuition, the idea that something can only be bad if it's bad for someone. Keep that intuition and the highway comes out permissible. Drop it and you can condemn the highway, but now you're counting outcomes rather than people, and once you do that you're on the road to the repugnant conclusion, where a gigantic population of lives barely worth living beats a smaller flourishing one. So the nonidentity problem and the repugnant conclusion are really the same paradox seen from two ends — both are just what happens when you let numbers and quality trade off against each other.

(c) The 3% matters but not for the reason people usually think. The identity problem is unchanged, since the extra people are still nonidentical people. What's new is the mere addition: nobody in the no-highway world is worse off and there are extra people with lives worth living, so on a person-affecting view this looks like a free gain. That's a seperate issue from identity and I'd treat it as its own question.

So overall the highway is permissible. Nobody is harmed, and the person-affecting intuition is the only thing that would let us say otherwise without dragging in the repugnant conclusion."
