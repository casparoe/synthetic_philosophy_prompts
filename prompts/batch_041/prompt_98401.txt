I'm a novelist — five books, no philosophy training past an undergrad minor — and I've rashly agreed to write a short essay for a literary magazine's special issue on fiction and moral language. I have it sketched out but I need you to go through it hard: on accuracy, on whether it's actually interesting, and above all on whether the central move survives contact with a philosopher who already hates it.

The essay is about the Frege-Geach problem for expressivism. Setup as I have it: moral sentences embed — if it's wrong to eat meat, it's wrong to feed it to your kids — and that antecedent isn't asserted, yet it has to mean what it means when you assert it, otherwise the inference is a pun and not modus ponens. But if "eating meat is wrong" merely expresses an attitude (disapproval, a plan, whatever), then what does the unasserted clause express? Nothing, apparently, so the inference isn't valid and we've lost moral logic. I know the standard escapes — Gibbard's possible worlds of norms, Blackburn's higher-order attitudes, or just biting the bullet and redefining validity — and I say a little about why each one feels like a patch.

My move, the part I need you to attack: I borrow the make-believe idea from aesthetics (Walton, props, sentences as things that tell you what to imagine). On my account a moral sentence is a prop: eating meat is wrong prescribes imagining things a certain way. An unasserted occurrence is still a prop — the conditional as a whole is a prop that licenses a transition inside the game: imagine accepting the first prop and you're to imagine accepting the second. Meaning just is the prop-role, so it's identical in both occurrences, and validity is the transition being licensed. No possible worlds of norms needed, and validity stays tied to something truth-like instead of becoming a merely verbal rule.

My worries, in order: (1) negation — what prop is "eating meat is not wrong"? The negation seems to be about the game rather than the content. (2) Is "imagine accepting the norm" just assertion in a costume? (3) Does it get the small cases right, e.g. disjunction introduction: from "eating meat is wrong" do I really get a licensed move to "eating meat is wrong or the moon is cheese"? (4) Does it dissolve the problem rather than solve it, and is that a cheat?

So please, no survey. Tell me which of these four, if any, is fatal, and give me a verdict on whether the make-believe move is a real solution or a relabeling of the same old attitude in prettier clothes. If you think I should abandon my move and go quasi-realist instead, say so and say why. And flag anything I've stated that's just flatly wrong about the problem itself, since I'm definately out of my depth.
