Ok, I need help thinking through something about promises, because the more I look at how promises actually get treated, the less I understand what anyone thinks a promise is.

Here's whats bugging me. In ordinary life a promise seems to bind because you gave your word. You can't just pay someone off to get out of it -- offering your friend cash to let you skip their wedding doesnt make skipping ok. If they tell you it's fine, you're released. And the wrong, when you break it, seems to be the breaking, not whatever they lost.

Contract law looks like it's playing a different game. It won't enforce a bare promise -- promise to hand someone $500 and you can just not, unless they relied on it or it was under seal. When it does enforce, it doesn't make you perform, it makes you pay damages putting the other side where they'd have been if you'd performed, which means a company can run the numbers, decide breaching is cheaper, breach, write a cheque, and that's legal. And the law will enforce a liquidated damages clause fixing a number in advance but not a penalty clause that punishes breach beyond the loss, so the promise can be priced, but only up to what the promisee was out. Actually making someone do the thing -- specific performance -- is the rare exception, for land and one-of-a-kind stuff.

Then there's crypto smart contracts, where the code just executes, so nobody can breach, and also nobody can be let off if the world changes, unless someone wrote that in.

So: what does the contract-law package actually presuppose about why promises bind? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not the one lawyers would recite. Does it presuppose that the bindingness tracks what the promisee stands to lose or relied on? That a promise transfers an entitlement the promisee now owns, so expectation damages are really just protecting property? Or only that promising is a convention we invented for mutual assurance, and the law enforces whatever bit of the convention is socially useful? Which of those does it strictly need, which does it merely sit ok with, and where is it underdetermined -- could two or three of those views license the exact same set of rules?

Second, is the package internally consistent? Breach-and-pay treats the promise as a price rather than a fence, but refusing penalty clauses treats the promisee's loss as the measure of the whole obligation, which smells more like reliance. Meanwhile ordinary morality, where you can be released and where breaking is wrong in itself, seems to presuppose something else again: that you handed someone authority over a bit of your future. Is that view actually incompatible with the contract rules, or is contract just a special-purpose practice sitting on top of it?

Third, if we swapped the presupposed view for the self-binding one -- the wrong is that you broke your own word and let down someone who was counting on it, losses irrelevant -- what would the law have to look like? Gratuitous promises enforceable, penalty clauses valid, performance as the default remedy? Would smart contracts then look like a horror show, or like the purest version of that view?

Also, someone told me Hume thought promise-keeping is an artificial virtue, invented rather than natural -- I might be mangling it. If thats roughly right, is the legal picture basically Humean, and if so can the everyday picture survive? Or does the Humean have to say that a promise to a dying friend who never finds out is nothing at all? I can't tell wether that's a reductio or just the bill coming due.

No reading lists or citations please, I don't want names and dates, I want the argument. Assume I know what utilitarianism and Kant are in the vaguest way and nothing else.
