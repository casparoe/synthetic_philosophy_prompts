I retired from a chemistry lab about five years ago and have been slowly teaching myself philosophy ever since, mostly by arguing with my old colleagues and reading whatever I can find, so apologies in advance if I use the jargon wrong. What's got me stuck right now is these language models. My grandkids got me using one, I got curious about how it works, and the more I read the less I can square two things that seem to be going on at once. The way you build one, as far as I can tell, is you scoop up an enormous pile of text off the internet and train the thing to guess the next word, over and over, with no eyes, no hands, no thermometer, no contact whatsoever with the stuff all those words are supposedly about. Then the field grades these systems by how well they answer questions, how correct their answers are on some exam or trivia list, how natural their sentences sound, and when a model does well the headline is that it knows things, that it understands the question. But the same people, often in the same pargraph, will turn around and insist it doesn't really understand anything, it's just crunching statistics over symbols. That contradiction is what I want you to dig into.

I gather philosophers have a phrase for the underlying worry, something about symbols needing to be grounded, and a friend in a robotics group tells me they've solved it by bolting a camera and a gripper onto a model so it's now grounded -- though they still seem to test it by making it answer text questions, which doesn't reassure me. What I actually want isn't a recitation of the old problem but an answer to this: what theory of meaning or understanding does the whole practice of training and grading these systems have to be presupposing? Does it only make sense if meaning is just the way words sit relative to other words, so that you get understanding for free out of the statistics of a big enough corpus -- in which case the people saying it doesn't really understand are quietly contradicting their own benchmark scores? Or does the practice secretly assume the opposite, that meaning requires some hookup to the world, in which case every good score is a small miracle we're papering over? Reconstruct the implicit view of meaning the practice commits you to, separate what's genuinely forced by the way these systems are built and tested from what's just stuff people happen to say about them, tell me which rival views of meaning the practice quietly rules out, and then say what would actually have to change -- the training, the benchmarks, the whole it-understands marketing, all of it -- if we swapped in the rival view instead.
