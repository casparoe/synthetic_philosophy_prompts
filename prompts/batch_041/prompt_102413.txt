I teach a seventy-five-minute session in an undergraduate seminar on minds and machines, and I have been trying to build a class around the MU-puzzle and the idea of jumping out of the system, or JOOTSing. The students are about sixteen, mostly philosophy majors with a couple from computer science, and they know sentential logic but no computability theory. What I want them to feel, rather than merely hear, is the difference between working inside a formal system and reasoning about that system from outside: the moment when one stops hunting for a derivation and starts proving that no derivation of the relevant kind can exist. I also want them to distinguish two exits that are often run together — proving a negative result about the given rules, and stepping back far enough to ask why those rules rather than others, or what would happen if one added a rule — because both are meta-level moves but they feel different, and the second is closer to what makes the idea unsettling.

Design the activity so that the rules and roles do the teaching. The students should start with the string MI and a small set of transformations (append U to any string ending in I; double the part after M; replace III by U; delete UU), try in groups to reach MU, and then be forced into a second phase in which they must argue about all possible derivations rather than produce one. I would like a role structure that separates inside players from an outside auditor, and a moment where the whole room commits to a verdict on whether MU is reachable before any invariant is introduced. Give me materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief questions that connect what happened to the inside/outside distinction, the predictable failures the first time — groups treating a long run of failures as proof of impossibility, auditors who merely apply the rules faster, someone who finds the invariant and immediately treats it as just another move — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about which part of JOOTSing this activity cannot reproduce: my suspicion is that it can stage the shift from derivation to metatheoretic argument, but not the stronger claim that a mind can always jump out of any system that models it. Tell me how to frame that limitation without turning the debrief into a lecture.
