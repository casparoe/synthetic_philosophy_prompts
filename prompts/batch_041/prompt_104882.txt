Something's been bugging me about how we talk about markets. There's a familiar arguement — I think from the rational-expectations crowd — that if two traders share the same priors and both know the other is rational, then anyone offering a trade is basically advertising that they think the price is wrong, so the other side should update against them and the trade collapses. On those assumptions, purely informational trading should mostly vanish. And yet markets are full of trades, and we still treat the price as the best estimate we've got — a headline says the market puts this at 30%, regulators treat prices as if they've soaked up everyone's knowledge, indexers assume you can't beat it. What does that practice — treating the price as knowledge — actually presuppose about the people trading? That they hold different priors, that some are noise traders or irrational, that they trade for liquidity or hedging rather than information, or that the price is a kind of statistic nobody individually has to hold? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, sort out what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits with, and say where it's underdetermined. And does treating prices this way commit us to anything about when rational disagreement is even possible?
