I teach a first-year interdisciplinary seminar called Language, Thought, and Power, and next week's session is the one that reliably goes off the rails. The topic is the default assumption in language: the way a term for one group of people passes as neutral until you notice who it fails to cover. My colleagues who have taught it report the same two failures, and I would like to forestall both. There are the students who arrive already convinced that this is a matter of etiquette and word-policing — so we are apparently not allowed to say "fireman" anymore — and there are the students who arrive convinced that the whole topic is a Whorfian claim about language determining thought, so that the moment anyone says "influence" they hear "determines," and the hour collapses into an argument about whether words cause sexism. I would like you to plan the fifty minutes rather than explain the material to me. What has to come first, and why does each component depend on the one before it? In particular: does the substitution test — take a term that reads as neutral, swap in the name of a group the speaker does not belong to, and see whether the neutrality survives — have to be in place before the students meet any historical case, or should the case come first and the test be extracted from it? Which single example should carry the session, and what must that example have and lack? It has to permit a mechanical substitution that students can perform on the spot, it has to have an available defender of the neutral reading whose position is respectable enough to require answering rather than ridiculing, and it must not be so inflammatory that the students start litigating the content instead of the structure. I am inclined to let Hofstadter's satire do the work — the "Person Paper," written straight-faced by a persona who rails against the "negrists" and "black libbers" who want "person" substituted for "white" everywhere, producing "chairwhite" and "mailwhite" and the pronouns "whe" and "ble," and who insists that "white is a mixture of all the colors of the rainbow, including black" — but I am not sure its very cleverness does not defeat the purpose: the students may laugh, admire the trick, and stop thinking. Tell me whether it should carry the session or arrive late as a payoff, and if not this, what should carry it.

Then map the misconceptions for me, in the order the students will actually form them, and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head off each one. My guesses are these, and I want to know which are real and which I have invented: that the issue is politeness rather than presupposition and default; that the remedy is lexical, so that saying "chairperson" and "they" closes the matter, leaving the deeper point about markedness — why "male nurse" and "lady doctor" are marked while "nurse" and "doctor" are not — entirely unabsorbed; that the satire is a straight-faced racist tract, or that its author is endorsing the persona, so that the text is read as an argument rather than as a mirror; that the argument is committed to linguistic determinism, which it is not; and that the race analogy is exact in one direction or the other, either "so the two cases are simply the same" or "the analogy fails because nobody actually says 'chairwhite'," which misses that the substitution is counterfactual by design. The room is cross-listed and includes students of color and students whose first language is not English, and one of my colleagues in the history department is likely to sit in and object that this is all aesthetic fussing; the plan should let me handle both without turning any student into the illustration. Finally, give me the one question I can ask in the last five minutes to see whether the idea has landed — ideally something that makes them run the diagnostic on a fresh case where gender is not at issue at all — and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud.
