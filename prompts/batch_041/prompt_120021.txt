I write a fortnightly column on philosophy for a general-readership magazine, and I am now assembling a long-form essay on the paradox of deontology — the charge that a morality of constraints is self-defeating, in that it forbids you to do a lesser wrong even when doing it would prevent a greater number of wrongs of precisely the sort it forbids, and so leaves the world worse by the theory's own lights. I have the material but not the argument, and I would rather settle the argument on paper before I try to make it readable.

Please write the essay. I am not looking for a survey of positions, and I am not interested in the tame version of the puzzle, in which a straw man is set up and knocked down in a paragraph. What I want is this.

State the paradox in the form in which it is actually threatening, and be explicit about what must be true of a constraint for the appearance of paradox to arise at all. If you think some familiar statements of the puzzle are artifacts of loose talk about maximizing, say so and show why.

Then take seriously the case I keep returning to. You are the sole trustee of a charitable fund. A kidnapper holds five people and will release them unharmed if, and only if, one innocent stranger is killed within the hour. You will not do the killing yourself and could not. But you can wire the fund's money to a professional killer, who is under no duress and who will act on receipt. Wire the money and one innocent person dies while five live; refuse and five die while the professional does nothing. A morality that forbids you to kill one to save five appears to forbid you to procure the killing as well. But a morality that permits the procurement has told you that you may arrange a killing in order to prevent more killings, which is exactly what the constraint was meant to rule out. Either the constraint reaches your act of hiring, and the paradox stands in full, or it does not, and constraints can be evaded by anyone willing to delegate. I want to know whether this case sharpens the paradox, restates it at one remove, or dissolves it.

Lay out the strongest defences a deontologist could mount — including any I have not anticipated — and say which survive the case and which do not. Then reach a verdict: is the paradox genuine, in the sense that deontology turns out to be incoherent or self-undermining, or can it be dispelled? Defend your answer rather than gesturing at one, and do not let it rest on a distinction you never cash. Include a working title, and a sentence stating what your conclusion means for a reader who has never studied moral philosophy. No citations or reading lists, please; I will handle sourcing.

After the essay, give me a JSON object with exactly these fields: "verdict" (one of exactly three strings: "genuine", "dispellable", "genuine but tolerable"); "thesis" (your conclusion in one sentence); "crux" (the single premise or distinction on which the verdict turns); "horn_taken" (which horn of the delegation dilemma you take, and why, in one sentence); "strongest_defence_that_fails" (the most tempting deontological reply, with the reason it fails); "what_would_overturn_the_verdict" (the argument or consideration that would change your mind); "confidence" (an integer from 1 to 5); and "plain_language_takeaway" (one sentence for a non-philosopher).
