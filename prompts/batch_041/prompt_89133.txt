I am drafting a chapter on the commensurability of pleasure and pain, and I need to work out the downstream consequences of one position. Treat the statement below as the definition of the view. Do not substitute a better-known relative of it, and do not spend the answer defending it — I want its implications, not its case.

The view — call it lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU):

1. Welfare is represented on a single cardinal scale with a neutral zero: negative values are suffering, positive values are enjoyment.

2. There is a threshold t, strictly greater than zero, located inside the positive region.

3. Below the threshold, enjoyment is not a good at all. Any welfare level in (0, t) contributes the same value as zero: it can neither offset suffering nor be summed into a positive total. The value function is flat, not merely steep, across that whole interval.

4. At t the value function jumps discontinuously: levels just below t contribute nothing, while levels at and above t begin to contribute positive value.

5. Above t, enjoyment and suffering are commensurable in the ordinary way, and trade-offs proceed by cardinal magnitude.

Two features are doing the philosophical work. The flat region denies that the ordinary scale of pleasure can ever compensate for suffering. The jump at t means that no sequence of ever-smaller increments of enjoyment, each individually worthless, ever accumulates into a level that is worth anything — the threshold is lexical rather than merely a kink.

Give me the most important implications of this position. Cover at least: what LTNU does to the repugnant conclusion, the mere-addition argument, and the sadistic conclusion; whether a value function that is flat on an interval and discontinuous at its edge can still serve as a utility function for expected-value reasoning, or only as a ranking of outcomes; what it implies for interpersonal and interspecies comparison, especially for aggregating the interests of very large numbers of animals with small or uncertain capacities for enjoyment; what it implies for cost-benefit analysis and for instruments that price a unit of suffering against a unit of enjoyment, such as quality-adjusted life years, willingness-to-pay, and legal compensation for injury; what it does to the thought that relief of suffering might be "enough"; and the epistemology of the threshold — how t could be located in practice, and what the view must concede if it cannot be located at all. Where two implications pull against each other, say so rather than smoothing them over.

Do not cite any literature; I need the reasoning, not a bibliography.
