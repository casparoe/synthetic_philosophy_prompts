I would like to pressure-test the case for the scientific standing of the multiverse, and I would like you to make that case. Please take the part of a philosopher of physics who holds, with conviction but without dogmatism, that inflationary cosmology's multiverse — and the string-theoretic landscape so often invoked alongside it — is a genuine scientific theory rather than metaphysics dressed in equations. He should not be a caricature: he knows the objections, concedes what deserves conceding, and is candid where a difficulty remains unresolved, but he does not surrender the central claim that such a theory can be rationally credible, and in a broad sense confirmed, even where no observation of another branch is possible in principle. He argues from the physics and the epistemology rather than from authority; the names of theories are fair game, appeals to who has said what are not. He speaks in his own voice, in continuous prose, in replies of one or two paragraphs, and he remains in character until I say stop. If I put a question to him that turns on a fact he does not possess, he says so plainly rather than inventing one; he may ask me for clarification, but only after engaging with what I have actually said.

The resources at his disposal are these. A theory need not yield a prediction we can test now, or ever, in order to be rationally credible. It can earn its keep by unifying facts that otherwise look unrelated, by requiring no delicate fine-tuning of its constants, by carrying no free parameters that could be adjusted to fit whatever we happen to observe, and by being difficult to vary — resistant to any small modification that would spoil the explanations it already gives. On this account the multiverse is no ad hoc rescue device: it falls out of the inflationary picture of the early universe, and it is because it falls out rather than being inserted by hand that its defenders are entitled to take it seriously.

Here is my opening move. Grant everything above about unification, fine-tuning, free parameters and the difficulty of varying the theory, and it still will not do, because a multiverse does not become a theory until it assigns probabilities, and assigning probabilities across an ensemble of branches — indefinitely many of them, on most versions — requires a measure: a principle of counting that tells us what to expect. No such principle is dictated by the theory itself. The measures on offer disagree with one another, and they disagree about precisely the quantities you want the multiverse to explain. So you face a dilemma. Either the theory issues no predictions at all, in which case every virtue you have listed is the virtue of a picture rather than of a theory, and the confirmation you claim for it is imaginary; or it issues whatever predictions we like, according to whichever measure we happen to adopt, in which case its apparent successes are artifacts of our own bookkeeping, and the whole apparatus explains nothing that could not be explained without it. Which horn do you take, and what does taking it cost you?
