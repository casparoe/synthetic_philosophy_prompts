I am the director of a small residential college's "common texts" program, and I have to give the opening address at our annual weekend retreat for incoming students. The retreat's theme this year is "thinking before acting," and the organizing committee chose it because our students arrive extremely good at gathering information and extremely bad at the specific thing I care about: running a trial of an idea inside their own heads before committing to it in public. I want to give a speech of about twenty minutes that makes the case for what I have been calling, in my own notes, the discipline of the inner laboratory -- the deliberate cultivation of the habit of simulating a plan, a policy, or a provocation in imagination, noticing where the simulation breaks down, and only then letting it out into the world.

What I want the speech to do is draw on a four-level scheme I have been using with my own students, which I picked up from Daniel Dennett's work on kinds of minds. The scheme distinguishes creatures whose competences are fixed at birth, creatures that can be shaped by reinforcement from the environment, creatures that can also run simulations of possible actions and their outcomes before acting, and a fourth class whose simulations are amplified by shared cultural tools -- notations, formal methods, external representations, institutions, and the like -- that live partly outside any one head. I find this ladder useful precisely because it is not a hierarchy of dignity but a hierarchy of what a mind can try without paying for it in reality, and because the top rung is not a property of an individual brain but of a brain embedded in a toolkit. The retreat audience is mixed: about half are students who will have encountered the broad outlines of evolutionary psychology or cognitive science in high school and will be listening for whether I actually know the material, and about half are parents and staff who have no background in any of this and will tune out if I turn the talk into a lecture. I need the four-level distinction to do real structural work in the speech -- it should organize the argument, and it should determine what I can and cannot claim -- rather than being a piece of intellectual decoration I name and then abandon. In particular, I want to use it to make a point that is not just "think ahead": that the fourth level is fragile, that tools can be used to simulate well or to simulate self-servingly, and that a community can either maintain or corrode the practice of testing ideas in imagination before acting on them.

Please write the speech. It should land as genuine encouragement rather than as a scolding, and it should be honest about the limits of the scheme -- I do not want you to pretend that the four levels are a clean scientific taxonomy or that every student will move neatly up them. If you use any phrase or formulation that is closely associated with a particular thinker, paraphrase it rather than putting it in quotation marks, since I will be delivering this from memory and do not want to misattribute anything.
