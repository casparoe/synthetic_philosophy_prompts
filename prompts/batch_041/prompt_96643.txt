I am writing a long-form piece on the metaphors we use for the online information environment, and before I commit to one in print I want an audit of the ecosystem metaphor that nearly every researcher and policy source I speak with reaches for. In their telling, platforms are habitats, attention is the nutrient flowing through the system, disinformation is an invasive species, and the environment as a whole can be healthy or degraded, resilient or fragile. The work the metaphor is asked to do is usually deregulatory: ecosystems are said to self-correct, to absorb or expel invaders without a manager, and heavy-handed intervention gets likened to the era of total fire suppression that left Western forests loaded with unburned fuel and set up the catastrophic fires that followed. One source argued that clearing out bad actors only pushes them into unmoderated spaces where they grow back stronger.

Please tell me how far the metaphor actually holds. I would like the correspondence laid out explicitly first: what plays the role of species, habitat, energy, predator, disturbance, and carrying capacity, and which of those mappings the deregulatory argument actually needs as opposed to which are decoration. Then the places the mapping breaks, sorted into the ones that matter for that argument and the ones that do not. I am especially curious whether ecology's own history is a liability here: my understanding is that the discipline spent much of the twentieth century fighting over whether a plant community is a unified entity that develops toward a stable climax state or merely a loose assemblage of species whose co-occurrence is largely coincidental, and that the second view largely won. If the metaphor's picture of a natural, healthy baseline was itself abandoned by the scientists whose field is being borrowed, say what that does to an argument that leans on self-correction and equilibrium. I would also like to know what the metaphor quietly imports from real ecosystems that the information environment may simply lack. Then tell me plainly whether the deregulatory conclusion survives the disanalogies or whether the metaphor was carrying it all along. And if a better comparison is available — I keep hearing epidemiology, and separately something about managed fisheries — say which one I should use instead and what it would let me say that the ecosystem cannot.

Please write your answer in American English.
