I have spent a lot of time arguing about this in comment sections and want to get it straight on paper, and I would like your help. Please write me a taxonomy, laid out as a decision tree, of the positions people actually hold on whether social punishment — getting someone fired, deplatformed, shunned, or publicly shamed — is punishment at all, and if it is, what would make it a legitimate one. I am not asking for a reading list and I do not want the tree organised around well-known names or schools; I want it built out of the questions that separate the views, with the positions slotted into the branches afterwards. I plan to use it with a discussion group of friends who are mostly not academics, so it needs to be answerable by someone who has opinions but no vocabulary.

Start the tree at the most basic fork: whether the phenomenon is punishment, or just the ordinary exercise of people's rights to speak, associate, hire, and refuse. If someone answers that it is not punishment, note that most of the rest of the tree is closed to them, and say briefly why that branch still gets populated anyway. From there, label every branch with a question phrased so that a person can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. The distinctions I care about are these: whether the people doing the sanctioning have any standing to do it; what the sanction is aimed at, the person or the platform; whether the justification offered is deterrence, desert, or sheer public denunciation; whether the comparison case in someone's head is state punishment — which drags in proportionality, notice, a chance to answer, and the ban on punishing twice for one act — or just ordinary social life; whether the harm that matters is to the target or to everyone watching who stays quiet; and whether there is any way back for the target, or whether the record is permanent.

I want the leaves to include at least the following: the view that this is just consequences and the whole fuss is invented; the view that private sanction is the only accountability the powerful ever actually face; the view that a crowd can never have standing to punish; the view that an employer firing someone is close enough to the state to owe that person a hearing; the view that nobody has a right to a platform; the view that deplatforming silences a person rather than removing a megaphone; the view that the distinctive wrong is the chill it puts on third parties; and the view that the distinctive wrong is that there is no exit. Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or a plain description if it has no name, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Build the thing so that it sorts someone who answers honestly rather than someone who already knows the labels. Six branching questions at most. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can honestly phrase, flag them as inseparable rather than papering over it. Also note any combination of answers that seems to have no defenders at all, and say whether you think that is because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to argue for it.

Keep the cases concrete throughout: a tenured professor, a gig worker with no contract, a company that fires someone for what they said off the clock, an apology that satisfies nobody, and an anonymous pile-on where no one in particular is accountable. No citations are needed and none are wanted.
