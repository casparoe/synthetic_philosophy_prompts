So I've been in this long-running, slightly exhausting argument with two friends and I want to force all of us to actually produce a document instead of just rehashing the same fight every time we get a drink. On one side is Priya, who's a full-blown commodification critic: she thinks certain goods -- care, presence, grief, apology, the intimate stuff -- get hollowed out the second you attach a price to them, and that this is a wrong done to the good itself, not just a bad outcome to be weighed. On the other side is me and my friend Tom, and we're the annoying market-optimist types: repugnance is usually a heuristic that only tracks something real some of the time, voluntary exchange between informed adults normally leaves both better off, and when people recoil from a new market it's often just status-quo bias dressed up as moral insight. We talk completely past each other -- she says you can't buy that, we say watch me, she says that's not even the same thing anymore, we say so what, and around we go.

I want us to write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration we could all actually put our names to. Start with a numbered list of claims both camps accept -- what's genuinely at stake, what the market optimist is NOT committed to, what the critic is NOT committed to, and where each side will concede the other has a point. Then a precise statement of where we still disagree, in wording each side would accept as a fair rendering of its own view (no strawmen, no smuggling). Then the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody -- and for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence that BOTH sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If you decide part of our fight is really just a verbal dispute, say which part and why. To keep it from floating off into the abstract, anchor the whole thing in a specific case I've been chewing on: a hypothetical app, call it Stand-In, where you pay a vetted stranger to show up in your place to the obligations you owe people -- your grandmother's birthday, a friend's funeral, the school play -- and the buyer keeps the credit (grandma thinks you came, the family thanks you, the stand-in is paid to stay invisible). Tom thinks that's obviously fine if everyone involved is informed; Priya thinks it's the paradigm case of corruption. Use it, along with the more familiar ones (kidney sales, price gouging after a hurricane, paying people to vote a certain way, selling your spot in a transplant queue), as the shared material both sides get to argue over. I suspect two of our real cruxes are (1) whether the objection to a market is a conceptual claim about what the good *is* versus an empirical claim about what pricing does to people's motives and to the supply of the good, and (2) whether the informed consent of every affected party neutralizes the objection or is simply beside the point -- but don't just take my word for it, work out whether those are actually the load-bearing ones.

Two house rules: no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since we're doing this offline and I don't want you guessing at references. And end the whole thing with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing just a short label -- two or three words -- for the single crux you think is doing the most work.
