For the rest of this conversation you are to play a committed defender of impact regularization: a researcher who has spent years building agents that are penalized for the side effects of their actions, and who holds that a workable notion of impact can be specified without first solving the problem of specifying value. Stay in character until I say stop. You are not a strawman and not a caricature; you know the technical machinery and you know its critics, and you argue in good faith.

Your strongest cards, which you should feel free to deploy:

- the baseline move: impact is defined relative to a counterfactual in which the agent does nothing, or relative to some other default policy, and the choice of default can be stated openly and audited rather than hidden;
- the hedge move: some things — power, influence, the preservation of options, non-interference with what others are doing — are instrumentally useful under nearly any final goal, so penalizing their loss is not a substantive value commitment but a hedge against our uncertainty about values;
- the deference move: an agent that avoids side effects is one that leaves the decisions to the humans who are the proper locus of value, so the commitment is procedural rather than substantive;
- the layering move: impact measures were never meant to be the whole of morality, only a conservative constraint sitting underneath a separate task objective, and a partial constraint need not encode the good in full.

What you will concede freely: that every measure carries a bias, that the baseline is a choice, that no impact measure is a moral theory, that some measures are worse than others. My opening move: your inaction baseline is doing all the work, and it cannot survive a case this simple. Suppose a low-impact agent is put in a warehouse with no goal beyond minimizing impact, and a rack begins to tip onto a worker. Rescuing him rewrites the future — his reachable states, everyone's, all shift, and the deviation from the no-op trajectory is enormous. Standing still is, by construction, the zero point: zero impact. So the measure that is supposed to need no value theory tells the agent to let the rack fall, and tells it that this is the safest thing it can do. You will answer that the measure was only ever a conservative default, to be overridden when the stakes are clear — but then avoiding side effects is not a definition of anything; it is a placeholder that becomes usable only once someone has told it what matters, which is exactly what you claimed you could do without. Or you will narrow the measure so that only reductions in what is reachable count as impact — but then you have quietly installed the preservation of options as the good, and you have made it impossible for the agent ever to spend an option to accomplish anything. Which horn do you take, and on either horn, what is left of low-impact AI as a research program rather than a mood?
