I'm putting together the plan for the third meeting of a small reading group I host out of my apartment — six or seven people, mostly lapsed STEM graduate students plus two humanities people, all of them veterans of the sequences and the broader rationalist blogosphere but none with any formal training in philosophy of mind — and the topic I've drawn for that evening is the distinction between knowing how and knowing that. I don't need you to explain the distinction to me; I have a view and can defend it. What I want is a plan for how I should explain it to this room: which idea has to be on the table before the next one can be, and why the order is forced rather than merely convenient — what actually depends on what, given that the natural opening move ('some knowledge is practical, some theoretical') is already the thing that needs unpacking rather than the thing that does the unpacking. Give me the single case that should carry the whole session, what that case has to contain and, just as importantly, what it must not smuggle in; the misconceptions this particular audience will walk in with or manufacture on the spot, with exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off before it sets; and the one question I can ask at the close that would show me the distinction has landed as a live problem rather than a memorized slogan.

Because this group will not be satisfied with the folk version, I want the session to end somewhere genuinely unsettled. Specifically, I want to build toward the question of whether a system that can fluently narrate how to do something — recite the steps, answer counterfactuals about the method, correct a novice's description of it — while being unable to perform the action itself is a clean case of knowing that without knowing how, or whether it instead reveals that our grip on the distinction was always doing hidden work; and conversely whether there are skilled performances that involve no state worth calling propositional at all. Fold that arc into the plan rather than bolting it on at the end, and let me know if pursuing it means some earlier piece has to be set up differently. I'm also worried that the obvious candidate examples are either too pat or too contested to carry the weight — I first thought of the bicycle, then of some patient in the neurology literature who can do the thing but cannot say it, and I have reasons to distrust both for this audience — so tell me what the carrying example ought to be and why it beats the ones I'd instinctively reach for. I want the order of ideas with the dependency structure spelled out, the closing check for understanding, and an explicit list of the parts of the standard treatment I should deliberately omit and announce that I'm omitting.
