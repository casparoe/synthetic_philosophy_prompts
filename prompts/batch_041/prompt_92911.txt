I want to practice defending the thirder position on the Sleeping Beauty problem, so please play a committed halfer for the rest of this conversation: sharp, well-informed, someone who has genuinely thought hard about self-locating belief and who will concede small points when pressed but will not surrender the claim that Beauty's credence in heads stays at one half. Stay in character until I say stop. Reply in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, the way one would argue in a long letter — and keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it feels like a live exchange. Reason about the problem however you like, but do not cite papers, books, or authors by name; I want the argument itself, not the literature.

Here is my opening move. The standard setup: on Sunday a fair coin is flipped and Beauty is put to sleep. If it lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday, and the experiment ends. If it lands tails, she is woken on Monday, put back to sleep with her memory of that waking erased, and woken again on Tuesday, and then the experiment ends. Whenever she wakes she cannot tell which day it is, and she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads. You halfers say one half, on the grounds that waking tells her nothing new; I say one third. But I don't want to rehash that, because I think the coin is a distraction doing illegitimate work. Change only this: replace the coin with a fair six-sided die. If the die shows one, Beauty is woken once, on Monday. On any of the other five outcomes, she is woken on six consecutive days, Monday through Saturday, again with amnesia between days. When she wakes and is asked her credence that the die showed one, the halfer rule plainly says one sixth, since the die is fair and the waking tells her nothing new. Yet the thirder's own counting gives one in thirty-one: for each of the five long outcomes she has six awakenings to be in, so the single one-outcome awakening is swamped. If my counting is right, then your no-new-information premise is doing no work — it licenses one sixth in the die case and one half in the coin case for no principled reason, and your answer is just whatever the naive prior was, dressed up. So here is what I want from you: either show me why the die case really is one sixth and the coin case really is one half without the principle quietly changing meaning between them, or concede that the halfer answer is unstable. Which is it?
