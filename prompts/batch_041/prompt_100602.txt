I keep arriving at verdicts about when centralized allocation can work, and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them. A planning board that sets prices for a fixed basket of consumer goods under stable demand and known technology: I think it does roughly as well as a market. The same board allocating capital goods across industries when the relevant knowledge is dispersed, tacit, and changing: I think it fails, and not merely because of incentive problems. A Lange-style board that adjusts prices by trial and error until markets clear, with managers told to minimize cost and follow a rule for investment: I think this works for consumer goods but not for investment, because the investment rule must encode the very knowledge the board lacks. A single firm coordinating its divisions by internal transfer prices: I think it works, and the calculation problem does not bite there in the same way. A system in which firms are worker-owned but prices are set by a central auctioneer that also runs a prediction market on future capital-goods prices: I think it is better than the Lange board but still not a market, and I cannot say exactly why. My tentative principle is that the calculation problem bites when the knowledge needed for allocation is generated in the course of exchange itself, so no prior aggregation can capture it. Does that principle survive these cases, or does it need repair? What does it say about a case I keep going back and forth on—a fully algorithmic planner that ingests every transaction in real time and updates prices continuously? Please state how confident you are in the principle you propose and identify the single verdict of mine you are least sure about.
