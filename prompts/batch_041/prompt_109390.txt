I spent most of last night arguing with my friend Rowan about epistemic injustice — she works on philosophy of science, I'm a lawyer who reads more of this literature than is good for me — and the argument ended where it always ends: she says my verdicts on particular cases are a mess that no single principle can capture, and I say the mess is in her principle, not in my verdicts. I'd like you to settle it. Take the role of someone who has thought seriously about the ethics of credibility and about when a credibility deficit wrongs the person it is aimed at. Work carefully through my cases before you say anything general, and please resist reciting the usual testimonial/hermeneutical taxonomy back at me — I know the taxonomy, and the question is whether it actually fits what I think. Here are my verdicts. (1) A grant panel discounts a proposal partly because the applicant speaks with a heavy regional accent: wrong. (2) An emergency physician halves the weight she gives a patient's own report of pain because the patient is a young woman: wrong. (3) A triage tool trained on historical data systematically down-weights self-reports from one demographic group; no clinician intends anything by it, and no individual is even identifiable as the cause: still wrong, though I cannot say who wrongs whom. (4) A friend discounts my account of being talked over in a meeting on the grounds that I am 'sensitive': wrong. (5) A referee discounts an author's claims because the author has a documented history of overclaiming: not wrong at all. (6) A jury discounts a witness who has previously committed perjury: not wrong. (7) I discount my brother's version of a family quarrel because he always casts himself as the wronged party: not wrong. (8) A hearer discounts a speaker's report of her own pain because, as a statistical matter, people from her group report pain at lower thresholds: wrong — and this is the case that makes trouble for me, because it looks structurally like (5) and (6). (9) A woman who has made it in a male-dominated field tells a junior woman that her complaint about the same treatment is 'not going to get you anywhere,' and stops taking it seriously: wrong, but a different flavor of wrong from (1)–(4), and I cannot say why. (10) A student who has absorbed the idea that people like her are bad at mathematics quietly discounts her own correct answer; nobody else does anything: something is wrong here, but not a wrong done by anyone present. (11) A worker experiences a pattern of subtle exclusion but has no vocabulary for it and cannot get anyone to grasp what she is describing; nobody disbelieves her, they simply cannot see the thing: wrong, but not a credibility deficit at all. (12) A hiring committee correctly concludes that a candidate's claim is unreliable, and reaches that conclusion by mocking her: the conclusion is fine, the manner is not. (13) A confident, well-spoken man is believed well beyond what his evidence supports: epistemically bad, but I do not think anyone has been wronged. (14) The one I keep going back and forth on: a parent discounts her ten-year-old's report of being bullied at school as 'kids exaggerate.' I want to say it is wrong, but I cannot say what makes it different from (5)–(7), and children do sometimes exaggerate.

The principle I defended to Rowan was roughly the Fricker-style one: a credibility deficit is an injustice when it is produced by identity prejudice rather than by evidence about this particular speaker's reliability, and when it deprives her of something she needs. That handles (1), (2), and (4), and leaves (5)–(7) alone, which is why I like it. Rowan says it dies on (3), (8), (9), (10), (11), and (12) — no prejudice, or no agent, or no deficit, or a deficit that is perfectly apt — and that once I patch it for each of those I will be left with a gerrymander rather than a principle. She also says (12) shows I am using 'wrong' for two different things and should stop.

Give me your best reconstruction. Start by saying, in your own words, what pattern you think my verdicts are tracking, including the ones that pull against each other; then set out two or three candidate principles, stated precisely, each with a note of which case forces each clause into it; then say what each candidate predicts for at least three cases I have not mentioned; then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what I should give up; and end with the bottom line — the single best principle you can state, or a clear statement of why the cases admit none. Please organize the response under exactly these headings, in this order: 'Reading the cases' (about 200 words), 'Candidate principles' (about 500 words), 'Cases you didn't give me' (about 350 words), 'What I have to give up' (about 250 words), and 'Bottom line' (about 100 words). Do not cite literature — argue from the cases — and flag any place where you think I have misdescribed one of my own verdicts.
