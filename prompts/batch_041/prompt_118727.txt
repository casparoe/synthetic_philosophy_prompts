You are a philosopher of history with serious footing in both metaphysics and metaethics, and I would like your help untangling a slogan that keeps doing heavy lifting in arguments around me, usually without anyone stopping to ask what it actually claims. The slogan, in the shortened form people around me use, is 'the arc of history bends toward justice'. I first met it as a teenager, in the mouth of a great-uncle who had lived through a coup, and he used it in the consoling register: things look terrible now, but the arc bends, so be patient. Since then I have heard it in at least three other registers, and I doubt they share a single underlying claim. At a climate demonstration an organizer used it as a reason for urgency, on the grounds that the arc bends only because people push on it, so get to work. In a graduate seminar a colleague used it in what sounded like a straightforwardly predictive register, as a well-supported empirical generalization about the long run, and someone objected that this is just Whig history: we call the victors' settlements 'justice' and then notice, unsurprisingly, that history produced them. And in a family argument about reparations an uncle used it to say that justice simply is whatever history eventually settles on, which struck me as either a truism or a horror depending on the reading.

What I want from you is a careful sorting rather than a defense or a debunking. Before you write anything, brainstorm privately about the range of distinct things the sentence could be asserting; then lay them out for me as a menu, running from the near-trivial to the metaphysically ambitious, with a verdict on each: is it a claim that could be true or false at all, and if so, is it platitudinous, substantive and defensible, or plainly false? I am especially interested in the difference between readings that posit a telos, an endpoint the process is aimed at, whether by design or immanently in the manner of the broadly Hegelian or Marxist philosophies of history, and readings that posit only a drift or a probabilistic tendency with no destination; and in whether the words inside the metaphor — an arc, a bending, a direction, a destination — smuggle in commitments that a careful version would have to surrender. I would also like to know what would have to be true for each reading to hold: what empirical regularities would have to obtain, what theory of moral truth (if any) it presupposes, what account of who or what does the bending, and over what units — individual lives, states, institutions, the species — the claim is to be evaluated.

Finally, tell me for each of the four uses I described which reading it actually needs, whether that reading survives your verdicts, and what a defensible version of that use would sound like. Where a reading turns out to be unfalsifiable, or to define justice as whatever happens to win, say so plainly, and say whether the slogan in those contexts is better understood not as an assertion at all but as an expression of hope or a commitment to act. Please be explicit about which steps in your reasoning are conceptual and which are empirical, and flag any place where you think the question I am asking is itself confused.
