Bit of background: I did an MA in philosophy years ago, drifted into nursing, and I've been doing hospice and palliative care for about six years now. Last month my team burned twenty minutes arguing over whether a patient had started actively dying yet — half of us yes, half of us no. What's been eating at me is that if you'd asked any of us the same question three days earlier, we'd of split the same way about that day too. Then I pulled the chart and read the notes from her admission and it was blindingly obvious how far she'd come. Nothing changes day to day, everything changes over a month, and my verdict flips depending on which direction I look from. Same person, same facts. It's been bugging me for weeks. I've thought alot about it.

I'm putting together a collection of philosophy prompts and I want this one to actually be hard, so here's what I'm after.

Take the role of a philosopher who specializes in vagueness and the sorites paradox — you know epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theory, contextualism, the whole map. Think carefully before you commit to anything. Start by sketching three or four candidate series and saying briefly why each does or doesn't do the job, then pick one and run with it.

The task: construct a sorites series engineered so that one and the same item gets opposite verdicts depending on which direction you run the series — ascending from the obvious end, then the whole thing in reverse — with the item and every fact about it held completely fixed. The two verdicts have to fall out of the case. Don't just declare that the judgments flip; that's stipulating the answer and it doesn't count. The whole point is to see which theories predict the flip and which can only clean up after it.

Constraints:

- Realistic. No colour patches, no bald men, no grains of sand, no sci-fi, no invented gadgets. Institutional — a clinic, a licensing office, a tenure committee, a regulatory threshold — the kind of thing that actually happens to people.
- One paragraph for the scenario itself. Analysis after.
- Finite series, both endpoints undisputed, and at least one crux item in the middle.
- The facts along the series have to be monotonic and fully known to whoever's judging. No hidden information, no ignorance of the facts. The vagueness has to live in the predicate, not in our evidence.
- Don't hand me my hospice example back. Construct something.

Then walk me through it. For each of epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theory, and contextualism: one line on what the view says the underlying problem is, then what it must say about the flip — does it predict it, merely accommodate it, or does it have to call one of the two verdicts flat-out mistaken? Say what each view assigns as the status of the crux item, and whether it can say the two verdicts differ in correctness. Then: which view comes out worst, and what's the strongest move its defender has to defuse the case? How would a contextualist distinguish a genuine context shift from me just being tired and inconsistent? Which single feature of your scenario is doing the real work, and if I strip it out, does the case collapse back into the boring colour-patch version? If you're unsure the case really satisfies one of the constraints, say so out loud instead of papering over it. No references or reading lists — I want the argument.
