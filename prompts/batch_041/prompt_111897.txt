For some years now I have been reading Borges on my own, without any training in philosophy, and the thing I cannot settle is how many different times he seems to believe in. At least three keep surfacing, and I can't get them to sit still together. In one, time is a circle: everything that has happened comes round again, the dreamer turns out to be dreamed, and the cycle shuts on itself. In another, time is a garden of forking paths, so that every choice I did not make is nevertheless lived out somewhere in the branching. In the third, time is an illusion altogether: strictly there is only this present moment and nothing ever really passes, so succession is something we add. The circle says the same thing returns; the fork says everything that could happen does; the illusion says nothing returns, because nothing ever leaves. I want a poem that holds all three without adjudicating among them, and I would much rather the form carry the argument than have the verse explain it.

Write it as a pantoum, six quatrains, in the voice of someone who has lived long enough to be unsure whether they are the dreamer or the dreamed — because a pantoum does two things at once: its lines come back, which is the circle, but they come back into new surroundings, which is the fork. Two further constraints. First, close the loop: make the final stanza's second and fourth lines the opening stanza's first and third lines, so that the poem ends by re-entering its own beginning and could be read round again. Second, let one recurring line mean something like the circle the first time it appears and something like the fork the last time, so that a reader can point to the single repetition where the sense turns over. Don't name Borges or any of his stories; the three pictures should be recognizable from inside the poem, and someone who has never read him should still find it a good poem.
