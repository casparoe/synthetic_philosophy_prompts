I am the head teaching assistant for a large second-year survey course in early modern philosophy, and I am working through the midterm essays before handing the marks up to the instructor. One of the short-essay questions, worth ten points, was the following: "Reconstruct Berkeley's so-called master argument for immaterialism as it is presented in the Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous. State the most serious objection to it, and then assess whether Berkeley's appeal to God rescues the argument. Your answer should make clear what the argument is meant to establish, what it does not establish, and how it bears on the status of minds or spirits." The marking scheme I have to apply gives credit for an accurate reconstruction in the student's own words, a fair statement of the strongest objection, a correct account of the work the appeal to God actually does, and a defensible verdict; it penalises misattributions of doctrine or text, and arguments that assume what they set out to prove. I have no prior impression of this student and would like the grade to rest on the page alone.

The essay I am looking at reads as follows:

"Berkeley's master argument is his central proof that material substance does not exist and that nothing can exist unperceived. We cannot conceive of anything except our own ideas, and whatever we perceive is perceived by us and so is an idea in a mind. It follows that to attempt to conceive of an object existing independently of any mind is to attempt to conceive of something unconceived, which is impossible; and so to exist is to be perceived. Berkeley develops this in the Principles of Human Knowledge, where Hylas concedes the premise and is forced to give up matter. The standard objection is that Berkeley equivocates between the act and the object of conception: when I conceive of a tree that no one is perceiving, the tree that I think about is a tree existing without a perceiver, and the fact that I am now thinking of it does not put a perceiver into the content of what I think. Berkeley can reply, however, that since we have no idea of anything but our own ideas, there is no mind-independent object for the objector to point to, and the objection therefore has no content; the master argument stands. The appeal to God then saves the view from collapsing into a skepticism in which things wink in and out of existence whenever we close our eyes: all things exist in the mind of God, who perceives them eternally, so the tree in the quadrangle goes on existing when no human is looking. Berkeley thus secures the reality and the continuity of the external world, though he denies that it is material. It is worth adding that on his view everything, including the minds that perceive, exists only insofar as it is perceived."

Please grade this essay out of ten and give me the comments that justify the grade, written so that I can paste them into the feedback form. Where a sentence is only loosely worded but not actually mistaken, distinguish that from a genuine error, and where the student reaches a conclusion that happens to be defensible by a bad route, say so. Please also tell me how you would split the ten points across the four rubric items, and flag any part of the essay that a careless reader would pass but that you think deserves comment.
