I lead a monthly philosophy reading group for a half-dozen intelligent non-philosophers — two software engineers, a lawyer, a retired physician, and a couple of others whose last formal encounter with the subject was an undergraduate survey course — and next month's session is on transcendental arguments. I have seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, and no assigned reading; I want to spend the time making the shape of the thing clear rather than marching through anyone's text. I know the topic well enough to talk about it, but I've never had to build it up from nothing for people who arrive without the vocabulary, and I'm worried that the moment I say "conditions of the possibility" I'll lose half the room to a nodding fog. So don't give me the explanation — give me the plan for the explanation. What has to be introduced first and what has to wait, and in each case why the later piece is unintelligible without the earlier one; where the load-bearing distinction between a transcendental argument and an ordinary inference to the best explanation has to be planted, and how early; and which of the standard dialectical moves I should name outright versus let the room discover.

I had thought the dream argument — that everything you are now experiencing could be a dream — would carry the whole session, but I suspect it is a trap: tell me whether it can bear the weight or whether I need a different case, and if I need a different one, what it must have and must not have. And I need the misconception map, in the order the room will actually produce them. I am expecting at least the "transcendental means transcendent, i.e. spooky or supernatural" confusion; the assumption that these arguments are meant to prove the external world exists and are therefore cheating; the collapse into "so it is just the best explanation"; and the suspicion that "you cannot deny X without presupposing X" is a verbal trick rather than an argument. For each, tell me the point in the sequence where I should head it off and roughly what I should say, rather than letting it form and repairing it afterward. Finally, give me one question to put to them at the end that would show whether they have actually grasped the form of the argument as opposed to being able to recite its conclusion, and tell me plainly what I should refuse to go into at this level — I am assuming both the historical machinery of Kant's own deduction and the question of whether the whole strategy can ever yield more than a conclusion about what we must believe are out of reach here, but say so if I am wrong about either.
