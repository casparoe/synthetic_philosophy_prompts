My philosophy of perception seminar has spent most of this term on the question of whether perception is cognitively penetrable, and we are further apart now than we were in September. I have come to suspect that we are not so much disagreeing as failing to engage, and I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of the argument.

The deadlock has a stable shape. One side holds that expectation, desire, and emotion change what is seen, and not merely what is believed or reported: the dieter for whom the cake looks more tempting, the anxious walker for whom the shadow resolves into a bear, the subject who has been led to expect a red object and sees red. On this view these are changes in perceptual experience itself. The other side accepts the phenomena but relocates them: what presents itself as cognitive influence is really pre-perceptual selection (attention, adaptation, task set) or post-perceptual judgment, memory, and response bias, and once those are controlled for, nothing left over deserves the name 'perceptual'. In class the exchange then turns on whether the controls are adequate, and adequacy seems to depend on whether the mechanism being controlled for lies inside or outside perception, which is precisely the matter in dispute. Both sides, I notice, still speak as though 'perception' named a stage in a processing hierarchy, and as though 'penetration' were simply the negation of the encapsulation that modularist accounts attribute to early vision; I want to know whether that inherited picture, rather than any experimental finding, is what keeps the argument turning.

One of us proposed a test case I cannot get out of my head: a subject whose expectations about an ambiguous stimulus are manipulated, and whose discrimination threshold, measured by a task that does not require reporting the stimulus, shifts accordingly. Does such a result settle anything, or does it merely relocate the dispute to whether the threshold task counts as perceptual?

What I want from you is a diagnosis. Is this a substantive disagreement about how the mind is in fact organized? A verbal one about the extension of 'perception', 'cognitive', and 'direct', which would dissolve if the parties agreed to talk about something else? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, one about whether the content of experience varies with central states and the other about whether the processes fixing that content are sensitive to them? If there is a hidden empirical question about where in the processing stream an effect is located, is that question even well posed, given that the stages are individuated by the very theories that disagree? Say what in each position is doing the real work, and tell me what would count as progress here: a criterion for the perceptual that does not presuppose one of the camps, a formal characterization of directness, an experiment the other side could not redescribe, or a decision to stop asking whether perception is penetrable and ask instead which effects are, under which conditions. If you think nothing would move it, say so, and say why.
