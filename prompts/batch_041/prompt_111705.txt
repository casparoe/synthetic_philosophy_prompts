I'm in a reading group on AI and moral status and we hit a wall last week. A friend brought in this argument, which she says she half-believes but can't defend, and none of us could figure out where it breaks. It goes:

(1) A system has experiences, and so is a proper object of moral concern, exactly when it realizes the right kind of computation -- the functional organization that, in humans, gives rise to experience. (2) Whether a physical system "realizes" a given computation isn't a brute fact about the system; for basically any physical system you can cook up a description under which it implements any finite computation you like (the trick where you treat its states as the rows of a giant look-up table, or assign the "program" to whatever causal grain of the thing you please). (3) So for any physical system there is some description under which it realizes the experience-producing computation. (4) So any physical system -- this coffee cup, the wall, a rock -- is having experiences, and is a proper object of moral concern, under some description. (5) But moral concern can't be that cheap: whether it's wrong to shatter the rock can't turn on which of infinitely many descriptions we happen to pick. (6) So either (1) is false -- realizing the right computation isn't what makes something a subject -- or "realizes" has to mean something much stronger than (2) allows.

I definately want to keep (1); it's the only thing that lets me say a suitably built machine could be a someone. But (5) also seems unarguable, and (2) is just... true? Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine, and what it costs to give it up. If the fix is that "realization" has to be a causal or mechanistic notion rather than a descriptive one, spell out what that buys and what it rules out -- because I have a nagging feeling the stronger notion is exactly what the anti-functionalists want and I'd be handing them the store. And does the answer change depending on whether the machine is built out of neurons, silicon, or levers and gears?
