I'm building a set of teaching materials on how psychiatric categories shape the very people they claim to describe, and I want a short story that dramatizes the phenomenon instead of explaining it. Here is the premise; please treat all clinical details as invented and do not import any real diagnostic manual, real disorder, or real researcher into the story. In a mid-sized coastal town whose fishing and shipbuilding economies have been contracting for a generation, the regional health authority adopts a new diagnosis — call it Chronic Diminishment Response, or CDR — meant to capture a pattern clinicians kept encountering but could not name: pervasive fatigue, a shrunken sense of the future, low-grade shame, and a habit of treating one's own plans as hypothetical. The diagnosis has five criteria, one of which, 'a reduced horizon of expectation,' is vague enough to stretch. What interests me is the feedback loop that follows. The category is introduced to describe a pattern; patients begin to understand themselves through it; their self-descriptions drift toward the criteria; and the clinicians who wrote the criteria, noticing that prevalence has doubled, quietly revise the criteria to match what they now hear in the clinic. I want the story to make that loop visible in the grain of particular lives rather than in a lecture.

Please build the story around two figures whose paths cross: a woman in her late fifties, recently laid off from the last cannery, who is handed the intake form during a routine visit; and a young psychiatrist who sat on the committee that wrote CDR and is now uneasy about it. Move through at least three distinct moments. In the first, the intake questionnaire's own wording hands the woman a vocabulary for her distress that she did not have before — let the reader watch the questions suggest their own answers. In the second, show the category doing something genuinely good: a support group, a name for what had been nameless, a way of being believed by a spouse. In the third, show the same category turned against her by someone else — an insurer, an employer, or a family member who uses it to mean she is simply unreliable — and then take us into the committee room, where the psychiatrist argues with a colleague over whether the rising numbers are evidence that CDR is real or evidence that the criteria have become circular, describing only what the form taught people to say.

Leave the story unresolved on the question I care about most: whether CDR discovered a kind of suffering that was always there or brought a new kind of person into being. Give both a realist and a constructivist a fair hearing inside the fiction — the realist pointing to the patients who say the label saved them, the constructivist pointing to the ones who say they were fine until someone told them what to call it — and let the psychiatrist end up genuinely uncertain, not converted. Aim for something between four and seven pages, in close third person, and resist the temptation to have any character state the lesson aloud.
