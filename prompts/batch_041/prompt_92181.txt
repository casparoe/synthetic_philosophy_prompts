I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and most of my working vocabulary for questions of collective choice comes from that corner of the internet rather than from any formal course in social choice. I think in terms of agents with utility functions, a mechanism designer who selects a social choice function, feasibility constraints kept distinct from incentive constraints, Pareto improvements, and — because it keeps coming up — the value-aggregation subproblem of the alignment problem, alongside the futarchy idea of voting on values while betting on beliefs. I can follow the mathematics at the level of a competent amateur, but I have never had this material taught to me systematically.

I want you to take Sen's liberal paradox and restate it in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. The paradox as I carry it around is this: we want a social decision rule to satisfy three conditions at once. Unrestricted domain, so that any profile of individual preference orderings is admissible. Weak Pareto, so that if every individual strictly prefers one alternative to another, society does too. And minimal liberalism, so that there are at least two individuals each of whom is decisive over at least one pair of alternatives — if that individual strictly prefers one of the pair to the other, society must as well. Sen's result is that no rule satisfies all three once there are at least two people and three alternatives; the usual illustration hands each of two people the right to decide whether he himself reads a particular book, and then exhibits the conflict.

Please translate each of those three conditions into my vocabulary, one at a time, and be explicit about the move you are making in each case: which condition maps onto a feasibility constraint, which onto an incentive constraint, which onto a constraint the designer simply honors, and which has to be redescribed as the assignment of a decision right over a coordinate to an agent. For each, tell me what survives without loss, what survives only under distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. I am especially suspicious that the natural rationalist move is to treat minimal liberalism as a species of property right and then reach for Coasean bargaining or a market to internalize the externality, and I want you to tell me whether that move dissolves the paradox or merely translates it into a different theorem, and why. If my vocabulary quietly converts a deontological constraint into an alienable entitlement, say so plainly.

Then the residue report proper: what in the original does my vocabulary fail to express? Is the leftover — the sense that a right is something society is not permitted to override even when overriding would be Pareto-superior — a real feature that mechanism design and decision theory cannot capture, or is it a sign that the original was trading on an ambiguity between what a rule is and what a rule ought to be? If the residue is real, say what it would cost me to add it: does my framework need a new primitive, or does it merely need me to be more careful about which constraints I am treating as chosen and which as binding? I would rather have an honest translation that confesses its losses than a smooth one that smuggles my framework's assumptions in as though they were neutral, so treat the residue as the point of the exercise.
