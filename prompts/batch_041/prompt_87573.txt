I have been reading John McTaggart's 1908 paper on the unreality of time, and there is a stretch early on that I can follow sentence by sentence but not as a single piece of reasoning. He writes:

It is possible, however, that this is merely subjective. It may be the case that the distinction introduced among positions in time by the A series — the distinction of past, present and future — is simply a constant illusion of our minds, and that the real nature of time only contains the distinction of the B series — the distinction of earlier and later. In that case we could not perceive time as it really is, but we might be able to think of it as it really is. This is not a very common view, but it has found able supporters. I believe it to be untenable, because, as I said above, it seems to me that the A series is essential to the nature of time, and that any difficulty in the way of regarding the A series as real is equally a difficulty in the way of regarding time as real. It would, I suppose, be universally admitted that time involves change. A particular thing, indeed, may exist unchanged through any amount of time. But when we ask what we mean by saying that there were different moments of time, or a certain duration of time, through which the thing was the same, we find that we mean that it remained the same while other things were changing. A universe in which nothing whatever changed (including the thoughts of the conscious beings in it) would be a timeless universe. If, then, a B series without an A series can constitute time, change must be possible without an A series. Let us suppose that the distinction of past, present and future does not apply to reality. Can change apply to reality? What is it that changes? Could we say that, in a time which formed a B series but not an A series, the change consisted in the fact that an event ceased to be an event, while another event began to be an event? If this were the case, we should certainly have got a change. But this is impossible. An event can never cease to be an event. It can never get out of any time series in which it once is.

What I cannot get straight is the work done by the claim that the distinction of past, present and future is as essential to time as the distinction of earlier and later. My instinct is that someone who believes in an eternal block universe — who holds that the past and the future are as real as the present, and that "now" is just where a given observer happens to sit — could accept everything else in this passage and simply deny that claim, treating the A series as a fact about us rather than about time. McTaggart seems to see that reply coming and to treat it as a position he has to refute rather than one he can set aside, and I want to understand why. In particular, why should it follow that if the B series alone were real, change would be impossible? And why does it matter that an event can never cease to be an event? Please lay the passage out as an argument: what each step is owed to, where a defender of the block universe would push back, and whether that pushback amounts to denying one of his premises or to granting them all and rejecting only his conclusion. I would rather have the reasoning taken slowly than a summary of where he ends up.
